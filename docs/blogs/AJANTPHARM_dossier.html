Three terms carry most of the weight. ROCE, or return on capital employed, measures how much operating profit the business earns on the money tied up in it. A figure of 35 percent means that for every 100 rupees of capital in the business, roughly 35 rupees of operating profit is produced before tax. Anything above 15 percent clears the first hurdle, and the framework gives extra credit above 18 percent. Cash conversion, measured as cash from operations divided by net profit over three years, asks whether reported profit turns into cash that actually arrives in the bank. A ratio of 1.0 means every rupee of profit came back as operating cash. A ratio below 0.70 means the profit figures are running ahead of the cash, which is the classic early sign of a business that books sales it has not collected.

The technical side uses the price history. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days. The 50-day average is the medium-term trend line and the 200-day average is the long-term one. A stock that trades above both, with both lines rising, is in what the framework calls Stage 2, the markup phase where buyers are in control. A stock that has stopped rising and is slipping below its trend lines is in Stage 3, the topping or pullback phase. The framework does not buy Stage 3 names, however good the business, because the chart says the market is not yet paying up for the story. 2 What the company does

This chapter explains the business in plain terms, because the rest of the report depends on what Ajanta actually sells and where. A pharmaceutical company that sells branded generics, which are medicines sold under the company's own brand names after the original patent has expired, earns its money differently from a company that makes generic copies for other firms to market. Ajanta is mainly the first kind. Its revenue depends on doctors prescribing its brands, on the number of medical representatives in the field, and on regulators approving its products and factories for export. The company describes itself as primarily engaged in the development, manufacturing and marketing of speciality pharmaceutical formulations, and the company's own description on Screener (as of 12 Oct 2026) matches that picture.

The business in brief

The company runs several businesses that report under one consolidated set of accounts. The largest by growth in the June 2026 quarter was the United States generics business, which sold 487 crore rupees, up 57 percent on a year earlier. In the same quarter India branded generics sold 509 crore rupees, up 24 percent, according to the Investorstack call summary (as of 30 Jun 2026). The India business is the company's home base and is built around doctors in a handful of therapy areas. The call summary puts the mix at cardiology 37 percent, ophthalmology 29 percent, dermatology 22 percent, pain management 10 percent and new therapy areas 2 percent. Ajanta says it is in the top ten of every core therapy segment, which is a claim about its position, not a figure this report could verify independently.

Africa and Asia form the emerging-market arm. Africa branded generics sold 295 crore rupees in the quarter, up 30 percent, per the Multibagg Q1 article (as of 30 Jun 2026). Asia branded generics sold 255 crore rupees, down 16 percent, according to the Tulsian AI results note (as of 30 Jun 2026). The Investorstack summary lists the same Asia line at 225 crore rupees, which does not reconcile with its own 16 percent decline against a base of 304 crore rupees, so the 255 crore figure is used here. The Asia decline is attributed to Middle East supply-chain disruption, and some dispatches slipped into the next quarter.

The company's facilities are the second half of the story. The most important plant for the United States business is Paithan in Maharashtra. A third-party analysis states that the Paithan site contributes about 70 percent of US sales, citing its own estimate on the Research Delta impact analysis (as of 21 Apr 2026). The company has not confirmed that figure in any primary document this research could read, so it is treated as a secondary estimate. The same analysis says 11 of the Paithan products have backup manufacturing elsewhere, and that is the reason the site concentration is a risk rather than a certainty of disruption.

Why the business model matters for this decision

Branded generics earn high margins when the brand is strong and the product is hard to copy quickly, but they lose margin as competitors arrive and the price falls. The company's own guidance in the June call was for US price erosion, which is why it expects US growth to slow to the mid-single digits in FY27 even though the June quarter grew 57 percent. A reader should therefore treat the Q1 US figure as a peak of a cycle rather than a new run rate, a point the next chapters return to. The business also depends on regulators. An FDA inspection that produces a serious finding can stop shipments from a plant for months, which is why the USFDA status of Paithan appears in the operating scorecard that this report did not run.

The company is also investing heavily. Management guided capital expenditure of about 400 crore rupees for FY27, of which about 100 crore is maintenance and the rest is expansion at the Pithampur site. Research and development is guided at about 5 percent of revenue, and five to seven US filings are planned for the year, with most launches weighted to the fourth quarter and subject to FDA approval. Each of those items is a claim about the future. A reader who wants to test the company's plan should check each one against the next two quarters of reported results.

What follows for an owner

For an owner of the shares, the important questions are whether the India and Africa franchises keep growing while US prices fall, and whether the Paithan plant stays clean enough to keep shipping. The first question is answered by segment results. The second is answered by inspection outcomes and by any notices to the exchanges. Both are covered in Chapters 3, 5 and 8. The business is good enough that the framework expects it to be a candidate in future cycles. The price is not yet good enough for a purchase today.

4 Layer 1 gates

Layer 1 is the set of hard pass-or-fail tests. The framework applies all of them, and a single failure rejects the candidate. The three financial gates

The return on capital gate is the first line. ROCE of 35 percent clears the 15 percent floor by a wide margin and clears the 18 percent level the framework reserves for Core names. The figure is the company's own ratio, computed by the data provider from audited accounts, so it is an output of reported numbers rather than an estimate. The debt gate is the second line. A debt-to-equity ratio of 0.06 is far under the 0.70 ceiling, and the sensitivity to the borrowings disagreement described in Chapter 3 is nil, because even the highest figure in the sources yields a ratio well below the ceiling. The cash conversion gate is the third line. The three-year figure of 0.89 clears 0.70 with some room. The gate is computed over cumulative figures, so it passes even though FY26 alone fails it at 0.50. The framework's three-year design is deliberate, because it smooths single-year noise, and it is the reason the FY26 weakness is a warning rather than a rejection. The cushion is thin, though: the 0.89 figure leans on FY25's 1.26, so a second year near FY26's 0.50 would pull the three-year figure toward the 0.70 floor.

The asymmetry gate

The asymmetry gate compares the reward from buying with the risk. The framework's measure is the distance from the entry to the target divided by the distance from the entry to the stop, and it needs two inputs that this research could not fully verify. The first is a target anchored to either a technical measured move or forward earnings multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio. The second is a stop placed according to the framework's stop rule, which requires the ledger's swing-low and moving-average levels. The stop reference is available from the ledger. The target is not. Forward earnings are available from a consensus median of 98.50 rupees for FY27, and the three-year median price-to-earnings ratio is not published in any source the research could open. The five-year medians seen in search snippets could not be opened and are not used. Because the target is unverified, the framework records the asymmetry gate as FAIL and the report does not invent a target to pass it.

The illustrative arithmetic is still useful for a reader, provided it is clearly labelled. The 52-week high of 3,796.0 rupees is a level the stock has already traded at, but it is not a measured move or an earnings-based value. If the 52-week high were used as a target, the reward-to-risk ratio would be about 4.0 to 1 against the tactical stop reference of 3,313.64 rupees, and about 1.0 to 1 against the core stop reference of 3,020.92 rupees. The difference is large, and it shows that the stop choice drives the answer. This is exactly why the framework requires the stop rule and the target to be fixed before the number is read, and why the report does not use these figures to argue for a purchase.

5 Layer 2 KPI scorecard

The operating scorecard is the second layer of the framework. It picks three to five measures that drive the specific business, and scores each as PASS, WATCH or FAIL. For a pharmaceutical company the framework names four measures: USFDA inspection compliance, gross margin stability, working capital and cash conversion, and the commercialisation of the pipeline. This report names US growth durability as the fifth.

Why the scorecard was not run

Layer 2 is only evaluated when Layer 1 passes. Because the technical gate fails, the scorecard is not scored, and this chapter does not give PASS, WATCH or FAIL labels. Scoring the operating measures now would give the reader a sense of a verdict the framework has already withheld. The chapter still explains what the scorecard would look at and what the public evidence currently shows, so that a reader who wants to follow the company's operating story has the facts in one place. Nothing in this chapter changes the verdict.

The measures the scorecard would use

USFDA compliance is the first measure. A Form 483 is the list of observations an FDA investigator writes at the end of an inspection, and it is a warning that the plant must fix problems. The ladder the framework would apply

The average true range, or ATR, is the stock's typical daily price movement over the last 14 sessions. It is the ruler that makes distances comparable across stocks with different prices. The ladder has four bands. A stock within half an ATR of its support gets the full size band. A stock within one ATR gets the standard size band. A stock within 1.2 ATR gets a limit order only at reduced size. A stock further away gets no buy, because the framework does not chase a price that has moved away from its base. The ATR here is 101.53 rupees, so the bands in rupees are 50.8 for the full band, 101.5 for the standard band and 121.8 for the limit-order band.

8 Fatal flaws and red team

A fatal flaw is a fact that would, on its own, make the investment wrong. A red-team review asks the strongest honest case against the verdict and the strongest honest case against the company. This chapter does both. It lists the flaws the evidence supports, and it sets out the bear and bull readings that a careful investor would weigh.

Flaw one: the promoter is reducing its stake and pledging shares

The promoter group's holding fell from 66.25 percent at March 2026 to 63.49 percent at June 2026, according to the Screener shareholding table (as of 30 Jun 2026).
