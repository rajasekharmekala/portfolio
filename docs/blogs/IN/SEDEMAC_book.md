## CHAPTER II: Inside the Segments — Products, Customers and Real-World Examples

> *"A product becomes a business when it passes from prototype to the customer’s production line."*
> — **Research Desk maxim**

Let us make the product tangible. A vehicle controller is a rugged electronic system that receives signals from sensors, applies control logic and sends commands to actuators. In an engine application it may coordinate fuel injection, ignition or related functions; in an integrated starter-generator system, the control electronics support engine start-stop and electrical functions. The unit is not a generic box. It must work with the OEM’s engine, wiring, diagnostics, software interfaces, packaging constraints and quality process. The customer is paying for a validated solution that performs reliably inside a larger machine. SEDEMAC says most of its present revenue comes from ECUs, and its latest earnings-call presentation described a close relationship between control-intensive ECU shipment volumes and revenue.

The RHP reports two operating segments: mobility and industrial. In FY25, mobility contributed 85.69% of revenue and industrial 14.31%; in the nine months ended December 2025, those proportions were 84.63% and 15.37%. These are segment revenue shares, not segment profit shares. The disclosed company-level segment schedule and public filings do not provide a basis here for a precise allocation of profit by product line, so we will not manufacture one. The mix is important because a single headline growth rate can hide two different demand engines: vehicles and industrial equipment. Yet both remain part of one supplier with shared engineering and manufacturing exposure.

Start with mobility. The company’s public materials identify engine control systems, integrated starter-generator controllers, ISG-plus-EFI combinations and motor controllers among the product propositions. A concrete example is a TVS Motor vehicle program using a control-intensive ECU. TVS is a named customer and the RHP reports its large revenue share, while the company’s presentation describes products rather than disclosing each vehicle’s commercial terms. The end-to-end sequence is: the OEM defines system requirements; SEDEMAC engineers hardware and control software for the relevant engine or vehicle architecture; prototypes are tested and calibrated; the OEM validates the system within its vehicle; production parts are manufactured and delivered against the OEM’s launch and forecast schedule. The OEM then installs the ECU in vehicles assembled for dealers and consumers. This is a documented business-model example, not a claim about a specific unpublished model award or a hidden unit price.

The commercial value of a mobility controller comes from more than the bill of materials. If it is integrated into vehicle start-stop, engine management or motor-control functions, the OEM must weigh performance, reliability, cost, regulatory needs and serviceability. Qualification creates switching friction because replacing a controller requires new engineering, validation and supply assurance. But switching costs are not absolute: an OEM can dual-source, redesign, renegotiate or move a program at a model refresh. The RHP itself names product acceptance, customer concentration, delivery quality and customer platform performance among business risks. You should therefore treat program integration as a potential moat that has to be renewed, not a permanent lock.

Now consider industrial. SEDEMAC’s disclosures identify genset and other industrial control applications. A representative end-to-end example is a generator-set manufacturer requiring an engine controller matched to its engine and electrical system. The OEM specifies start, sensing, protection and operating behaviors; the supplier develops and validates the ECU with that hardware; accepted production units ship to the genset maker; the maker integrates the controller into a generator package sold to an end user that needs backup or prime power. The source record supports the industrial segment and genset exposure, but it does not name the particular customer in this hypothetical walkthrough. Nor does it disclose a standard list price. Pricing is therefore best described as a negotiated OEM program price for a qualified unit, potentially affected by volume, components, engineering scope and contractual terms; exact customer prices are unavailable.

A segment label is not the same as diversification. Industrial represented 14.31% of FY25 revenue and 15.37% of nine-month FY26 revenue. That is a modest but visible second demand stream. The mobility segment itself also contains different technologies and vehicle classes, but public disclosures in this evidence set do not give enough contribution detail to calculate individual product-family revenue or profit margins. The current presentation’s shipment metric can help investors track the broad ECU engine, but it should not be allocated to a particular product or customer unless the company provides that bridge.

Think of the supply chain in three linked loops. First, the engineering loop: customer requirements, prototype, calibration, validation and design freeze. Second, the production loop: sourcing of semiconductor and PCB inputs, manufacturing, testing, quality release and delivery. Third, the commercial loop: forecasts, purchase orders, invoicing, collections and warranty feedback. A delay or defect in one loop can have consequences in the others. The prospectus reports dependence on top suppliers and imports of critical items, including semiconductors and printed circuit boards from China. That creates exposure to availability, lead times and geopolitical disruption even when the customer relationship is sound.

Across the US, India and Europe, the closest parallel is the embedded supplier model: a US engine-control specialist selling into OEM platforms, an Indian electronics supplier serving a two-wheeler maker, or a European powertrain-control company supporting generator or vehicle systems. The analogy explains the qualification cycle, not the unreported economics. The buyer is an OEM; the end user is often remote from the supplier; and each new production program has to earn its place through reliability, cost and timing. The next chapter will ask who manages that relationship from sale to cash, because a controller shipped is not yet a controller paid for.

Two details help you avoid confusing technical novelty with earnings power. First, a controller’s engineering content can be high while its selling price remains constrained by an OEM’s target cost. The customer compares performance against alternatives, total vehicle cost and the program’s volume commitments. Second, product categories can overlap. A single integrated system may combine functions that earlier required separate modules, so counting product labels can overstate the number of independent revenue engines. The company’s public unit metric—control-intensive ECUs shipped—offers a useful volume indicator, but management says the figure has a strong correlation with revenue rather than providing a fully disclosed price-volume-mix bridge. An investor should watch shipments, realized sales and margins together.

Geography needs the same discipline. The company’s RHP says India represented more than 90% of revenue in the periods presented. It also serves OEMs in the US and Europe, but the existence of export customers does not yet make the business geographically diversified. A region may be strategically important before it becomes material to reported revenue. When the company adds international programs, you and I should ask whether those wins contribute meaningful sales, bring different customers and reduce dependence on domestic two-wheeler platforms—or simply add another destination for the same concentrated product strategy. Until a disclosure quantifies the mix, treat overseas reach as an opportunity and a diligence question, not as a completed hedge.

The unit volume disclosure makes the transition from engineering story to manufacturing story easier to test. In the Q2 FY27 call, management described about 4.6 million control-intensive ECUs sold on a trailing-twelve-month basis, up 44% year over year, and said this measure correlates strongly with revenue. That does not disclose average selling price, nor does it isolate revenue by product family. It does create a useful cross-check: if unit shipments keep growing, sales should broadly respond unless price and mix move materially against the company. If they diverge, investors should ask whether the difference reflects lower-priced products, customer mix, timing, or a changed definition. The metric is valuable precisely because it is repeated quarterly, but it must be read alongside revenue and operating margin rather than as a substitute for either.

The product adoption stages matter too. An early proposition can require intensive engineering and customer attention while contributing little revenue; once it is validated and adopted across additional models, volume may scale faster than the fixed engineering base. The inverse is also true: a program can stall before broader adoption, or a platform can be discontinued before the supplier has recovered its development effort. Management’s call emphasized that it does not disclose unannounced products or detailed future program information, which is understandable from a competitive standpoint but leaves external investors with limited pipeline visibility. That information gap should affect how confidently we capitalize announced opportunities. Track shipped units, named customer breadth, realized margin and cash collections as adoption moves from trial to scale.

## CHAPTER III: How It Is Sold and Delivered — Distribution and Unit Economics

> *"The sale is only the midpoint; the cash arrives after engineering, production, delivery and collection."*
> — **Research Desk maxim**

SEDEMAC sells business-to-business, primarily to OEMs. The end user may be a rider, a fleet operator or a facility manager, but the purchasing relationship sits upstream with the company that designs and assembles the vehicle or equipment. That changes the sales motion. The supplier must enter early enough in a product program to understand technical requirements, develop and test the electronics, clear the customer’s validation process and align manufacturing to a launch schedule. This is not the same as placing a commodity part on a distributor shelf. A successful design-in may create repeat production orders over a model’s life, while the next program requires another round of customer confidence.

The practical route to market is direct OEM engagement and program integration. The RHP describes relationships with a select group of anchor customers, including TVS Motor, and notes that products are delivered to customers in India mainly by road and to international customers primarily by sea through third-party logistics providers engaged on a need basis. That gives the company flexibility but leaves transportation performance partly outside its control. The filing also identifies concentration among top suppliers and imported critical inputs such as semiconductors and PCBs, including imports from China. The distribution map therefore runs from a small set of vendors, through Pune manufacturing, to OEM plants and export destinations.

Cost of materials consumed was ₹662.11 crore, or about ₹62.6 per ₹100 revenue. The resulting pre-tax profit was approximately ₹14.2 and net profit about ₹9.8 per ₹100 of revenue. Small differences can appear from rounding. This gives you a reported income-statement waterfall, not a unit-level contribution margin.

Now follow that same ₹100 beyond accrual profit. The snapshot’s FY26 cash-flow series records cash from operations of ₹128 crore and free cash flow of negative ₹47 crore against ₹1,058 crore of sales. That equates to around ₹12.1 of operating cash inflow and negative ₹4.4 of free cash flow per ₹100 revenue. The precise bridge from net income to operating cash includes working-capital movements, taxes and non-cash charges; free cash flow depends on the dataset’s treatment of investment spending. It would be wrong to subtract the income-statement depreciation number from PAT and call the result cash. What matters is that reported profit and capital available after operating and investing needs are different measures.

The business has a customer-credit cycle as well as an engineering cycle. Still, the direction deserves attention: faster sales growth often consumes cash if receivables, inventory and supplier terms do not keep pace. A design win can be economically attractive on the income statement while requiring the supplier to finance components and finished goods before collecting from an OEM.

The procurement chain adds another timing problem. Long component lead times can prompt precautionary inventory accumulation. If materials arrive before production, cash has left but revenue has not yet been recognized. If finished units ship under customer credit terms, revenue may be recognized before cash arrives. The earnings-call discussion linked working capital with supply-side pressures, while management also described raw-material inflation as a reason EBITDA margin eased in Q2 FY27. Management’s explanation is relevant; the balance-sheet and cash-flow statements are the test of whether the pressure reverses.

A US industrial-control supplier, an Indian automotive electronics maker and a European OEM supplier may all use direct technical selling, but the contract structure differs by buyer, regulation, logistics and scale. The common lesson is to trace four clocks: design and validation time, component lead time, production and delivery time, and collection time. The first clock determines how long engineering resources are tied up before a program scales. The second and third determine inventory and delivery risk. The fourth decides when accounting earnings become usable cash.

The result is a company whose distribution advantage is not geographic reach through thousands of outlets. It is technical access to OEM programs, dependable qualification and execution at production volume. That channel can be sticky after a product succeeds, but it concentrates bargaining power in a few customers and makes operational reliability central to renewal. ## CHAPTER IV: The Battlefield — Rivals, Switching Costs & Moat Reality

> *"A moat is a pattern of customer behavior that survives a competitor's next offer."*
> — **Research Desk maxim**

You and I can start the competitive analysis at the point where an OEM has to choose a controller for a real machine. The purchase decision is not simply a bid for a box of electronics. It is a decision about which supplier will help the vehicle or engine behave as designed, pass its validation program, meet the applicable requirements and keep production moving after launch. The controller is a component, but the engineering work around it reaches into the customer's product. That is where switching costs can arise.

Imagine a two-wheeler program moving from design into testing. The OEM and supplier settle the interface, define how the controller responds to operating conditions, check diagnostics and test the application across a range of conditions. Engineers work through failures and revisions. After qualification, the supplier has learned the quirks of that program and the OEM has accumulated test evidence around the chosen part. Replacing the supplier mid-program can mean repeating work, delaying a launch or accepting reliability risk. That friction protects an incumbent. It does not prevent the OEM from re-bidding the next platform or adding a second source.

The distinction matters because investors often compress the entire story into the word 'moat.' A more useful question is: what would the customer have to redo to switch, and what would it gain by switching? If a rival offers a meaningful cost or performance improvement, the OEM can decide that new qualification expense is worthwhile. If SEDEMAC helps solve a difficult application and supplies consistently, the customer may prefer continuity. The business has reported that a large share of sales comes from a small group of customers. If the anchor customer builds fewer vehicles, changes sourcing, or migrates to a different design, revenue can fall faster than the supplier can replace it.
