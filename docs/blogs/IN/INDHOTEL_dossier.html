Before you read

The Indian Hotels Company (IHCL) is the Tata group's hotel business. It runs the Taj, Vivanta, SeleQtions, Gateway and Ginger brands across 670 hotels, counting those still being built[3]. The business is strong: revenue grew 21% in the September 2026 quarter, it holds far more cash than debt, and CARE Ratings upgraded it to its top credit grade in October[1][16]. The shares closed at ₹812.85, at a 52-week high, and the price already assumes about 23% growth a year for ten years[33]. Our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: we would buy on a pullback to between ₹717.70 and ₹739.55, not at today's price, and the call is wrong if the price closes below ₹682.75 or hotel demand weakens sharply.

1. What the company does

IHCL gets paid when a guest sleeps, eats or celebrates in one of its hotels. The company runs hotels in India and in a few other countries, from the Taj Mahal Palace in Mumbai to a Ginger budget hotel near an airport. In the quarter to September 2026 the company earned ₹2,566 crore of revenue.[1] A crore is ten million rupees, so that is about ₹25.7 billion in three months. Most of it came from the hotel business: ₹2,284 crore, against ₹285 crore from air catering.[5]

There are three ways IHCL can be in a hotel. It can own the building, it can lease it, or it can manage it for an owner. When IHCL owns or leases, it books all the room and restaurant sales, and it pays all the costs. When it manages, it keeps a fee and the owner carries the building cost. At 30 September 2026 IHCL had 390 operating hotels with 34,213 keys (a key is one lettable room). Of these, 136 hotels with 13,399 keys were owned, 20 were joint ventures and 234 with 19,091 keys were managed.[7] The pipeline of signed hotels is even more tilted to the managed model: 214 of 280 hotels are managed.[7]

This matters because the two models behave differently. An owned hotel can earn a high profit in a good year, but it has fixed costs, so profit falls fast in a bad year. A managed hotel earns a smaller, steadier fee. Management says 94% of its 34,800-key pipeline is capital light, which means the owner or a lessor pays for the building and IHCL puts in little of its own money.[23] The management fee income rose 25% to ₹156 crore in the quarter, from ₹125 crore a year earlier.[8] That is still small next to ₹2,566 crore of total revenue, so IHCL today is mostly an owner-operator with a growing fee business on top.

The brands sit on a ladder. Taj is the luxury brand and Taj hotels brought in 68% of the enterprise revenue in FY26.[17] Vivanta and SeleQtions are upscale, Gateway is upper mid-range, and Ginger is the budget-to-mid-range brand with its own revenue of ₹193 crore in the quarter.[9] Newer additions include Brij (an experiential leisure brand), Atmantan (a wellness destination), Tree of Life (private resorts) and ama (bungalow stays).[3][5] Outside hotels, IHCL consolidates Taj SATS, an air catering business that supplies airline meals.[6] Brand Finance ranks Taj as the world's strongest luxury hotel brand for 2026.[3] A trade publication reported the same headline figures on 23 October.[26]

Who pays? Mostly Indian leisure travellers, wedding hosts and corporate event planners. Of room revenue in the first half of FY27, 67% came from transient and leisure guests, 20% from MICE (meetings, incentives, conferences and exhibitions), 9% from corporate guests, 3% from long stays and 2% from airline crew.[12] Management said domestic guests give about 90% of the business today.[24] No single customer matters much, which is a strength. The weakness is that demand follows travel and celebrations, and those fall quickly in a shock.

How the money is made, in one example

Take IHCL's own, standalone hotels. In the September quarter they earned a RevPAR of ₹13,860 per night, up 28%, and occupancy rose from 75% to 82%.[5][24] RevPAR means revenue per available room: total room revenue divided by every room on offer, whether sold or not. It rises when the hotel sells more nights, or sells them at a higher price, and it is the number the industry watches first. Standalone operating revenue grew 26% to ₹1,336 crore, and the operating profit grew 40% to ₹520 crore.[4] Profit grew faster than revenue because rent, salaries and maintenance do not rise with each extra guest.

The same effect works in reverse. In the year to March 2021, when travel stopped, group revenue fell to ₹1,575 crore and the company made an operating loss of ₹362 crore.[19] Anyone looking at a hotel company is therefore looking at two things at once: a strong business at the moment, and a business that depends on the economy and on events that nobody controls.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is operating profit before interest, tax and the yearly write-off of buildings and equipment. It is a quick read of what the hotels earn before financing and accounting charges.
  • Operating margin is operating profit divided by revenue. A higher number means more of each rupee of sales is kept after running costs.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is yearly operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. It shows how well the company earns on what it has invested.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares. It is the profit that belongs to one share.
  • Free cash flow is the cash from operations left after buying buildings and equipment. It is often shortened to FCF (free cash flow). It is the cash that can be paid out or saved.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the business collects from running hotels, before investing and financing. It is harder to flatter than reported profit.
  • Net debt is borrowings minus cash. A negative number means the company holds more cash than debt.
  • Price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by the profit per share. A P/E of 50 means the buyer pays ₹50 for each ₹1 of yearly profit.
  • Guidance is the company's own forecast for the coming period. A consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow the stock.
  • Capital expenditure is cash spent on buildings, renovations and equipment that last more than a year.
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in day-to-day operations, such as unpaid guest bills, less what the company owes suppliers.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The first strength is the brand and the locations. Taj hotels sit in places that are hard to copy: the Taj Mahal Palace in Mumbai, the Taj Lake Palace in Udaipur and the Taj Falaknuma Palace in Hyderabad. Two Taj hotels, the Taj Lake Palace and the Taj Falaknuma Palace, hold three Michelin Keys, and the company says Taj is the only hotel brand in India with three-Michelin-Key hotels.[3][5] That helps pricing. Over FY26, the average room rate rose to ₹18,507 from ₹17,216, while occupancy held at 78%.[17] A company that can raise prices without losing guests has pricing power, which is the best test of a brand.

The second strength is how supply is being added. According to the Hotelivate study cited by IHCL, new rooms in the eight biggest cities will grow 5.2% a year from FY26 to FY31, while smaller cities will add 12.0% a year.[10] IHCL's renovated flagship hotels are in Goa, Delhi, Bengaluru and Udaipur, and the first three are in the big-city list where supply grows slowest.[4] IHCL's slide also notes that about 60% of new announced supply is outside the top 20 cities, and that only 72% of announced supply is actually in active development.[10] We cannot verify these supply forecasts ourselves, but they support the view that demand may outgrow supply for a few years.

The third strength is how the company grows. It adds hotels mostly under management contracts, so it does not need to buy land or build. CARE Ratings puts the operating portfolio at 32% capital-heavy and 68% capital-light, and about 93% of the pipeline in capital-light forms.[17] A fee on revenue stays positive even when a hotel's profit falls, so the fee business smooths the ride in a downturn. The company says it expects to open 5,000 or more keys a year, and the pipeline holds 34,800 keys.[7]

The fourth strength is the balance sheet. IHCL has gross cash of ₹4,369 crore against borrowings of ₹116 crore at September 2026; its lease liabilities, which are promises to pay rent, are ₹3,355 crore.[11] CARE puts interest cover at 14.52 times for FY26 and says the company is in a net cash position even after treating leases as debt.[18] The parent, Tata Sons, holds 35.66% and has supported the company in the past: CARE notes that Tata Sons took part in the FY22 rights issue.[15][17] This is a sound platform from which to keep investing.

There are real weaknesses. Taj hotels were 68% of enterprise revenue in FY26, so a problem at the Taj brand would hit the whole group.[17] The company's overseas hotels earn lower margins: the operating margin of St. James Court in London fell from 30.6% to 27.0% in the first half, and the US subsidiary (UOH Inc.) earns 2.5%.[13] Taj SATS, the air catering business, saw its EBITDA fall 23% in the quarter.[5] And the company has a long-running lease dispute with the Mumbai Port Trust over the land under the Taj Mahal Palace: the Trust has claimed ₹1,874 crore, while IHCL says it owes no more than ₹163.56 crore.[18] CARE regards the impact as manageable, but the outcome is not in the company's hands.

Cyclicality: what a bad year looks like

The best evidence on cyclicality is IHCL's own history. Revenue was ₹4,463 crore in FY20, fell to ₹1,575 crore in FY21 and recovered to ₹3,056 crore in FY22.[19] The company lost ₹796 crore in FY21 and ₹265 crore in FY22.[19] Return on capital employed was negative 6% in FY21.[21] The balance sheet was repaired with about ₹4,000 crore of new equity raised in FY22 through a rights issue and a share placement to institutions.[17] Since FY23 the margin has held between 31% and 33%.[19] The recovery is strong, but it is only four years old, and no full slowdown has tested the managed-contract fee business.

The model also works in reverse in good years. The company's standalone margin rose four percentage points in the quarter because sales grew faster than fixed costs.[4] Investors who buy at the top of an expansion pay for a margin that falls quickly if sales slow. That is why the price paid matters more here than for a steady business.

Competition and where IHCL stands

IHCL competes with other listed Indian chains and with global brands that manage hotels in India. The listed rivals are smaller: Chalet Hotels has a market value of ₹17,703 crore, EIH (Oberoi) ₹18,314 crore, Leela Palaces ₹20,093 crore, ITC Hotels ₹34,529 crore and Juniper Hotels ₹4,392 crore, against IHCL at ₹1,15,312 crore.[22][28][29][30][31][32] IHCL's return on capital employed, at 17.1%, is above Leela (8.73%) and ITC Hotels (11.2%) and below EIH (20.7%).[22][29][30][32] Chapter 3 sets out the full peer matrix. The takeaway is that IHCL is the largest company on the list, with 81% of its pipeline in management contracts, and the market already pays the highest price for it.[23]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management earns credit when it sets targets and then beats them. Here guidance means the company's own published forecast for the year. It had earlier forecast revenue growth of 12% to 14% for FY27. After the first half it raised the forecast to 15% to 17%, which is 300 basis points higher (a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point).[2][23] First-half revenue growth was 18%, so the raised forecast is already consistent with what has been reported.[6] The company also said it would reach a portfolio of 700 hotels under its Accelerate 2030 plan. At 670 hotels, including the pipeline, management now says it will reach that number before the end of this financial year and will announce a new target by the middle of November (stated by management, not yet confirmed in a filing).[3][23]

The record is not perfect. On the call management said Taj SATS is already improving and that it expects it to go back to its earlier level, and even beyond, in the second half. In the quarter its EBITDA fell 23%, so the promise is still to be tested.[5][24] The London hotel's margin fell 3.6 points over the first half. Management said the delayed works there are completed and that London revenue rose by double digits in October; we have not seen October figures in any filing.[13][24] And only 20 of the 50 or more planned openings have happened. The remaining 30 must open in six months, so the second half carries a heavy execution load.[7][23]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks two questions. First, does the company clear the hard red lines, which are the solvency and governance limits in our scoring rules? Second, does it qualify as a compounder: a business with high returns and steady growth at a fair price? We also look at direction, because a company can pass on levels while its margin or cash flow weakens.

The technical test uses the Weinstein stage scale. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend, and only a Stage 4 breakdown rejects a stock on the chart alone.

The reward test is asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk: the possible gain to the target divided by the possible loss to the stop. The floor for a core holding is 2.0 to 1. We measure distance from support in ATR (average true range), the typical daily price swing. The entry zone is the price band where we plan to buy, using a limit order, which is an order that fills only at your price or better. For the calculation we use debt-to-equity, which is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, and the interest cover, which is how many times operating profit covers interest.

The number

IHCL turned 1.18 rupees of operating cash into every 1 rupee of profit over the last three years, and holds about ₹4,253 crore of net cash before leases.

How it was calculated

We added cash from operations for FY24, FY25 and FY26 (₹1,935 crore, ₹2,194 crore and ₹2,471 crore, a total of ₹6,600 crore) and divided by the sum of Screener's net profit for the same years (₹1,330 crore, ₹2,038 crore and ₹2,247 crore, a total of ₹5,615 crore). Net cash is gross cash of ₹4,369 crore less borrowings of ₹116 crore.[19][20][11]

The inputs

Cash from operations and net profit come from Screener's consolidated tables.[19][20] Cash and borrowings at 30 September 2026 come from the company's balance sheet.[11] Counting lease liabilities of ₹3,355 crore as debt turns the net cash into about ₹898 crore.[11]

The range

If profit is measured after minorities, the conversion ratio is a little higher, about 1.26 (earnings per share times 142.3 crore shares gives ₹5,251 crore of profit over the three years), because the denominator is smaller.[15][19] If the Mumbai Port Trust claim of ₹1,874 crore were paid in full, net cash before leases would fall from ₹4,253 crore to about ₹2,379 crore, which is still positive.

If the number is off

A conversion ratio below 0.70 would break the compounder test. We would then reduce the size of any purchase, because the profit would no longer be backed by cash.

4. What the price is doing

The chart is where IHCL falls short. Here is the vocabulary in plain words. A 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing price over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. They are slow lines that prices tend to return to. These tools are simple. They show where the price is, not where it is going.

The relative strength index (RSI) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been rising; above 70 means the stock has run hard. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing, here about ₹21.85, and we use it as a ruler for distances.

Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend. A base is a sideways stretch the price builds before a rise, the pivot is the top of that base, a breakout is a move above it on heavy volume, and a retest is a pullback that tests the old level from above. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut. IHCL closed at ₹812.85 on 27 October 2026, at its 52-week high and 9.5% above its 20-day average.[33]

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on the future. The Reverse DCF works backwards: it asks what yearly growth the market must be assuming for ten years, given today's price. At ₹812.85, using a 12% discount rate and a 20 times exit multiple, the market is pricing in about 22.95% a year growth in free cash flow for a decade.[33] Today's free cash flow yield is only 1.49%, which is low.[33]

Is that growth reasonable? IHCL's operating profit grew 21.0% a year from FY23 to FY26, and cash from operations grew 15.1% a year.[19][20] Free cash flow grew only 6.6% a year over the same period, because the company reinvested heavily: ₹1,191 crore in FY23, ₹1,444 crore in FY26.[20] Holding 23% growth in free cash flow for ten years would take it from ₹1,716 crore to about ₹13,500 crore. That is a demanding path, far above the 6.6% a year that free cash flow actually grew from FY23 to FY26, so the price leaves little margin for error.[33]

Our variant perception

Where do we differ from the price and the consensus? On the business, we are with the optimists. The consensus FY27 earnings per share of ₹16.21 implies ₹11.24 in the second half, because the first half earned ₹4.97.[22][33] Second-half profit in FY26 was ₹10.56 per share but included a gain on investments, so once that gain is removed the forecast needs about 23% to 34% growth in the second half, against 22% reported for the first half (₹4.08 to ₹4.97 per share).[22][14] Our working: we removed either the extra other income of the December 2025 quarter (₹334 crore less a normal ₹80 crore, taxed at 22%, about ₹1.39 a share) or the full ₹399 crore gain on sale of investments (taxed at 22%, about ₹2.19 a share), which leaves ₹9.17 or ₹8.37 of underlying second-half profit per share.[22][14] We think the FY27 forecast is fair to slightly demanding, and the FY28 forecast may be cautious, because the merger with Oriental Hotels adds 6% to 7% to standalone revenue from April 2027 and the wedding calendar is longer.[13][24]

On price, we differ from the buyers. At 42.4 times FY28 earnings, the shares already pay for the FY28 beat. A buyer today gets the business's growth but also takes the risk that the multiple falls from 50 times this year's earnings to the mid-40s as consensus earnings growth settles at 18.4% in FY28.[33] Anand Rathi, which rates the stock BUY with a ₹900 target at 23.5 times FY29E EBITDA, expects EBITDA margin to rise to about 36% by FY28, from 33% in FY26. We see that as a plausible but demanding path.[25] Our central view is a 6% rise from here to ₹863.55 over a year, but the fall to ₹630 (30% likely) is slightly more probable than the rise to ₹984 (25% likely).

The part the consensus may be missing is that much of the recent strength is event-driven. Management itself says Delhi had the benefit of the BRICS summit in the quarter, although it argues that Delhi would still have grown in the late 20s without it, and that standalone RevPAR growth would have been a comfortable double-digit number without the summit.[24] The summit is one event in a calendar full of them, and we cannot test the adjusted figures ourselves. If event revenue fades faster than new hotels arrive, growth would settle near the lower end of guidance, and the multiple would shrink with it. That is the variant risk we price into the bear case.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate rests on one assumption that could fail. This chapter names that assumption, says what would show it is wrong and when we will know. It then builds the strongest honest case against our own view.

Weakest assumption behind the FY28 earnings estimate

The weakest link is that RevPAR keeps growing in double digits once the large events of 2026 are in the base. The September-quarter RevPAR growth of 25% to 28% came with the BRICS summit and other events in Delhi, and with renovated hotels coming back into service; management argues the growth would have been double-digit without the summit.[5][24] Anand Rathi notes that renovated properties recorded RevPAR growth of about 20% in the first half, a boost that fades once those hotels are in the base.[25] If RevPAR growth falls to 8% to 10%, FY28 earnings per share would be nearer ₹17.50 than ₹19.19. We will know from the December-quarter result, which management says benefits from a strong wedding calendar, and from the March-quarter result, which faces a high comparison from a year earlier.[24] The date of the next board meeting has not been announced.

Weakest assumption behind the 45 times multiple

We assume the multiple falls only by about 10% over a year. At 52 times trailing earnings, the stock is priced for near perfection. Peers trade at 25 to 43 times earnings, and the hotel sector's earnings are cyclical.[27][28][29][30][32] If the market treats IHCL as a cyclical stock again, the multiple could fall to the mid-30s, which is the bear case. Taken alone, with consensus earnings of ₹19.19 unchanged, a multiple of 36 gives ₹690.84, a 15.0% fall from the close. We will know within weeks, because the multiple reacts to the broad market. The Nifty 50 is in a downtrend with only 17.8% of its 500 largest stocks above their 50-day average.[33]

Red-team bear case

The strongest case against buying is this. The stock is at a high, the price assumes 23% growth for ten years, and consensus earnings have moved little (+1.5% and +2.7% over 90 days). Free cash flow has grown only 6.6% a year over three years.[20] Overseas hotels and Taj SATS are both weaker than a year ago.[5][13] A bad outcome in the lease dispute at the Taj Mahal Palace would cost cash, and the company is also committing about ₹3,600 crore to capital spending.[18] Meanwhile 81% of the pipeline is management contracts, which depend on outside owners finishing their hotels on time.[23] In a market where only 17.8% of stocks are above their 50-day average, a high-P/E stock can fall hard if foreign investors, who cut their holding from 26.96% in March 2025 to 22.12% in September 2026, keep selling.[22]
