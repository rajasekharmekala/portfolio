Before you read

National Securities Depository Ltd (NSDL) is one of India's two securities depositories, the electronic vault that records who owns shares and bonds. It holds about 86 percent of the value in custody, yet only one in five demat accounts, and it earns mostly from company fees.[12][11] We reject the stock for now because its price is in a Stage 4 decline: the ₹706.90 close sits under falling 50-day and 200-day averages.[33][35] The financial tests pass and the operating-KPI test passes at its bare minimum, so the file stays open with reference levels of ₹688.00 to ₹695.70. The 2.31 gain-to-loss ratio uses the ₹780 base case and falls to 0.70 at the ₹729 weighted value, and the case fails if new-account share fades or costs keep rising.

1. What the company does

Think of NSDL as the land registry for shares. Until the mid-1990s, a share was a paper certificate that could be lost, forged or delayed in the post. NSDL began turning those papers into electronic entries in 1996, and today almost every listed share in India is held that way. Each investor opens a demat account (a bank-style account that holds securities instead of cash). The account is opened through a depository participant, usually a bank or a broker, which acts as NSDL's agent in front of the customer.[11]

At 30 June 2026, NSDL had 4.56 crore demat accounts, 317 depository participants and more than 57,000 service centres. It served over 115,000 issuers (companies whose securities it holds) and held about 86 percent of the value of securities in custody, roughly ₹535 lakh crore.[12][11] The other depository, Central Depository Services (India) Ltd (CDSL), holds the rest of the value but most of the accounts: it reported 18.59 crore accounts on the same date.[29] The two companies are a regulated duopoly.

The people who pay NSDL are mostly not retail investors. Companies pay an annual custody fee for every security they have registered. Brokers and banks pay fees when securities move, are pledged as loan collateral, or are issued in a new offering. Companies also pay for electronic voting. In FY26, custody fees were ₹312 crore of the ₹705 crore standalone operating revenue, or 44 percent.[4] Recurring fees were 55.5 percent of operating revenue in the June 2026 quarter, up from 48.2 percent a year earlier.[11] The offer document says NSDL earns its revenue mainly from participants, issuers and transaction fees, so the account-opening and yearly charges an investor sees are set and kept by the broker or bank.[7]

That explains an odd fact. NSDL has about 19.7 percent of accounts (4.56 crore of 23.16 crore) but 43.2 percent of the industry's depository income.[11][12] Its customers are companies, foreign funds and large institutions, which hold big values, while CDSL holds most of the accounts. NSDL therefore earns from the size of the market and the number of issuers, not only from the number of new investors.

Two subsidiaries that change the consolidated picture

NSDL owns two subsidiaries. NSDL Database Management Ltd (NDML) runs a KYC registration agency (a shared store of investor identity checks), the national insurance repository (electronic policies), the online system for special economic zones and other services. NSDL Payments Bank Ltd (NPBL) is a payments bank: it takes deposits, runs digital payments and sells cards. NSDL holds 95.05 percent of the bank.[11]

The bank's FY26 total income was ₹748.6 crore, but its profit was ₹15.9 crore, and its cost-to-income ratio was 97.9 percent.[22] That matters because consolidated revenue includes the bank's flows. The watchlist entry that first put NSDL on our list quoted trailing sales growth of 24 percent (we call it the first-list entry). That figure is correct on the Screener consolidated page, but the depository alone grew standalone sales 13 percent over the same period.[1][2] Management says the standalone company supplies about 91 percent of consolidated profit.[12]

Treasury income: a second source of profit

Because NSDL collects fees before it pays costs, it holds a large pool of cash and investments. At 31 March 2026 the standalone balance sheet showed ₹1,405.8 crore of non-current investments, ₹548.4 crore of current investments, ₹188.5 crore of bank deposits and ₹72.4 crore of cash, about ₹2,215 crore in all.[3] The company earned ₹130.4 crore of other income in FY26, which was 27 percent of standalone profit before tax. Interest was ₹87.8 crore of it; fair-value gains on mutual funds were ₹23.0 crore; and a dividend from NDML was ₹18.3 crore.[4]

A reader who values NSDL should keep two profit streams apart: the fee business, which grows with the market, and the treasury income, which depends on interest rates and on bond and fund prices.

Who owns NSDL

NSDL has no identifiable promoter (a controlling owner) in the legal sense.[8] SEBI limits who can own a depository. A person may hold up to 5 percent. A stock exchange, depository, bank, insurer or public financial institution may hold up to 15 percent. All foreign holders together may hold up to 49 percent.[5] Before the IPO, IDBI Bank held 26.10 percent and the National Stock Exchange held 24.00 percent, both above the 15 percent ceiling. SEBI had frozen voting rights and withheld dividends on the excess.[6][9]

The August 2025 offer was a pure sale of 5.01 crore shares at ₹800 by six holders: IDBI Bank, NSE, HDFC Bank, the UTI administrator, State Bank of India and Union Bank of India. NSDL received none of the ₹4,011 crore.[23] At 30 June 2026, IDBI Bank and NSE each held 15.0 percent. HDFC Bank held about 6.9 percent, and the UTI administrator about 5.1 percent. These two figures follow from their pre-offer stakes less the shares each sold in the offer.[11][6][23] By September 2026, Screener showed foreign institutions at 8.24 percent (15.89 percent a year earlier), domestic institutions at 38.18 percent and the public at 53.59 percent, with 9.08 lakh shareholders.[1] The meaning for a buyer is that the holders who must sell have sold, but several large holders still sit above 5 percent and none is under a lock-in now.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Depository: an institution that keeps electronic records of who owns shares and bonds, much as a land registry records property.
  • Demat account: an account that holds shares in electronic form; it is opened through a depository participant.
  • Depository participant (DP): a bank or broker that opens demat accounts and acts as the depository's agent.
  • Issuer: a company whose shares or bonds are held in the depository; issuers pay the annual custody fee.
  • Custody fee: the yearly fee a depository charges an issuer for each security it keeps on record.
  • Recurring revenue: fees that repeat every year without a new sale, such as the custody fee.
  • Unlisted company: a company whose shares are not traded on an exchange but are held in demat form; NSDL had about 72 percent of this segment.
  • KYC registration agency (KRA): a shared store of investor identity checks that brokers can reuse.
  • Payments bank: a bank that takes deposits and handles payments but cannot lend; NSDL Payments Bank is the subsidiary.
  • Market infrastructure institution (MII): an exchange, clearing house or depository that SEBI licenses and caps ownership of.
  • Other income: profit that is not from the main fee business; for NSDL it is mostly interest and fund gains on its cash pool.
  • Stage 2 and Stage 4: Stage 2 is the rising uptrend phase of a stock; Stage 4 is the phase in which a stock trends down below a falling long-term average, as used in the Weinstein stage method.
  • RSI: the relative strength index, a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has moved; a reading under 30 is called oversold.
  • ATR: the average true range, the typical daily price swing in rupees, used here to measure the distance to support and to place stops.
  • EPS: earnings per share, which is net profit divided by the number of shares.
  • EBITDA: earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges.
  • ROCE: the return on capital employed, which is operating profit as a share of the capital the business uses; ROE is the same idea measured on shareholders' equity.
  • CASA: current and savings account balances, the low-cost deposits that a bank holds for customers.
  • Free cash flow (FCF): the cash a business generates from operations after spending on equipment and technology, which is the cash left to pay owners.
  • Operating margin: operating profit as a share of revenue, which is the part of each rupee of sales left after running costs.
  • Price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio: the share price divided by earnings per share; a P/E of 36 means investors pay 36 rupees for each rupee of yearly profit.
  • Debt-to-equity: borrowings divided by shareholders' equity; zero means no debt.
  • Guidance: management's official forecast of its own results; guidance is not a promise.
  • Asymmetry (reward-to-risk): the gain from today's price to the target divided by the loss from today's price to the stop; a ratio of 2.0 means the possible gain is twice the possible loss.
  • Entry zone, limit order and stop: the entry zone is the price band where a purchase would be considered; a limit order is an instruction to buy only at a set price or lower; the stop is the price at which a position is sold to cap the loss.
  • Base and pivot: a base is a flat stretch of trading that can form a floor for a new rise; the pivot is the price that must be passed to confirm that the stock is leaving the base.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

NSDL's position is strong where companies choose a depository and weak where retail investors choose a broker. The strength is hard to copy. SEBI licenses only two depositories, and the regulator caps ownership so that no single exchange or bank controls either one.[5][32] NSDL serves 115,107 issuers, holds 99.9 percent of foreign portfolio investor holdings, 85.8 percent of total custody value and 66.8 percent of the value held by individuals and families.[11]

The weakness is the retail account race. The industry added about 70.3 lakh accounts in the June 2026 quarter, and NSDL took 12.4 lakh of them, a 17.6 percent share. The prospectus, quoting a CRISIL report, says that fintech brokers (digital brokers that sign up customers on an app) went from 5 percent of the market in FY2016 to 70 percent in FY2025, and warns that failing to win them would hand more share to CDSL.[11][12][7] At 31 March 2025 NSDL had 39.45 million accounts against CDSL's 152.98 million.[7] That gap was built over several years and will not close quickly.

Two facts moderate this weakness. First, the 17.6 percent share rose from 14.0 percent in the March 2026 quarter, and management attributes about 20 percent of recent gross additions to fintech brokers, up from about 2 percent a few quarters ago.[12][13] Second, a single large DP's flow rose from 70,000 accounts to 700,000 in a year, and one large fintech broker that had used the competitor moved fully to NSDL.[15] These are early wins. The CEO also said directly on the 31 July 2026 call that progress with named clients does not mean anything until numbers show it, and asked analysts not to treat it as guidance.[12]

Evidence for the moat: share of money, not of accounts

The moat shows up in revenue share. NSDL's standalone income was 43.2 percent of the two depositories' total income in FY26, more than double its 19.7 percent share of accounts. The same measure was 41.3 percent over the first nine months of FY26.[11][14] The reason is that the fee the company earns scales with the number of securities and issuers, which NSDL leads at 69.9 percent of issuers and about 72 percent of unlisted company equity.[11][13] The top ten depository participants made up only 15.15 percent of depository revenue in FY2025, down from 17.55 percent in FY2023, so no single broker can hold the company to ransom.[9]

Unlisted companies are the clearest example. About 33,000 unlisted companies joined in FY25 and 30,000 in FY26. Each pays a one-time joining fee of ₹15,000 and then an annual custody fee.[12][16] Folios (individual holdings of one security) rose from about 11.9 crore to about 14 crore in a year.[12] This wave is fading: companies admitted fell from 11,552 in the September 2025 quarter to 4,059 in March 2026, and to 3,406 in June 2026.[20][13][11] Management said in November 2025 that run rates were expected to slow, and they did.[16]

Where the moat leaks: price rules, discounts and a second source of competition

The prospectus states that the amount charged for depository services is highly regulated.[7] The evidence since listing is mixed. The regulator cut KYC fetch charges by 20 percent, and NDML's KYC revenue fell about 2 percent, which management called a good outcome because volumes made up most of the cut.[12] A request to raise the annual issuer fee has not moved: in May 2026 management said that nothing had happened on that front.[15] Meanwhile, NSDL chose to waive settlement charges for three years on new accounts under its Youth plan and Women's plan, which together account for 18 to 20 percent of incremental additions.[12] Waivers help win share. They also mean that new accounts yield little settlement revenue until the waiver ends.

CDSL is the only direct competitor, and it is the larger business by accounts. On Screener's figures, CDSL earns a 51 percent operating margin on consolidated sales of ₹1,145 crore, has a 3-year profit growth rate of 18 percent, and an ROE of 24.5 percent.[25] NSDL's consolidated ROE is 16.0 percent and its 3-year profit growth rate is 15 percent.[1] On the November 2025 call, an analyst noted that issuing ISINs for unlisted companies, once NSDL's alone, had also gone to the competitor. Management did not dispute it and said it aimed to hold its unlisted share within about two points of 70 to 72 percent.[16]

Market-linked revenue and the IPO pipeline

About a quarter of operating revenue moves with market activity: corporate actions and IPO fees were ₹87.0 crore in FY26 (12.3 percent of revenue, down 18.5 percent), settlement fees ₹56.0 crore (down 17.3 percent) and e-voting ₹46.4 crore.[4] In the June 2026 quarter the picture improved. Corporate action and IPO fees rose 19.9 percent to ₹18.9 crore, e-voting rose 31.3 percent, and pledge fees (charged when shares are pledged for loans, including margin funding by brokers) rose 28.5 percent to ₹17.2 crore. Settlement fees were flat at ₹14.8 crore.[11]

Management said that the IPO market was muted in the June 2026 quarter, and that activity is expected to pick up for the rest of the calendar year, going by the pipeline it sees.[12] Each new offering adds shareholders and accounts as well as one-time fees, and the benefit flows to NSDL in proportion to its share of new accounts. In the March 2026 quarter, one large IPO went more to the rival than to NSDL, which management called episodic.[15] For an owner, the IPO pipeline helps the fee lines most in the second half of FY27, but it is a market-timing factor that the company does not control.

Governance and management continuity

The chairman is a former State Bank of India executive, and the managing director, Vijay Chandok, joined from the ICICI group about 18 months before the July 2026 call. In his words, the IPO was completed seven months after he joined.[12][11] In September 2026, the chief technology officer resigned, and a new one, Ashish Dandekar, started on 29 September.[1] The company is in the middle of a technology rebuild that management sized at two to three years. A change at the technology head is a risk to its schedule, and nothing in the filings tells us whether the departure was planned.

The prospectus lists old regulatory matters. In 2006, SEBI ordered NSDL and others to repay ₹115.8 crore for fake IPO-allotment accounts, and NSDL's share was ₹90.0 crore. The matter is almost twenty years old, and we did not trace its current status.[9] More recently, a regulatory settlement fee was booked as a one-off cost in the March 2025 quarter, which management cited as the reason expenses fell in the next quarter.[17]

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A depository is a toll gate on India's capital market. The numbers that run it are how many accounts it wins, how much recurring fee it earns from companies, whether its costs grow slower than its fees, whether regulators let it keep its prices, and whether profit turns into cash. Those five measures are the Layer 2 scorecard. Each is scored as PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0), and the rulebook passes a company with at least 3.0 of 5.0 points and no more than one FAIL.[35] The test for each KPI is set out in the table so that you can see the cut-off, not just the score.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has held five earnings calls since the listing: 12 August 2025, 13 November 2025, a call that Screener lists under February 2026, 2 May 2026 and 31 July 2026.[1] We read the transcripts of four. The early-2026 transcript could not be located on the company site, so that quarter is covered from its press release.[17][16][15][12][19] The team set out a clear plan on the first calls: finish the IPO, spend on technology and people for two to three years, win new brokers, and let operating leverage follow. The record against that plan is mixed. Promises that were in management's control (the listing, new broker onboarding, the dividend) were kept. Promises about margins were not, yet.

The sharpest gap is on cost. In November 2025 the company said that costs would be front-loaded and could hurt margins.[16] In May 2026 it said that the current and previous years together were the peak for technology spending, that hiring in the new year would be much lower, and that it had started tightening the employee band in April.[15] In the June 2026 quarter, employee cost was still 37.8 percent higher than a year earlier, technology cost was 53.8 percent higher, and operating profit was flat.[11] Management then said it had done what it wanted on hiring and that costs from recent hires would flow through coming quarters.[12] So the promise of plateauing costs is partly delivered in intent and not yet in the numbers.

3. The numbers, in plain words

NSDL reports two sets of numbers, and they tell different stories. The standalone accounts are the depository itself. The consolidated accounts add NDML and the Payments Bank. The bank's revenue is large and its margin is thin. So consolidated sales grow faster than consolidated profit. We show both and say which one governs each test.[1][2] All amounts are in ₹ crore. The fiscal year ends in March.

The first-list entry for this name was unverified, so we checked each of its figures. ROCE of 22.1 percent and ROE of 16.0 percent match the Screener page. Its P/E of 36.0 is 36.3 on Screener on 28 October, 36.4 on Dhan the same day, and 36.4 at the 27 October close using trailing earnings per share of ₹19.42. Trailing sales growth of 24 percent is true for the consolidated accounts but only 13 percent for the standalone depository. The share of net new accounts of 17.6 percent in Q1 FY27 and 14 percent in Q4 FY26 match the company's figures, though 17.6 percent was also the figure for Q2 FY26. The rise of fintech brokers from about 2 percent to almost 20 percent is management's own statement about its additions.[1][2][12][11][20]

The business is asset-light. It has almost no debt. It collects fees before it pays costs. It holds a pool of cash and investments. That shapes how we read the return ratios. Return on capital employed (ROCE) for NSDL is 22.1 percent. Return on equity (ROE) is 16.0 percent.[1] For a company with no debt and a large treasury, the two measures nearly coincide. We use ROE as the governing return. The shareholder funds are largely cash and investments, about ₹2,215 crore on standalone equity of ₹2,128 crore. That dilutes the ratio. The fee business itself earns a very high return on the small capital it uses.[3]
