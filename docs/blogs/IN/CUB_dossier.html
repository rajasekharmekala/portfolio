1. What the company does

City Union Bank is a bank, which means it borrows money from savers and lends it to borrowers at a higher price. It was founded in 1904 in Kumbakonam, a town in the Cauvery river delta of Tamil Nadu, and its head office is still there. [1] On 30 September 2026 it had 1,002 branches: 253 in big cities, 242 in urban areas, 301 in semi-urban towns and 206 in villages, and about 772 of them were in South India (77%). A year earlier the south held 740 of 889 branches (83%), a count taken from ICICI Direct's November 2025 note and consistent with ICRA's 83%. The share fell because only 32 of the 113 branches added since then are in the south, by our arithmetic. [10] [7] [11] Its customers are small manufacturers, traders and textile units, farmers, and households that pledge gold jewellery for cash. [2] [12] The bank has had one profitable year after another for more than a century, a record the management uses as its main selling point. [10]

A bank earns money from the gap between what it charges borrowers and what it pays depositors. That gap in rupees is called net interest income. In Q2 FY27 it was ₹872.8 crore, up 31% on a year earlier. [3] [13] Divided by the assets that earn interest, the gap becomes the net interest margin (NIM). CUB's NIM was 3.87% in the quarter against 3.63% a year earlier and 3.78% in the June quarter. [14] [15] The bank also earns fees, treasury gains and recoveries on loans it had already written off, which together gave ₹350.1 crore of other income in the quarter, 35% more than a year earlier. [3] [16] Of that other income, about ₹107 crore came from the sale of old bad loans to an asset reconstruction company, which will not repeat at the same size. [7]

Who pays the bank? Borrowers on a few main books. At 30 September 2026 gross loans were ₹72,442 crore. Loans to micro, small and medium businesses (MSME) were ₹28,084 crore, or 38.8%. Gold loans, counting both farm and non-farm loans backed by jewellery, were ₹21,726 crore, or 30.0%. [2] Retail loans excluding gold were ₹6,741 crore (9.3%), commercial real estate ₹4,164 crore (5.7%), and loans to finance companies ₹1,630 crore (2.3%). [2] A gold loan is a loan against jewellery that the borrower leaves with the bank. If the borrower stops paying, the bank sells the gold, so the loan is safe as long as the gold is worth more than the loan. The average yield on the gold book is 10.69%. [2] Management wants gold to stay at 31% to 32% of loans, and MSME at 55% to 60% over time. [7] [17]

Who funds the bank? Depositors. Total deposits were ₹83,893 crore, up 21% in a year. Only ₹22,350 crore of that, or 26.6%, was current and savings account (CASA) money, the cheapest kind because the bank pays little or nothing on it. [3] [13] The rest is term deposits, which pay a fixed rate. About 48% of all deposits are term deposits below ₹3 crore, which is the granular retail money that is hard to lose quickly. [2] [7] The cost of deposits was 5.56% in Q2, the same as in Q1. [14] [7] Borrowings from other lenders and the market rose to ₹5,280 crore from ₹2,183 crore a year earlier, which cost more than deposits. [14] About 55% of loans float with a market benchmark, 33% carry a fixed rate (mainly the gold book) and 12% are linked to the bank's own cost of funds, so a rate change reaches most loans within a few months. [7]

A bank cannot be read like a factory. A factory borrows to build machines, so debt-to-equity shows how much risk it carries, and cash flow from operations shows whether its profit turns into cash. For a bank, deposits are the raw material it lends out, so debt-to-equity is not meaningful, and operating cash flow swings with every rupee of new loans and deposits. CUB's operating cash flow was ₹802.5 crore in FY26 and ₹1,128.3 crore in FY25, against profits of ₹1,326.2 crore and ₹1,123.6 crore, so three-year cash flow over profit is 0.68; we show it for completeness and give it no weight. [18] We replace these tests with return on equity, return on assets, bad-loan ratios, capital ratios, margin, deposit mix, cost-to-income and credit cost, which is how the Reserve Bank and the rating agencies read a bank. [19] [20] The same logic is why a free cash flow (FCF) model, which values a company on the cash left after it buys machines, does not fit a bank. Chapter 5 uses a price-to-book test against return on equity instead.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Net interest income (NII): interest earned on loans and investments minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings.
  • Net interest margin (NIM): NII as a share of the assets that earn interest; a rising NIM means the bank keeps more of each rupee lent.
  • CASA: current and savings account deposits, the cheapest funding a bank has.
  • GNPA (gross non-performing assets) and NNPA (net non-performing assets): loans overdue by more than 90 days, before and after the bank sets aside money against them.
  • EPS (earnings per share): profit divided by the number of shares; after the 1:3 bonus in June 2026 CUB has about 99.08 crore shares. [23] [24]
  • Provision coverage ratio (PCR): the share of bad loans already covered by money set aside. CUB reports 82% including loans written off in the books but still pursued, and 67% without them. [3]
  • Technical write-off (TW): a loan removed from the books but still pursued for recovery. Recoveries on such loans count as income when received.
  • Capital adequacy ratio (CRAR): the bank's own capital as a share of its risk-weighted loans. The Reserve Bank's minimum is 11.50%. [12]
  • Return on assets (ROA) and return on equity (ROE): yearly profit as a share of total assets and of shareholders' money.
  • Price-to-book (P/B): the share price divided by net worth per share; a bank trading at 1.0 costs the same as its accounting value.
  • Special mention accounts (SMA): loans paying late but not yet bad; SMA-2 means 61 to 90 days overdue.
  • Loan-to-value (LTV): the loan as a share of the value of the pledged gold or property.
  • Expected credit loss (ECL): new Reserve Bank accounting from 1 April 2027 that makes banks set aside money for losses they expect, not only for those that have occurred. [25]
  • ARC: asset reconstruction company, a firm that buys bad loans from banks.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Four things make the business strong. First, the loans are secured and the bad-loan trend is clean. Gross bad loans fell from 4.70% of loans in March 2022 to 1.91% in March 2026 and 1.44% in September 2026, and net bad loans fell from 2.95% to 0.68% and then 0.48%. Both ratios have fallen at every year-end since March 2022, and gross bad loans fell again from 1.73% in June 2026. [26] [7] Second, capital is large. The capital adequacy ratio was 21.26% against a minimum of 11.50%, and the core part was 20.43%. [3] [12] The bank has not raised outside capital since 2015, so this cushion was earned from profits. [19] Third, returns are rising. Return on assets was 1.62% and return on equity 15.01% in Q2 FY27, against 1.56% and 13.35% for FY26. [3] [12] Fourth, costs are falling relative to income: the cost-to-income ratio was 45.96% in Q2 against 49.16% a year earlier. [14]

Four things make it weaker than the best private banks. The first is funding. CASA was 26.6% of deposits at September 2026 against 28% at March 2026, and the cost of deposits of 5.56% is above the private-bank average, which ICRA names as the reason its NIM sits below peers. [3] [12] [19] The second is geography: ICRA found 83% of branches in South India and 63% in Tamil Nadu in September 2025, with 86% of loans and 90% of deposits from the south. The south share of branches has since fallen to 77%, as Chapter 1 shows, but the loan and deposit shares for September 2026 are not published. [19] [10] The third is gold. Gold loans were ₹16,330 crore (28% of loans) in September 2025 and ₹21,726 crore (30%) a year later, a 33% rise in twelve months, even though the gold price has fallen more than 20% from its January 2026 peak. [2] [27] The fourth is a legacy of weaker years: the Reserve Bank fined the bank ₹66 lakh in February 2024 because the bad loans it reported differed materially from what the inspection found, and the bad-loan ratio was still 3.99% in March 2024. [28] [26]

How does this compare with other South Indian listed banks? On bad loans CUB's 1.44% is higher than Tamilnad Mercantile Bank (0.61%), Karur Vysya Bank (0.69%) and South Indian Bank (1.24%), and lower than CSB Bank (1.58%) and DCB Bank (2.29%). [29] [30] [31] [32] [33] Plainly, CUB sits in the middle of the group on asset quality. On price it is the most expensive of the six on earnings (15.08 times) and second on book value (2.01 times, behind Karur Vysya at 2.43), using the later 29 October close, against 7.6 to 11.6 times earnings and 0.93 to 2.43 times book for the others. At our ₹215.00 session close the figures are 14.3 times and 1.90 times. [9] That premium is the question for the rest of this book: is it a fair price for a 15% return on equity and a century-old record, or is it already too high?

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter gives the numbers first and the judgement afterwards. The figures come from the bank's own filings, which are the Q2 FY27 investor presentation and the FY26 annual report, and from Trendlyne for the multi-year cash flow line and the quarterly profit series. [45] [34] [18] Layer 1 compares where each number stands with the direction it is moving, because a bank that is improving fast can be a better buy than a steady one at the same price. [6]

How the money is made, step by step. The bank starts with interest on loans, a yield of 9.85% on advances in Q2. It subtracts what it pays depositors (a cost of deposits of 5.56%) and on borrowings. It keeps a net interest margin of 3.87% on its earning assets. [3] [14] It adds fees, trading gains and recoveries, and subtracts staff and branch costs, which are 45.96% of its net income. It sets aside money for bad loans, which was ₹60 crore in Q2 plus ₹50 crore of extra buffer. It pays tax at about 23%. The result is a return on assets of 1.62%. [3] [14]

Multiply that by the leverage the bank runs. Its assets of ₹1,03,462 crore are about 9.2 times its equity of ₹11,193 crore, so a 1.62% return on assets becomes a return on equity of about 15%. [14] That is why the ROE gap between 13.35% for FY26 and 15.01% for Q2 is the key number in this book: it moved by 1.7 points in two quarters, and most of the move comes from a wider margin, lower costs and recoveries. [12] [3]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

An estimate is only as good as its weakest assumption. For each of the estimates in Chapters 3 and 5 we name the assumption that carries the most weight, the evidence that would show it is wrong, and the date by which we will know. We then give the strongest honest case against our own view. [6]

Weakest assumption behind the FY28 profit of ₹1,885 crore: the margin holds near 3.9% and credit cost near 0.4%

The estimate needs a net interest margin near 3.9% while deposits become dearer. Management expects the cost of deposits to rise by about 10 basis points next quarter, and system credit is growing faster than deposits, with certificates of deposit issuance up to ₹74.6 thousand crore at the end of September. [7] [47] The repo rate has risen by 25 basis points to 5.50%, which lifts the yield on the 55% of loans linked to it within about three months, but the 33% fixed-rate book, mainly gold, does not reprice, and deposits reprice as they mature. [7] [47] The evidence that would prove us wrong is a Q3 FY27 NIM below 3.80% or a cost of deposits above 5.75%. We will know when the Q3 results arrive, in late January or early February 2027. The Q3 FY26 results and call were on 2 February 2026. [51]

Credit cost is the second half of the same assumption. The bank guided to about 0.4% in the medium term, and its credit cost after recoveries was negative in the first half. [49] [26] The Reserve Bank's expected-credit-loss rules begin on 1 April 2027, and the bank estimates a shortfall of about ₹270 crore, charged to reserves and not to the profit and loss account at first. [25] [7] After that date provisions will be higher for each new loan that turns weak, which CRISIL says will lift credit costs across the system. [56]

Weakest assumption behind the ₹250 value: the market still pays about 1.8 times FY28 book if return on equity reaches 14.6%

Our value assumes the bank keeps a premium to its neighbours. At the later 29 October quotes, TMB trades at 1.19 times book with a 14.7% return on equity, South Indian Bank at 1.30 times with 13.9% and CSB at 1.06 times with 14.1%. CUB trades at 1.90 times book at our ₹215.00 close (2.01 times at the 29 October quote), with a return on equity of 15.01% in the latest quarter and 13.3% on the aggregator basis. [9] [29] [31] [32] [3] Only Karur Vysya Bank, at 2.43 times with a return on equity of 21.0%, earns a higher premium on a clearly better return. [30] The ₹250 value needs return on equity of 14.6% that lasts. If it did not, the multiple could move toward the 1.3 to 1.5 times that the peer group pays, which on a book value of ₹138.5 would put the value at ₹180 to ₹210. The evidence would be a recovery-adjusted return on equity, defined in the hinge test below, that stays under 14% for two straight quarters. We will know at the Q3 FY27 and Q4 FY27 results, in early February and late April 2027.

Weakest assumption behind the bear value of ₹165: gold and credit shocks are less likely than moderate outcomes

Gold is 30% of loans, and the gold price fell more than 20% from its January 2026 peak to ₹1,43,458 on 28 October. [2] [27] The bank's gold bad loans are only ₹9.5 crore, and loans are usually made at 60% to 65% of the gold's value. [7] Our arithmetic: a loan made at 60% to 65% of value stands at 75% to 81% of value after a further 20% price fall, which is at or above the 75% cap for large loans and just under the 85% cap for small ones. [22] The bank would have to ask borrowers for margin payments on many accounts, and loans that are not topped up within the permitted period become bad loans. The evidence that would prove us wrong is a gold price below about ₹1,15,000 a 10 grams, a further fall of about 20% from the 28 October level, or a rise in gold-loan overdue accounts above 1% of the book. We would see it first in the daily gold price, and in the Q3 FY27 results.

The single hinge claim. The test has three parts, all measured on the Q3 FY27 results, and all three must pass.
