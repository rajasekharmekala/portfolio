1. What the company does

Britannia Industries is an Indian packaged-food company. Its main product is the biscuit: Good Day, Tiger, Marie Gold, NutriChoice and Milk Bikis are the household names.[13] It also sells cake, rusk, bread, croissants, wafers and dairy products such as cheese, ghee and milk drinks, and it exports to the Middle East, Africa and North America.[8][1] Non-biscuit foods are described on the August 2026 call as about a quarter of sales, a figure put by an analyst that management did not dispute.[8] In FY26 consolidated revenue from operations was ₹19,151.59 crore and profit attributable to owners was ₹2,533.49 crore.[1] The company runs 20 factories.[7]

The money flows through distributors. In FY26 all of sales went to 5,299 dealers and distributors, up from 4,659 a year earlier, and the ten largest took 14.70% of sales.[7] Crisil counts direct reach at about 28.7 lakh outlets in March 2025, up from 7.3 lakh in fiscal 2014.[13] Most of the volume sells in small packs. Between 60% and 65% of biscuits are sold at the ₹5 and ₹10 price points.[9] That matters because a shopper who pays ₹5 usually will not accept ₹5.50, so when ingredients get dearer Britannia usually shrinks the pack instead of raising the price, a move managers call shrinkflation.[8] On the August 2026 call management said it recovered only about half of the cost inflation of the June quarter through such price moves.[8]

Three margins describe how much of each rupee of sales is kept. The gross margin is sales minus the cost of ingredients and packaging, shown as a share of sales; Motilal Oswal puts it at 41.5% in the June 2026 quarter.[19] EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is the profit from running the business before financing and accounting charges, and its margin was 16.8% in that quarter, against 16.4% a year earlier.[21] The net margin is final profit as a share of sales; it was 13.4% for FY26 by the company’s own count.[9] The operating margin is profit after all running costs but before interest and tax, shown as a share of sales; the annual report gives 16.7% for FY26 on that basis.[2] The gap between the two operating figures (Screener shows 18% for the year) comes from how each source treats other operating income and some expense lines, so this report quotes the source it names each time.[16]

Sales reach shoppers through four routes. General trade means the small neighbourhood shops and wholesalers, and it is by far the largest route. Modern trade means supermarket chains. E-commerce, which was 4% of domestic sales in FY25, rose to 6% in FY26, and above 12% once the ₹5 and ₹10 packs that barely sell online are set aside.[9] Quick commerce, the 10-minute delivery apps, is now 80% to 85% of e-commerce sales.[8] The fourth route is institutional supply, such as the defence canteen channel.[8] The foreign business is not reported as a share of revenue in the sources we read; the annual report shows standalone foreign exchange earnings of ₹442.04 crore, which is 2.4% of standalone revenue of ₹18,445.82 crore.[6][1] It was hit in early 2026 when shipping through the Strait of Hormuz stopped.[9]

Britannia belongs to the Wadia group. The promoter group holds 50.55% of the shares, almost all through step-down subsidiaries of Bombay Burmah Trading, and none of those shares is pledged.[15][31][12] Leadership changed in the past year. Mr. Varun Berry resigned as managing director and chief executive on 10 November 2025 after 13 years.[26] Mr. Rakshit Hargave, previously chief executive of the Aditya Birla group’s paint business Birla Opus, took over on 15 December 2025 for a five-year term.[26][5] His first full year is therefore FY27, which is why the next two results matter for judging his plan.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Britannia’s strength is that it is the biggest brand owner in a habit-forming category with a very large reach. Crisil puts its biscuit market share at more than a third by value and notes that direct reach rose almost four-fold in eleven years.[13] Parle is the other national giant. Its FY25 revenue was ₹15,568.49 crore against ₹17,942.67 crore for Britannia, so the two are closer than a single “market leader” label suggests.[33] The category itself is large, valued at about ₹1.16 lakh crore and expected by one forecast to grow 6.8% a year, and a second forecast puts growth at 5.70% a year in dollar terms.[33][34]

The financial evidence of a strong business is in the filings. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit a business earns on each rupee of capital it uses, and Britannia’s was 56% in FY26.[16] Working capital is the money tied up in running a business day to day, such as stock and unpaid bills less what is owed to suppliers. Britannia’s is negative at 28 days, meaning suppliers fund the business.[16] It also spends little to stay in business. Consolidated purchases of plant and equipment were ₹205.57 crore in FY26, against cash from operations of ₹2,611.60 crore.[3] Crisil rates the company AAA with a stable outlook, and it reaffirmed that on 6 October 2026.[14][13]

The weaknesses are about growth, price points and competition. Sales grew 6% a year over three years and 8% over five, and profit grew 8% and 6%, according to Screener.[16] Earnings per share were ₹96.39 in FY23 and ₹105.18 in FY26, which is about 3% a year.[16] On the November 2025 call Mr. Berry said the large players were losing “a slight” share to “multiple local regional competitors”, and that some national rivals were using discounts in modern trade.[11] The new chief executive named regional competition as a strategic priority on his first call.[10] Britannia has not published a current market share figure for itself, so we cannot size the loss.[8]

Where the competitive edge is

The edge is distribution and cost. Britannia served 5,299 dealers and distributors in FY26, 640 more than the year before, and says its cost-saving programme now runs at ten times the discipline of 2013-14.[7][9] The cost programme matters because input costs move more than prices can. In the June 2026 quarter LPG and piped gas used in baking rose to 2.5 times the February price in April and May and settled near 1.5 times; palm oil rose more than 20% to about ₹140; sugar rose again after the quarter ended.[8][19] A company that cannot raise the ₹5 price must find savings elsewhere, and a large buyer with forward cover does that better. Management said it buys palm oil forward and was covered for another five months as of May 2026.[9]

What this means for an owner: the edge is real, but it protects the level of profit more than its growth. The record shows it. The operating margin was 19% in FY24 and 17.8% in FY25 on Crisil’s count, and 16.7% in FY26 on the company’s own, while sales grew in single digits.[13][2] The next subsection looks at what could erode it.

Where the business is thin or exposed

Four exposures stand out. First, the ₹5 and ₹10 pack structure lets regional rivals undercut: after GST on most packaged food fell from 12-18% to 5% in September 2025, rivals priced ₹5 packs at ₹4.50 and ₹10 packs at ₹9, while Britannia mostly added biscuits to the pack, although some of its packs, such as Nice Time, also showed odd prices in shops. Management said this confused the price printed on packs and the price shops paid in rural and wholesale trade.[25][27][29][9][8] Management said the dual pricing ended in June 2026, but it hit April and the first half of May.[8] Second, commodity cost: wheat, sugar, edible oil, milk and fuel all move together with oil prices, and the annual report names them as the key risk.[6][8]

Third, health. A consumer shift away from refined-flour snacks is a slow risk, and the CEO said on the August 2026 call that Britannia has teams building a health and protein platform and said an acquisition is on the table, but gave no product or date.[8] Fourth, leadership is new: the chief executive changed in November 2025, and the CEO said in August 2026 that the senior team was now complete.[26][8] A small tax matter is open too: a ₹6.37 crore GST demand from March 2026 for FY21 to FY24, which the company called immaterial and said it would appeal.[30] None of these breaks the franchise. Together they explain why growth has been slow and why the price has fallen: the market is paying for the franchise, not for a quick recovery.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of results show a steady, high-quality business that has grown slowly. Sales rose from ₹14,136 crore in FY22 to ₹19,152 crore in FY26, a gain of 35.5% or 7.9% a year, and the trailing twelve months to June 2026 reached ₹19,529 crore.[16] Operating profit on Screener’s basis rose from ₹2,201 crore to ₹3,514 crore, and net profit from ₹1,516 crore to ₹2,537 crore. EPS (earnings per share) is the company’s profit divided across all its shares, and it went from ₹63.31 to ₹105.18.[16] The five-year profit growth is only 6% a year because FY21 was a high starting point at ₹1,851 crore, and the three-year figure is 8%.[16]

Cash follows profit closely. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before any borrowing or investing, and it was ₹2,612 crore in FY26 against profit of ₹2,537 crore.[16] Free cash flow (FCF) is cash flow from operations minus spending on plant and equipment, and Screener shows ₹2,408 crore for FY26, which is a 2.2% yield on a market value of ₹1,10,012 crore.[16] The company pays out most of what it earns: the FY26 final dividend of ₹2,179.86 crore is 85% of standalone profit.[1] That leaves little to build up cash, and it explains why borrowings rose from ₹1,247 crore to ₹1,380 crore in FY26 even though profit and cash were both higher.[16]

The balance sheet is a strength. Borrowings fell from a peak of ₹2,997 crore in FY23 to ₹1,380 crore, and investments of ₹3,610 crore are larger than borrowings.[16] The debt-to-equity ratio is borrowings divided by shareholders’ money; on the consolidated FY26 balance sheet borrowings of ₹1,357.83 crore sit against equity of ₹5,135.88 crore, which is 0.26, and interest cover is 30.7 times on the company’s ratio table.[4][2] Crisil’s last full rationale, from October 2025, put gearing at 0.3 and kept its highest rating.[13]

One feature of the latest year flatters the growth rate. The tax charge in the March 2026 quarter was ₹105.43 crore on a pre-tax profit of ₹785.11 crore, a 13% rate against the 26% of other quarters, because some income-tax cases were closed and provisions released.[24][9][17] At a 26% rate the tax would have been about ₹204 crore, so profit was about ₹99 crore (₹4.1 a share) higher than a normal quarter. Without it FY26 EPS would be near ₹101 and the trailing profit growth of 19% would be closer to 14%.[24][17]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 tests the business against fixed floors, then asks two further questions. The first is level versus trajectory: where the numbers stand, and which way they are moving. The second is archetype: which of three templates the stock fits. Our chart rule uses the Weinstein stage scale. Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend. Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend, in which the price sits below a falling 50-day average that itself sits below a falling 200-day average. A base is the sideways floor a stock builds before a new advance, and the pivot is the top of that floor. Test C, our turnaround test, asks whether a fallen stock has built such a floor and begun to turn up.[42] The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) is the share price divided by a year’s earnings per share. The asymmetry of a trade, also called reward-to-risk, is the potential gain divided by the potential loss from an entry price, measured to a target and to a stop. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned. Our technical routing rule says only Stage 4 with Test C failing is a technical disqualifier, and every other chart state routes to waiting for support.[42]

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on future profit. A Reverse DCF works backwards from the price to find the yearly profit growth the market must expect. We solve for the growth rate that, over ten years, discounted at 12% a year and with the business valued at 20 times profit at the end of year ten, equals today’s market value. The ledger starts from a market value of ₹1,10,011.77 crore and trailing profit of ₹2,592.78 crore, which is the market value divided by the trailing P/E.[41] It solves to 16.59% a year for ten years. That is the first number to hold against reality.

Reality has three reference points. First, history: profit grew 8% a year over three years and 6% over five, and the 19% trailing figure is flattered by the March 2026 tax benefit.[16][24] Second, the street: the average analyst has EPS rising from ₹105.18 in FY26 to ₹129.78 in FY28, which is 11.1% a year, and Motilal Oswal models a profit growth of 14% a year for FY26 to FY28.[41][16][19] Third, Nuvama expects profit of ₹3,834 crore in FY29 against ₹2,534 crore in FY26, about 14.8% a year over three years.[20] Even the bullish forecasts sit below 16.59% a year, and they need to hold for ten years, not three. In plain terms: the price is not cheap on cash-flow maths, whatever the fall from the high suggests.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one weak assumption, and this chapter names it, says what evidence would prove it wrong, and says when we will see that evidence. We also state the strongest honest case against our own view, which is the red-team bear case. The dates below are the dates of publication, not guaranteed events.

The earnings estimate: that margin can be rebuilt

Our FY27 EPS of ₹108 assumes the September and December quarters give back some of last year’s 20% margin but not all of it. The weakest part is cost. LPG and piped gas were 1.5 times February prices at the time of the August call, palm oil was up 20% to about ₹140, and sugar rose ₹7 a kilo after the quarter ended.[8][19] Management has priced for only half of this. If fuel stays high and rivals do not raise prices, EPS stays near ₹106. The evidence will arrive on 5 November 2026, when the September quarter and its EBITDA margin are reported, with a call on 6 November, and again with the December quarter in early February 2027, a date not yet announced.[17]

The multiple: that 42 times holds

Our base case keeps the P/E at 42.2. The weakest part is that the stock has already fallen from about 71 times to 42 times in two and a half years, and nothing yet says it has stopped.[40][17] If growth stays near the 6% to 8% the company has shown in the past, peers such as Hindustan Unilever at 38.1 and Dabur at 32.0 suggest where a slower-growing brand business trades. A fall to 36 times with EPS unchanged at ₹121 gives ₹4,356, a 4.6% loss, so the multiple alone can remove the upside.[37][39] The evidence is the price itself over the next two quarters, and whether domestic funds, which now own 21.41%, keep buying as foreign holdings fall.[18]

The red-team bear case, in full

The strongest case against our view is that the stock is a good business at a price that still leaves little room. Free cash flow of ₹2,408 crore is a 2.2% yield and the earnings yield is 2.4%, so a buyer earns little in cash and depends on growth that has been 6% to 8% a year.[16] Growth now depends on shrinkflation, which cannot be repeated for ever, and on regional rivals not taking more of the ₹5 and ₹10 trade.[8][11] The new chief executive has made three calls and promised a health and protein platform without a product or a date.[8] Foreign holdings have fallen from 18.99% to 12.94% over the 11 quarters since December 2023.[18] In that case the stock falls to about ₹4,030 (36 times ₹112) and the stop at ₹4,406.83 would not hold on a gap down. The loss from entry in that case could be 11.8% against a planned 3.4%. We give this case a 30% probability because of the evidence in the scenario table: a Stage 4 chart, a foreign holding down for 11 quarters, a first-year chief executive and unresolved cost inflation.
