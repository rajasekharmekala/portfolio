Before you read

Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) is India's main government maker of military aircraft, helicopters and jet engines, and the state owns 71.64% of it.[5] It looks excellent on paper: no debt, a 32% return on capital and signed orders of ₹2,54,538 crore, which is 7.7 times last year's sales.[2] Our verdict is REJECTED for now, because at ₹4,989 the gain we can defend is only 1.48 times the loss we would accept, and our rule asks for 3.0 times. No Tejas Mk1A fighter has been delivered yet, so only two of five operating checks pass.[9] A price near ₹4,735 would fix only the payoff-ratio test, and the verdict moves only if HAL also delivers jets steadily and the operating checks reach four of five.

1. What the company does

HAL designs, builds, repairs and upgrades military aircraft, helicopters, jet engines and the electronics inside them. It is a government company under the Ministry of Defence, and the Indian armed forces are almost its only customer. The annual report says about 98% of the company's turnover, about 99% of its trade receivables and about 100% of its customer advances come from the government and related bodies.[1] So when you buy HAL shares you are buying a slice of India's defence procurement budget, which the Union Budget for 2026-27 raised to a record ₹7.85 lakh crore.[23]

The business has two halves. In the year to March 2026 HAL had revenue of ₹33,050 crore. Of that, ₹9,227 crore came from making new equipment and ₹20,524 crore from repair and overhaul work, with exports of ₹501 crore.[2] Management puts the split at roughly 28% new manufacturing, 62% repair and overhaul and 10% other.[2] Repair and overhaul is steady work: HAL services the aircraft and engines the air force already flies. New manufacturing is lumpy, because HAL books the sale only when it delivers the finished item, not as it builds.[2]

That delivery rule explains the shape of the year. In the quarter ended March 2026 HAL booked ₹13,942 crore of sales, which is about 42% of the whole year, while the June 2026 quarter brought ₹5,515 crore.[30] A delayed aircraft therefore moves a whole quarter. It also means HAL carries a very large inventory: ₹30,851 crore at March 2026, up from ₹21,676 crore a year earlier.[1] Management says about ₹8,100 crore of it is for Tejas aircraft that have not been delivered.[2]

How HAL gets paid is unusual. The Ministry of Defence pays large advances and milestone payments before delivery. At March 2026 these customer prepayments stood at ₹73,936 crore, against ₹52,219 crore a year earlier.[1][4] HAL parks that money in bank deposits (₹46,197 crore at March 2026) and earned about ₹3,743 crore of other income in FY26, which is about 31% of its profit before tax.[1][30] Contracts also carry clauses that pass higher foreign-exchange and material costs on to the customer, so HAL's profit per aircraft is set by the contract, not by the market.[2]

What HAL makes, in plain terms

Fixed-wing aircraft come first. HAL builds the Su-30MKI fighter under a Russian licence, the Tejas light fighter with the state design agency ADA, the Dornier-228 transport and the new HTT-40 basic trainer. A contract signed in December 2024 asks HAL for 12 more Su-30MKI fighters at ₹13,500 crore.[20] The 97 Tejas Mk1A order of ₹62,370 crore, signed in September 2025, adds 68 fighters and 29 two-seaters, with deliveries meant to start in 2027-28.[22]

Helicopters are the second group: the ALH Dhruv, the Light Combat Helicopter Prachand and the Light Utility Helicopter. In March 2025 the ministry signed two contracts worth ₹62,700 crore for 156 Prachand helicopters, to be delivered over five years starting in the third year.[28][29] Engines are the third group, and the AL-31FP contract of about ₹26,000 crore for 240 engines (signed September 2024, to finish by 2032) is the biggest single engine order.[18][19]

Engines matter for a second reason. Several HAL products depend on engines HAL does not design: the American GE F404 for Tejas Mk1A, the Honeywell engine for HTT-40 and the Russian-origin AL-31FP. The CMD told analysts that supply-chain slowness is global and that GE had its own casting problems.[2] This outside dependence is the weak link in the whole story, and Chapters 2B and 6 return to it.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • order book is the total value of signed contracts that have not yet been delivered and turned into sales.
  • relative strength index (RSI) is a meter from 0 to 100 that shows how fast a price has been moving. Above 70 is often called overheated and below 30 oversold.
  • average true range (ATR) is the typical size of one day's price swing, in rupees. We use it to measure how far a price sits from support.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is operating profit before those four items. HAL reports a total EBITDA that includes interest income and an operating EBITDA that leaves it out.
  • return on capital employed (ROCE) is the yearly profit before interest and tax as a share of the capital the business uses. It tells you how well each rupee of capital is put to work.
  • earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit divided by its number of shares.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and paying for plants and equipment.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business brings in from its own work, before buying assets and before financing.
  • working capital is the money tied up in day-to-day running: stock, bills customers have not yet paid, and money owed to suppliers.
  • guidance is a company's own forecast for the coming period.
  • consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow a stock.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by the profit per share. It shows how many rupees you pay for each rupee of yearly profit.
  • book-to-bill is new orders divided by sales in the same period. A figure above 1 means the order book is growing.
  • operating margin is the share of each rupee of sales left after the running costs of the business.
  • debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Zero means the company has no borrowings.
  • net debt is borrowings minus the cash the company holds. A negative figure means more cash than debt.
  • capital expenditure is spending on plants, machines and software that last for years.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing prices over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. They are slowly moving lines that a price often bounces from.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the four-stage chart scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • base is a sideways stretch of trading that a stock builds before a larger move.
  • pivot is the top of a base, the level a price must clear to start a new leg up.
  • breakout is a move that takes the price above a pivot on rising volume.
  • stop loss is the price at which we admit the idea was wrong and sell.
  • limit order is an order to buy only at a chosen price or lower, so it never chases the market.
  • entry zone is the band of prices where we would be willing to buy.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain we expect divided by the loss we would take. The formula is (target − price) ÷ (price − stop).
  • measured move is a price target found by adding the height of a base to the level where the price breaks out of it.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

HAL's strength is position, not product. It is the only Indian company that builds fighters, combat helicopters and fighter engines at scale, and the government has made indigenous buying a policy. The credit agency CARE rates it AAA with a stable outlook and calls it the core defence aviation supplier to the government.[4] The order book shows the result: ₹2,54,538 crore at 31 March 2026, up from ₹1,89,302 crore a year earlier, after ₹97,028 crore of new orders in FY26.[2] That is 7.7 times FY26 revenue, so on paper the factories are booked for most of a decade.

The balance sheet adds to that strength. Borrowings are zero, finance cost in FY26 was only ₹5.8 crore, and the company held ₹46,197 crore in cash and bank deposits at March 2026.[1] CARE notes that the average time HAL takes to collect its bills was 55 days in FY25 and that total receivables stayed under ₹4,800 crore for four years.[4] The ministry is a slow but certain payer. A government customer that pays and a regulator that wants home-made equipment are a very strong moat.

The weakness sits in execution. HAL's sales grew only 2% in FY25 and 7% in FY26, while its order book rose from ₹94,127 crore in April 2024 to ₹2,54,538 crore in March 2026.[30][3][2] The Tejas Mk1A contract of February 2021 for 83 jets was meant to start deliveries in February 2024, and as of 3 October 2026 not one jet had reached the air force.[9] A summary of the FY26 annual report says 35 jets were due by March 2026 under the original schedule, and none was handed over.[25] Competition from private firms is real but so far limited to parts and structures. CARE warns of some rise in private-sector competition, and HAL itself outsources some helicopter structures to private firms.[4][10]

Pricing power is limited, because the customer sets the price. Contracts include foreign-exchange and material escalation clauses, so margins are steady, but there is no room to raise prices when costs rise.[2] Delay is also costly. HAL's standalone provision for liquidated damages (penalties for late delivery) rose to ₹1,814 crore at March 2026, after ₹722 crore was added in FY26.[1] The ministry has warned HAL that it too could face penalties for missing its schedule, while HAL has charged GE for the engine delays.[9]

Government ownership and what it means for a shareholder

The President of India holds 47,91,02,424 shares, which is 71.64% of the company, and the BSE filing for June 2026 states that no promoter shares are pledged or otherwise encumbered.[1][5] The holding has been 71.64% since March 2024, so the state has not sold any since then.[30] That is good for governance of the balance sheet, but it also leaves an overhang: by 7 July 2026 the government had sold stakes in six other state companies in FY27, raising ₹18,561 crore, and CARE lists a fall in the state's holding below a majority as a rating trigger.[26][4] We found no announcement of a HAL share sale, but a sale is a policy choice we cannot rule out.

The board is also thin. The annual report shows five independent director posts lying vacant at March 2026, and the auditors' report drew attention to board and committee quorum rules that were missed for a period because of the vacancies. The report says the committees were later reconstituted.[1] A new chairman, Ravi K, took charge on 1 May 2026, and the Q4 call in May 2026 was his first.[2] Dividends follow government rules: the board paid ₹35 per share in interim dividend and recommended ₹10 final for FY26, a total of ₹45 or about 33% of profit, in line with the minimum-dividend guidelines of the government's investment department.[1]

What this means for an owner

If you own HAL, the order book protects you from a collapse in demand, and the balance sheet protects you from a funding scare. Neither protects you from slow delivery, which is where the profit growth is decided. The market already pays a full price for that protection, as Chapters 3 and 5 show. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record is the test of whether the order book will convert. We compared what the company said at its May 2025 call with what happened by the May 2026 call and by mid-October 2026. The pattern is clear. On things HAL controls inside its own factories, it delivers. On things that depend on an outside supplier or on the customer's certification, it has slipped, and each time it has reset the target to the next few months.

The engine promise is the clearest case. In May 2025 the then CMD said GE had confirmed 12 F404 engines by December 2025 and that 12 aircraft would be ready that year.[3] By March 2026 six engines had arrived, and by the end of September 2026 the total was 14.[16] The delivery date moved too. In May 2026 the CMD expected first delivery in August or September and about 20 aircraft in FY27.[2] By mid-September he said about 10 aircraft were possible and declined to commit to a number for March 2027.[14][10] As of the 3 October report no jet had been handed over, and a review with the air force was set for after mid-October.[9]

Financial guidance was missed by a smaller margin. HAL guided FY26 revenue growth at 8% to 10% and a 31% operating margin, and delivered 7% growth and a 30% margin.[3][2] For FY27 it guides 10% to 12% growth, and the first quarter grew 14.4%, so this promise is on track so far.[2][6] On capital allocation, HAL paid ₹45 per share for FY26, about 33% of profit, in line with the government's minimum-dividend guidelines.[1] It also spent ₹2,386 crore on capital expenditure against a plan of ₹12,000 crore by 2030, so that plan has four more years to run and is only on track so far.[2]
