1. What the company does

Hindustan Aeronautics Limited designs, builds, repairs and upgrades military aircraft, helicopters, jet engines and the electronics inside them. Its customers are India's armed forces: the Air Force, Army, Navy and Coast Guard. The company's own notes say it was set up to meet their needs in aerospace, and it works through five production complexes[3]. The President of India, acting through the Ministry of Defence, holds 71.64 percent of the shares, and the stock trades on both the Bombay and National exchanges[3]. Around 98 percent of turnover, 99 percent of customer bills and 100 percent of customer advances are with the government or its bodies[3].

HAL earns money in two ways. The first is manufacturing: new fighters, trainers, helicopters and engines. The second is repair, overhaul and spares, called ROH, which keeps the armed forces' existing fleets flying. In FY26, manufacturing brought in ₹9,227 crore and ROH ₹20,524 crore, and management expects the mix to move from roughly 30:70 toward 50:50 as new platforms arrive[5]. Manufacturing revenue counts only at delivery, not as work is done, which management confirmed on the May 2026 call[5]. A fighter can take years to build and add nothing to sales until the day it is handed over.

That rule makes revenue lumpy. In the March 2026 quarter HAL booked ₹13,942 crore of sales, which is 42 percent of the year's ₹33,089 crore, and in the June 2026 quarter it booked ₹5,515 crore[1]. The same pattern held in FY25, when the March quarter carried 44 percent of the year[1]. A reader who looks only at one quarter will misjudge the year, and a reader who looks only at the year will miss how much hangs on a handful of delivery dates.

What keeps the factory busy is the order book: ₹2,54,538 crore at 31 March 2026, up from ₹1,89,302 crore a year earlier, or about 7.7 times FY26 revenue[5][3]. FY26 brought ₹97,028 crore of fresh orders, led by 97 Tejas Mk1A fighters worth over ₹62,370 crore, signed 25 September 2025, with deliveries to begin in 2027-28 and run for six years[5][8]. A second large block is 156 Prachand light combat helicopters worth ₹62,700 crore, signed on 28 March 2025[9]. Behind this sits the national budget: the Ministry of Defence gets ₹7.85 lakh crore in 2026-27, and ₹1.39 lakh crore of it is earmarked for domestic industry[10]. Management expects about ₹90,000 crore of further orders, including repair work, within two years, among them 143 Advanced Light Helicopters for the Army and the upgrade of 40 Dornier aircraft[5]. The Defence Acquisition Council gave in-principle approval for more Advanced Light Helicopters on 7 September 2026, which is a step toward a contract and not the contract itself[11].

Two money ideas run through the rest of this report. The first is working capital, the cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills, less what suppliers have not yet been paid. HAL's stock stood at ₹30,851 crore at March 2026, up from ₹21,676 crore a year earlier[3]. The second is that the customers pay in advance. Non-current customer advances and milestone receipts, which the accounts call contract liabilities, stood at ₹44,689 crore at March 2026 against ₹24,821 crore a year before[3]. Customer money therefore funds the factory, HAL's consolidated borrowings were only ₹66 crore[1], and the interest earned on the cash that results was ₹3,087 crore in FY26, about a quarter of pre-tax profit[3]. Chapter 3 shows why this matters for how far the reported cash flow can be trusted.

Who the customer is and why that matters

With one customer, HAL does not compete for each sale in the usual way. The Ministry of Defence picks the platform, agrees a price, pays advances and sets delivery dates. That is why the order book is so large and why HAL's own credit rating is AAA: CARE rates its ₹6,050 crore of bank facilities AAA with a stable outlook and ICRA rates them [ICRA]AAA stable[14][15]. HAL's growth is capped by what the armed forces can absorb and the budget pays for, and CARE names that dependence as a risk[14].

The 2026-27 Defence Ministry allocation of ₹7.85 lakh crore is 15.19 percent above the previous year's budget estimate[10]. What this means for an owner: demand is policy-backed for years, but conversion into revenue depends on HAL's delivery speed, which chapter 2 examines.

How the price of an aircraft is set

Many of HAL's repeat products are priced by a Ministry process called the final price quote, set by a price negotiation committee. The company's accounts state that the third such committee's prices run only up to 2022-23, and that the fourth is still in progress, so prices for 2023-24 onward remain unsettled[3]. In the meantime HAL books revenue at the last agreed price plus indices, and for the June 2026 quarter it used the 2024-25 prices without any escalation[4]. The final settlement can move past revenue up or down, so reported margins are partly provisional.

For an owner, the point is that a part of what looks like profit today may be revised when the Ministry fixes the final price. The risk chapter returns to this, and the September-quarter results are the next place to look for a note on it[4].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

HAL's strength is position, not price. ICRA calls it the sole domestic supplier of aircraft, helicopters, engines, avionics and accessories for the armed forces[15]. The company has absorbed the technology to build the MiG-21, MiG-27, Su-30MKI and Jaguar under licence, and now designs its own helicopters and trainers; it filed 223 intellectual-property applications in FY26 and holds 1,226 granted rights[3][5]. It spent ₹2,794 crore on research and development in FY26, about 8.4 percent of revenue[5]. Few firms in India can match that, and ICRA says the capital and time needed keep private competitors small[15].

Scale shows in the numbers. Revenue has grown from ₹24,620 crore in FY22 to ₹33,089 crore in FY26, and the consolidated operating margin, which is profit before other income as a share of sales, has held between 22 and 32 percent, with 30 percent in FY26[1]. Return on capital employed, called ROCE (return on capital employed), was 32.0 percent[1]. The company declared ₹45 a share of dividend for FY26, worth ₹3,009 crore[3].

Why the position is hard to copy

A fighter programme needs design teams, test pilots, certification and a supplier network built over decades. HAL has all of them, and it can show products flying. Its latest orders are also deep: the 97-aircraft Tejas contract alone carries over 64 percent local content and about 105 Indian suppliers[8]. The 156-helicopter contract involves over 250 domestic companies[9]. The moat is therefore a mix of regulation, relationships and accumulated engineering, and it is wide in military helicopters, trainers and Su-30 related work.

It is not unbreachable. CARE says there might be some increase in competition from the private sector[14]. On the call in May 2026 the chairman said the Air Force's request for the next-generation fighter covers development of prototypes only and has not said the same builder will make it[5]. HAL's licence to keep winning is strong in what it builds now, and not guaranteed in what comes next.

Where the model is weak

The weak point is delivery. Management promised double-digit growth from FY25 and for years after, and revenue grew 2.0 percent in FY25 and 6.8 percent in FY26[7][1]. The main cause is a single imported part: the GE F404 engine, which had reached only 14 units delivered for the Tejas by September 2026 against a contract for 99 (secondary)[18]. HAL also depends on Honeywell for the HTT-40's engine, where 3 of the first 16 engines had arrived by June 2026 (secondary)[21]. Even the faster 2026 deliveries leave HAL waiting on the Air Force to accept the aircraft: the chairman says radar software changes and certification flying remain[16].

HAL also carries two governance gaps. Five of seven independent-director seats were vacant at 31 March 2026[3], and in the June 2026 results HAL stated it could not reconstitute its Audit Committee for lack of independent directors[4]. Neither is unusual for a government company, but a minority owner cannot fix either, and both weaken the check on provisions and prices. Smaller loose ends exist too: on 2 October 2026 HAL agreed to take the other half of a helicopter-training simulator venture from CAE Canada at nil cost, and that venture, with ₹80.95 crore of FY26 turnover, carries a going-concern note of its own[13][4].

Where the cash comes from, and why it matters

HAL is a rare industrial company whose customers fund its factory. Advances and milestone payments from the Ministry rose by about ₹19,900 crore in FY26 on the balance sheet[3]. CARE notes that the large interest-free advances mean HAL uses none of its bank limits[14]. The advantage is no debt and a large cash pile that earns interest. The risk is that advances are spent on parts and labour, and when they stop growing, so does the cash flow they inflate. Chapter 3 puts a number on this.

What this means for an owner: the balance sheet is safe, but about a quarter of pre-tax profit is interest on customer money and margins rest on provisional prices. The next chapter scores the business and checks what management said against what it did.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk compares what management said on dated occasions with what then happened. Three different chairmen led the three calls used here, and Ravi K has been Chairman and Managing Director only since 1 May 2026[7][6][5]. The Director Finance, Dr Barenya Senapati, spoke on the calls in 2025 and 2026 and gave the financial guidance — management's official forecast —, the company's own forecast, on both[6][5].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions, and this chapter names the weakest of each, the evidence that would show it wrong, and when we will know. The next table does that for the central value. The ratio at the close is the one the key-figures strip prints; the ratio at ₹4,620.21 is the one the scoring rules use for a limit-order setup[36].

Weakest assumption of the earnings estimate: 10 Tejas deliveries in FY27

The central case needs the Air Force to accept about 10 Tejas Mk1A by March 2027. The evidence for it is real. HAL has prepared about 27 airframes and calls the programme 90 to 95 percent complete, 14 GE engines have arrived, GE has promised more by December, and the chairman says engines on hand support 10 aircraft (secondary)[16][18]. The evidence against is also real. The review between HAL and the Air Force, expected in September, was pushed back, and HAL says remaining radar issues, software changes and certification flying stand between it and handover, so it has not committed to a date (secondary)[17][16]. The Air Force would accept the aircraft with some capabilities deferred under exemptions, according to one secondary report that we do not rely on for any number[20].

The one rule used throughout is this. No Tejas Mk1A handed over by 31 December 2026 cuts the case, and fewer than 10 handed over by 31 March 2027 fails it. Warning signs short of that are no firm date from HAL at the September-quarter results, or an Air Force statement that acceptance needs the full weapons package. We will know at the September-quarter results (date not announced; the listing rule's 45-day limit is 14 November 2026, which we have not confirmed), at the next HAL-Air Force review, and finally on 31 March 2027[26].

Weakest assumption of the margin: provisional pricing and damages

The central case holds the operating margin near 29.5 percent. Two items could pull it down. HAL's prices for 2023-24 onward are provisional, built on the last agreed price plus indices, and the June 2026 quarter used 2024-25 prices with no escalation[3][4]. A final settlement could move revenue either way, including for past years. Second, HAL provisioned ₹722 crore for liquidated damages in FY26 and ₹658 crore in FY25, and it faces further damages exposure to the Air Force on late Tejas; it has also invoked damages against GE, which management declined to quantify (secondary)[3][28][5]. An accident in FY25 led to an ₹800 crore provision for a replacement aircraft[5].

What would show the estimate wrong: an operating margin below 22 percent in the September quarter, two points under the 24 percent of a year ago, or revenue growth below 5 percent, or a price-settlement note in the results. We will know at each quarterly result and at the fourth price negotiation outcome, whose date is not published.

Weakest assumption of the value: a 33.9 multiple in a sector that is falling

The value uses a multiple of 33.9, below the 29-month median of 35.1 but well above the 10th percentile of 30.0 that the series has touched[35]. The sector is under pressure: the Nifty India Defence index fell 2.60 percent on 23 October 2026 and BEL and Mazagon Dock are down 14 and 34 percent over a year (secondary)[25][29][30]. A group-wide de-rating can take HAL's multiple lower even if its earnings come in on plan. At 30.0 times, with earnings unchanged, the value is about ₹4,797, only 1.5 percent above the close, and the ratio is 0.24 at the close and 0.93 at the zone top, below the floor. Earnings growth of 8.3 percent a year would normally pull a 33.9 multiple down, not hold it, so keeping 33.9 is an assumption and not a forecast. Our portfolio also holds Astra Microwave, a defence supplier that won a ₹2,205 crore radar order from HAL (secondary), so a sector fall would hit Astra Microwave and a HAL position together[24][33].

What would show the estimate wrong: HAL's trailing P/E falling below 32.5 without a fall in earnings. A refreshed ratio at the then-current multiple, and at 32.5 times, is a prerequisite to any entry. We will know daily, since the price is public.

Weakest assumption of the cash view: that advances keep arriving

The balance sheet is safe because customers prepay. Standalone non-current customer advances and milestone receipts rose to ₹44,689 crore from ₹24,821 crore, and the cash flow statement's wider line, financial liabilities, provisions and other liabilities, added ₹20,344 crore to FY26 operating cash flow of ₹10,920 crore on the same standalone scope[3]. That line is not customer advances alone, and the filed text we hold does not separate advances from provisions and other liabilities, so removing it gives a sensitivity of about minus ₹9,424 crore and not a normalised cash flow. ICRA notes that HAL's outside liabilities were 2.0 times its net worth at March 2025, and says advances without bank guarantees give comfort[15]. If orders slow, advances stop growing, stock is no longer funded by customers, and interest income, about a quarter of pre-tax profit, falls with the cash balance. CARE reported free cash of ₹43,465 crore at September 2025, which is the pool the interest comes from[14].

What would show the estimate wrong: a year-end balance sheet showing contract liabilities falling while stock stays high, or a three-year cash-to-profit ratio below 0.7. A negative half-year operating cash flow is a prompt to re-underwrite, not a score, because the half-year baseline is not in the sources we hold. The half-year statement comes with the September-quarter results, as the exchange rules require it only at half-year and year end[4].

The red-team bear case

The strongest honest case against owning HAL goes like this. The stock trades at 34 times profit, and the price needs about 15 percent a year of cash flow growth for ten years, while revenue has grown 7 percent a year and the last two years missed a double-digit promise[35][1][7]. A quarter of profit is interest on customer-funded cash, and some margin rests on prices not yet settled[3][4]. The Tejas was due to start deliveries in March 2024, has missed each dated promise since May 2025 and depends on one foreign engine maker[16][6][19][18]. The board lacks most of its independent directors, and its owner is also its customer[3][4]. In that world the stock goes to ₹3,650, a fall of 22.8 percent. The stop at ₹4,431.11 is an exit instruction and not a loss ceiling: the adjusted price series shows a one-day fall of 17.8 percent on 4 June 2024 (own computation)[35].

Where the bear case is weakest: the business has no debt, an AAA rating, a customer that prepays and ₹2,54,538 crore of orders, so the downside is a valuation fall and not an impairment[14][15][5]. That is why the verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT and not REJECTED.
