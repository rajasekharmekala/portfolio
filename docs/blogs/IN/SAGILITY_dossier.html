1. What the company does

Sagility is a company that takes over the repetitive paperwork and phone work of American healthcare. Its main customers are health insurers, called payers. When an insurer needs claims processed, member questions answered, nurses to review medical cases, or payments checked for errors, Sagility staff do that work. In the quarter to 30 September 2026, payers supplied ₹18,262 million of ₹20,321 million revenue, about 89.9%.[3][4] The rest comes from providers, such as hospitals and physician groups, where Sagility runs patient registration, billing and collection of unpaid bills, a field called revenue cycle management.[5]

The people sit in India, the Philippines, the US, Jamaica and Colombia.[5] At the end of Q2 FY27 the company employed 51,486 people across 28 delivery sites, and it served 7 of the top US health plans.[1][6] All revenue comes from customers in the US, billed in dollars, while most costs are in rupees and other currencies.[5] That mix explains a number you will see again: Q2 revenue grew 22.5% in rupees but 12.2% in constant currency, which strips out the effect of the falling rupee.[1] The 10-point gap is currency, not new work.

The business has a short history as a separate company. It was formed in January 2022 when a fund managed by the private-equity firm EQT bought the healthcare operations of Hinduja Global Solutions (HGS) through a Dutch holding company, Sagility B.V.[7] The shares listed on the NSE and BSE on 12 November 2024 through a pure offer for sale at ₹30, so the company itself raised no money.[5][8] Sagility B.V. has since sold down from 82.39% to 50.95% of the shares.[9]

Money comes in three ways. The first is steady-state fees for ongoing work. The second is seasonal volume: health plans enrol people during the autumn open enrolment and Medicare annual enrolment periods (AEP), so Q3 and Q4 are the stronger half and Q1 and Q2 the weaker.[10] The third is large new deals; in Q2 management cited a payment integrity win worth over $10 million.[11] Management says clients keep asking for lower costs, and that most of the productivity gain from artificial intelligence (AI) is handed back to clients through price.[11]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position rests on three things: scale in a narrow field, long client relationships, and domain skill. Its largest ten client groups now each bring in more than US$20 million a year, up from four such clients in FY23.[12] Both Indian rating agencies call out the established position and the repeat revenue. Crisil gave an A/Stable rating and ICRA upgraded to BBB+ before withdrawing at the company's request.[5][13] A rating is a lender's view of repayment risk, so it is a useful outside check on balance-sheet strength but says little about growth.

The weakness is concentration. The top three client groups made 60.1% of trailing revenue and the top ten made 84.4%.[12] That is better than FY23, when the top three made 72.4%, but still means that one lost contract or a merger between two insurers could move the whole company.[14] The company added 17 new clients in FY26, and in H1 FY27 it booked US$40.2 million of annual contract value, almost all from 31 existing clients and one new logo.[15][1] Crisil lists the same risk and notes that all revenue comes from the US.[5] Management accepts that clients want cost taken out every year.[11]

Profit quality needs a careful reading. Reported net profit for FY26 was ₹925 crore on sales of ₹7,193 crore, but about two-thirds of the ₹12,450 crore of assets is goodwill and acquired intangibles created when EQT bought the business.[16][17] Goodwill is the premium paid above the value of physical and identifiable assets. Because it sits on the balance sheet, the reported return on capital employed (ROCE) looks low at 13.4%.[16] The company's own adjusted ROCE, which removes goodwill and intangibles from capital, is 58.9%.[18][19] Neither number is wrong. The first measures what the owner paid, the second measures what the operations need to run.

The other part of the story is operational. Operating margins have stayed in a 22% to 27% band over the last eight quarters.[16] Minimum wages in Karnataka and Telangana rose by law from May and June 2026, and management said the full impact would land in Q2.[11][10] Voluntary attrition, which is staff leaving by choice, was 25.7% annualised in Q2, down from 28.6% in Q1.[3] Wage inflation and high attrition are the two risks ICRA names for the industry.[13] A final point of strength is the balance sheet: borrowings fell from ₹2,532 crore in March 2024 to ₹902 crore in September 2026, and net debt (debt minus cash) was only 0.06 times adjusted EBITDA after Q1.[16][10] So the business is strong in cash and relationships, and weaker in client spread and in the rate of growth it can keep once the rupee stops helping.

Adjusted EBITDA margin is the company's own measure and is higher than the reported operating margin used in our valuation model, so the two must not be mixed.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means comparing what management said it would do with what it delivered. Sagility's record is good on the numbers it controls. For FY25 it guided to mid-teens growth and a broker note records that it delivered 15% in US dollars.[23] For FY26 it raised its growth guidance in January 2026 after Q3 constant-currency growth of 29.1%, and the annual report shows FY26 revenue of ₹71,929 million, up 23.6% in constant currency.[23][15] The January guidance figure itself is not in the pages we could read, so we cannot score the size of the beat.

The margin record is also good this year. In July management held a 24% to 25% adjusted margin guide and said the full wage impact would land in Q2.[10][10] Q2 then printed 26.3% and the guide moved up to 25% to 26%.[1][11] Management also cited a favourable currency move and a better mix of work by country as part of the gain, so some of the lift will not repeat if the rupee turns.[11] For context, the adjusted margin for the whole of FY26 was 25.3%, against 26.4% a year earlier, which the annual report ties to a higher onshore mix.[19][18] We found no FY26 margin guidance, so we cannot score that year as met or missed.

The weak spot is the provider business. Management has said for several quarters that it wants provider growth to pick up. In Q2 provider revenue grew 8.2% against 24.4% for payers, and management said some project revenue from one provider client had tapered off.[3][11] The Chirok Health purchase in October 2026 is the answer on offer. Chirok has about $13 million of trailing revenue and the purchase adds medical coding and documentation skills.[11][1] It is too early to judge.

On capital use, the company repaid borrowings to its parent of ₹2,937 million in FY26, paid lease costs of ₹1,843 million and paid an interim dividend of ₹234 million.[18] It has just raised the interim dividend to ₹0.15 per share from ₹0.05.[6] It also made three acquisitions in under two years: BroadPath for ₹502 crore in January 2025, CareSeed (board approval on 11 June 2026) and Chirok in October 2026.[13][24][1]

3. The numbers, in plain words

The numbers tell a story of fast growth, steady margins and strong cash, hidden under accounting charges. Sales rose from ₹4,218 crore in FY23 to ₹7,193 crore in FY26 and ₹7,991 crore over the twelve months to September 2026, which is a three-year compound growth rate of 19%.[16][16] Operating profit moved from ₹1,039 crore to ₹1,946 crore on the same basis.[16] Net profit rose from ₹144 crore in FY23 to ₹925 crore in FY26, an 86% three-year compound rate, and was ₹1,026 crore over the latest twelve months. The 86% is flattered by a very low starting year when interest and amortisation were far heavier.[16]

Cash is the better news. The annual report puts FY26 operating cash flow at ₹12,030 million, almost level with ₹12,141 million a year earlier.[18] On Screener's consolidated view, operating cash flow was ₹973 crore, ₹1,214 crore and ₹1,203 crore in FY24, FY25 and FY26.[16] Free cash flow (FCF) is operating cash flow minus capital spending. It was ₹791 crore, ₹1,092 crore and ₹1,011 crore in the same years. Over three years the company collected ₹3,390 crore of operating cash against ₹1,692 crore of net profit, a ratio of 2.00.[26] The ratio is high because depreciation and amortisation of ₹508 crore a year, which includes amortisation of intangibles from the carve-out, reduces profit but not cash.[16] One caution: FY26 free cash flow dipped from FY25, and debtor days, the average time clients take to pay, rose from 83 to 93.[16]

Debt has fallen sharply. Borrowings were ₹4,788 crore at March 2022, ₹2,532 crore at March 2024 and ₹902 crore at September 2026, while reserves rose from ₹2,158 crore to ₹5,549 crore.[16] The annual report shows a debt-to-equity ratio (borrowings divided by equity) of 0.11 and interest cover of 13.82 times for FY26.[18] Interest cost fell from ₹215 crore in FY23 to ₹87 crore over the latest twelve months.[16] The low point of the picture is return on equity, which was 10.6% on Screener's measure because the equity figure includes the goodwill.[16]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 sets static floors from the scoring rules. Return on capital employed must be at least 15%, and an ex-goodwill reading is allowed. Debt-to-equity must be under 0.70. Three-year cash conversion (CFO divided by PAT) must be at least 0.70. Promoter pledge must be under 20%. There must be no Stage 4 breakdown. Stage 4 is the falling phase of a four-stage price cycle, and Stage 2 is the rising phase. Technical routing also uses the RSI (relative strength index, a 0 to 100 momentum gauge) and the ATR (average true range, the typical daily price swing). Next we ask which archetype fits and whether the asymmetry, meaning reward divided by risk, clears the floor. The entry zone is the price band where we would place buy orders, and a limit order is an order to buy at a chosen price or lower. A base target is the price we expect in the middle case. A price breakout is a move above a prior high. A pivot is the price level that must break for that to happen. Sagility fails one static level on the reported basis, ROCE at 13.4%. It passes on the company's adjusted basis at 58.9%. So we treat it as a TURNAROUND_VALUE_INFLECTION case with a 2.5 to 1 asymmetry floor.[16][18]
