Before you read

Sagility does back-office and clinical work for American health insurers, mostly from India, and it earned ₹7,193 crore of revenue in the year to March 2026[1][2]. Our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: the business is sound, but at ₹45.30 the reward is 2.98 times the risk against a floor of 3.5, and the floor is met only at ₹44.86 or lower[51]. The appeal is a 24 to 25 percent margin, almost no debt and a price that assumes only 7.5 percent yearly growth for ten years[9][51]. The doubts are that three clients give 60 percent of revenue, the owner holds 50.95 percent under lender security, and a US$115.9 million lawsuit sits in the notes[5][26][4]. The idea is wrong if the September margin prints below 23 percent on 22 October 2026 or the price closes below ₹41.48[30].

1. What the company does

Sagility does the paperwork and the phone work that American health insurers and care providers prefer to hand to someone else. It employs 47,307 people at 29 delivery sites[10] in India, the Philippines, the United States, Jamaica and Colombia,[8] and every rupee of its revenue comes from customers in the United States.[5] The company calls itself a healthcare operations partner. In plain terms it is an outsourcing firm that has chosen one industry and stayed in it: it reports a single business segment, business process management services.[11] The shares have been listed on the National Stock Exchange (NSE) and the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE) since 12 November 2024.[8]

There are two kinds of customer. A payer is the health insurer that pays for care, such as a Medicare Advantage plan. A provider is the hospital, doctor group or laboratory that delivers care and then bills for it. Payers gave 89.6 percent of Q1 FY27 revenue and providers 10.4 percent.[9] For payers, Sagility handles claims, payment integrity (catching wrong payments), clinical review, provider network files and member-facing front offices. For providers, it handles billing and collection, which the industry calls revenue cycle management.[8] It serves 109 client groups and says that 7 of the 10 largest US health plans are among them.[10][49][12]

The company is paid mainly for people and for work done. Management says it still wins most work on an effort basis, meaning a price per full-time employee or per transaction, although it wants to move toward managed services where it takes responsibility for a whole process and a committed result.[9] It does not raise prices: on the Q1 FY27 call the chief executive said no pricing levers were used and that the pressure on prices runs the other way.[9] Revenue per employee is therefore a function of headcount, mix of onshore and offshore work, and the rupee-dollar rate. On TTM revenue of ₹76,174 million and 47,307 staff, that is about ₹16.1 lakh of revenue per head,[10] and employee cost took 62.7 percent of FY26 revenue (₹45,102.76 million of ₹71,928.51 million).[2] That share is the lever behind every margin conversation in this dossier.

Sagility's revenue has a rhythm. US health plans enrol members in the autumn and winter (the open enrolment and annual enrolment periods for Medicare), and the company adds staff to answer calls and process applications. Seasonal revenue was ₹2,260 million in Q4 FY26 and ₹4,527 million over the twelve months to June 2026, about 5.9 percent of revenue.[10] Management says the seasonal share was 3 percent in FY25 and 6 percent in FY26, because BroadPath (bought in January 2025) does more enrolment work, and expects about the same share in FY27.[9] The practical effect is that the June quarter looks weaker than the March quarter by design, and the September-quarter results due on 22 October 2026 are the first read on how big the autumn season will be.[9][30]

The ownership history matters for how you read the balance sheet. The business was carved out of the healthcare unit of Hinduja Global Solutions on 6 January 2022 and bought by a holding company, Sagility B.V., whose ultimate owner is the private-equity group EQT AB.[25][15][5] That purchase left goodwill of ₹6,405.15 crore and other intangibles of ₹2,037.02 crore on the March 2026 balance sheet, together 67 percent of total assets of ₹12,601.08 crore,[2] which is why the reported return on capital looks low while the operating business earns well. Chapter 3 shows both views. The promoter sold 16.427 percent of the company on 17 November 2025 and now holds 50.95 percent.[26]

How a contract turns into profit

A typical contract is a statement of work for a defined process, for example processing claims for a health plan's Medicare Advantage members. The client pays per employee assigned or per claim handled. The cost to Sagility is the wage of the people doing the work, plus supervision, technology and the lease on the delivery site. Offshore staff cost far less than onshore staff, so work that moves from a US site to India or the Philippines lowers the client's bill and raises Sagility's margin. Management confirmed this on the Q1 FY27 call: one client within the fourth- and fifth-largest group is moving a large volume offshore, which lowers that client's revenue but, in the chief executive's words, should in the general sense add to margin.[9] The margin that results is an adjusted EBITDA margin of 24.0 percent in Q1 FY27 and 25.3 percent in FY26. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, which is profit before financing costs, tax and accounting write-downs.[10][13] That margin is stated before certain items the company excludes (acquisition earn-out payments, stock appreciation rights and other income), so Chapter 3 also shows the audited figures.

The tax rate sits near 25 percent (24.6 percent in Q1 FY27: ₹706.67 million on ₹2,874.78 million of profit before tax).[11] Interest costs are small and falling. The company owes ₹5,670.42 million of non-convertible bonds to its parent, Sagility B.V., and plans to repay all of it in FY27.[5][10] After that, the only debt-like item is the lease liability for its offices, ₹5,114 million at June 2026.[10]

Acquisitions, and what they bought

Sagility has made small deals to widen what it can sell and who it can sell to. BroadPath, bought in January 2025 for US$55.69 million (₹4,825.36 million), brought mid-sized health plans and enrolment work; its revenue was about US$13.5 to 13.6 million in the quarter to June 2026.[6][9] CareSeed, bought on 11 June 2026 for up to US$30.0 million, of which up to US$12.5 million depends on performance over three years, adds quality-reporting software for Medicare Advantage plans (US$5.1 million of revenue in calendar 2025, with a 31.4 percent EBITDA margin) and 26 new small client groups.[11][9] The prices are modest. BroadPath cost about 0.87 times the ₹5,527.77 million of annual revenue it would have added in FY25 on a full-year basis (our arithmetic from the filed figures).[6] CareSeed's total price of US$30.0 million is about 5.9 times its calendar-2025 revenue, and about 18.7 times its EBITDA at the full price (our arithmetic), though the fair value booked for the deal is lower because US$12.5 million of the price depends on results.[11][9] Management says it looks for capabilities rather than size, so deals should stay small.[9]

  • Adjusted EBITDA: the company's own operating profit measure, which leaves out earn-out costs, stock appreciation rights and other income including currency gains and losses.

  • Open enrolment: the autumn and winter weeks when US consumers choose health plans, which load the company's call centres and processing teams.
  • DSO (days sales outstanding): how many days of revenue are waiting to be collected; 80 days at June 2026.
  • Managed service: a contract in which Sagility runs a whole process and is paid for results rather than hours.
  • HEDIS: a set of quality measures that US health plans must report; CareSeed makes software for it.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Our reading is that Sagility has a narrow moat: real, but narrow. The moat comes from domain depth and from being wired into the client's daily process. It is narrow because the company has almost no pricing power and a few customers carry most of the revenue. The evidence for the moat is tenure. The company says its average client has stayed 18 years and that 95 percent of clients are retained,[48] and the chief executive said on the Q3 FY26 call that the top five clients have been with the company for about 18 years.[15] The prospectus adds that none of the ten largest clients had ended a relationship in the periods it covers, although contracts are normally not exclusive,[25] so a client can move work elsewhere at any time. Tenure is a company claim and cannot be tested from the filings, so we treat it as supporting evidence and not as proof.

The market is large and still lightly outsourced. An industry study commissioned by the company for its listing, by Everest Group, valued US healthcare operations spending at about US$201.1 billion in 2023 and put the outsourced part at about US$45.0 billion, a penetration of 21.5 to 23.5 percent.[24] It expected outsourced spending to grow about 8.7 percent a year from 2023 to 2028, with payer outsourcing growing 7.0 percent and provider outsourcing 12.5 percent.[24] Sagility's share of the outsourced pool was about 1.23 percent in 2023,[24] so the company is small in a big pool. The catch, as management itself says, is that the largest insurers already outsource 50 to 60 percent of their operations, so growth with them comes from adding service lines such as clinical review and payment integrity, not from a first outsourcing wave.[15] The top five clients still grew 11.7 percent in FY26,[13] which fits that account.

Against listed peers the company is mid-sized and earns a good operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of revenue. Sagility's trailing figure is 24 percent on Screener's definition, against 16 percent for Firstsource, 25 percent for eClerx and 34 percent for Inventurus Knowledge Solutions, a healthcare technology and services company that sells mainly to US physician groups.[1][35][37][36] The prospectus names the specialist competitors as CorroHealth, Shearwater Health and Omega Healthcare, and the broad firms as Accenture, Cognizant and EXL; it also lists product companies such as Optum and Cotiviti.[24] Most of these are private or foreign, so the listed comparison in Chapter 3 is imperfect, and the prospectus itself says no listed company is an immediate comparable in size and model.[24]

Financial strength is a real part of the case. Crisil rates the bank facilities A/Stable, citing an established market position, strong revenue growth and a healthy financial profile, and names concentration and industry threats as the weak points.[8] At 30 June 2026 the company had ₹9,674 million of cash and investments against ₹5,777 million of borrowings, and net debt (borrowings and leases less cash) of ₹1,110 million, which is 0.06 times adjusted EBITDA.[10][50] That balance sheet means the business does not need a good share price to fund itself, which matters when a promoter is still selling.

Customer concentration, and how fast it is changing

Three customers each gave more than 10 percent of FY26 revenue, together ₹43,076.76 million, or 59.9 percent of ₹71,928.51 million, up from ₹35,983.30 million in FY25.[5][2] The FY25 figure is 64.6 percent of that year's revenue of ₹55,699.18 million on this customer-level basis. The 66.2 percent quoted below for FY25 is the company's top-three client-group share, a different basis because a client group adds up a client's affiliates. The trend line and the Layer 2 threshold use the client-group series, because the company publishes it for five years; on either basis FY26 is 59.9 percent and the direction is down.[5][2][10] Crisil, working from FY25 data, put the top three at 66 percent and called concentration a weakness.[8] The promoter's owner, EQT, said that the top three made about 80 percent of revenue when it invested and that the figure was in the early 60s by 2025.[47] The trend is therefore down: 72.4 percent in FY23, 68.3 percent in FY24, 66.2 percent in FY25 and 59.9 percent in FY26.[10] The latest twelve months show 60.1 percent, a small step up because the top clients still grow, and the company says that is one reason concentration does not fall faster.

Why this matters for a buyer: concentration is a risk you can see in the cash conversion cycle, in the pricing the clients can demand and in the damage if one of them insources or switches vendor. It is also the main reason we rate the Layer 2 concentration measure WATCH in Chapter 2B and not PASS. The company's Q1 FY27 answer on the subject was that insourcing offshore work to a client's own US sites would raise the client's cost, so it is unlikely while the clients are under cost pressure.[9] That is a reasonable economic argument, but it is not a contract.

Pricing power, artificial intelligence and the risk to billing by the hour

Sagility has no pricing power today. Management said so directly on the Q1 FY27 call: no revenue growth came from price rises, and the movement in prices is in the opposite direction.[9] It also expects some of its revenue to be given back as clients ask for the savings that automation brings. On the Q4 FY25 call it put that annual give-back at 1.5 to 2 percent of revenue and said it could reach 4 to 5 percent in two to three years;[21] on the Q4 FY26 call it said it expected about 2 percent in FY27, up from a historic 1 to 1.5 percent.[13] On the Q1 FY27 call it said that the same range could climb to 200 basis points soon and perhaps higher.[9] A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point.

The exposure is clearest in the example the chief executive gave: if a client is doing a process with 100 people and agrees to artificial intelligence, the company may need 80.[9] Under a price-per-head contract, that is a 20 percent revenue loss on that process unless Sagility wins the freed work or switches to outcome-based pricing. The company's answer is its managed-service model and its orchestration platform (Synchrony), plus its domain depth: an Everest survey of 50 payer leaders, which it commissioned, found that 70 percent had adopted AI but only 10 percent saw a measurable gain, and that 70 percent want domain-led partners.[9][10] The survey is sponsored by the company, so read it as a sales argument. Even so, it fits what the company reports: organic growth of 14.9 percent in constant currency in Q1 FY27 means no visible damage so far.[9]

The insurer cycle and the volume risk

Sagility earns more when insurers have more members and more work, and its pitch works best when insurers are under cost pressure. Those two forces can pull in different directions. Insurers' costs eased in 2026 (a trade-press survey of second-quarter results reached the same conclusion):[45] UnitedHealth's medical cost ratio (the share of premiums spent on care) was 86.7 percent in the second quarter against 89.4 percent a year earlier, and Centene's health benefits ratio was 89.6 percent against 93.0 percent.[44][43] At the same time membership shrank. UnitedHealth's Medicare Advantage members fell from 8.350 million to 7.565 million, and Centene's marketplace members fell from 5,862,800 to 3,494,700.[44][43] Federal payment policy turned friendlier for Medicare Advantage in April 2026, when the final payment rate for 2027 rose 2.48 percent against 0.09 percent in the earlier proposal.[41][42] The company says it sees no general slowdown in spending and that cost pressure keeps rising,[9] which is consistent with its 14.9 percent organic growth, but easing insurer margins could lower the urgency of outsourcing deals later. Management itself said that some managed-service talks have run three to six months.[9]

Rules, politics and wages

Two outside forces can change the economics. The first is US policy on outsourcing. The Halting International Relocation of Employment Act, introduced in the Senate in September 2025, would put a 25 percent excise tax on payments to foreign persons for services that benefit US consumers; a law-firm summary says its prospects are unclear and that an attempt to pass it by unanimous consent was blocked.[46] The prospectus also lists anti-outsourcing legislation as a risk.[25] All of Sagility's revenue is billed to US customers,[5] so an enacted tax on foreign service payments would hit it directly. We assign it a low but real probability and no value in our central case.

The second is wages in India. Karnataka raised its statutory minimum wage from 22 May 2026 and Telangana from 1 June 2026. Management puts the full-year effect at about 120 basis points of margin, with ₹70 million falling in Q1 and a further ₹151 million one-time charge for gratuity and leave pay booked below the operating line;[9][11] 65 to 70 percent of its headcount works in Bengaluru and Hyderabad.[9] It expects to absorb the cost within the 24 to 25 percent margin guide through efficiency gains and a weaker rupee, with the full mitigation taking 12 to 18 months.[9]

Layer 2 asks which operating numbers decide whether this particular business is healthy. For an outsourcing company that bills for people, four readings do that job: how fast it grows without acquisitions and currency help, how much new work it signs, whether it keeps its margin while wages rise, and how dependent it is on a few buyers. We scored each as PASS, WATCH or FAIL against a threshold stated beside it. Headcount attrition and working capital days (working capital is the cash tied up in unpaid customer bills and day-to-day costs) are tracked in the text because they move through the margin and through Layer 1 cash conversion, so counting them twice would flatter or punish the same facts.

The result is three PASS and one WATCH, a score of 3 of 4. That clears the framework's minimum of 3 of 4 for a name to qualify, but only just: one slip, for example a margin below 24 percent, would drop it to 2 of 4. Attrition is the main thing we left out of the score. Voluntary attrition was 28.6 percent in Q1 FY27 against 27.6 percent a year earlier and 38.1 percent in Q4 FY26, and the trailing twelve-month figure is 30.5 percent.[10] Management attributes the Q4 spike to the larger seasonal workforce; the Q1 figure fell back.[13] At 28.6 percent a year, the company must replace roughly one in every three or four employees each year, which is the cost of doing business in this industry and also a drag on quality and margin.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record on promises is good where it can be checked. Guidance is a company's own published forecast, and the test is whether it was met, beaten or quietly dropped. For FY26 the company guided in May 2025 to organic growth of low to mid teens in constant currency,[21] raised its total growth guide to 20 percent plus in July 2025,[19] to 21 percent plus in October 2025,[17] and to 22.5 percent in January 2026,[15] and then delivered 23.6 percent in constant currency, with organic growth of 15 percent.[13] On margin it guided to 24 to 25 percent and delivered 25.3 percent.[13] That is a company that set the bar low and cleared it three times.

The debt promise was also kept to the rupee. In November 2024 the finance team said the promoter bonds would be repaid by ₹2,490 million in FY25 and ₹2,350 million in FY26;[22] the accounts show repayments of ₹2,489.58 million and ₹2,350.00 million.[5] The remaining ₹5,670.42 million is due to be repaid in FY27, and a question at the March 2026 investor day referred to a deleveraging event in January 2027,[5][23] so FY27 is the test of the last instalment. Two other promises are unproven. The dividend policy the CFO said in May 2025 might come within a couple of quarters is still not in the filings we found, although a first interim dividend of ₹0.05 and a final of ₹0.10 per share were declared for FY26.[21][6] And the 3 percent employee share scheme is approved but not yet granted, so the cost of those shares is not yet in the numbers.[29][9]

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter lays out five years of revenue, profit, cash and debt, then runs the Layer 1 tests. Two warnings help the reader. First, the company was assembled in stages: the healthcare business was acquired from its predecessor on 6 January 2022, so the first column, FY22, is a stub covering only the months from that date to 31 March 2022, and the next full year is FY23.[25] Screener labels the stub as eight months; the prospectus says the period starts on 6 January 2022, so we call it a stub and do not read anything into it. Second, the figures are in rupee crore (one crore is ten million), and the dollar is the currency that drives the business, so rupee growth is flattered when the rupee weakens.

Revenue rose from ₹4,218 crore in FY23 to ₹7,193 crore in FY26, a compound growth rate of 19.5 percent a year over three years, and the trailing twelve months to June 2026 reached ₹7,617 crore.[1] That headline is larger than the underlying business growth. In FY26 revenue rose 29.1 percent in rupees, 23.6 percent in constant currency, and 15 percent organically, so about half of the headline came from the weaker rupee and from the BroadPath acquisition.[13] The operating margin has been steady at 23 to 25 percent on Screener's measure.[1] Net profit grew far faster than revenue, from ₹144 crore in FY23 to ₹925 crore in FY26 (about 86 percent a year over three years, from a low starting point), because three costs fell as a share of revenue: depreciation and amortisation fell about a third between FY24 and FY25 (₹689.21 crore to ₹466.86 crore),[7] finance costs fell 46 percent between FY24 and FY26 (₹185.15 crore to ₹99.16 crore),[7][2] and the tax charge was only ₹13.42 crore in FY24 because of deferred tax credits.[7] We did not find a filed explanation for the FY25 drop in amortisation, so treat the FY25 step-up in profit as partly a change in accounting charges and not all operating improvement.

Earnings per share (EPS), the profit divided across the shares, grew slower than profit in FY24 and FY25 because the share count rose. The promoter converted loans into equity: equity share capital rose from ₹1,919 crore to ₹4,679 crore between FY23 and FY25, and 39.4 crore new shares were issued in FY25 alone for non-cash consideration.[1][3] The share count has been flat at 468.13 crore since then.[3] That conversion is the cost of the debt reduction, so you should compare profit growth per share, not total profit, when you judge the trend.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 is the framework's test of financial safety and quality. It has two parts. The first part, the hard red lines, is a set of yes-or-no tests where a single failure rejects the name regardless of how good the rest looks: debt-to-equity (borrowings divided by shareholders' money, a measure of debt load) at 1.20 or above or interest cover below 2.0, a promoter pledge of 20 percent or more of the promoter holding, three-year cash from operations below 0.40 of profit, or a Stage 4 breakdown. The second part is the static floors, which a compounder must clear, and the trajectory, which is the direction of travel. A company can miss a static floor and still qualify as a turnaround if the direction is clearly up and the price is low enough. Four terms recur. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses. A Stage 2 stock is in a confirmed uptrend, and a Stage 4 stock is in a confirmed downtrend. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain from the price to the target divided by the loss from the price to the stop. A measured move is the height of a sideways pattern projected above its top, and we use earnings times a multiple instead.

Our variant perception

Variant perception means saying where our view differs from what the price and the consensus assume, and why. There are two differences, and they point in opposite directions. Against the sceptics, we think the price is too low for the business: a company growing 14.9 percent organically in constant currency, signing US$30 million or more of new annual work each quarter and carrying almost no debt is valued by the market at a price implying 7.5 percent growth for a decade, and at 15 times the ledger's FY2 earnings of ₹3.00.[9][14][10][51] The pessimistic case (that artificial intelligence erodes per-head billing) is real, but management's guide already nets out 1.5 to 2 percent of annual give-back, and the Q1 numbers show no damage.[21][9] So on fundamentals we lean to the cheap side.

Against the bulls, we think the Street target of ₹56.42 is a destination and the price has reasons to stay below it for now. First, supply: the promoter sold 16.427 percent in one bulk deal on 17 November 2025 and still holds 50.95 percent, all of it under a non-disposal undertaking and with the holding companies' lenders holding security over the chain above it.[26][27] Nothing we read says another sale is planned. Our inference is that a private-equity owner with lender-secured holding companies will sell down over time, and the price has to absorb that supply when it comes. Second, margin: the 120 basis points of wage cost lands in full from Q2, and the quarter just reported was helped by more than 100 basis points of currency gain, so a rupee recovery would show up directly in the margin.[9] Third, the stock has bounced 4.93 percent in two weeks and sits 2.2 percent above its 50-day average while the market regime is Downtrend.[51] These are reasons to buy at a lower price, not reasons to avoid the stock. The gap between value and price is why the verdict is a limit order near the 50-day line.

One more difference concerns the quality of the profit. Statutory profit is about 17 percent lower than adjusted profit (₹993 crore against ₹1,200.6 crore), and most of the gap is non-cash charges, chiefly amortisation, which the company's schedule runs to FY32 for the carve-out intangibles.[1][10] Many Indian brokers value the company on adjusted earnings. We value it on statutory earnings in the base case, because the amortisation reflects a price the owner really paid. That makes our value lower than one built on adjusted earnings would be.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on a choice that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest choice behind each estimate, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when that evidence arrives. It then sets out the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, the red-team bear case, and names the one claim on which the whole view hangs. The date that matters most is 22 October 2026, when the board meets to approve the September-quarter results and to consider an interim dividend, and the company holds its earnings webinar at 7:30 PM India time.[30][31]

Weakest assumption behind the earnings estimate: a 24.5 percent margin through FY28

The earnings per share of ₹2.45 for FY27 and ₹2.97 for FY28 assume the margin stays at the middle of the 24 to 25 percent guide. Three forces push against that. Wages: the 120 basis point cost of the Karnataka and Telangana minimum wage rises lands in full from Q2, because Q1 carried only about 40 days of it (₹70 million in the quarter).[9] Artificial intelligence: management expects about 2 percent of revenue to be given back in FY27, more than in earlier years.[13] The rupee: management said that favourable currency movement contributed more than 100 basis points to the Q1 margin and that the guide holds partly because of the currency.[9] The company said it hopes to reach the upper end of the range if the wage outcome is softer, so there is some upside if the rules change.[9]

What would show us wrong: an adjusted EBITDA margin below 23 percent in the September quarter, a cut in the 24 to 25 percent guide, or a stronger rupee without a matching cost saving. When we will know: the Q2 FY27 result on 22 October 2026 and the Q3 FY27 result in about January 2027.[30] Management said it would give a better full-year view by the end of Q2, once the autumn season is visible.[9]

Weakest assumption behind the base value: the multiple holds at 21.3 times

The ₹56.70 base value needs the market to keep paying 21.3 times trailing earnings. That figure is the median of the last year, and the multiple has drifted down from 31.8 in October 2025 to 20.6 now as earnings rose and the price went sideways.[40] A drifting multiple is the market's way of saying it does not trust the earnings to last, or that supply is heavy. If it settles at the July 2026 low of 18.4 times, the same earnings give ₹48.90, only 8 percent above the close.[40] Evidence that would show us wrong: a close below 19 times trailing earnings (about ₹40 on today's trailing profit) after a good result, or a bulk deal by the promoter at a discount. Bulk deals are disclosed by the exchanges the same day, so this evidence can arrive at any time.

Weakest assumption behind the cash and return estimates

The three-year cash conversion of 2.00 times is flattered by the years of low tax and by the FY25 India tax refunds that the CFO described; FY26, the first year of full tax and of heavy receivables, is 1.30.[13][3] The test is whether FY27 stays above 1.0. The half-year cash flow in the 22 October filing gives the first read, and the audited FY27 accounts, due about May 2027, give the full year. For the return on capital, the weak assumption is that goodwill can be ignored. The goodwill is tested for impairment, the carve-out was a 2022 purchase, and a write-down would hit equity and profit even though it would not change cash.[2] Evidence of trouble would be an impairment charge in the half-year results or the FY27 annual report.

Weakest assumption behind the probabilities

We put 30 percent on the bear case. That number is judgement, not measurement. It would be too high if the autumn season is bigger than last year's and the owners are quiet, and too low if the market regime stays Downtrend and a block sale arrives with a margin miss. The September-quarter result narrows it: a clean result moves it toward 20 percent, and a margin miss moves it toward 40 percent.

The red-team bear case, stated as strongly as the facts allow

The red-team view is that Sagility is a price-taker in an industry where the customer's easiest cost cut is the supplier. Three clients give 60 percent of revenue, and in this industry a single client's decision to shift work offshore already cut the revenue of the fourth and fifth largest clients' cohort.[5][9] Management concedes it has no pricing power and expects the AI give-back to double from the historic 1 to 1.5 percent, and that it may reach 4 to 5 percent in two to three years.[9][13][21] Insurers' own margins are recovering (UnitedHealth's medical cost ratio fell 2.7 points; Centene's by 3.4 points), which weakens the urgency that drove outsourcing, while their memberships are shrinking, which cuts the volume Sagility is paid on.[44][43] Part of the headline margin came from the rupee, and the company excludes real costs from its adjusted numbers: statutory profit for the last twelve months is ₹993 crore against adjusted ₹1,200.6 crore.[1][10] The reported return on capital is 13 percent because two-thirds of assets are acquisition goodwill and intangibles.[2] There is also a US$115.9 million lawsuit (₹1,093.92 crore, 5.2 percent of the market capitalisation) in the contingent liabilities; the former owners have agreed to indemnify the group and US$45 million of bank guarantee from Barclays on their behalf expires on 31 January 2027.[4][51] Two senior executives left within eleven months, one independent director has a regulator's summons from another company, and the promoter's shares are locked under lender security with an eventual sale ahead of them.[5][32][25][26] On this view 21 times trailing statutory earnings is a fair price for a business whose revenue per head is capped, and the stock should sit in a range until the supply is gone.

Our answer to the red team: the points on concentration, pricing, the rupee and the owners are right and are why the verdict is a limit order at a better price and not a buy now. We do not accept the view that the revenue is capped. Q1 FY27 organic growth was 14.9 percent in constant currency, steady-state revenue rose 5.1 percent over the previous quarter after stripping out the autumn season, and the company signed US$35.3 million of new annual work in the quarter.[9] The lawsuit is real but ring-fenced by contract, and the filing records that the Northern District of Illinois granted summary judgment to the group on the claim for declaratory relief, dismissed the fraudulent-transfer claim without prejudice, awarded the group its litigation costs and treats the Illinois proceedings as complete. The Texas proceedings, where the vendor's damages claim remains, are still open, and management believes the Illinois ruling that the liability stayed with the former owners should carry weight there. That belief is management's, not a court's.[4] The red team's best point is the one on the owners: it cannot be dated, and it cannot be hedged except by price.
