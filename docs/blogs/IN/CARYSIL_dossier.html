Carysil makes quartz kitchen sinks in Gujarat for global brands such as IKEA and Grohe, and it also sells stainless steel sinks, faucets and built-in appliances. 1. What the company does

Carysil Limited makes kitchen sinks. Its main product is the composite quartz sink: crushed quartz stone bound with acrylic resin and moulded under heat, which gives a hard bowl that resists stains and scratches.[1][2] The company was set up in 1987 in collaboration with Schock, a German sink maker, and it still runs on that German process.[2][1] It also makes stainless steel sinks, faucets and built-in kitchen appliances such as chimneys and hobs, and it sells bath products under the Sternhagen brand.[1] Its United States and United Kingdom subsidiaries sell stone worktops, which the company calls surfaces.[2] In the quarter ended June 2026, quartz sinks gave 51 percent of revenue, stainless steel sinks 12 percent, kitchen appliances and others 11.8 percent, and surfaces 25 percent.[3]

Most of the money comes from outside India. Exports were 81 percent of revenue in the year to March 2026.[4] In the June 2026 quarter the United Kingdom gave 33.5 percent of revenue, the United States 26.7 percent, India 21.5 percent, Europe 12.4 percent and the rest of the world 6.0 percent.[2] The buyers are large brands that put their own name on the product, a trade known as original equipment manufacturing, or OEM. Carysil says it is the sole quartz sink supplier to Grohe, that it has a long-term agreement with Karran USA for 150,000 quartz sinks a year, and that it has supplied IKEA since August 2020.[2] In India it sells under its own Carysil brand through more than 4,500 dealers and 180 galleries.[4]

A quartz sink plant has high fixed costs. The moulds, ovens and buildings cost the same whether the plant is half full or nearly full, so profit depends on how full the plant is. The company says the plant ran at about 78 percent of capacity in the year to March 2026.[6] For the June 2026 quarter the investor slides say 80 percent, while the managing director said 88 percent on the call.[2][3] Other swing factors are the price of methyl methacrylate (MMA), the acrylic raw material, which rose 30 to 35 percent in early 2026 and was mostly passed on to customers, plus ocean freight and the United States tariff.[6] The gross margin is revenue minus raw material cost, shown as a share of revenue. It was 54.8 percent in the June 2026 quarter, down from 55.9 percent in the March quarter.[2] The operating margin is revenue minus all running costs, shown as a share of revenue. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before those four items, and its margin was 21.2 percent in the June quarter.[2]

The German process is the claim the idea note leans on. Carysil's annual report calls the company Asia's only quartz sink maker with proprietary German-engineered technology and says it is one of only four companies worldwide with that capability.[4] It also ranks Carysil as the fourth-largest quartz sink maker in the world.[4] That is not a duopoly. A duopoly has two firms, and this is a club of four in which Carysil is fourth by size. The company does not name the other three, so we cannot test the claim firm by firm.[4] One secondary source says the technology was originally sourced from Schock, which fits the company's own history.[21]

Management now wants to become a one-stop supplier of kitchen and bath products, a plan it calls Carysil 2.0.[4] It expects to spend ₹80 to 90 crore (a crore is ten million rupees) on new plants this year: ₹40 to 50 crore on quartz sinks, ₹20 crore on stainless steel sinks and ₹20 crore on faucets and appliances.[3] Quartz sink capacity should rise from 1.0 million to 1.25 million sinks a year by the end of the 2027 financial year, and faucet and appliance capacity should double to 100,000 units each.[2] The annual report adds a wider investment plan of ₹300 crore through 2030-31.[4] Section 2 asks whether the business behind these plans is as strong as the idea note says.

How the company says it will grow

Management has repeated the same targets for several quarters. It wants revenue to grow 15 to 20 percent a year and EBITDA margins to stay between 18 and 20 percent.[4] On the August 2026 call it kept its 15 percent revenue target for the 2027 financial year and said margins were tracking toward the upper end of the band.[3][18] It also set a goal of adding another ₹1,000 crore of sales over five years, which needs about 15 percent growth a year.[3] In the annual report it commits to bring borrowings down to the level of its cash by 2030.[4] These are the promises we test in section 2B.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • CMP means current market price, the latest traded price of the share.
  • reverse DCF (reverse discounted cash flow) works backward from today's price to the yearly growth the price assumes; a basis point (bps) is one-hundredth of a percentage point.
  • SMA is a simple moving average, and DMA is the same idea named by days; both are plain averages of recent closing prices.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit a business earns on the money tied up in it, shown as a percent.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided across all of its shares.
  • free cash flow is cash left after running the business and paying for plants and machines. FCF (free cash flow) is the short form used in some tables.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself collects before any borrowing or investing.
  • capital expenditure is money spent on plants, machines and buildings. It is often called capex.
  • working capital is the cash tied up in stock, unpaid customer bills and supplier credit during day-to-day operations.
  • net debt is borrowings minus cash and bank balances. debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money.
  • price-to-earnings is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit; the short form is P/E.
  • guidance is the company's own published forecast for the coming period.
  • consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for a number.
  • dilution is the fall in each owner's share of the company when new shares are issued.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has moved; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in rupees; we use it to measure distances on the chart.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 trading days; the 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the same idea over 50 and 200 days.
  • Stage 2 is the rising uptrend phase in the Weinstein scheme, and Stage 4 is the falling downtrend phase. Stage 1 is the flat floor before an uptrend and Stage 3 is the top.
  • base is a sideways stretch where the price builds a floor, and pivot is the top of that floor, the level a breakout must clear. A retest is a pullback that tests the level from above.
  • stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry is the ratio of possible gain to possible loss from the buy price, also called reward-to-risk: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of this business is a mix of technology, cost and customer trust. Few firms can make composite quartz sinks to Western quality, and Carysil makes them at an Indian cost base in one campus at Bhavnagar.[2][4] The annual report calls it the lowest-cost producer in the premium quartz and steel sink category.[4] That is a claim by the company, not an independent ranking, so we treat it as a lead and look for outside evidence in the customer wins.

The customer wins are real and recent. The annual report says Carysil's share of IKEA's non-United States quartz sink buying rose from about 25 percent to 75 percent.[4] The company is the sole quartz sink supplier to Grohe, and Kohler has almost doubled its steel sink volumes, according to the managing director.[2][3] On the August 2026 call the managing director added an extended agreement with Home Depot in the United States and Canada, a tie-up with Häfele in Australia and New Zealand, and first orders from Amazon in the United States.[3][17] Each win adds volume to a plant whose fixed costs are already paid, which is why the operating margin has moved up as the plant filled.

The weakness is that the same customers create concentration. The annual report lists dependence on the United States market, IKEA and a few large customers as a risk.[4] Karran and Lowe's alone matter enough that one secondary source calls Karran a single point of concentration, and it puts United States exposure near 21 percent of revenue.[21] The company does not publish the share of sales taken by its top five customers, so we cannot size that risk from filings. We note it as unpublished and treat it as a reason to start with a small position.

Where the business is weak

The first weak spot is how much cash the business ties up. Screener shows inventory at 319 days in the year to March 2026, up from 212 days in FY22, and a cash conversion cycle of 257 days against 126 days in FY22.[1] The company's own standalone ratio shows inventory turnover of 82 days on a different basis, so the two numbers are not the same measure.[4] Our own calculation on the consolidated balance sheet is inventory of ₹259.1 crore divided by FY26 sales of ₹924 crore, times 365, which is about 102 days of sales.[2][1] Screener's 319 days is a larger number because it measures stock against a smaller base than sales, probably cost, although the page does not say.[1] Either way, stock is growing faster than we would like, and ICRA, the rating agency, lists working capital intensity and net working capital of 36 percent of operating income as a constraint on the rating.[7]

The second weak spot is that free cash flow is thin. Screener shows free cash flow of minus ₹2 crore in FY26 and minus ₹38 crore in FY24, because the company spends heavily on plants.[1] Cash from operations of ₹109 crore in FY26 was almost fully spent: investing outflows were ₹89 crore.[1][2] The plan to spend ₹80 to 90 crore this year, and at least ₹50 to 60 crore every year after, keeps free cash flow near zero until the new capacity fills.[3]

The third weak spot is the United Kingdom, which was 33.5 percent of June-quarter revenue. Management said on the August call that the United Kingdom is going through a tight phase, and the Q4 FY26 call summary says about 15 new customers were added over three quarters to offset it.[2][3][6] The fourth is the domestic branded business, which was only ₹173 crore in FY26 against ₹170 crore the year before. We return to that number in section 2B.[4][5]

Ownership and credit quality

Promoters hold 41.34 percent and have pledged none of their shares.[8][1] Chirag Parekh, the chairman and managing director, holds 30.35 percent himself.[8] The promoter stake fell from 43.84 percent in March 2024 to 41.37 percent by September 2024 while the holding of domestic institutions rose from 7.20 percent to 11.09 percent. That fits the qualified institutional placement (QIP) of new shares that ICRA says was raised in FY2025, a form of dilution, although we did not find the share-sale filing itself.[1][7] Institutions have since increased their share: foreign investors rose to 2.11 percent and domestic institutions to 12.07 percent by September 2026.[1][8] ICRA reaffirmed its [ICRA]A rating with a stable outlook on 26 March 2026 for ₹193.40 crore of bank lines. It says a downgrade could follow if total debt to operating profit stays above 2.0 times.[7] One data provider puts total debt at 1.62 times EBITDA at March 2026, which leaves a cushion of about 0.4 times.[13] Section 2B moves from the broad picture to the five numbers that run this business.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 of our test picks three to five numbers that run this particular business and scores each one: PASS is worth 1.0 point, WATCH 0.5 and FAIL zero. The company qualifies if it scores at least 3.0 out of 5.0 and has at most one FAIL. We chose five numbers for Carysil. Each one answers a different question about whether the plants, the customers, the brand and the cash are all working.

The third is growth of the Indian branded business, which is the part management says will lift margins and reduce reliance on exports. The fourth is cash conversion, meaning how much of EBITDA ends up as cash after tax, because inventory has been growing faster than sales. If only the multiple falls to 22 times, base EPS of ₹46.8 gives ₹1,030, 4 percent above today. If both happen, 40.4 times 22 is ₹889, which is 10 percent below today and below our ₹911.90 stop, even with revenue exactly on plan. We treat that joint outcome as a tail inside the bear case, not as the base. It is the reason we scale the position and make every order depend on the result review.

Weakest assumption behind the reverse DCF: profit is a fair stand-in for cash

Profit is a poor stand-in for cash when the company spends more than half of it on plants. Free cash flow was minus ₹2 crore in FY26 and minus ₹35 crore over five years.[1] The managing director says a minimum ₹50 to 60 crore of capex a year is needed to grow 15 percent.[3] Evidence that we are wrong: free cash flow staying negative once the 2027 plants start. We will know when the FY27 cash flow statement is published with the annual results in May 2027.

The red-team bear case

The strongest honest case against the stock has five parts. First, the stock is in a falling trend in a falling market, and our own gate says no momentum.[28] Second, the company is spending ₹80 to 90 crore on plants while quartz volume grew only 6 percent in the June quarter, which management blames on shipping delays; if the delays were demand, the new capacity arrives into a slower market.[3] Third, cash is locked in stock: inventory days of 319, a cash conversion cycle of 257 days and negative free cash flow.[1] Fourth, the United Kingdom, a third of revenue, is in what management calls a tight phase, and the tariff cut from 50 percent to 18 percent could reverse.[3][16][22] Fifth, the Indian branded business, the main margin story, grew 1.8 percent in FY26 on annual-report figures.[4][5] In that bear case, EPS stays near ₹33 and the multiple falls to 22 times, which gives about ₹720.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT FOR SUPPORT. The rule behind it is our technical routing rule: a company that passes the red lines and a business test, but sits in a Stage 1 base with no momentum, stays on the shortlist and waits for a limit order to fill near support. Carysil passes all five red lines, qualifies as a compounder, scores 4.0 out of 5.0 on its operating numbers and clears our reward-to-risk floor at both the current price and the top of the entry zone. It fails on timing, and on two questions the 4 November 2026 result will answer: whether quartz volume holds up and whether prices hold. No order is placed today at ₹988.90, or at any time before that result is read and tested.

After the result we place orders only if all three conditions hold. First, the headline numbers meet our bar: revenue of at least ₹277 crore, EBITDA margin of at least 19 percent and EPS of at least ₹10. Second, the quartz test passes: quartz sink volume of at least 217,000 units, which is 10 percent above the 197,600 of a year earlier, and no sign of price cuts, meaning a gross margin of at least 53 percent and no reversal of the United States discount roll-back.[2][3] Third, the price is at or below ₹1,017.90 and above the ₹948.10 floor. If the company does not publish quartz volume for the quarter, the quartz test has not been passed. We then place no order and wait for the next quarter or a company answer, because a good total can hide a weak core plant.

Once all three conditions hold, the price decides what we do. Between ₹950 and ₹975, the entry zone, we place two equal limit orders, one at ₹975 and one at ₹955. If the price is already below ₹975, the first order fills at once, which is the intent because the zone is where we want to own the stock. Between ₹975 and ₹1,017.90, which includes today's ₹988.90, we do not buy at the market. We place the same two limit orders below the price and let the market come to us. Above ₹1,017.90 we place nothing, because reward-to-risk is below 2 to 1. Below ₹948.10 we place nothing, because the floor has broken. Unfilled orders are cancelled before the next results date and must be re-confirmed after it. The zone is anchored on the 63-day low of ₹948.10, and the stop is one ATR below that floor: ₹948.10 minus ₹36.24 is ₹911.86, which we round to ₹911.90. The bottom of the zone is 0.05 ATR above support, a full-size band on our ladder, and the top is 0.74 ATR above it, the standard band.[28]

Sizing follows our risk-unit rule, which works like this in words: take the share of the portfolio we are willing to lose if the stop is hit, divide it by how far the stop sits below the buy price, and cap the answer at the limit for the tier. Carysil is tier 3, which carries a risk budget of 50 basis points (0.50 percent of the portfolio) and a cap of 3.5 percent in one name, because the company has concentrated customers, thin free cash flow and a small market value. The stop is 6.5 percent below ₹975, so the budget alone would allow 7.7 percent, and the cap of 3.5 percent binds. A fall back to a 22 times multiple would leave only 0.87 to 1 at ₹975 even if profit arrives as planned, so we buy only half the cap, 1.75 percent, at first. We add the second half only after the February 2027 result shows quartz volume growth of at least 10 percent again and a margin of at least 18.5 percent, and only at a price at or below ₹1,017.90. Whether the portfolio has room for another core holding is a portfolio-level check that this dossier does not make.

The loss figures are plans, not limits. If the stop is hit with a sale at ₹911.90, the planned loss is about 0.11 percent of the portfolio at the 1.75 percent starter, and 0.23 percent at the full 3.5 percent. Our stop works on a closing price, so the sale happens the next day, and a result-driven gap or thin trading can fill lower. As a sensitivity, if the stock fell to our bear price of ₹720 before we could sell, a 3.5 percent weight bought at ₹975 would lose about 0.92 percent of the portfolio, which is 3.5 percent times one minus 720 over 975. The 1.75 percent starter would lose about 0.46 percent.

The base target is ₹1,230, the bull target is ₹1,540 and the bear case is ₹720. Reward-to-risk is 3.1 to 1 at today's price and 4.0 to 1 at the top of the entry zone, against a floor of 2.0 for core holdings. The price at which it falls to 2.0 is ₹1,017.90, so we do not buy above that level whatever the news. If the stock later returns to Stage 2, we hold winners and trail the stop to the rising 50-day average rather than selling on a high RSI.
