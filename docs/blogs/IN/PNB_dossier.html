1. What the company does

A bank is a business that borrows money from many people and lends it to fewer people at a higher price. Punjab National Bank (PNB) holds ₹17,77,240 crore of deposits and has lent out ₹13,41,194 crore, so it lends about 75.5 paise of every rupee it holds[10]. The gap between what it earns on loans and investments and what it pays on deposits is called net interest income. In the September 2026 quarter that gap was ₹11,601 crore, up 10.8% on the year[12]. Net interest income, not fees, is the main engine.

The bank earned ₹34,585 crore of interest in the quarter. Loans produced ₹24,323 crore of it and government and corporate bond holdings produced ₹8,293 crore[16]. It paid ₹22,984 crore of interest, of which ₹21,434 crore went to depositors[16]. Because the bank earns and pays interest on balances worth a hundred times its profit, a change of a few hundredths of a percentage point in the margin moves profit by hundreds of crores. This is why every bank report starts with margin.

Who pays PNB? Households, farmers and small firms pay most of the interest. The retail, agriculture and MSME (micro, small and medium enterprise) loans, which the bank calls RAM, were ₹7,21,996 crore, or 57.4% of the loan book, and grew 13.6% in a year[18]. Large corporate loans were ₹5,35,088 crore[18]. Management has been replacing low-yield corporate and inter-bank participation loans with higher-yield RAM loans, and says it shed about ₹58,000 crore of low-yield advances in the quarter[23].

The second source of income is what the bank calls other income. It was ₹3,601 crore in the quarter, down 17.1%, because treasury profit fell from ₹1,769 crore to ₹753 crore[16]. Fee income rose 16.0% to ₹1,954 crore[12]. A third, less visible, source is recovery from loans written off in earlier years: ₹808 crore this quarter[16]. The bank does not control the timing of recoveries and treasury gains, which is why management itself reports a separate core operating profit that leaves both out[25].

The state owns the bank. The Government of India holds 70.08% of the 1,149.29 crore shares[20]. That ownership gives depositors comfort, brings public-policy lending targets such as government flagship schemes, and limits how fast the bank can change its cost base. It also means the owner can sell shares or inject capital, which affects the share count and is discussed in Chapter 6.

Where the money comes from: deposits

Deposits come in three kinds. Current-account balances (₹83,053 crore) and savings balances (₹5,42,893 crore) are cheap and sticky. Together they are called CASA (current and savings account), and they were 36.6% of domestic deposits, against 37.3% a year earlier and a management goal of 38% for FY27[18][14]. Term deposits (₹10,85,895 crore) cost more. A bank with a high CASA share pays less for funds and earns a wider margin, so a slipping CASA share is a cost.

In Q2 the bank raised US$2.7 billion of foreign-currency (FCNR) deposits and used them to repay expensive bulk deposits and certificates of deposit, cutting that high-cost group from about 19% to about 17% of deposits[25]. That is why the cost of deposits fell in the quarter even as deposit growth rose to 9.9%[10]. It is also a reminder that part of the margin gain came from a one-time swap of funding, not from a new customer franchise.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Net interest margin (NIM): net interest income divided by earning assets, shown as a percentage. PNB's global NIM was 2.58% in Q2 FY27[9].
  • Gross NPA and net NPA: loans where the borrower has stopped paying for 90 days. Gross is before provisions; net is after. PNB: 2.55% gross and 0.25% net[11].
  • Provision coverage ratio (PCR): the share of bad loans already covered by provisions. PNB reports 97.32%, counting technical write-offs[11].
  • CRAR and CET-1: capital as a share of risk-weighted assets. CRAR is the total; CET-1 is the highest-quality part. PNB: 17.95% and 14.44%[10][15].
  • Credit cost: loan-loss provisions as a percentage of loans, per year. PNB reports 0.25% for the quarter[14].
  • RSI (relative strength index): a 0 to 100 gauge of recent buying pressure. Above 70 is stretched and below 30 is oversold. PNB is at 59.77[40].
  • ATR (average true range): the typical daily price swing over 14 days. PNB's is ₹3.10, and distance from support is counted in these steps[40].
  • EPS (earnings per share): profit divided by the number of shares. PNB earned ₹4.81 in Q2 FY27[17].
  • FCF and free cash flow: cash left after capital spending. It is not meaningful for a bank, so this report uses profit instead.
  • Stage 2 and Stage 4: the trend phases of a stock. Stage 2 is a rising uptrend and Stage 4 is a falling downtrend[40].
  • Base, pivot and breakout: a base is a flat price floor after a fall, the pivot is the price that ends it, and a breakout is a close above that level.
  • Limit order and entry zone: a limit order buys only at or below a chosen price, and the entry zone is the price range chosen for that order.
  • Asymmetry and reward-to-risk: the expected gain divided by the loss if the stop is hit. The floor here is 2.5 to 1.
  • Price-to-earnings and debt-to-equity: price divided by earnings per share, and borrowings divided by shareholder equity. The second is not used for banks.
  • Slippage: new loans that turned bad in the period. PNB: 0.77% annualised, ₹2,395 crore of fresh additions[11][21].
  • Tier, risk budget and portfolio heat: the framework sorts positions into four conviction tiers that cap position size (Tier 1 up to 10%, Tier 3 2% to 3.5%). The risk budget, also called portfolio heat, is the loss accepted if the stop is hit: 50 basis points (0.5% of the portfolio) for Tier 3.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is stronger than it was, but it is still a public-sector bank with a thin margin. The clearest evidence is asset quality. Gross bad loans fell from 6.96% of loans in September 2023 to 3.45% a year ago and 2.55% now, while net bad loans fell from 1.47% to 0.25%[6][11]. The bank holds provisions covering 97.32% of its bad loans, so even if the remaining ₹34,190 crore of gross bad loans were never recovered, the net loss to equity would be small[11].

Capital is the second strength. The total capital ratio is 17.95% and the highest-quality tier (CET-1) is 14.44%, against a regulatory minimum for CET-1 of 8% that management quotes[15][25]. CET-1 rose from 12.75% a year ago, which means the bank is retaining enough profit to fund 14.7% loan growth without asking shareholders for money, at least for now[15][10]. Risk-weighted assets are ₹9,48,163 crore[15].

The weaker side is the cost of funds and the revenue mix. PNB's CASA share is 36.6% and has slipped, and the bank's margin of 2.58% is below the 2.8% to 2.9% it promised for FY26 and still below its FY27 goal of 2.60% to 2.70%[14][27]. Operating profit grew only 4.2% in the quarter against a goal of 9% to 10% for the year, because treasury profit fell by ₹1,016 crore[12][14][16]. The competitive position is therefore a large, trusted franchise with improving credit discipline, and a margin that is still catching up.

Where did the quarter's profit growth come from? Profit before tax rose ₹851 crore. Provisions fell from ₹643 crore to ₹97 crore, a ₹546 crore swing, and operating profit added the other ₹305 crore[16]. So about 64% of the pre-tax gain came from lower provisions. This does not mean the profit is unreal; it means the quality test for the next two quarters is whether operating profit growth speeds up to take over from provisions. Management's core operating profit, which excludes treasury and written-off recoveries, grew 29.4% and was 79% of operating profit, against 64% a year earlier[25]. That measure is management's own, so we treat it as encouraging rather than proven.

The moat, if any, is distribution and trust: 10,481 domestic branches, 53,672 touch points, 32,276 banking correspondents and a 95.7% digital share of transactions[10][11]. Government ownership lowers funding stress in a crisis. It does not give pricing power. The bank has opened 162 of a planned 250 new branches this year, 119 of them in the south and west[26].

What could break the strength

Agriculture is the weakest loan book: its gross bad-loan ratio was 8.63% against 10.30% a year ago, and the managing director said the team needs to be very careful there[19][25]. MSME bad loans fell from 10.24% to 6.08%[19]. One infrastructure (road-sector) corporate account caused a slippage this quarter, which management called a one-off; it is the first corporate slippage in a long time[25].

The next test is the new accounting rule for loan losses. Banks are moving to an expected-credit-loss (ECL) model, where provisions are set on expected rather than incurred losses. Management now estimates the one-time effect at about ₹8,000 crore, down from ₹9,000 to ₹10,000 crore, and has set aside a ₹1,150 crore extra provision on standard loans and a ₹2,560 crore floating provision as buffers[24][22]. That is a cushion, not a guarantee.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 asks which three to five measures run this particular business and whether each is healthy. For a bank we pick five: net interest margin, asset quality, capital, the deposit franchise (CASA share), and core earnings growth. Each is scored PASS (1.0), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0), and the bank qualifies when the weighted score is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 with at most one FAIL.

We chose these five because they explain almost all of a bank's profit and risk. Margin drives revenue. Asset quality drives losses. Capital sets how fast the bank can grow. CASA drives the cost of funds. Core earnings growth shows whether profit rests on operations or on one-off items. D/E and cash-flow-to-profit ratios are not used, because a bank's operating cash flow mostly records changes in deposits and loans.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management credibility is mixed and the pattern is clear. On asset quality, management delivered what it promised. For FY26 it guided gross NPA below 3% and delivered 2.95%, net NPA of 0.35% and delivered 0.29%, credit cost below 0.5% and delivered 0.18%, and a slippage ratio below 1% and delivered 0.60%[27]. On margin, it did not. It guided NIM of 2.8% to 2.9% and delivered 2.57%, guided net interest income growth of 7% and delivered -1.9%, and guided return on assets above 1% and delivered 0.89%[27].

The reasons were partly outside its control. The December 2025 rate cut touched only 26 days of Q3 but the whole of Q4, and deposit rates stayed sticky and did not fully offset the lower loan yields, as management said on the May 2026 call[30]. Part of the return-on-assets miss came from a one-time tax charge of ₹3,324 crore in Q1 FY26 when the bank moved to the new tax regime[39]. In FY25 the bank also revised its gross NPA goal twice, to 3.50% to 3.75%, and still delivered 3.95%[29]. The score on 13 FY26 parameters is 8 met or beaten and 5 missed[27].

For FY27 the half-year read is mixed. Credit growth of 14.7% is ahead of the 12% to 13% goal, deposit growth of 9.9% is at the top of its range, and credit cost of 0.25% is inside the below-0.4% goal[14]. CASA at 36.6% against 38%, operating profit growth of 5.2% for the half year against 9% to 10%, and recoveries of ₹5,594 crore against a goal above ₹13,000 crore are behind[14]. Management itself now says ₹12,000 to ₹13,000 crore of recovery is a challenging figure[25].

Capital allocation is the other half of walk-the-talk. It is opening 250 branches, has run down inter-bank participation loans and is adding digital lending, with ₹22,018 crore of digital sanctions in the quarter[26][10]. Each of these choices is consistent with a margin and efficiency plan. The test is whether the margin follows.

The number

We estimate PNB's earnings per share for FY27 at ₹19.00.

How it was calculated

Half-year earnings per share was ₹9.38. We assume the second half repeats the Q2 figure of ₹4.81 each quarter, so the second half adds ₹9.62.

The inputs

Half-year EPS ₹9.38 and Q2 EPS ₹4.81 from the Q2 FY27 presentation[17]. Q2 profit of ₹5,532 crore and 1,149.29 crore shares[9][20].

The range

Bull ₹19.80 if profit reaches the ₹6,000 crore quarterly level the managing director targets (₹6,000 crore on 1,149.29 crore shares is ₹5.22 a quarter)[32]. Bear ₹17.60 if the second half averages ₹4.10 a quarter because provisions rise toward 0.5% credit cost.

If the number is off

If earnings per share is ₹17.60 instead of ₹19.00, March 2028 book value is about ₹4 lower and the central target about ₹3.60 lower, at about ₹132. That cuts reward-to-risk at ₹115.50 to about 2.2 to 1, below the 2.5 floor, so a weak second half would force the top of the entry zone down to about ₹114.80 or the verdict to REJECTED for this zone.

The number

We estimate book value per share at March 2028 at ₹148 before an expected-credit-loss charge and ₹143 after our ₹5 haircut.

How it was calculated

We start from ₹123.64 at September 2026, add 80% of the remaining FY27 profit (₹9.62 a share, 20% paid out), then add 80% of FY28 earnings of ₹20.90 (10% growth on ₹19.00). That gives ₹148. We then subtract ₹5 for the expected-credit-loss (ECL) transition.

The inputs

Book value ₹123.64 and payout near 20%[17][7]. FY28 growth of 10% reflects loan growth of about 12% to 14% against a flat margin near 2.65%; the margin goal is from the Q4 FY26 call[14][30]. ECL bridge (our assumption): management puts the one-time effect at about ₹8,000 crore, which is ₹6.96 per share before tax on 1,149.29 crore shares; at a tax rate near 25% that is about ₹5.2 after tax[24][20]. We do not deduct the ₹1,150 crore extra standard provision already booked (about ₹0.7 a share after tax) or the ₹2,560 crore floating provision, because management has not said how much of either will be used against the ECL charge[22][24]. The timing of recognition is not confirmed, so ₹5 is a conservative net figure.

The range

Bull ₹145 after the haircut (₹150 if FY27 earnings per share is ₹19.80 and FY28 is ₹22.50). Bear ₹139 after the haircut (₹144 if FY27 and FY28 are both ₹17.60). If the buffers absorb the whole charge the haircut is nil and book value is ₹148; if none is absorbed it is about ₹5.

If the number is off

If the ECL charge is larger or earlier than ₹5 a share, the target multiple applies to a lower book value and the reward-to-risk at the top of the entry zone falls below the 2.5 floor, so the zone would have to be lowered further.

5. What could happen next

At ₹121.27 the market values PNB at ₹1,39,537 crore, or 6.61 times trailing earnings[1]. This chapter asks what growth that price assumes, how analysts are moving, where we disagree, and what the bull, base and bear cases are worth.

For a bank we run the reverse discounted cash flow on trailing profit, because free cash flow is not meaningful for a lender. We use the framework's 12% discount rate and 20 times terminal multiple, then cross-check with price to book, which is how banks are normally valued.

Our variant perception

Most investors treat PNB as a state bank with a falling margin and one-off profit. Our variant view has three parts. First, core earnings are better than the headline. Core operating profit grew 29.4% and made up 79% of operating profit, so the bank no longer depends on treasury gains and recoveries as it did[25]. Second, the price already assumes much less than the bank earns: a return on equity near 12% is priced against 17%[17]. Third, brokers are moving up, not down: four brokerages put targets at ₹140 after the quarter[31][33].

Where we differ from the bulls is timing and quality. Provisions of ₹97 crore are the main reason profit grew 12.8% while operating profit grew 4.2%[16]. Management guides credit cost of 0.25% to 0.30%, which is the level it has just reported, so there is no cushion left in the numbers[24]. The Trendlyne average target is ₹146, but its own consensus row prints ₹129.94 on the same page, which shows that the target data is noisy[33]. We therefore anchor our ₹136 target on book value after an ECL haircut and a multiple below 1.0, a little below the ₹140 the four brokers print, and we buy only on a pullback.

The number

We estimate that today's price implies about -8.3% a year of profit growth, and about 11.9% sustainable return on equity.

How it was calculated

We solve for the ten-year growth rate that makes the present value of ten years of profit plus a 20-times terminal value equal to the market capitalisation, at a 12% discount rate. Separately, we invert the price-to-book formula.

The inputs

Market capitalisation ₹1,39,537 crore and trailing net profit ₹21,111 crore[1][2]. Price ₹121.27 and book value per share ₹123.64[40][17].

The range

At a 7-times terminal multiple, the implied growth is about -3.0% a year; on dividends only, +13.3% a year at 20 times. In the bull case (ROE 17% sustained) the justified price to book would be about 2.0x; in the bear case (ROE 12%) it is about 1.0x, which is today's price.

If the number is off

If the true sustainable ROE is 14% rather than 12%, the justified price to book becomes about 1.4x (growth of 7%, cost of equity 12%) and the stock would be about 40% undervalued; if it is 10%, the justified multiple is about 0.6x and today's price is too high.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate has a weakest assumption. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong and when that evidence will arrive. Dates for results are not yet announced for Q3 FY27; the Q3 results are expected after the quarter ends on 31 December 2026, likely in January 2027, and the date is unverified. The thresholds below are the only ones used anywhere in this report: margin below 2.55% or gross bad loans above 2.70% breaks the case, margin at or above 2.65% with gross bad loans at or below 2.50% and credit cost at or below 0.30% strengthens it, and anything between leaves the verdict at WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The bad-loan band is 20 basis points wide because the ratio moved by 17 to 26 basis points a quarter during the repair, so a smaller move is noise and a rise above 2.70% would be a reversal of that trend.

Weakest assumption 1: credit cost stays near 0.25%

Our earnings per share path assumes provisions stay low. Provisions fell to ₹97 crore in Q2 from ₹643 crore a year ago, and the bank also took ₹808 crore from written-off recoveries, wrote off ₹1,824 crore and has a ₹1,150 crore extra provision on standard loans[16][21][22]. If credit cost doubled to 0.5%, quarterly profit before tax would fall by about ₹840 crore (0.25 percentage points a year on ₹13.4 lakh crore of loans, divided by four), about 11% of Q2 pre-tax profit of ₹7,435 crore[10][16]. The evidence that we are wrong would be gross bad loans above 2.70% or a slippage ratio above 0.9% for a second quarter. Both appear in the Q3 FY27 filing.

Weakest assumption 2: the margin keeps rising

The central case needs margin to reach about 2.65%. Management expects 7 to 9 basis points more in Q3, ties it to the 25-basis-point repo-rate hike, says repo moves reach repo-linked loans the next working day, and put the repo-linked share of the book at 56%[23]. That works in reverse if rates are cut again: after the December 2025 cut, loan yields fell for the whole of Q4 and deposit rates did not fully offset the fall[30]. The evidence that we are wrong would be a Q3 margin below 2.55% or a CASA share below 36%.

Weakest assumption 3: the Government stays a patient owner

The state holds 70.08%. Equity capital rose 4.4% in FY25, and a further sale or issue would dilute earnings per share[20][4]. Public-sector banks also face policy-directed lending. We treat the risk of a large share sale or capital raise as real but unquantified; no announcement was found in the fetched sources. The evidence that we are wrong would be a stock-exchange notice of a sale, qualified institutional placement or offer for sale.

Red-team bear case

The strongest case against buying is that the 17% return on equity is flattered by three items that cannot repeat: provisions of 0.25%, written-off recoveries of ₹808 crore and a tax rate of 26%. If credit cost is 0.55%, recoveries halve and the margin stays at 2.55%, return on assets would be about 0.85% and return on equity about 14%. The market already prices about 12%, so the upside shrinks to the dividend yield of 2.47% plus book-value growth, and a weak market could push the stock toward 0.75 times book, near the 52-week low of ₹98.5[1][40]. This is why the entry is a limit order and not a market order.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT with gate status PASSED_CONDITIONAL. The business passes the bank-specific gates, and Layer 2 scores 3.0 out of 5.0 with one FAIL. But reward-to-risk clears the 2.5 to 1 turnaround floor only at the limit-order price: 1.10 to 1 at ₹121.27, against 2.67 to 1 at ₹115.50[40].

Layer 3 maps price to support. The stock is 3.84 average-true-range steps above the 20-day swing low of ₹109.37, which is the no-buy band above 1.6 steps. It is only 1.53 steps above the 20-day and 50-day averages near ₹116.50, which is the starter-or-limit band. A fill between ₹114.20 and ₹115.50 would sit on or just below those averages. That is within 0.75 steps of the 50-day average, the full-size or standard band. So the plan is a limit order only. The verdict becomes ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP only when the stock also closes back above ₹116.50.

The central target is ₹136, the bull ₹160 and the bear ₹98, from Chapter 5. The target already deducts a ₹5 per-share haircut for the expected-credit-loss transition. Without it the target would be ₹140 and the entry zone could reach ₹116.50. The stop is ₹107.82. It is the swing low of ₹109.37 minus half an average-true-range of ₹3.10, as the tactical bucket requires[40]. It sits 6.65% below ₹115.50. A close below it means the idea is broken, and the position is sold.

The market regime also governs size. The regime label is Downtrend, the Nifty 50 is in Stage 4, and only 18.1% of stocks are above their 50-day average. So the pipeline's market gate asks for no fresh position until the Nifty 50 closes above its 50-day average of 23,337.15 for three sessions[40]. We expect this gate to take longer than the pullback. It needs a gain of about 2.6% in the index, from 22,749.75, and three straight closes above the line in a downtrend. The stock needs only a 4.8% dip to ₹115.50.

Size follows risk. The framework sorts positions into four conviction tiers that cap size. Tier 3, the third band, allows 2% to 3.5% of the portfolio. We chose it because this is a turnaround in a Downtrend market with one failed KPI. The risk budget, which the framework also calls portfolio heat, is the loss we accept if the stop is hit. For Tier 3 that is 50 basis points, or 0.5% of the portfolio. A stop 6.65% below entry would allow a 7.5% position under that budget, but the Tier 3 ceiling caps it at 3.5%, as the table below shows. If the stock runs, let winners run. Do not trim only because the 14-day RSI passes 70 or the weight passes 15%. Trail the stop to the rising 21-day or 50-day average while trend and estimates hold, up to the framework's 22% runaway-winner cap.
