1. What the company does

Indo Tech Transformers Limited makes the large grey boxes that sit in electricity substations and beside factories and wind farms. A transformer works like a gearbox for electricity: it raises the voltage so power can travel far with little loss, then lowers it again so a factory, a home or a pump can use it. The company began in 1975 as a partnership, became a public company in 1992, and today runs one plant at Kancheepuram in Tamil Nadu with an installed capacity of about 14,000 MVA, a unit that measures transformer size[31][29]. It has built more than 66,000 transformers, and its range covers distribution units for the last mile, power and large power units up to 315 MVA and the 400 kV voltage class, units for solar, wind and hydro plants, and special builds such as inverter, converter and mobile-substation units[3]. The company reports a single segment, the manufacture and sale of transformers[4].

The money logic is simple to state and hard to run. In the year to March 2026 the company booked revenue of ₹782.08 crore and spent ₹595.81 crore on materials, which is 76 percent of revenue, before a ₹41.14 crore addition to unsold stock is netted off[1]. Steel, copper and transformer oil are the main inputs, so profit depends on whether customer contracts let the company pass on metal price moves, and on how well it fills the plant. Staff costs were only ₹38.42 crore for 353 employees, about 4.9 percent of revenue, so the factory is run with a lean payroll and most of the cost is bought-in material[1]. Each extra rupee of sales therefore brings a thin but steady slice of profit, and margin gains come from product mix, not from cutting labour.

Who buys the output matters as much as what is built. The investor deck lists state grid companies such as APTRANSCO and KPTCL, NTPC, solar and wind developers such as Adani and JSW, and industrial buyers in steel, cement, textiles and data centres[3]. Recent filings show the mix in action: a ₹165.55 crore order for 26 power transformers from AP Transco, a ₹55 crore order from Waaree Renewable for five units, and a ₹42.88 crore order from the Leap Green Energy group that includes the company's first 400 kV-class units[9][10][11][30]. The company does not publish how much of its revenue comes from its ten largest customers, and we searched for it without success, so concentration cannot be measured and is treated as an unknown risk[1][3]. Tender-based utility work is lumpy, and renewable developers sometimes cancel when projects slip: one developer cancelled a ₹64.99 crore order for six transformers in March 2026 because of a project delay, which the press reported as a 4 percent fall in the share that day[12][13][43].

Ownership is the part of the story that changes the answer. Shirdi Sai Electricals Limited, itself a transformer maker, bought control in September 2020 and held 75 percent until June 2026[29][26]. The parent is also a customer and a supplier: in the year to March 2026 the company sold ₹23.96 crore of goods to it, bought ₹11.14 crore of goods from it, and earned ₹1.92 crore of interest from it, and shareholders approved up to ₹100 crore each way for the current year[1]. The parent sold 5 lakh shares in the open market on 29 and 30 June 2026, which cut its holding to 70.29 percent, and it has pledged most of what remains to lenders of its own[22][23][18]. Chapters 2 and 3 test the business and the numbers, and Chapter 3 returns to the pledge, because it is the single fact that decides the verdict.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • RSI is the relative strength index, a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has moved. Below 30 is called oversold, which means the price fell unusually fast.
  • ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price swing over 14 days. We measure distances to support in ATR units so that a calm stock and a wild stock are judged fairly.
  • EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges.
  • ROCE is return on capital employed: operating profit before interest divided by the money tied up in the business (total assets less short-term liabilities). A high figure means each rupee invested earns a lot.
  • EPS is earnings per share, the profit after tax divided by the number of shares.
  • FCF is free cash flow, which means cash from operations minus the money spent on plant and equipment. The same words are the full name of the measure, so free cash flow is the cash left for owners after the business has paid to stay in business and grow.
  • BESS stands for battery energy storage system, a large bank of batteries that stores power for the grid. It needs transformers to connect to the grid.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing prices of the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. The 200-day line is the long-trend marker, and a rising line means the longer trend is up.
  • Stage 2 is the rising phase of a stock's life, when the price sits above rising 50-day and 200-day averages; Stage 4 is the falling phase, when the price sits below falling 50-day and 200-day averages. Both come from the Weinstein stage method used in our ledger.
  • Base is a trading range where a stock rests after a fall or rise. The 63-day base low is the lowest price of the last 63 trading days. A pivot is the top or bottom edge of that range where traders watch for a decision.
  • Breakout is a close above the top of a range. A retest is a later dip back to that old edge to see if it holds as support.
  • Stop loss is the price at which we would admit the idea was wrong and sell. A limit order is an instruction to buy only at a named price or lower, and the entry zone is the band of prices where we would place such orders.
  • Asymmetry is the ratio of what we could gain to what we could lose; reward-to-risk is the same idea, measured as gain to the target divided by loss to the stop loss. Our floor is 2.0 for core ideas and 2.5 for tactical and turnaround ideas.
  • Operating margin is operating profit divided by sales; gross margin is sales less the direct cost of what was sold, as a share of sales; net margin is final profit after tax as a share of sales.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' equity; net debt is borrowings minus cash. A figure near zero means the company is funded by its owners and not by lenders.
  • Price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by the last twelve months of earnings per share. It says how many years of current profit the price is paying for.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates from running itself, after tax and after changes in stock and unpaid bills; working capital is the money tied up in stock and customer bills less what suppliers are still owed.
  • Capital expenditure is money spent on plants, machines and buildings; guidance is what management says it expects to do or earn; dilution is the loss of ownership share when a company issues new shares. A buyback is the reverse, when a company repurchases its own shares.
  • Consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who cover a stock; measured move is a target taken from the height of a chart pattern; book-to-bill is new orders divided by sales; take rate is the share of a transaction a platform keeps. The last two are not used for this company.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business starts with how much it earns on the money tied up in it. In the year to March 2026 the company earned operating profit before interest of about ₹125.93 crore on capital employed of ₹381.96 crore, which is a return of 33.0 percent, matching the 33.0 percent in the company's own key indicators[1]. Screener shows 40.4 percent because it measures capital differently, and the investor deck says 38 percent. We use the audited 33.0 percent as the floor and treat the others as upper readings[26][3]. Debt is almost absent: borrowings were ₹5.19 crore against equity of ₹373.71 crore, a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.01, and finance costs of ₹2.22 crore were covered more than fifty times by operating profit[1]. Few industrial companies in India run with that little borrowed money while growing sales at 28 percent a year.

The demand case is large and long. The national plan for power transmission for 2023 to 2032 carries a cost of about ₹9.15 lakh crore, and a newer national plan to 2035-36 adds about 8,27,600 MVA of substation capacity at an estimated ₹7.93 lakh crore[33][34]. Market researchers expect the Indian transformer market to grow at about 8.22 percent a year to 2031, while the company's own deck uses a ₹16,063 crore to ₹28,744 crore path from FY22 to FY30E[32][3]. Abroad, lead times for large power transformers now run above two years, which shows how tight the global supply chain is[36]. Rival Transformers and Rectifiers (India) reported an unexecuted order book 32 percent higher than a year ago and second-quarter inflows up 97 percent, which shows the industry order cycle is still rising[35]. Indo Tech has positioned itself for this: capacity rose from 9,000 MVA in FY25 to 12,000 MVA in FY26 and 14,000 MVA by June 2026, and the NTPC approval in July 2026 opens the 400 kV class for battery storage projects[29][6].

The weak points are inside the factory's cash cycle. Inventory rose 46 percent in one year to ₹196.58 crore, and inventory days climbed from 114 to 131, so cash is being absorbed by raw material and part-built units even while profit rises[1][26]. Cash from operations was ₹56.88 crore against profit after tax of ₹92.77 crore, a ratio of 0.61, and after ₹34.05 crore of plant spending the free cash flow was only ₹22.83 crore[1]. The company also lost money in three of the five years from FY16 to FY20, and ROCE was 2.7 percent or lower in every one of those years, so the present profitability is about five years old and has not been tested by a full downturn[1][26]. Labour has also been a factor: in July 2025 the company went to court against three trade unions that held a sit-in strike inside the factory, and the interim order was lifted in October 2025 while the main suit continues[14][15][16][42].

Taken together, the business is strong on returns and balance sheet and still unproven on cash conversion and on how it behaves when orders slow. That is why we score it with specific operating measures in Chapter 2B instead of relying on the headline profit growth. The next question is what has driven the jump in profit, and the two subsections that follow answer it before the scorecard.

What turned the profit around after 2020

For most of the 2010s the company barely earned anything: profit after tax was negative in FY17, FY18 and FY19 and only ₹1.92 crore in FY20[1]. Control moved to Shirdi Sai Electricals in September 2020, and the turnaround that followed came from three changes working together: a fuller plant, better prices, and a cleaner balance sheet[29]. Profit after tax rose from ₹12.19 crore in FY22 to ₹92.77 crore in FY26 while equity, which started at ₹145.12 crore, rose to ₹373.71 crore[1]. The operating margin on gross turnover moved from 7 percent to 16.5 percent over the same period, and net margin from 4 percent to 12 percent[1]. A turnaround of this size usually means two things for an owner: the best part of the percentage gain is behind the company, and the base year was so low that the growth figures look larger than the business really is. The next subsection looks at the cost of the plan to keep growing.

What the new capacity plan costs and how it will be paid for

In December 2024 the board approved ₹75 crore of capital expenditure to reach 16,000 MVA. Eighteen months later the approved total is ₹495 crore for a plan to reach 50,000 MVA by FY29, after steps of ₹25 crore in November 2025, ₹35 crore in May 2026 and ₹360 crore in June 2026[8][7]. The company says the plan will be financed from internal accruals and term loans with no equity dilution[3]. Against that, the whole of FY24 to FY26 produced cash from operations of ₹143.95 crore, and cash plus bank deposits stood at ₹113.71 crore at March 2026, so about ₹381 crore of the plan has no funded source in the statements today[1][2]. Borrowing most of that would lift debt-to-equity from 0.01 to roughly 1.0 on March 2026 equity. That is still under our 1.20 red line, but it ends the near-zero-debt profile that the high ROCE currently enjoys. A filing detail adds caution: the June 2026 letter lists existing capacity of 14,000 MVA, a proposed addition of 25,000 MVA and a target of 50,000 MVA, and 14,000 plus 25,000 is 39,000, not 50,000[7]. The company should reconcile that figure, and the answer decides how much of the ₹495 crore is really needed.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Headline returns tell you the business has been good. They do not tell you whether the next year will be good, and for that we pick a handful of measures that fit this particular company and score each one. A transformer maker lives or dies on four things: whether orders keep arriving, whether the plant is full and growing on schedule, whether customers pay on time, and whether profit turns into cash and holds its margin as metal prices move. We score each measure PASS (1.0), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). The set qualifies when the total reaches 3.0 out of 5.0 with at most one FAIL.

Five measures fit this company. Order cover shows demand, utilisation and capacity delivery show execution, debtor days show customer quality, cash conversion shows whether profit is real, and margin resilience shows pricing power over steel and copper. The scoring uses audited FY26 figures, the June 2026 quarter and the September 2026 filings, and it does not use the watchlist seed, which claimed 4 of 4 healthy measures without showing any of them.

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on the future, and a good way to read the bet is to work backward from the price. A reverse discounted cash flow, or reverse DCF, asks what growth rate the company must deliver every year for ten years for today's price to earn a 12 percent return, assuming the business is then worth 20 times its cash flow. If the answer is modest, the price is cheap against the record, and if it is demanding, the market has already paid for the good news. We compare that with how analyst forecasts are moving, which is called estimate revision, and then state where we differ.

Two warnings apply. The ledger's reverse DCF is a quick proxy built on the last twelve months of net profit, and it prices the stock off a market capitalisation of ₹2,869.42 crore, which implies a share price near ₹2,702, a later quote than the 23 October close of ₹2,648.30. At the ledger close the 1.062 crore shares are worth ₹2,812.49 crore, so the proxy is about 2 percent high. Second, no analyst forecast exists for this company in the sources we could read, so the consensus estimate rows below are empty by fact, not by omission, and we searched three ways before concluding that[27].

Where we differ from the market starts with what the market is pricing. The ledger's 11.5 percent suggests a stock that is cheap relative to history. Base: FY28 earnings per share of ₹113.0 times 28.3, the trailing multiple at the ledger close (₹2,648.30 divided by ₹93.50), equals ₹3,198. The ledger's 28.89 comes from a later quote near ₹2,702. At 28.9 times the base would be ₹3,265 and the weighted value ₹2,911, and we show that case as a disclosed alternative. Bull: ₹145.8 times 30 equals ₹4,374, rounded to ₹4,370. Bear: FY27 bear earnings per share of ₹80.9 times 22 equals ₹1,780. Probabilities are 50, 15 and 35 percent. The weighted value is 0.50 times 3,198 plus 0.15 times 4,370 plus 0.35 times 1,780, which is 1,599 plus 655.5 plus 623, or ₹2,877.5, rounded to ₹2,878.

The inputs

The three-year median price-to-earnings multiple is not published on the free pages we could read, so we use the trailing 28.3 times at the ledger close (28.9 times on the later quote), 30 times for the bull case, which is below the 35.8 times seen in mid-September, and 22 times for the bear case.[27][26]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report rests on a few assumptions, and the honest way to end the analysis is to name the weakest one under each estimate and then argue the opposite case as hard as we can. This chapter does both: it lists the weakest assumption for the cash conversion, return, reverse DCF and target estimates, builds the strongest case against our REJECTED verdict and against the base target, and names the one claim on which the whole decision hangs. The last part gives the dates when each question will be answered.

Weakest assumption in the cash conversion and return estimates

The cash conversion estimate of 0.71 assumes that the FY26 stock build was working-capital timing and not a permanent change. If inventory days stay near 131 and sales keep rising at 25 percent, the business will need to keep funding stock and the ratio will stay near 0.6, which would fail the 0.70 compounder line while still passing the 0.40 red-line floor[1][26]. The return estimate has a similar weak point: 33.0 percent return on capital employed is measured on a capital base that has not yet absorbed the ₹495 crore plan. If that plan is built and earns little for two years, ROCE could fall toward the high teens before it recovers, still above our 15 percent floor but far from the 40.4 percent the seed quoted. The practical test is whether FY27 cash from operations exceeds ₹80 crore, which would show conversion rising with profit.

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF and the earnings estimates

The reverse DCF assumes that the three-year average of owner cash flow, ₹28.69 crore, is a fair base. It is probably too low, because plant spending in FY26 included growth capacity and not just upkeep, and it may be too high if stock builds again. Across five different bases the implied growth ranges from 11.5 to 31.1 percent, so the conclusion that the price is not cheap on cash depends on which base you choose, and we have shown all five so you can judge. The earnings estimates assume an operating margin of 14.5 percent in FY27 and 14.0 percent in FY28 and a 25 percent tax rate. The company's margin has moved between 10 and 17 percent in individual quarters, so a few weeks of copper or steel inflation could move FY27 earnings per share by more than ₹7 for each point of margin[26]. The estimate also has no analyst estimates to anchor it, so it is one view with no external check.

Weakest assumption in the targets and the probability of each scenario

The base target of ₹3,198 multiplies FY28 earnings per share by 28.3, the trailing multiple at the ledger close (28.9 times on the later quote gives ₹3,265), because the three-year median multiple is not published on the pages we could read. If the multiple should be lower, because the stock deserves a governance discount, the target falls ₹113 for every point of multiple. Reward-to-risk is already below 2.0 at the ledger-close multiple, and at 25 times it drops to 0.60 at the close and 0.68 at ₹2,635. The scenario probabilities are our judgement. A 35 percent chance of the bear case is high for a company with strong orders, and a reader who thinks the pledge is harmless could cut it to 25 percent and raise the bull case to 25 percent, which gives about ₹3,137. We use 35 percent because the pledged block is about twice the shares held outside the promoter group and the lenders' pledge terms, including any price at which they can act, are not public.

The strongest case against our REJECTED verdict

The bull case against us is that the pledge is a financing tool for a solvent group, not a distress signal. The parent borrowed from credit funds against a block of the listed company's shares, those funds hold a trustee-managed pledge over a stated 26 percent block, and the pledge ratio has moved up mostly because the parent sold some unencumbered shares. The company itself has almost no debt, a better rating (India Ratings BBB+ with a positive outlook in August 2026), and a growing order book, and none of its audited numbers is affected by the pledge[5][1]. If the parent redeems the debentures or releases shares, the red line clears and the stock would be a quality compounder at a modest multiple. We accept that this could happen, and we do not say the stock cannot rise. We say our rule treats a pledge above 20 percent of the promoter's holding as a disqualifier, and the evidence of June to October 2026 does not point to its clearing soon.

The strongest case for our verdict, the hinge claim and when it will be known

The case for the verdict is arithmetic. If the share price fell to the ₹1,709 level at which the 26 percent block was pledged in November 2025, that block would be worth about ₹472 crore against debentures of ₹750 crore, and the lenders would hold less than two-thirds cover from that block alone[23][20]. The unencumbered promoter stake is only 13,12,764 shares, or 12.36 percent of the company, so the parent has little left to add if the lenders ask for more collateral[19]. A forced sale of even part of 61,52,236 pledged shares would be several times a day's trading volume, and the 29.71 percent of the company held outside the promoter group is thin. The hinge claim is therefore that the pledge is a financing choice of a solvent parent and not a stress signal. Our evidence says it is not safe to assume that, and it will be tested at four dates: the Sep 2026 quarter results, due by 14 November 2026 and not yet scheduled. The December 2026 shareholding pattern in mid-January 2027. Any Reg 31 pledge or release filing, which is disclosed within days. And the parent's own filings on its debenture schedule[27][25].

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED. The rule is the Layer 1 hard red line on promoter pledge. Of the promoter's holding, 82.41 percent is pledged, against a limit of 20 percent. The company is not owned, and no order should be placed at any price while the pledge stands above 20 percent. This is a governance verdict and not a judgement that the factory is poor. The business passes the other red lines and scores 4.0 out of 5.0 on its operating measures. Its fundamentals fit a thin compounder, although reward-to-risk is below the floor at the ledger close. The report records the levels it would use so that the file can be reopened quickly.

The 20-day swing low and the 63-day base low are the same price.
