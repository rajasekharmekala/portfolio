1. What the company does

Quality Power Electrical Equipments Ltd was founded in 2001 and is based in Sangli, Maharashtra. It makes equipment that sits between a power plant and the people who use the power. Reactors are coils that smooth and limit current. Instrument transformers let utilities measure very high voltages safely. Power-quality systems keep a grid steady when wind and solar output jumps around.[1][7] The group runs seven plants in India and Turkey and employs about 1,500 people. It sells to more than 200 customers in over 100 countries. These include Hitachi Energy, Siemens, Siemens Energy, GE Vernova T&D India, Adani Energy Solutions and POWERGRID.[7] A crore is ten million, and every rupee figure here is in crore unless stated otherwise.

Most of the group was bought or part-bought, so read it as six businesses under one listed parent. Quality Power Engineering Projects (100 percent owned) makes reactors, filters and line traps in Sangli, Pune and Kochi. Mehru Electrical & Mechanical Engineers (51 percent) makes instrument transformers up to 500 kV in Bhiwadi, Rajasthan. It was bought on 6 March 2025 with ₹120 crore of the IPO money.[5][7] Endoks Enerji (51 percent, in Turkey) builds static var compensators and STATCOMs, which steady a grid. It now also builds power converters for battery storage. Sukrut Electric (50 percent, a joint venture with Yash Highvoltage, bought for about ₹5.2 crore) makes transformer tanks and switchgear parts. A 26 percent stake in Nebeskie Labs adds monitoring software.[3][4][7][22][23]

Management ranks the three product families by growth. Power electronics comes first, high-voltage power products second, and ancillary parts third. Power electronics means Endoks and its BESS work. BESS stands for battery energy storage system, a large battery bank joined to the grid. It needs a power conversion system, which management says is 40 to 50 percent of a storage project's cost.[6][8] Power products are the reactors and instrument transformers used in direct-current links, data-centre substations and grid plants. Ancillary parts are winding wire and tanks made in-house. The group bills these internally, so they do not count as outside sales.[6]

Who pays, and how fast, comes next. Customers are grid operators, equipment makers such as Hitachi Energy and Siemens, renewable developers and data-centre builders. In the June 2026 quarter the group won reactors for a US data centre worth ₹48.3 crore, due in about twelve months. It also won a Japanese FACTS order of ₹40.9 crore, due by December 2027.[7] Orders are large and slow to build. Sales trail order wins by 12 to 15 months, which is management's stated delivery window. Cash arrives in stages, and each customer must first approve the factory itself.

How the group makes money on each rupee of sales

Gross margin means sales minus the cost of materials, shown as a share of sales. For FY26 the group's gross margin was 43.3 percent. In the June 2026 quarter it was 47.2 percent, against 44.6 percent a year earlier, because the mix shifted to high-voltage and power-quality work.[7] Reported EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, here including other income) was ₹64.7 crore, or 25.2 percent of income. That is after a ₹7.82 crore non-cash loss from Turkish hyperinflation accounting. Without the loss, management puts EBITDA at ₹72.5 crore, or 28.3 percent.[6][26]

Input costs explain why margins move. Aluminium is bought months before it is wound into a coil, so a price spike reaches Quality Power's results about six months later. Copper and oil move Mehru's margin within about eight weeks. Chips for storage inverters are priced by volume, and large buyers get discounts of 40 to 50 percent.[8][6] Management therefore guides to a floor, not a point. Model about 20 percent, or high-teens, EBITDA margin. Expect a dip in the December 2026 quarter, when the new Sangli plant's fixed costs arrive before its output does.[6]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position rests on qualification, not on price. A customer will not accept a new high-voltage factory until it has audited it. Management says Sangli faces about 60 audits, spread over about six months from the day trial production starts.[6] Global equipment makers audit first, with five or six audits from Hitachi alone. Then come the Adani and POWERGRID audits for the direct-current links already booked. About eight audits therefore come before the plant can take its next project.[6] That is a real barrier to entry. It is also why the plant cannot lift sales quickly.

The company can show proof of its engineering. The Sangli and Bhiwadi laboratories are accredited to ISO 17025 for systems up to 800 kV. They can test to 2,500 kV and 15,000 amps, which most small suppliers cannot.[7] Management says Hitachi, Siemens and GE buy more instrument transformers from Mehru than they make themselves. It also says Hitachi and Siemens do not make coil products, and GE has only a small plant in Brazil.[8] The main rival in reactors is Trench Electric, once part of Siemens. In instrument transformers it is Arteche of Spain, whose sales management puts above ₹3,500 crore. That is about eleven times Mehru's FY26 sales of ₹318 crore.[8]

The financial evidence agrees with the story. FY26 return on capital employed was 32 percent. Borrowings were ₹35.5 crore against total equity of ₹773 crore, and cash and bank balances were ₹248 crore.[1][2] The June 2026 gross margin of 47.2 percent points to pricing power. Management says every order in the book was booked above its margin target.[6][7] Margins are still volatile. The quarterly operating margin (operating profit as a share of sales) read 28 percent in December 2025, 11 percent in March 2026 and 18 percent in June 2026. Chapter 3 explains why.[1]

The weaker side is easy to miss in a headline. About 34.6 percent of FY26 profit belongs to minority holders of Mehru and Endoks. That is ₹64.2 crore of the ₹185.5 crore total. An ordinary QPOWER shareholder's share is ₹121.3 crore.[2] Other income was ₹59.7 crore, or 27.6 percent of pre-tax profit. It included ₹16.8 crore of currency gains and ₹30.8 crore of interest on cash left over from the IPO. None of that is core manufacturing profit.[3] Both facts matter because the ₹10,538 crore market value at the 28 October close is a price for the shareholder's slice only.

The February 2025 issue was unusual in one way. Nearly three-quarters of its money went to the sellers, not to the company. Only ₹225 crore of the ₹858.70 crore was new money, and ₹633.70 crore was promoters selling shares.[13][5] The promoter group held 73.91 percent after the issue and still does.[14] Promoter pledge means shares pledged to lenders as loan security. It has been 0.0 percent in every quarter since listing. About 27 percent of promoter shares also remain under the regulatory lock-in.[14]

The price move is not explained by earnings alone. On 9 January 2026 the company issued a press note after reports that India might ease limits on Chinese bidders for government electrical contracts. The stock touched its 52-week low of ₹581 on 27 January.[28][13] It then rose almost threefold to ₹1,681 by 6 October. That stretch included record FY26 results, an order book that grew from over ₹1,400 crore to ₹1,945 crore, and the Winwin term sheet.[13][29] Over the same period FY26 profit attributable to owners rose 1.8 times, from ₹66.2 crore to ₹121.3 crore. At its high the share price was about four times the issue price, so valuation did most of the lifting.[2]

Why the group is exposed to politics, currency and one subsidiary

Endoks makes up 41 percent of the order book. It is accounted for under Ind AS 29, the rule for companies in hyperinflationary economies. The rule forces a non-cash charge when Turkish prices rise faster than the restated currency value. The charge was ₹25.7 crore in Q4 FY26 and ₹7.82 crore in Q1 FY27.[8][6] The charge is non-cash. It still lowers reported profit each quarter that Turkish inflation stays high, so earnings are harder to forecast than orders.

Policy risk cuts both ways. Easier rules for Chinese bidders would threaten transformers and switchgear. The company's press note replied that its direct-current and FACTS work is protected by qualification rules. It added that a significant part of its revenue is in dollars.[28] Management said the Chinese firms named in the reports run no operating factories in India. They also still face 60 to 70 percent local-content norms.[6] Demand backdrop is supportive. The national transmission plan to 2032 calls for ₹9.15 lakh crore of investment and nine direct-current systems of 33.25 GW. India's storage pipeline has passed 100 GW, yet only 10.3 GW is built.[20][21] That gap is why management calls its US$60 million storage-inverter figure a pipeline and not an order book.[6]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Guidance is the best test of a promoter team, because a number said aloud can be checked later. On money, this team has a good record. It began FY26 guiding to about ₹800 crore of sales and raised that to ₹900 crore. It delivered ₹1,007 crore at a 23.5 percent EBITDA margin, against a 20 to 22 percent aim.[8][9] The ₹1,945.5 crore order book at 30 June also beat the ₹1,500 to ₹1,800 crore that management wanted by the start of FY28.[8][6]

On plants and funding, the record is weaker. The February 2026 call said Sangli had been advanced to June 2026. In May the date moved to July-August. In August it became an August trial. Manufacturing began on 20 October, about four months after the first date.[9][8][7][25] The share raise also grew. In May it was an enabling resolution for up to US$75 million, to be used only as needed. In August it was less than ₹500 crore. The September notice asked for ₹700 crore plus a ₹175 crore promoter loan.[8][6][10]

Two smaller promises were not kept. Mehru's internal margin target was 22 to 23 percent, and Q1 delivered about 18 percent. The guide was reset to about 18 percent. In transformers, management bid US$80 to US$100 million in the US and lost much of it. It now says it is unsure about scaling that line.[6][8] Neither is large. Both show confidence running ahead of delivery wherever a new factory or a third party is involved.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue first. Consolidated sales rose from ₹183 crore in FY22 to ₹947 crore in FY26. That is about 51 percent a year over four years. The twelve months to June 2026 stand at ₹1,003 crore.[1] Read the curve with care. It contains an acquisition (Mehru, consolidated from 6 March 2025). It contains a Turkish subsidiary reported in a hyperinflation currency. Equity capital also rose from ₹0.15 crore in FY23 to ₹72 crore in FY24 and ₹77 crore after the February 2025 listing. So earnings per share before FY25 cannot be compared with later years.[1][3]

Profit is the next layer. Net profit rose from ₹42 crore to ₹186 crore over the four years. Earnings per share (EPS, the profit attributable to one share) rose from ₹9.10 in FY25 to ₹15.67 in FY26.[2][7] Only 65.4 percent of FY26 profit, ₹121.3 crore of ₹185.5 crore, belongs to owners of the listed company. The other 34.6 percent belongs to the 49 percent holders of Mehru and Endoks. EPS is built on the owners' share.[2] In the June 2026 quarter the owners' share was 77 percent. Quality Power's own sales grew fastest (₹69 crore against ₹37 crore) while the Turkish subsidiary's sales dipped. EPS of ₹4.66 times 7.74 crore shares gives ₹36.1 crore of a ₹46.7 crore profit.[6][7]

Cash is where the story is less comfortable. FY26 cash from operations was ₹80 crore against ₹186 crore of profit. Customers' unpaid bills rose by ₹105.7 crore, and the company spent ₹129 crore on plant. Screener's free cash flow line shows minus ₹31 crore, because it counts about ₹111 crore of capex. On the annual report's plant spending it would be about minus ₹49 crore.[1][2] The balance sheet absorbed it easily, with ₹248 crore of cash and bank balances and ₹35.5 crore of borrowings. Still, a company that asks shareholders for ₹700 crore while free cash flow is negative is telling you that growth costs more cash than it makes.[2][10]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

It also said insulators take 18 to 24 months to arrive.[8] If the audits take nine months, not six, FY28 sales could be nearer ₹1,400 crore than ₹1,725 crore. That is our bear case, and it puts EPS at ₹14.7.

Flaw 2: profit quality, minorities and cash conversion

The profit that reaches shareholders is weaker than the headline. Other income was 27.6 percent of FY26 pre-tax profit. Owners got 65.4 percent of the profit. Cash from operations was 0.43 of profit, while customers' unpaid bills rose ₹105.7 crore.[2][3] The June 2026 EBITDA margin of 25.2 percent is after a ₹7.82 crore Turkish charge. Yet the December 2025 quarter (28 percent) and March 2026 quarter (11 percent) show how far results swing on accounting and timing.[1][6] If other income halves as IPO cash is spent and currency gains do not repeat, pre-tax profit would be about ₹30 crore lower than if other income repeated FY26's ₹59.7 crore.

Flaw 3: financing, related-party dealings and dilution

The joint managing director and promoter, Bharanidharan Pandyan, will lend ₹175 crore. The loan is unsecured, at 9 percent, for 11 years with a one-year pause on repayment. It passed at the 19 October meeting with 90.93 percent of the votes polled by non-promoters. Those votes were about 7.2 percent of all shares.[10][12] The loan helps the balance sheet. But the notice states it as 184.74 percent of annual turnover, when ₹175 crore over ₹947 crore is 18.5 percent.

An NSE letter of 7 October prompted a corrigendum dated 9 October. It fixed a 90-day average price (₹1,238.49, not ₹1,456.40) and a share count (10,17,123, not 10,19,635). The turnover percentage is not among its four corrections.[10][11] Three drafting slips in one notice are small alone. Together they ask for closer scrutiny of every later filing.

The share count will rise about 8.0 percent. The swap adds 10,17,123 shares at ₹1,460. A ₹700 crore raise at ₹1,360.70 adds about 5.14 million more. In August the raise had been described as less than ₹500 crore.[10][6] FY26 related-party dealings also include ₹70 crore borrowed from, and repaid to, whole-time director Mrs Chitra Pandyan within the year. That is clean, but it shows how flexibly cash moves inside the family.[5]

Flaw 4: Turkey, storage margins and policy

Endoks is 41 percent of the order book. Its storage inverters earn 10 to 12 percent margins, which management hopes will reach 17 to 18 percent at volume. Its older lines earn about 25 percent. Battery pass-through earns only 2 to 3 percent.[6][8] The company has US$60 million of storage inverters in its pipeline. India has 100 GW of storage tendered but only 10.3 GW built, so pipelines convert slowly.[21] A policy shift on Chinese bidders, a Turkish lira shock, or a shortage of the chips used in inverters would each hit this part of the group first.[28][6]
