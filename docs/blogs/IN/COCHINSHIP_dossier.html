1. What the company does

Cochin Shipyard Limited, called CSL here, builds and repairs ships. Its main yard is at Kochi in Kerala. It is a central public sector company, which means the government owns most of it: the President of India holds 63.33% of the shares, down from 67.91% after a government share sale in July 2026 at a floor price of ₹1,400.[17][18] The group also owns two shipbuilding subsidiaries, Udupi Cochin Shipyard and Hooghly Cochin Shipyard, and runs five ship repair units at Kochi, Mumbai, Kolkata and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.[7][11] At 31 March 2026 it had 4,013 people, of whom 1,882 worked on contract.[7]

CSL earns money in two ways. Shipbuilding is the larger one. A customer signs a contract for one vessel or a series, CSL books revenue as the work moves forward, and the customer pays in stages. Ship repair is the second way. A ship comes in for a refit, a dry-dock stay or an emergency fix, and the job ends in weeks or months. In FY26 shipbuilding brought in ₹3,365.57 crore, or 67% of revenue, and ship repair brought in ₹1,656.30 crore, or 33%.[2] In the June 2026 quarter the mix moved to 64% shipbuilding and 36% repair, because shipbuilding revenue rose 59.5% while repair revenue fell 37.4%.[2]

The two businesses earn very different margins, and the mix drives profit. Management says shipbuilding normally earns a 10% to 12% operating margin and ship repair 20% to 22%, with building near 70% of revenue and repair near 30%, which blends to about 14%.[9] The operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales, where operating profit is what is left after the costs of running the business but before other income, interest and tax. It was 23% in FY24, 19% in FY25 and 16.5% in FY26, and it was about 15% over the last twelve months.[35] Management itself quotes EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), and it says that figure includes interest income.[9] The 14% blended figure matches the 13% to 15% that the segment margins and mix imply, so we read it as a margin before other income.[9]

The FY26 shipbuilding margin of 20.0% is far above the 10% to 12% that management calls normal, and the filing does not say why, so we treat 10% to 12% as the run-rate[2][9].

Who pays, and when

The buyer decides how cash arrives. For Indian Navy and Coast Guard orders, the customer pays milestone advances as the vessel is built, which keeps cash flowing in during construction.[12] European export customers pay differently. The chief executive described the usual terms as 10% on signing, 10% at keel laying, 10% at launch and about 70% at delivery, so only about 30% of the price arrives while the ship is being built.[9] The rating agency adds that advances on export orders are only 20% to 30%, and that CSL has used bank lines since FY26 to fund the gap.[12]

This is why working capital matters so much here. Working capital is the money tied up in running the business day to day: material bought, work in progress and bills not yet paid by customers, less what the company owes suppliers and the advances customers have paid. At 31 March 2026 inventories were ₹2,288.36 crore, other current assets (mostly contract assets and advances) were ₹5,278.11 crore, and other current liabilities (mostly customer advances) were ₹4,958.74 crore.[3] When a large share of the order book is export work paid at delivery, the company can look profitable on paper for years while cash flows out, and then see a large inflow in the delivery quarter.

Where the profit comes from

Profit before tax in FY26 was ₹999.03 crore. Other income was ₹409 crore of it, or 41%.[35][3] The cash flow statement shows ₹167.21 crore of interest income and ₹207.62 crore of provisions that were no longer needed and were released back into profit, against ₹140.54 crore released in FY25.[3] Interest income comes from the ₹2,405.94 crore of cash and bank balances, and the chairman said in September 2026 that the interest stream has shrunk because the company spent about ₹3,000 crore on the new dry dock and the International Ship Repair Facility from its own cash.[3][9] A reader should therefore treat other income and write-backs as useful but not repeatable, and judge the business on operating profit.

Customers and concentration

The unfinished order book was about ₹21,900 crore in September 2026: defence ₹11,900 crore (54%), commercial export ₹7,200 crore (33%), commercial domestic ₹1,600 crore (7%) and ship repair ₹1,200 crore (5.5%).[11] The rating agency counts the Indian Navy at about 57% of the order book in June 2026, down from about 66% a year earlier.[12][13] The export book rests on a few large European customers, such as CMA CGM of France for six LNG-fuelled feeder container ships and Svitzer of Denmark for electric tugs.[25][28] A single late delivery to one of these customers can move a quarter's revenue and cash, which is the concentration risk in this business.

Cycles and policy

Shipbuilding is cyclical. East Asia controls roughly 93% of world capacity and China alone holds over 60% of the commercial order book, so Indian yards win export work only when they can match price and delivery.[7] The government is trying to change that. The September 2025 cabinet package of ₹69,725 crore includes a Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Scheme of ₹24,736 crore running to 2036 and a ₹25,000 crore Maritime Development Fund, and the revised assistance policy pays 15% of the cost for ships below ₹100 crore, 20% above that and up to 25% for green or hybrid ships with enough local content.[19][7] CSL already counts this assistance in the sale price of its vessels, so a change in the rules would show up directly in revenue.[3]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Order book: the value of signed contracts that CSL has not yet recognised as revenue.
  • Milestone billing: payment released when a build stage, such as keel laying or launch, is reached.
  • ISRF: the International Ship Repair Facility at Kochi, a repair yard with a 6,000-tonne ship lift and six workstations.[7]
  • Liquidated damages: a penalty a builder pays when it delivers late.
  • OFS: an offer for sale, the stock exchange route the government used to sell part of its stake.
  • CPSE: a central public sector enterprise, a company the central government controls.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

CSL's strength is physical scale and a protected relationship with the state. It is the largest central public sector shipyard by capacity, and it is the only Indian yard to have built an aircraft carrier, INS Vikrant, a project it won by nomination from the government.[12] Its new dry dock is 310 metres long with a 600-tonne crane and can build or repair Suezmax and Capesize ships and aircraft carriers, and the ISRF can repair up to 82 ships a year.[11] In ship repair it claims about 45% of the Indian market and says it is the only yard that refits aircraft carriers in India.[11] It is also a Category A shipyard, so it can bid for the largest Navy programmes such as the P-17 Bravo frigates.[9]

Credit quality is the second strength. CARE Ratings reaffirmed CARE AAA with a Stable outlook on 12 October 2026 and enhanced one of the bank lines it rates to ₹14,810 crore, and the group also carries an IND AAA rating.[12][7] Its borrowings excluding lease liabilities were ₹1,098.86 crore against equity of ₹5,872.76 crore, a ratio of 0.19, and it held ₹2,405.94 crore of cash and bank balances, so net cash was about ₹1,307 crore at 31 March 2026.[3] The company reports an interest service coverage ratio of 11.81 for FY26 and 8.99 for the June 2026 quarter.[2]

The weaknesses are just as concrete. First, CSL is small next to its closest rival. Mazagon Dock had trailing sales of ₹12,840 crore against CSL's ₹5,047 crore, and its return on capital employed is 36.0% against CSL's 16.2%.[34][35] ROCE (return on capital employed) is profit before interest and tax divided by the capital invested in the business, so it shows how well the yard turns capital into profit. CSL's ROCE fell from 21% in FY24 to 16% in FY26.[35] Second, the company missed its own revenue forecast: it said in August 2025 that FY26 revenue would grow 14% to 15%, and it grew 4.2%.[10][35] Third, two Andaman passenger ferries ordered in 2016 for ₹819 crore are about 55% complete, delivery dates passed in 2023, and building is suspended while the buyer and the company agree changes; the company has provided ₹215.70 crore of liquidated damages and has bank guarantees of ₹1,124.12 crore pending renewal.[2]

Fourth, state ownership cuts both ways. The Government of India chooses the directors, and at 31 March 2026 the board had one independent director against a requirement of six; the stock exchanges fined the company ₹16,22,500 each for the lapse in the period April to December 2025, and a second independent director joined in August 2026, which let the company form its statutory committees.[7] The chairman and managing director post is held on additional charge by the director for finance, who also signs the accounts.[2][11]

Evidence that the moat is real

Three facts support a durable position. The share of Navy orders in the order book fell from about 88% to about 57%, which shows CSL can win commercial work in Europe without losing its defence franchise.[12] CSL has delivered 219 vessels, of which 52 went to foreign owners in places such as Germany, Norway, the Netherlands and the United States.[12] And the two large investments, the new dry dock and the ISRF, were built for about ₹2,770 crore, which the chairman says was spent without any loan, so a competitor would need years and several thousand crore to copy them.[11][9]

Evidence that the moat is narrower than it looks

The same facts have a second reading. Ship repair is the highest-margin business, yet its revenue fell 11.2% in FY26 to ₹1,656.30 crore, and fell 37.4% in the June 2026 quarter.[7][2] Management admits the earlier high margins came from two aircraft carrier refits and from nominated Navy work, and that future defence orders will be won in tenders, which tends to lower margins.[9] Management's own EBITDA figure also counts interest income, and the cash pile that earned it has since been spent on the new docks.[9] When the government raises support for the whole sector, every Indian yard gains, so policy lifts CSL's order flow but does not by itself widen its lead.

What this means for an owner

An owner gets a government-backed yard with a large visible order book and a clean balance sheet, but not a high-return business today: the return on capital is 16%, margins are being reset lower by management's own forecast, and cash conversion is negative. 3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter puts numbers on the story. The headline is that CSL is solvent and has grown over five years, but profit has fallen since its FY25 peak and cash has gone out of the business for three years running. Sales rose from ₹3,191 crore in FY22 to ₹5,022 crore in FY26, which is 12.0% a year over four years, and they were ₹5,047 crore over the last twelve months.[35] Profit after tax was ₹717 crore in FY26 against ₹827 crore in FY25, a fall of 13.3%, and ₹680 crore over the last twelve months, which is 19.1% below the twelve months before.[35]

Three-year growth looks far better than five-year growth because the starting year, FY23, was a low one: sales were only ₹2,365 crore and profit ₹305 crore. From that year the three-year sales growth is 28.5% a year and the profit growth is 33.0% a year.[35] A reader should lean on the longer view: five-year sales growth is 12% and five-year profit growth is 4%.[35]

The last twelve months were hit by one quarter. Q4 FY26 sales of ₹1,484 crore were 15.6% below the ₹1,758 crore of Q4 FY25, and Q1 FY27 profit was ₹151 crore against ₹188 crore.[35][2] The Yahoo field in our ledger that shows profit growth of -19.3% is consistent with these filings: it matches the quarterly profit fall of 19.4% and the twelve-month fall of 19.1%.[1][2][35] The seed figure of a 12% fall in trailing sales that came with this name does not match the filings, which show a 1.4% fall over the last twelve months, because the last four quarters of sales total ₹5,047 crore against ₹5,118 crore for the four before.[35] The other seed figures differ too.
