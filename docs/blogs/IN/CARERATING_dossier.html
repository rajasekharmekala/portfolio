1. What the company does

CARE Ratings is a Mumbai company, founded in 1993, that sells opinions about whether borrowers will repay what they owe. Those opinions are called credit ratings, and the company trades as CareEdge. It is India's second-largest credit rating agency, licensed by SEBI, the market regulator, and accredited by the Reserve Bank of India, the central bank.[2][6] A company that wants a bank loan or wants to sell a bond usually needs a rating first, because banks and bond investors rely on an outside opinion. The borrower pays the agency a fee for that opinion, which is why this model is called issuer-pays.

The fee has two parts. A first rating fee is charged when the opinion is created. A smaller yearly monitoring fee, called a surveillance fee, is charged for as long as the rating stays alive, so each new rating adds income that keeps coming back. Management calls the business an annuity for this reason.[9] The company does not split fees between first ratings and monitoring, and it does not say how much comes from bank loan ratings against bond ratings.[9] That gap matters, because bank loan ratings follow bank credit, which grew 19.1% in the year to August 2026, while bond ratings follow bond sales, which grew only 1.7% in the first half of FY27.[2]

Money comes in through two groups of businesses. The ratings group earned ₹423.05 crore in FY26, which is 89.4% of the ₹473.07 crore the whole company sold. It includes the Indian ratings arm, the African arm in Mauritius and South Africa, a 51% owned Nepal arm, and CareEdge Global, a ratings arm in the GIFT City financial zone that rates countries.[4][2][6] The non-ratings group earned ₹50.01 crore. It sells credit software and consulting through CARE Analytics and Advisory, and sells ESG ratings through CARE ESG Ratings. ESG means environmental, social and governance, a company's record on pollution, people and management quality.[4][6]

The cost structure is mostly people. The company had 608 full-time staff in March 2026, up from 598 a year earlier and 563 in March 2024, and about 83% of them hold professional or postgraduate degrees.[6][7][8] Employee costs took 45.4% of group revenue in FY26 (₹214.67 crore on ₹473.07 crore)[6] and fell to 43.5% in the first half of FY27 from 46.5% a year earlier.[1] Because a rating needs analysts but not factories, the company spends very little on equipment. It also holds a large pile of treasury investments, ₹915.52 crore at March 2026, which earns interest and sits in profit as other income.[6]

Results arrive in a lumpy pattern. Revenue in the September quarter of FY27 was ₹164.13 crore against ₹111.68 crore in the June quarter.[1][3] Management has told investors to judge the company on a full year, not quarter by quarter.[5] We follow that advice in the number tables below.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Credit rating: an opinion on whether a borrower will repay its debt, shown as a letter grade such as AAA (safest) down to D (in default).
  • Surveillance fee: the yearly fee an agency earns for keeping a rating up to date after the first rating is issued.
  • Bank loan rating: a rating on a company's bank borrowings. Banks need it to set how much capital they must hold against the loan.
  • Issuer-pays model: the borrower, not the investor, pays for the rating, which creates a possible conflict of interest that SEBI polices.
  • EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. In this report it is the same as operating profit, which is sales less employee and other running costs, before other income, interest and tax.
  • Operating margin is operating profit divided by revenue, so it shows how many paise of each rupee of sales are left after running costs.
  • Other income: mostly interest the company earns on its cash and investments, not on selling ratings.
  • ROCE is return on capital employed: profit before interest and tax divided by the money tied up in the business. A pile of idle cash counts as capital, so it lowers the ratio.
  • Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by the profit earned per share in a year. A stock at 25 times costs 25 rupees for each rupee of yearly profit.
  • EPS means earnings per share. It is the year's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow, which is the cash left after the company pays for equipment and software.
  • Stage 1 (basing): a price pattern in which a stock has stopped falling and moves sideways while buyers and sellers balance out. Stage 2 is a rising markup, and Stage 4 is a falling markdown.
  • A base is the flat resting zone inside Stage 1. A pivot is the price level where a stock would leave the base, and a breakout is a close above that level on rising volume.
  • RSI is the 14-day relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has moved. A reading of 30 is oversold and 70 is overbought. ATR is the 14-day average true range, the typical daily price swing in rupees.
  • A limit order is an order to buy only at or below a price you set. The entry zone is the price range where we would place such an order.
  • Asymmetry and reward-to-risk are two names for the same ratio: the gain from the entry price to the target, divided by the loss from the entry price to the stop. A ratio of 2.0 means we risk 1 rupee to make 2.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong because borrowers need its product. Banks and bond investors rely on an outside rating, and the Reserve Bank of India's new capital rules for banks, issued on 27 April 2026 and effective from 1 April 2027, tie the risk weights banks use to the ratings of approved agencies.[20] Only eight agencies were registered with SEBI at March 2026, and three of them are listed: CARE, ICRA and CRISIL Ratings.[15] SEBI also requires agencies to monitor and review each rating, which is why surveillance fees recur.[17] These facts help explain a 42% operating margin and a debt-free balance sheet.[12]

The business is weaker than it looks in one place: its share of new listed bond ratings is small. SEBI counts 725 new rated issues of listed securities in 2025-26. CRISIL took 339 of them (46.8%), ICRA 20.1% and India Ratings 19.3%. The chart's legend order and its labels point to CARE at about 4%, behind Acuité at about 6%.[15] We read that 4% from the chart's legend order, since the text of the chapter gives figures only for the top three. Management's own claim is different: a 24% to 25% share of incremental ratings by count and by debt rated, measured across all debt including bank loans, and only for new work in the year.[9] The two numbers use different bases and cannot be set against each other, but they agree on one point. CARE is a strong number two or three in bank loan ratings and a small player in new listed bond ratings.

Quality evidence is mixed. Management says its one-year stability rates, which show how often a rating stays put, are at or above the industry average for the five years to 2026, and that its default record sits inside the benchmark in the new RBI rules.[9] The charts behind those claims did not survive text extraction, so we could not check the numbers. SEBI's own tally shows CARE with three defaults among its rated issuers in 2025-26, all from below investment grade, against four a year earlier, and with 20 downgrades, the highest count along with India Ratings.[15] More downgrades can also mean tougher scrutiny. We read it as neutral.

Competitors and how CARE compares

CRISIL is the larger rival. Its ratings services segment made 29.5% of its revenue and the rest is research and analytics, so it is a different kind of company.[27] ICRA is the closest like-for-like peer. In the September 2026 quarter ICRA's ratings and related services revenue rose 17.3%, against 21% for CARE's ratings group, although ICRA's total revenue rose 30.2% because its risk and analytics arm grew 52.2%.[26][2] India Ratings is not listed, so its profit figures are not public.

On profit, CARE is the faster grower this quarter. CARE's net profit rose 26.5% in the September 2026 quarter.[1] ICRA's rose 9.2%, and CRISIL's rose about 6%, from ₹193 crore to ₹205 crore.[25][13] CARE's 42% operating margin is the highest of the three, against 36% for ICRA and 29% for CRISIL.[12][14][13]

Cyclicality and customers

Rating volumes follow how much companies borrow. Corporate bond sales fell 3.2% in FY26 and bank credit grew 16.1%.[4] Bank loan ratings carried the growth, which is why management stressed the bank side on the May 2026 call.[9] The Reserve Bank raised its policy rate by 0.25 percentage points to 5.50% in October 2026, and management links earlier bond sales to firms borrowing before the expected rate-hike cycle.[2] If borrowers pause, new ratings fall first, yet surveillance fees on 5,200-plus rated entities keep arriving.[9]

Customer concentration is low as far as anyone can tell. CARE does not publish a top-client list. The largest single client fee mentioned in the SEBI order about the former managing director was ₹7.1 crore from DHFL in FY19, when group revenue was about ₹319 crore, or roughly 2.2%.[16][12] That case, set out in Chapter 6, is the reason to keep the governance record in view.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 takes the case against us. A red team is a reviewer whose job is to make the best honest argument that the verdict is wrong. We state the strongest version of each argument, what evidence would prove it, and when that evidence arrives.

Flaw 1: the multiple does the work

Our ₹2,000 target depends on the stock holding 25 times earnings on FY28 profit. At 22 times, ICRA's level, the target is ₹1,751 and the payoff at the ₹1,611.60 close is 0.79 to 1. The company already has the highest margin of the three listed agencies, and a 42% margin is a high base to compound from.[12][14][13] The stock traded between 27 and 45 times earnings over the last 29 months while earnings grew by a fifth or more a year.[22][12] If growth falls to 10%, we assume a 22 times multiple, which is our judgment and not a sourced forecast. Today's 25.1 times is already below the lowest of the 29 monthly readings, 27.0, so the median gives little protection.

Evidence that would prove this right is a second half in which profit grows less than 10%. We will know when the third-quarter results are published. Q3 FY26 came out on 11 February 2026, so we expect early to mid February 2027, but the date is not announced.

Flaw 2: the franchise may be smaller than the headline share

Management says it wins 24% to 25% of incremental ratings.[9] SEBI's chart implies about 4% of fresh ratings on listed debt, against 46.8% for CRISIL.[15] The two bases differ, but management credited a buoyant bank loan market for the FY26 growth, and if bank loan ratings are a price-driven business, fee increases could stop quickly. A bank loan rating is a standard product, and one analyst on the May 2026 call described it as fairly commoditised.[9] The firm will not say what share of revenue comes from bank loans, so investors cannot test this.

Evidence that would prove this right is ratings revenue growth falling below bank credit growth of about 19%. The next two quarterly releases will show it.

Flaw 3: a legal or regulatory event

The company's past is not clean. A 2023 SEBI order found that the former managing director interfered in the DHFL rating and barred him from SEBI-registered firms for two years, though the Supreme Court upheld a tribunal ruling in his favour in March 2026.[16][18] SEBI's ₹1 crore penalty over one client's ratings is under appeal, and the 63 Moons suit, which carries a deposit of 10% of the claim, implies a claim of about ₹231.6 crore and has already tied up ₹23.16 crore in fixed deposits.[6][19] A loss on the claim would cost about 4.7% of the market value, if CARE bore all of it, and it is one of many defendants.[19] The wider risk is trust. Evidence that would prove this right is a Securities Appellate Tribunal ruling against CARE, a new SEBI action, or a court finding in the 63 Moons case. We cannot give dates because none are published.
