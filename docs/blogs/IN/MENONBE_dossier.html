1. What the company does

Menon Bearings Limited trades on the NSE as MENONBE and on the BSE as 523828[20]. It was incorporated in 1991 as part of the Kolhapur-based Menon group, and Nitin Menon is its executive chairman[5]. The group makes three kinds of product. The oldest is the engine bearing, a thin steel shell lined with a softer metal that sits between a spinning shaft and the engine block so that metal does not grind on metal. The company also makes bushes, which are small bearing sleeves, thrust washers and bi-metal strips for heavy-duty engines[5][6]. Its subsidiary Menon Alkop Limited makes aluminium die castings and machined components, and Menon Brakes makes brake lining and brake shoes[5].

Almost all of the money comes from the bearing division. In FY26 the group's revenue from operations was ₹293.81 crore[1]. The company's own split, which counts a small amount of operating income, puts bi-metal products at 72.34 percent, Alkop at 24.57 percent and brakes at 3.09 percent of FY26 sales[7]. By channel, 49.42 percent went to vehicle makers, called original equipment manufacturers or OEMs, 34.14 percent to exports, and the remaining 16.44 percent to replacement parts and other sales[7]. The annual report puts direct exports at ₹97.18 crore in FY26, up from ₹66.87 crore[1].

The customers are heavy-vehicle and tractor engine makers. CRISIL Ratings names John Deere, Tata Motors, International Tractors and Brakes India as key clients, and says the company is the sole or a major supplier of bearings to most of them[5]. On the July 2026 call management said John Deere has bought from it as a single source for more than 20 years, and named Allison Transmission and Magna as bearing customers and Concentric as a route to Tesla[10].

CRISIL says 80 to 90 percent of revenue comes from the auto sector and the rest from oil and gas, electrical and engineering users, and that most of the auto revenue is commercial vehicles and tractors, which are cyclical[5]. Management puts tractors plus heavy and light commercial vehicles at about 50 percent of sales[10]. The company runs five plants in Kolhapur on 27 acres and an office and warehouse in Indianapolis[6].

How the company earns money, and what the price of metal does to it

A bearing is sold on a price list tied to the cost of its metals. CRISIL says raw materials, mainly steel, aluminium scrap and metal sheets, are 45 to 50 percent of cost, and that customer contracts have clauses that adjust selling prices for raw-material moves, with a lag[5]. Management says the lag is one quarter[11]. In July 2026 it said raw material prices had risen about 20 to 25 percent and that margins had still improved, and that carbide tool prices had risen 300 percent and were not reimbursed by customers[10]. Aluminium is the metal that matters for Alkop. Management quoted aluminium rates of 'INR340, INR370', against 'INR250 rupees or INR200' earlier; the unit is not stated and we read it as rupees a kilogram[10]. The next paragraphs say what the group owns to make these goods.

Plants, capacity and what it costs to add more

Bi-metal capacity rose from 486 lakh units in FY25 to 580 lakh units in FY26, a rise of about 19 percent, and brake capacity from 30 lakh to 50 lakh pieces, about 67 percent[6]. Management says the plants can handle about ₹500 crore of group revenue with modular additions, and that new capacity earns an asset turn of about 2.5[10]. It plans ₹25 crore of bi-metal capital spending over two years, paid from internal accruals[11][10]. The capacity numbers matter because the share price needs profit to grow about 13.5 percent a year for ten years, or 15.4 percent from FY26 profit and 22.2 percent from FY26 cash (Chapter 5). If the bearing lines are already near 87 percent use, as management said in the same breath as 80 percent, growth beyond about ₹330 crore depends on the new machines arriving on time.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business is simple. It is a specialist in a narrow field, earns 25.5 percent on its capital, owes little and had a record quarter in June 2026[20][6]. The case against it is also simple. It sells most of its output into two cyclical vehicle markets, collects its bills slowly and trades above its own history. Chapter 2B scores these points. This chapter gives the evidence.

Vehicle demand: strong now, and cyclical

The Society of Indian Automobile Manufacturers reported record second-quarter sales in every main segment for July to September 2026. Commercial vehicles were 3,09,063 units, up 28.7 percent. Medium and heavy trucks, which use the most bearings, were 1,21,171 units, up 37.0 percent. Two-wheelers, where brakes are sold, were 64,32,842 units, up 15.2 percent[24]. Tractors were weaker. Domestic sales fell 18.14 percent in September 2026, and Mahindra said the festive season had moved into October and that September 2025 had been lifted by a tax cut[25][26]. Management says tractors plus heavy and light commercial vehicles are about half of sales, and that its first and second quarters are the cyclical ones[10]. CRISIL says a sharp fall in either market would hurt the credit profile[5].

Electric vehicles: a long-dated risk for the biggest division

The annual report says demand for the company's engine bearings 'remains linked to ICE vehicle production, especially in commercial vehicles, tractors, and two-wheelers', and that electric mobility may gradually affect some traditional components while opening chances in aluminium parts[1]. Management put the current EV share of Alkop at 4 to 5 percent, from three or four customers, including Tata Motors EV models[10]. An engine bearing has no use in a battery-electric truck, which is our own inference. So the bi-metal division, 72.34 percent of sales, is exposed over a long horizon, although trucks, tractors, generators and compressors electrify more slowly than cars. The risk bears on terminal value, which is 68 percent of what the price implies in Chapter 5.

Customers, concentration and the order book

The company says no customer is above 10 to 12 percent of business, and the annual report shows one segment, 'Auto Components', so there is no audited customer table[6][1]. The contracted order book in May 2025 was only ₹23 crore[15]. We cannot test concentration, and new Alkop business takes nine to twelve months to start[10]. We know the names, the long single-source ties, and that CRISIL sees diversity by client but concentration by industry[5]. In July 2026 management listed ₹75 crore of quotation requests, expected to win 75 to 80 percent, and saw ₹65 to ₹75 crore of extra business from its June trip to Canada and the United States[10].

Margins: a step up that we do not treat as permanent

On the company's own basis the EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) margin was 17.4 percent in September 2025, 20.5 percent in December, 25.3 percent in March 2026 and 23.8 percent in June 2026[9][8][6]. Management credits export mix, efficiency and, in brakes, product mix, and says it will 'sustain around 20% to 21% of margins over a period of time'[11][10]. The last four quarters add to revenue of ₹324.15 crore, EBITDA of ₹71.74 crore (22.1 percent) and profit of ₹43.93 crore, or EPS of ₹7.84. The audited margins were about 20.4 percent in FY24, 18.6 percent in FY25 and 21.5 percent in FY26[5][7]. We use 21 percent for the rest of FY27 and 20.5 percent in FY28, and stress it in Chapter 6.

Working capital: customers are paying more slowly

Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and customer bills, less what suppliers are owed. Consolidated trade receivables rose to ₹90.77 crore at 31 March 2026 from ₹60.63 crore a year earlier, up 49.7 percent against revenue growth of 22.8 percent[1]. The ageing table shows ₹72.22 crore under six months old and ₹18.38 crore between six months and a year. A year earlier the older bucket was ₹6.42 crore[1]. Screener, a secondary source, shows debtor days of 111 for FY26 against 91 and 94 before, and a cash conversion cycle of 144 days[20]. CRISIL lists a stretch in the working capital cycle among its downgrade triggers, and management now asks new African merchant exporters for 100 percent advance[5][10]. What this means for you: the profit is real in the accounts but is arriving more slowly as cash. Chapter 2B scores this as the one weak measure.

Governance, ownership and credit quality

The promoter group, the Menon family and Menon United Private Limited, held 66.66 percent at 30 September 2026, down from 68.44 percent at 30 June. Nitin Menon held 27.56 percent and Menon United 24.30 percent[16][17]. A news report of a takeover-code disclosure says three family members sold 9,96,909 shares in the open market, and another says Persistence Capital Fund I bought 8.75 lakh of them at ₹240 on 17 August 2026. Both are secondary sources[22][23]. Arun Aradhye, who is 70, became managing director on 4 March 2026 after serving as whole-time director and chief financial officer. CRISIL reaffirmed BBB+ with a stable outlook and A2 on 23 April 2026 for ₹20.96 crore of bank facilities[5]. What this means for you: the family still controls the company and none of its shares is pledged, but it is selling into a rising price, and a free float of about one-third makes the price sensitive to that supply.
