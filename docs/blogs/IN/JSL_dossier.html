1. What the company does

Jindal Stainless Limited makes stainless steel, which is steel mixed with chromium, and often nickel, so that it does not rust. The company began in 1970 at Hisar in Haryana, and its annual report calls it India's largest and the world's third-largest stainless steel maker[24]. It has 16 plants and processing sites in India, Spain and Indonesia, and its turnover in the year to March 2026 was ₹42,955 crore (about 4.86 billion US dollars)[4]. A crore is ten million, so that is roughly ₹430 billion.

Customers are mostly other manufacturers. Car makers use stainless steel for exhaust parts. Railways and metro builders use it for coaches. Pipe and tube makers turn it into the shiny pipes seen in homes and factories. Lift, appliance and building firms use it too[4]. The company sells flat products (slabs, coils, plates and sheets) and long products (wire rods and rebar), plus blade steel and coin blanks[4].

Revenue is tonnes sold multiplied by the price per tonne. In the quarter to September 2026 the company sold 6,64,378 tonnes for revenue of ₹12,874 crore, or about ₹193.8 thousand a tonne[5][4]. That price moves with nickel and ferrochrome, the two main metals that go into the steel. Management says it does not hedge: 'this is all back-to-back'[12], and in 2026 it said cost increases are passed on to customers, with a lag[8][7]. The profit that matters is therefore operating profit per tonne, which was ₹21.6 thousand in the September quarter[4].

In the September 2026 quarter 89 percent of tonnes went to Indian customers and 11 percent were exported[4]. The mix by steel series was 37 percent in the 200 series, 47 percent in the 300 series and 16 percent in the 400 series[7]. This split matters later, because the surge of cheap Chinese imports that management describes is mainly in the 200 series[7].

The company controls much of its own supply chain. It melts steel in India at Jajpur in Odisha (2.2 million tonnes a year) and Hisar in Haryana (0.80 million)[18]. In March 2026 it started a joint-venture melt shop in Indonesia that can make 1.2 million tonnes a year of slabs, and the company has the right to buy all of them[18][30]. It also owns 49 percent of a nickel pig iron plant in Indonesia, which it bought into for 157 million US dollars to secure nickel[18]. Total melting capacity is now 4.2 million tonnes a year[30].

The promoter group, led by the Jindal family, owned 62.54 percent of the shares at September 2026, up from 60.87 percent at March 2025[1]. Only 0.60 percent of the promoters' shares are pledged to lenders[33].

Where the capacity stands and what is still being built

Melting is ahead of selling. The company can melt 4.2 million tonnes a year, but it sold 2.57 million tonnes in the year to March 2026[30][13]. The gap is being closed from the rolling side. Rolling is the step that turns slabs into coils and sheets. The managing director's letter says cold-rolling capacity will rise from 2.05 million to 2.67 million tonnes a year by FY28, and a new hot-rolling line of 1.1 million tonnes is being readied at Jajpur[23][30]. The CEO told analysts on 23 October 2026 that some cold-rolling mills were being commissioned and that the hot-rolling line should start by the end of this year or early next year[7].

Beyond that sits a proposed coastal plant in Maharashtra. It is planned at 4 million tonnes a year, built in phases, at a cost the press reports as ₹40,000 crore, with the first phase 'in the next four years'[31]. Land has been the hold-up. In May 2026 the company said land buying was 'in full force'. On 23 October 2026 the CEO said it would take 'another quarter or two' before a clear plan[10][7]. The company's target is to sell 3.5 million tonnes a year by FY29, which is about 36 percent above the FY26 level[7][13]. We cover the maths of that target in chapter 2B.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • relative strength index (RSI) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast a share price has moved lately; readings under 30 are called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • Relative strength against the Nifty 50 is different from the RSI. It is the share's return minus the index's return over the same period, so a positive number means the share has beaten the market.
  • ATR stands for average true range, the average size of a stock's daily price swing in rupees; it is used to measure how far the price sits from a level.
  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a profit measure that ignores borrowing costs, tax and the wearing out of equipment.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share, which is the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • free cash flow is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment.
  • FCF is the short form of free cash flow, as defined in the line above.
  • operating margin is the profit left from each rupee of sales after the costs of running the business, before interest and tax.
  • debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' money; below 0.7 is a comfortable level.
  • net debt is borrowings minus the cash a company holds.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share; it tells you how many years of current profit you pay for up front.
  • guidance is a forecast that the company itself publishes for a coming period.
  • Stage 2 is the Weinstein stage of a confirmed uptrend, and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend; Stage 1 is a flat floor after a decline.
  • A base is a flat stretch of trading, often several months long, that a price builds before its next big move; its pivot is the top of that stretch.
  • A breakout is a move above the pivot on strong trading volume, and a retest is the pullback that comes back to check the pivot from above.
  • A stop loss is the price at which we agree the thesis is wrong and we sell; a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better.
  • A reverse DCF works backwards from today's share price to the yearly profit growth that the price needs; the discount rate is the yearly return an investor demands, which is used to shrink future cash to today's value.
  • The entry zone is the band of prices where we would be willing to buy, and asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A good test of a steel maker's position is whether it earns the same profit per tonne when raw materials swing. Jindal Stainless has done that. The rating agency CARE says the company has kept operating profit near ₹20,000 per tonne in each of the last five years, despite swings in nickel and chromium prices and the threat from imports[18]. In the year to March 2026 we calculate ₹21,669 per tonne (₹5,560 crore of operating profit on 25,65,902 tonnes)[11][13]. The company is also large. CARE calls it India's largest stainless maker with a leading position in the domestic and international market[18].

The balance sheet adds strength. CARE raised the long-term rating from AA to AA+ on 25 August 2026 and reaffirmed AA+ on 5 October[19][20][18]. It points to debt falling in recent years even while the company built plants and bought Chromeni Steels for ₹1,618 crore[18].

Where the strength is

Integration is the first strength. The Indonesian nickel plant and melt shop cut the company's dependence on bought-in nickel and give it slabs it can roll in India[18]. Spreading across series is the second. The company has more than 120 grades[22], so it can shift output to whichever product earns most. In Q1 FY27, when gas shortages hit, it leaned on thinner and higher-value products and still grew profit[8].

Scale in a small market is the third. The annual report's source puts India's stainless market at 5.8 billion US dollars in 2025 and 12.1 billion by 2034, and gives the growth rate as 8.1 percent a year[22]. A leader with 4.2 million tonnes of melting has first claim on that growth. The fourth is the brand and channel work: a loyalty scheme with more than 1.3 lakh members, of which 19,000-plus are retailers and 1.13 lakh-plus are fabricators[4]. The company says the scheme helps buyers tell genuine company steel from counterfeits[23].

Where the business is weak

The company is a price taker on its main raw materials. In the September 2026 quarter nickel averaged 16,573 US dollars a tonne, up 10.4 percent on a year earlier, and ferrochrome rose 13.1 percent[4]. That is why revenue grew 18.2 percent while tonnes grew only 2.5 percent[5]. Most of the revenue growth is dearer metal passed through, not more steel sold. Profit per tonne was ₹21.6 thousand against ₹21.4 thousand a year ago, so profit per tonne held, but the operating margin on sales fell from 12.7 percent to 11.1 percent (our division of ₹1,388 crore by ₹10,893 crore and ₹1,433 crore by ₹12,874 crore)[4].

Imports are the second weakness. Management says Chinese flat-product imports in July 2026 were 2.9 times the FY26 monthly average, and the first four months of FY27 equal about 63 percent of the whole of FY26's Chinese imports[5]. Chinese customs data show 54.6 thousand tonnes sent to India in June, a 29-month high, with first-half exports to India up 104.5 percent[28]. This follows a year when imports were falling: BigMint estimated finished flat imports for FY26 at 0.85 million tonnes against 1.17 million in FY25[29]. The annual report also says imports from China and Vietnam, often routed through ASEAN countries, kept domestic pricing under pressure in FY26[21][23]. The rise came after the April 2026 exemption from the quality-control order. The end date of that exemption is disputed. A trade site reads the order as covering bills of lading dated up to 26 October 2026[27]. The CEO describes the quality rule as suspended to 31 March[7]. We treat the position as uncertain and do not rely on it for the Layer 2 score in chapter 2B.

Returns on capital are the third. The company earned a return on capital employed of 44 percent in FY22, 22 percent in FY24, 18 percent in FY25 and 19 percent in FY26[1]. FY22 was a price spike, so 44 percent is not a normal level. But the slide from FY24 to FY25 shows that the business earns less when imports press on prices. Fourth, execution risk: the Maharashtra land delay and the slow start of Indonesian sales (chapter 2B) show that building capacity is not the same as selling steel.

What this means for an owner

The business is good and the owner has a real asset: a leading position, low debt and steady profit per tonne. But the profit pool is shared with imports the government has so far chosen not to block, and the next two quarters decide how much of the new capacity turns into sold steel. The next chapter checks, with five measures that suit a stainless steel maker, how each of these strengths and weaknesses is showing up in the numbers.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate rests on an assumption that can fail. This chapter names the weakest one under each estimate, says what evidence would show it is wrong, and says when we will know. It also gives the strongest case against our own conclusion, in two directions: that the value is too generous, and that the rejection is too harsh.

Earnings estimate: volume growth of 10 percent in FY28

The weakest assumption in the FY28E estimate is volume. We assume 10 percent growth on 2,668 thousand tonnes. That needs the new cold-rolling lines to be commissioned, the hot-rolling line to start by about early 2027, and Indonesian slabs to be sold through Indian mills. The CEO said on 23 October 2026 that some cold-rolling mills were being commissioned and the hot-rolling line should start by the end of this year or early next[7]. In H1 FY27 tonnes fell 2.3 percent[5]. To meet even the low end of the FY27 plan, the second half has to run at about 1,423 thousand tonnes, or about 10 percent above the 1,292 thousand of H2 FY26 (650 plus 642 thousand, our addition)[4].

Evidence that would show the assumption wrong: Q3 FY27 tonnes below about 650 thousand, which is no growth on the 650 thousand of a year ago. Evidence that would support it: tonnes of 700 thousand or more with profit per tonne of ₹20,000 or more. The Q3 FY27 result is likely in late January 2027; the company has not announced the date, and its last two third-quarter calls were in January.

Value estimate: a multiple of 19 times

The multiple is a judgement, not a measurement. Today's 17.54 times is 18 percent below the 3-year median of 21.3 times, but that median was set when the company's return on capital employed was 22 percent and profit was growing faster[2][1]. Return on capital is now 19 percent, estimates for next year are being cut, and free cash flow is close to zero. A market that pays 17 times, as ICICI Securities does[14], would give ₹769 on our FY28E.

Evidence that would show the 19 times too high: a Q3 FY27 result where profit per tonne is below ₹20,000 or an anti-dumping finding that excludes the grades the company sells. Evidence that would support it: a duty recommendation by the DGTR with a deadline of 28 March 2027, and the quality rule coming back. Management says it expects a decision by 31 December 2026, though the formal deadline is 28 March 2027[7][26].

Red-team bear case: why our value may be too generous

The strongest case against our value starts with cash. Free cash flow was ₹755 crore in FY26, and about zero over the last twelve months in the ledger[1][34]. The company plans ₹2,400 to 2,600 crore of spending a year[7], and plants under construction have grown to ₹2,887 crore[1]. A business that spends everything it earns, at a 19 percent return and falling, does not deserve the median multiple of a business that does not. The Indonesian nickel plant is volatile by management's own account[7], and the quarter's ₹116 crore exceptional gain flatters profit.

There is also exposure that the headline numbers hide. The 200 series, at 37 percent of tonnes, faces the import surge[7]. Europe's duty-free quota fell from about 12,000 tonnes to 5,500 or 6,000[7]. The CEO also said related-party deals with a promoter-group blast furnace are expected, at arm's length[7]. Valued on bear-case earnings of ₹35.08 at 14 times, the share would be worth ₹491, a 30.8 percent fall.

Red-team case against the rejection itself: why we may be too harsh

The best argument against REJECTED is that the share trades at 17.54 times earnings with a Street mean target 21 percent above the price (₹858 against ₹709.80)[16], and that the chart rule is mechanical. A bounce from the ₹667 floor would pass Test C in one session. A fair challenge is that we are late if the chart turns on 28 October.

The CEO describes it as suspended to 31 March 2027; the trade site reads the April order as ending on 26 October for new shipments; and a June 2026 gazette order defers some entries to 31 March 2027. We could not read the ministry's own listing, so we treat the position as uncertain.
