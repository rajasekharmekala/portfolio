1. What the company does

Vedant Fashions designs, sources and sells clothes people wear to weddings and family celebrations in India. The men's brand Manyavar started in 1999, the women's brand Mohey in 2015, the premium brand Twamev in 2019, and the value-priced Diwas in 2024. The company also owns Mebaz, a South Indian brand it bought in 2017. The company was set up in Kolkata by the Modi family in 2002 and listed on the stock exchanges in February 2022.[1][12] Think of it as the one national name a family walks into when the wedding date is fixed and every relative needs an outfit.

The customer pays the shelf price, which the company calls the retail selling price. Most stores are run by franchise partners who own the shop fit-out and hire the staff. Vedant Fashions sells them the clothes. Its own reported revenue is the shelf price, minus the GST tax, minus the partner's share. Management describes two partner models, one with an 18% margin and one with 29.5%, both plus GST.[4] The result is that reported revenue is only about 71% of what customers spend. In FY26, customers spent ₹2,008 crore in these stores, while the company reported revenue of ₹1,436 crore.[3][1]

The company does not borrow, but it does pay rent. It signs most of the store leases itself.[4] Under the accounting rule Ind AS 116, rent on leased shops is not shown as an operating cost. It is shown as depreciation on a right-of-use asset and as interest on a lease liability. At March 2026 the asset stood at ₹422 crore and the liability at ₹458 crore, and FY26 lease interest was ₹44 crore.[3][9] In FY26 those lease payments were ₹192 crore, or 13.4% of revenue.[9] So the headline operating margin of 44.3% is flattering. This margin means operating profit as a share of sales, and the company measures it as EBITDA, which stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, divided by revenue. After subtracting the lease payments, our calculation gives about 31%. The rating agency Crisil reports about 30%.[12]

The network is large and still mostly in one format. At March 2026 the company had 669 exclusive brand outlets, the stores that sell only its own brands, in 252 cities, covering 1.79 million square feet.[3] From June 2026 it reports domestic stores separately: 501 outlets in 205 cities, plus 136 shop-in-shops (small counters inside other retailers) and 14 international stores.[1] Multi-brand dealers, shop-in-shops and online channels together are only about 5% of revenue today.[2] The year has a season. The wedding months from November to March carry most of the business, while the July-to-September quarter is the quiet one.[2]

The cash pile and what it earns

Vedant Fashions holds ₹1,225 crore in mutual funds, bonds and bank balances, which is 62% of its shareholders' equity of ₹1,964 crore.[3] That money earned ₹85 crore of interest and investment gains before tax in FY26, which is 17% of pre-tax profit.[9] A reader who wants the clothing business alone should strip that income out. The company does this in its own return measure, which we use in Chapter 3.[1]

The company pays out about half its profit each year. Dividends paid were ₹194 crore in FY26 against profit of ₹376 crore, and the final dividend for FY26 was ₹7.75 a share, with the ex-dividend date on 21 September 2026.[9][28] The company has no subsidiary, associate or joint venture. The only subsidiary it ever had, Manyavar Creations Private Limited, was merged into the parent in November 2024.[9][10] We looked for an entity called Eternal Wear in the filings we fetched and found none, so Mohey, Twamev, Mebaz and Diwas are brands inside the one company, not separate investments.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Retail sales (sales of our customers): what shoppers pay at the shelf price across all channels. The company's revenue is lower because it excludes GST and the franchise partner's share.
  • EBO, SIS, MBO: an exclusive brand outlet sells only Vedant's brands; a shop-in-shop is a counter inside another store; a multi-brand outlet is an independent dealer.
  • Ind AS 116: the accounting rule that moves store rent out of operating cost and into depreciation and interest, which lifts reported operating profit.
  • Same-store sales growth (SSSG): the change in sales from stores that were open in both periods, which strips out the effect of opening or closing shops.
  • Reward-to-risk: the gain to our target divided by the loss to our stop. A ratio of 2.0 means we stand to make twice what we risk.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The first strength is brand and pricing. Manyavar sells at one price online and offline and runs no end-of-season sale.[1] That policy only works when shoppers accept the price, and the numbers say they largely do. Gross margin means the share of each rupee of sales left after the cost of the clothes. It was 65.7% in FY26 and 65.7% in Q1 FY27.[3][1] The earlier level was 67.2% to 67.4% in FY23 to FY25. The slip came mainly from a GST change in September 2025. The tax on about 90% of its products rose from 12% to 18%, and the company did not raise shelf prices to match.[6] The premium brand Twamev grew about 40% in Q3 FY26, with same-store growth of 12%.[6]

The second strength is reach. The network is 669 exclusive stores in 252 cities, and Crisil cites the large retail footprint as the foundation of a leading market position.[3][12] The company keeps a design pipeline of roughly 2.3 to 2.4 times the number of new designs it launched before, refills stores automatically by pin code, and trains shop staff by phone every morning.[8][1] Inventory is lean at 34 days on a trailing basis, which matters because unsold celebration clothes are hard to discount.[2]

The weakness is growth. Crisil notes that sales stayed in a range of ₹1,360 to ₹1,440 crore over the three years to FY26, after growing 13% a year until FY23.[12] Same-store sales were negative in the two years before FY26 and rose only about 3% in FY26.[12] Management gave four reasons in May 2025: weak spending by mid-premium shoppers, a poor market in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, a flood of new competitor stores, and fewer wedding dates.[8] It also said that without Andhra Pradesh and Telangana the same-store figure for FY25 was flat.[8] Add the GST step in FY26 and rising rents, and the profit line fell even as sales edged up.

Competition is real but may be thinning. Management says the number of stores selling men's Indian wear may have tripled in two to three years, yet no rival has taken a large share.[8] In July 2026 it said its own survey of eight top states shows net store closures by other players, many of them large stores.[2] That is management's reading, not an independent count, so we treat it as a lead to verify through two or three more quarters. Management itself said it wants to see 'another 2 to 3 quarters' before drawing a conclusion.[2]

Store economics for a franchise partner

The partner's return decides whether the network can grow. Management says a store needs yearly sales of about ₹7,000 per square foot in a Tier 3 town, ₹8,500 in Tier 2 and ₹10,000 to ₹10,500 in Tier 1 to give the partner a good return, and that fit-out costs ₹2,100 to ₹2,150 per square foot in smaller towns and ₹2,500 in the big cities.[2] An average shop is about 3,000 square feet.[2] Our arithmetic: a 3,000-square-foot Tier 2 store costs the partner roughly ₹63 lakh to fit out and needs about ₹2.55 crore of yearly sales to clear the bar. Across the network, FY26 retail sales of ₹2,008 crore on 1.79 million square feet works out to about ₹11,200 per square foot, or about ₹10,600 if we remove the roughly 5% sold through other channels. That sits around the Tier 1 line, so the average store clears the bar without much room.[3][2]

The company does not publish a payback period or return figure for partners, and we found no source that does. Management says only that partners have earned 'a great ROI' and that partner willingness is not the limit to growth. The limit it names is rent: it wants each new store to be sustainable for 12 to 15 years at today's rent levels.

Ownership, rating and governance

Promoters hold 74.94% and none of their shares is pledged, according to the September 2026 filing.[13] Crisil reaffirmed its AA/Stable rating on 2 July 2026, which carries nil reliance on external debt and interest cover of 11.2 times.[12] It names a fall in the rent-adjusted operating margin to below 25% as a reason to cut the rating.[12] Two governance facts stand out. The chief financial officer, Rahul Murarka, resigned on 15 September 2026 and leaves on 14 November 2026, and we found no successor named in the filings listed to 26 October.[21] The company also disclosed a GST order-in-appeal dated 28 August 2026 and a Legal Metrology compounding order, with no amount stated on the pages we fetched.[21]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record is mixed. In May 2025 it said the answer to slow growth was more designs, faster digital campaigns and better store training, and that the 'current need of the business' was like-to-like growth.[8] It then delivered a same-store gain of 2.7% for FY26 and 4.6% in Q4, but also a fall of 4.5% in Q3.[3][5] In February 2026 it said the GST hit would 'normalize to a large extent'.[6] Gross margin did recover from 65.0% to 65.7% between Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27, but it is still below the level a year earlier, and the GST change will sit in the comparison until Q3 FY27.[2]

On cash, management delivered. Cash conversion, measured as operating cash flow to profit excluding finance income, rose from 69% in FY25 to 98% in FY26 and 101% for the twelve months to June 2026.[3][2] On capital allocation the company pays out about half its profit, spends little on capital (₹12 crore in FY26), makes no acquisitions and does not buy back shares, so the treasury portfolio keeps growing from ₹1,068 crore to ₹1,225 crore in one year.[3][9] That is safe. It also drags the return on equity down to 20.03% in FY26 from 34.12% in FY23.[9][11]

Management gave no number for FY27. In May 2026 it declined to give a particular guidance on same-store growth. Guidance means the company's own forecast of its coming results.[4] In July 2026 an analyst asked whether high-single-digit same-store growth for the remaining nine months was doable. Management replied that it was a fair understanding.[2] The same call warned that September and October might be 'slightly more difficult'.
