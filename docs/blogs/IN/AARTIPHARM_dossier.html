1. What the company does

Aarti Pharmalabs Limited is a chemical maker that sells to other drug and drink makers. It runs seven manufacturing units and three research centres, and it employs more than 2,400 people[11]. The company was part of Aarti Industries until a court-approved demerger, sanctioned on 21 September 2022, made it a separate listed business[24]. The Gogri family, led by Chairman Rashesh Gogri, still runs it, and the board has approved his move to managing director from 1 October 2026, subject to shareholder approval[26]. Its plants sit in Tarapur, Dombivli and Vapi, with a newer site at Atali in Gujarat[11].

The business has three lines, and they behave very differently. The first is xanthine derivatives: caffeine, theophylline and related chemicals that go into cola and energy drinks, asthma medicines and supplements. This line was 57 percent of June 2026 quarter sales, 74 percent of its volume went to beverage customers and 79 percent of its sales were exports[12][11]. The second is generic active pharmaceutical ingredients (the chemicals that do the work in a pill) and the intermediates used to make them. That was 30 percent of sales[11]. The third is contract development and manufacturing (CDMO), where a drug company hires Aarti to design a manufacturing route and then make the ingredient for it. It was 7 percent of June 2026 sales but is the line the company wants to grow most[11][12].

Money comes in as a price per kilogram multiplied by volume. In xanthines the price follows raw-material costs, so large customers have pass-through contracts and the remaining sales are priced on the spot market[12]. In June 2026 the company said its raw-material costs had risen 50 to 100 percent after the West Asia conflict, and it passed that on to customers, which lifted both sales and margin[12]. In generic ingredients the price is set by competition and falls over time, which is why management says it must keep adding new molecules just to stand still[12]. In CDMO the price is set by a contract and the work is lumpy: the company said revenue will again arrive mostly in the second half of the year[12]. Management guides to an EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) margin of 22 to 25 percent of standalone sales for FY27[12].

Accounting makes the group's size hard to compare across years. From 1 April 2025 its 50 percent stake in Ganesh Polychem Limited is treated as a joint venture, so that company's sales no longer appear in group sales and only its profit share does[9]. That is why consolidated sales fell from ₹2,115 crore in FY25 to ₹1,819 crore in FY26 even though the standalone company, which excludes the joint venture, grew from ₹1,771 crore to ₹1,798 crore[23][14][9]. Readers should compare standalone numbers across that break and consolidated numbers only within the same basis.

The three lines in more detail

Xanthines are made by a few scaled producers worldwide, and China holds most of the capacity. The chairman said that, outside a few makers in India and Europe, 80 to 90 percent of the competition is in China[12]. The global market is about US$1,593 million in 2025 and is forecast to reach US$2,456 million by 2031, a 7.5 percent yearly rise[7]. Aarti says its xanthine chain is backward integrated and does not depend on Chinese supply, which buyers value more since supply shocks[7]. That claim is partly offset by Crisil's finding that 25 to 30 percent of the group's raw materials are imported from China[23].

The generic ingredient line is a long list of mature molecules. The chairman said the top ten products make 60 to 70 percent of this line's sales and hold between 10 and 50 percent global share[12]. Pricing pressure on existing molecules continues, so the company has started a process-cost project and is developing 11 new molecules[12][11]. Gross margin here is about 45 to 50 percent, below the company average[12].

The CDMO line grew about ninefold in four years, from ₹32 crore in FY22 to ₹276 crore in FY26, and management guides to 40 to 50 percent growth in FY27 and a ₹1,000 crore target around FY29 or FY30[7][12]. It built a 440 kilolitre multipurpose block at Atali in Gujarat and approved a second, dedicated 405 kilolitre block there for ₹149 crore, to be financed from internal cash and borrowings[26][11].

Who owns it and how it is financed

At 30 September 2026 the promoter group held 39.66 percent, down from 46.46 percent in March 2024[3]. Public holders owned 43.78 percent, foreign portfolio investors 8.16 percent and domestic institutions 8.40 percent[3]. The group is financed by bank debt rated AA-/Stable by Crisil, which in October 2026 raised the rated bank limit from ₹700 crore to ₹1,000 crore[23]. Borrowings rose from ₹413 crore in March 2025 to ₹754 crore in March 2026 as the company spent on new plants[2].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Aarti Pharmalabs is a good manufacturer with a weaker year behind it and a strong quarter just reported. The strengths are real. It is one of the largest xanthine makers in the world, its plants carry US, European and other regulatory approvals, its CDMO line is growing quickly and its bank rating is AA-[11][23]. The weaknesses are also real. Return on capital employed (ROCE, profit before interest and tax as a share of the capital in the business) fell from 18.74 percent in FY25 to 11.46 percent in FY26 on the company's own measure[5]. Profit fell 36 percent in FY26, from ₹272 crore to ₹175 crore[1].

The competitive position rests on four things. Capacity: xanthine capacity has gone from 5,000 to 9,600 tonnes a year, which the chairman says will make it the second-largest producer in the world by capacity, although the investor presentation still says third-largest[11][12]. Approvals: three plants are approved by the US drug regulator, and the company holds 60 US drug master files and 44 European certificates[11]. Integration: the chairman describes xanthines as fully backward integrated[7]. Policy: China removed export tax rebates on xanthines from April 2026, and the chairman called that a lasting change for at least the next few years[12].

The competitive position is not wide enough to protect margins on its own. In generic ingredients the company says competitive pressure continues and that Middle East costs were hard to pass on in the intermediates business[14]. In xanthines the chairman splits the recent price rise 50-50 between lasting and temporary[12]. And the chairman said raw-material prices rose 50 to 100 percent after the West Asia conflict and had eased to 25 to 50 percent above normal by July[12]. A business whose margin moves with input prices has a moat in volume and approvals, not in pricing.

Where the strength is

Volume and capacity are the clearest strengths. Xanthine volume grew 25 percent in the June 2026 quarter on the year, and the company produced about 6,000 tonnes before the new capacity started, so there is room to grow volume without new building[12]. Management expects 80 percent capacity use by FY28[12]. CDMO is the second strength: 37 projects are already in commercial supply, up from 21 three years earlier, and nearly all FY26 CDMO revenue came from late-stage and commercial molecules[12][14].

The balance sheet is a third strength. Debt to equity (borrowings divided by shareholders' funds) is 0.36 at March 2026 on our working, and the annual report puts net debt, which is borrowings minus cash and liquid investments, at 0.35 of equity[2][5]. Crisil measures gearing at 0.38 times with interest cover of 8.25 times[23]. Crisil expects cash accruals of ₹300 to ₹330 crore against term debt repayment of ₹50 to ₹55 crore[23]. That is enough to fund the planned spending without stress, even though debt has risen.

Where the weakness is

Cash tied up in stock and customer bills is the main weakness. Inventory days rose from 199 to 280 and the cash conversion cycle (days to turn spending into collected cash) rose from 204 to 251 between FY25 and FY26[2]. Crisil says gross current assets were 278 days of sales in March 2026 against 219 a year earlier[23]. Free cash flow (FCF), which is cash from operations minus capital spending, was negative ₹186 crore in FY26 and negative ₹114 crore in FY25[2]. The company funded the gap with borrowing, so debt rose by ₹341 crore in one year[2].

Earnings are volatile. Consolidated profit was ₹217 crore in FY24, ₹272 crore in FY25 and ₹175 crore in FY26, and quarterly profit per share ranged from ₹3.08 to ₹9.75 over two years[1]. FY26 also carried a ₹33 crore net foreign-exchange loss, which Crisil links mainly to revaluing a foreign-currency term loan[14][23]. The annual report separately shows a ₹29.73 crore fair-value charge on a currency contract, and the filings do not say how much of the two figures overlap[6]. A business with this much variation deserves a lower multiple than the steady compounders it is often compared with.

The pricing window in xanthines

The June 2026 quarter shows how much price can matter. Gross margin touched almost 56 percent, an analyst said on the call, against the 50 percent the chairman called normal for the company[12]. The chairman answered that the quarter was exceptional on sales, that realisation per kilogram will fall in the next quarter, and that prices had already come down from the peak[12]. He guided FY27 xanthine sales to ₹900 to ₹1,100 crore and later said it could land at ₹900 to ₹1,000 crore, and the company had booked about ₹305 crore in the first quarter[12][11]. On the lower range, ₹900 to ₹1,000 crore, that leaves about ₹198 to ₹232 crore a quarter for the other three quarters, which is 24 to 35 percent below the June quarter run rate. The stock's recent rise therefore rests partly on a quarter that management itself does not expect to repeat.

Governance, regulatory and legal flags

We checked three items flagged in the watchlist seed text: a Form 483, a promoter pledge and a stamp duty dispute. All three are real, and none alone breaches a red line. First, the US drug regulator inspected the Tarapur Unit-IV plant, and the inspection ended on 27 March 2026 with a Form 483 carrying one observation, which the company called procedural[15][16]. The company said it would send its corrective plan in the allowed time[15]. We found no later disclosure of the regulator's classification, so the outcome is open. Unit-IV is the company's US-approved plant for generic and CDMO ingredients, so a worse finding would matter[11].

Second, promoters pledged 4,307,013 shares, which is 11.98 percent of their holding of 35,964,425 shares at September 2026[19]. The pledge was created on 25 March 2026 by Mirik Gogri, who pledged all 2,784,094 of his shares against a ₹75 crore loan, and by Renil Gogri, who pledged 1,519,919 shares[17][18]. At the same time the promoter group sold a net 29,02,975 shares between September 2025 and September 2026, cutting its stake from 42.88 percent to 39.66 percent[20][21].

Third, the Gujarat stamp authority ordered the company on 29 August 2026 to pay ₹59.50 crore in stamp duty and penalties on the 2022 demerger, made up of ₹25 crore duty, a penalty equal to the shortfall of ₹25 crore and a further penalty of ₹9.50 crore[24][25]. The company filed a writ petition in the Gujarat High Court and says it has good grounds[24][25]. The amount equals about 28 percent of trailing profit of ₹213 crore and is not in the March 2026 contingent liabilities, which list only ₹7.94 crore of bank guarantees[8][1]. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record is mixed. In May 2025 it guided the standalone EBITDA to grow 12 to 15 percent in FY26 on the higher FY25 level[13]. Standalone EBITDA instead fell from ₹4,265 million to ₹4,061 million, a 4.8 percent drop, as higher freight, energy and solvent costs and early costs of the Atali site cut margin[9][23]. That is the largest miss in the record. Profit after tax fell from ₹257 crore to ₹176 crore on a standalone basis[14].

On the items that management controls directly, delivery was better. CDMO revenue growth of 32.7 percent was inside the 30 to 40 percent guided range[13][11]. Capital spending came in at about ₹400 crore by management's count, and ₹448 crore in the cash flow statement once intangibles are included, against ₹400 to ₹450 crore guided[13][14][4]. The xanthine block was commissioned by the end of the June 2026 quarter against a guide of Q1 FY27[13][12]. The Atali site was late: in May 2025 management expected full ramp-up by the end of FY26, and in May 2026 it said the site was past start-up issues and that phase 1 would be fully operational by the end of the June quarter[13][14].

We reject it as a base case because the chairman himself said the quarter was exceptional, and because the return on capital, debt and cash cycle do not yet show the business earning its growth capital.

The pledge adds a second, smaller counter-argument. Mirik Gogri's loan carries a security cover of 2.37 at the ₹639 share value of the date, so the pledged shares would still cover the ₹75 crore loan down to about ₹270 a share[17]. A forced sale is therefore a tail risk, not a central one. The concern is not margin calls. It is that promoters are borrowing against shares and selling shares while the company funds growth with debt.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is REJECTED. The rule is the Layer 1 archetype test in our scoring file. It says a candidate must fit one of three archetypes[33]. This stock fits none. The compounder needs return on capital of 15 percent, and the company earns 10.7 percent. The turnaround route needs two improving trend signals and a discounted price. The company has one signal and trades at a 29 percent premium to its own median multiple. The tactical route needs a Stage 2 uptrend, and the stock is in Stage 3[3][32]. No hard red line is breached today. So this is a rejection of the setup, not a flag of distress.

The chart does not cause the rejection. Our rules send a Stage 3 pullback above a rising 200-day average to a wait posture[33]. Had the business tests passed, the 0.95 ATR distance to the ₹782.00 support would map to the STANDARD band. The verdict would then be ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP. The tests did not pass. The reasons are return on capital, price and trajectory.

We keep an entry zone of ₹745.00 to ₹770.00. This is the band of prices where buying is planned if the verdict changes. The zone lies between the 20-day swing low of ₹782.00 and the 200-day average of ₹744.59. The stop is ₹741.06. At ₹770 the reward to risk against our central value of ₹861.50 is 3.16 to 1. This is not a live order. It marks where the arithmetic would clear the floor if the Layer 1 conditions change. The stop is only ₹28.94 below the top of the zone. A stock with a daily swing of ₹40.94 could hit that stop on ordinary noise. That is one more reason to wait until the Layer 1 conditions are met. Stop clearance is only 0.71 ATR at ₹770 and 0.10 ATR at ₹745. At any future re-underwriting we must re-derive support, ATR, stop, target and size together before the zone is executable. The frozen levels must not be reused as they stand.

One more heading follows, and it needs a short introduction here. The next heading, Layer 3, covers how to enter and how large to buy. It applies only after the business tests pass, so here every size is zero. The table has seven rows. The first two repeat the archetype and the verdict. The entry zone and stop are the hypothetical levels above. The targets give the gain side of reward-to-risk. The sizing row turns a stop distance into a position size. The last row, let-winners-run, is not applicable: not held. Sizing uses four terms. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent. The risk budget is the share of the portfolio we accept losing if the stop is hit, counted in basis points. A tier is the conviction grade from TIER_1 to TIER_4, and TIER_0 means no live order. Portfolio heat is the total risk budget across all open positions. A TIER_3 position carries a 50 basis point risk budget. A stop distance of 3.76 percent from ₹770 would allow about 13.3 percent. The tier cap of 3.5 percent would bind first. With no live order the tier is TIER_0 and the weight is 0 percent[33].
