1. What the company does & day-to-day vertical operations

Interarch began in 1983 with suspended metal ceilings. It added engineered roofing and cladding in 1993. It later became a turnkey provider of pre-engineered steel buildings. Today it joins structural design, factory work, shipping, site management and erection. Customers buy a finished frame or building envelope by a set date. They do not buy only tonnes of fabricated steel. The company overview describes this model and its project work on site.

An order starts with customer needs: footprint, span, height, crane loads, wind, seismic forces, process needs, site access and delivery dates. Sales and engineers set scope, loads, cost and timing. They detail main frames, secondary members, joints and interfaces. After the customer approves drawings and terms, purchasing and production reserve steel and coating materials. A delivery plan guides the work. Sales are won before production starts. Margin then depends on accurate bids and fewer design changes, cost overruns, rework and site delays.

At the plant, workers cut, drill, form and weld steel when needed. They inspect it and finish the main and secondary frames. The bill of materials may also include roof sheets, wall cladding, insulation, trims, gutters and fasteners. Some parts may be made in-house and others bought. Interarch says its PEB parts are designed and made at its plants. It says no cutting or welding should be needed at the customer's site; bolted joints are used there. This is a company description, not an outside site audit. Factory-made parts can reduce site work. They also make drawing control, quality and dispatch timing more important. See the PEB systems page and the PEB product guide.

Logistics is part of the product. Frames, purlins, sheets and accessories must arrive in the right order. This lets erectors lift and assemble them without blocking the work area. Large jobs may have many bays, cranes, platforms and other trades on site. Interarch's EPC materials describe project control, site coordination, safety and quality teams. These services may set it apart from a basic steel fabricator. They also need cash. Engineering and materials may be paid for before billing milestones and customer approval.

On site, crews set columns and frames. They check alignment, add bracing and install roof and wall systems. They also coordinate with civil, electrical, heating and cooling, and process contractors. The turnkey contract may include project management, installation and erection. Each contract sets the exact scope. Testing, documents and customer approval can affect final bills and retention cash. The EPC page explains this work. The contract still needs a job-by-job review.

Interarch earns revenue through a project system. Orders must pass design, plant, steel supply, dispatch and erection. Gross margin depends on steel cost and price terms. It also depends on waste, outside work, freight and site labour. A full order book helps only when jobs finish at fair margins and customers pay. In a May 2026 call, management said large jobs collect at work stages. It also said receivables rise with job size. This may explain some cash use. The audited FY26 cash-flow statement shows that the cash cost was real.

Interarch sells more than PEB frames. It sells TRACDEK roof and wall systems and TRAC ceilings. It also targets industrial, commercial and some infrastructure jobs. These lines share steel work, design skills and customer channels. But the public data do not show each line's sales, margin or cash needs. The company began with ceilings, then added roofing and cladding before PEB. This shows a range of skills. It does not prove a separate profit stream for every product.

The building products around the structural frame

TRACDEK covers roof sheets, wall cladding, standing-seam systems, trims and related parts. TRAC ceilings serve indoor uses. Both draw on Interarch's factory history. These products can be specified with the main building and may improve design fit. The roof and wall page and TRACDEK guide show their scope. Current sources do not show their separate sales or margins. This report gives them no separate value.

Interarch also markets non-industrial and large structures through its Interarch Life guides and project sheets. One company case sheet covers a G+6 data centre at Rabale, Navi Mumbai. It gives an area of 11,000 square metres and a height of 37 metres. The work covered design, factory supply and erection. Others supplied the block walls. This case shows one engineering use. It does not show a repeatable data-centre market for Interarch. See the company case sheet. Confirm similar work with customers before calling it a proven business line.

Heavy steel is a newer capability. On the August 2026 call, management said trial production had started in Andhra Pradesh. It named high-rise buildings, data centres, power stations, steel and fertilizer plants, and ports as target jobs. These orders may have more design value. They also need more design checks, welding control and on-site planning. The key signs are accepted customer orders and steady paid output. Nameplate capacity alone is not enough.

2. Competitive moat, industry structure & external learning hub

Interarch joins design, factory work, delivery and site assembly. One team can plan the design, factory work, deliveries and site schedule. This may reduce handoffs for the customer. It is not a patent or software lock-in. Other firms can bid for the same work. Price, quality and delivery still matter.

A customer may return to a supplier that knows the site, design rules and work crew. The Q1 FY27 deck says repeat orders were 72% of FY26 revenue, down from 82% in FY25. It also says three of the five named customer groups had worked with the company for over five years. This points to lasting ties, but not to a fixed share of future orders. New customers can help growth. Repeat work is often easier to win and deliver. The source set does not show top-customer revenue or profit in enough detail.

Demand may come from factories, warehouses, data centres, electronics, power and other large sites. Management said those areas were active in its August call. The FY26 revenue mix still leans on industrial and factory work. That link helps when firms build plants. It also makes sales depend on permits, funding, land and customer plans. Management says a pre-engineered building can save 50% to 60% of build time. This is a company claim; no outside time study was found.

Pennar, Everest and Kirby also sell engineered building systems. Their size, markets and accounts differ, so this is not a financial ranking. This review makes no market-share claim. The main question is simple: can Interarch use its new plants well, deliver on time and turn sales into cash?

Is the company reaching its potential & are the CEOs walking the talk?

FY26 sales beat the prior company target. In May 2026, Managing Director Arvind Nanda said the target had been about ₹1,720 crore and the result was nearly ₹1,900 crore. The audited result was ₹1,898.0 crore. EBITDA reached ₹176.3 crore. Margin eased to 9.3% from 9.4% in FY25, so higher sales did not lift margin.

Cash tells a weaker story. The FY26 audited cash-flow statement shows ₹18.8 crore used in operations, after ₹53.1 crore generated in FY25. The Q1 deck shows a ₹164.6 crore use of working capital in FY26. Receivables, stock and other assets all grew. Management said large jobs collect against work stages. That can explain some delay, but investors still need to see the cash arrive.

On the August 2026 call, Nanda said, “we need to take orders we can deliver on time.” This is the right limit for a project builder. The next test is whether the new plants add output without a cash squeeze. Kheda Phase 1 began in July. More PEB and heavy-steel lines were planned for FY27. No Q2 result was in the reviewed files by 30 October; the board was due to review results on 4 November.

The August call set FY27 sales at ₹2,150–₹2,200 crore and FY28 sales at ₹2,700 crore. It also set a 9.5%–10% EBITDA margin goal for FY28. These are goals, not results. Q1 sales were ₹459.6 crore and the margin was 8.6%. We should wait for H1 sales, cash and plant updates before using the FY28 goal in a base case.

3. Financial trajectory & valuation sanity check

FY26 was a strong sales year. Revenue rose 30.6% to ₹1,898.0 crore. EBITDA reached ₹176.3 crore. PAT was ₹134.5 crore. EBITDA margin slipped to 9.3% from 9.4%. PAT margin fell to 7.1% from 7.4%. In Q1 FY27, revenue rose 20.7%. EBITDA margin was 8.6%. PAT was ₹28.2 crore, just below ₹28.4 crore a year earlier. Sales grew, but net profit did not.

The balance sheet has little debt. Still, cash flow makes the label “debt-free” too simple. At 31 March 2026, cash was ₹20.6 crore. Current and long-term borrowings totalled ₹15.0 crore. Lease debt was ₹2.5 crore. On this narrow count, cash exceeded borrowings and lease debt by about ₹3.1 crore. Other bank balances and investments are left out. Cash fell from ₹84.7 crore to ₹20.6 crore. Operating cash flow was negative.

Receivables and contract assets need close review. Trade receivables rose to ₹288.2 crore from ₹211.0 crore. Contract assets rose to ₹85.0 crore from ₹48.9 crore. Stock also grew. These changes used working capital. Some rise is normal when a project business expands. It becomes a credit risk if clients delay sign-off, cash ages or contract assets do not turn into bills. Short-term loans may then fund the growth.

A simple valuation check divides the 30 October close of ₹1,460.40 by FY26 audited basic EPS of ₹80.41. The result is 18.16 times FY26 earnings. Both numbers use the pre-split share basis. 5. Multi-horizon investment view: short-term momentum, medium-term catalysts & scenarios

The next result is the key near-term event. The company said on 27 October that its board would review Q2 and H1 FY27 results on 4 November. This report uses 30 October as its market cutoff, so it assumes no Q2 result. The next filing should show orders, margins, cash and plant progress. Until then, the Q1 outlook is still a goal.

Over the next year, the company plans more PEB and heavy-steel capacity at Kheda and Andhra Pradesh. It also described a planned 76%-owned joint venture with ER Steel Inc. for open-web steel joists. 6. Key risks, thesis breakers & bear case

The bear case starts with cash. Sales grew, but operating cash was negative in FY26. Cash fell. Receivables and contract assets rose. The share is also far below falling trend averages. If jobs need more cash or new plants open late, sales may rise while cash returns weaken.

The next results must show progress in cash and execution. Q2 FY27 results were due after this report's cutoff. A tax notice asks for records and explanations. Interarch said the financial effect was not known. This remains a legal process risk. It is not proof of wrongdoing or a tax bill.

Cash conversion and project financing

A negative cash-flow year can reflect timing in a growing project business. It is not harmless until cash returns. The FY26 statement shows a large use of working capital. The investor deck also shows higher receivables and stock. If clients approve milestones slowly, cash limits buying and growth. Track receivable days, contract assets, advances, retention, reserves and short-term loans in later filings.

In May 2026, management said larger jobs collect against work stages. It also said its clients were established customers. This may explain some delay. It does not reduce the cash tied up or the timing risk. Check later receipts and aged bills. Do not rely on reassurance alone.

Capacity ramp and execution dilution

Kheda PEB Phase 1 and the Andhra heavy-steel lines add possible capacity. Costs come before full use. The Q1 deck reports 2,21,000 MTPA installed and about 1,89,500 MTPA usable. But its footnote dates the plant table to October 2025. The same table includes a Kheda line from 2026. It calls 2,21,000 MTPA a post-Phase 1 total. These dates do not fit. Treat this as a company figure, not verified output. Track tonnes made and plant use.

Heavy steel may add design value. It also needs new steel grades, weld checks, lift plans and customer sign-off. Trial runs do not prove sales. A planned start date does not prove profit. Delays leave fixed costs before sales. A fast ramp may raise defects and rework.

Policy, legal and fundraising uncertainty

The FY26 annual report covers a tax search in August 2025. It said no demand had been raised by the report date. Interarch filed a return for the block period in February 2026. On 15 September 2026, the company reported a new Section 142(1) notice. It covered 1 April 2019 to 17 October 2025. The notice asked for records by 29 September. It did not find hidden income or set a tax demand. The company said the cost, if any, was not known. We found no later result in company or exchange filings by 30 October. The matter remains open.

The August board proposed a QIP of up to ₹250 crore. New shares would dilute owners unless the return on that cash is strong. The Q1 deck also described a proposed North American joint venture, with Interarch to own 76%. The plan called for about ₹80 crore of total funding. These were plans and approvals, not completed deals. Track the final issue terms, cash use and JV spend.

Steel, contracts and demand concentration

Steel is a large input. A project quote may come weeks before steel is bought. If contracts do not pass on price moves, a sudden rise can cut margin. A price fall may lead clients to ask for lower prices or delay an order. Large jobs also depend on site access and milestone approvals. FY26 sales were still concentrated in factory and industrial work. A pause in private plant spending could cut orders and plant use.

Exports and North American plans add new markets. They also bring currency, code, shipping, partner and cash risks. Value those plans after repeat orders and collected cash appear.
