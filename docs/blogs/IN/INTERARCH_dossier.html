1. What the company does

Interarch Building Solutions sells finished steel buildings. A customer such as a battery maker, a warehouse developer or a data-centre operator tells Interarch what it needs: floor area, height, crane loads, wind and earthquake zone. Interarch then designs the whole frame, makes every steel part in its own plants, trucks the parts to the site in the order they will be bolted together, and sends a certified builder to erect them.[2][11] The client gets one price, one date and one party to blame if something goes wrong. That single-point responsibility is what a pre-engineered building (PEB) maker sells, and the managing director described it on the May 2026 call as being the client's first capital-goods supplier.[11]

The company was incorporated in 1983 and listed on the NSE and BSE on 26 August 2024 at an issue price of ₹900 per share. The issue had a fresh part of ₹199.99 crore and a sale of shares by existing holders of ₹400.29 crore.[4] The fresh money is the reason the balance sheet carries net cash. At 31 March 2026 it held ₹81.2 crore of cash and bank balances against ₹15.0 crore of borrowings, a net cash position of ₹66.2 crore before counting ₹14.1 crore of current investments.[2]

Most of the money comes from turnkey PEB contracts, where Interarch supplies and erects the building. In FY26 these contracts brought in ₹1,618 crore of the ₹1,898 crore total, while plain sales of building parts and other income from operations made up the rest.[2] Customers are mainly industrial. About 86 percent of FY26 revenue came from industrial and manufacturing buildings and about 11 percent from infrastructure such as warehouses and data centres.[2] The latest order wins in the presentation include Ahluwalia Contracts, Torrent Electricals and L&T Construction.[2]

The cost structure explains why steel prices matter more than anything else. Raw material, mostly steel plate and coil, was about 60 percent of FY26 revenue (₹1,143.4 crore of cost of goods sold on ₹1,898.0 crore of revenue).[2] Interarch quotes a fixed price for the whole building and manages steel cost by buying against orders. Management said on the May 2026 call that it checks steel prices daily and is not forced to quote at one steel price.[11] When steel jumps between the quote and the purchase, the margin takes the hit, which is why the EBITDA margin (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a measure of operating profit as a share of sales) has stayed in a narrow 8.7 to 9.5 percent band for four years instead of rising with scale.[2]

The business is also capacity-bound. It makes buildings in five integrated plants plus the first phase of a sixth in Gujarat, with installed capacity of 221,000 tonnes a year, the second largest among integrated PEB makers.[2] Management says usable capacity is typically 80 to 85 percent of installed capacity, and DAM Capital estimates that FY26 output of about 160,000 tonnes ran at 79.6 percent of installed capacity, so the plants were close to their practical ceiling.[7][24][25] New orders therefore turn into revenue only as fast as capacity and site crews allow. That is why the company plans to spend about ₹129 crore on new capacity this year and about ₹133 crore next year.[12]

How an order turns into cash

A typical order takes 10 to 12 months to complete, and the customer pays an advance of 15 to 20 percent. The three orders disclosed since the June quarter show this pattern: ₹128 crore on 18 August (10 months, 20 percent advance), ₹89 crore on 30 September (10 months, 15 percent advance) and ₹61 crore on 6 October (12 months, 20 percent advance).[20][21][22] The rest is billed at milestones: material dispatch, erection and completion. When a client delays a milestone, the money is stuck as a receivable even though the revenue has been booked, and that is what happened in FY26.[11]

Because the company erects as well as supplies, it also carries contract assets (work done but not yet billed) and contract liabilities (advances received for work not yet done). At 31 March 2026 contract assets were ₹85.0 crore and contract liabilities were ₹183.2 crore.[2] The balance between these, plus receivables and inventory, decides whether profit turns into cash, and Chapter 3 measures that balance in days.

What changed in the last six months

Three things changed after the June 2026 quarter. First, the Gujarat plant started production in July 2026 and the heavy structural steel plant in Andhra Pradesh was inaugurated on 24 September 2026 with an initial capacity of 24,000 tonnes a year.[2][19] Second, shareholders approved a fund-raising of up to ₹250 crore through a qualified institutional placement (QIP, a sale of new shares to large institutions) and a five-for-one stock split at the annual general meeting on 10 September 2026.[18] Third, the Income Tax Department sent a notice on 14 September 2026 asking for accounts and explanations for the block period from 1 April 2019 to 17 October 2025, a follow-up to the search at the company in August 2025.[16][17]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of this business is that large industrial clients buy on track record, not on lowest price. The managing director said on the May 2026 call that price is secondary in these orders because design, engineering, on-time supply and erection matter more.[11] The numbers fit that claim. Repeat orders were 72 percent of FY26 revenue (82 percent in FY25), and the company says it completed more than 850 PEB contracts between FY15 and FY26.[2] A client building a battery plant on a tight schedule has little reason to hand the job to an untested supplier.

Scale is the second strength. The top six players hold 80 to 85 percent of the organised PEB market and grew 11.4 percent a year from FY19 to FY23 against 8.5 percent for the whole industry, according to the industry data from the IPO prospectus that the company reproduces.[2] DAM Capital expects the organised segment to grow about 13 percent a year to FY30 and the whole PEB market about 10 percent a year to about ₹340 billion.[24] On PEB-segment revenue, Interarch's ₹18,980 million is about 1.4 times Epack Prefab's ₹13,659 million and about 1.9 times M&B Engineering's ₹9,846 million, the two other listed pure PEB names.[24] On the total revenue in the Chapter 3 peer matrix (₹19, ₹15 and ₹13 billion) the multiples are about 1.3 and 1.5, because Epack and M&B also earn some revenue outside PEB.[24]

The balance sheet is the third strength. Borrowings of ₹15.0 crore sit against equity of ₹881.1 crore, and Crisil reaffirmed a long-term rating of A/Stable and a short-term rating of A1 on ₹600 crore of bank facilities on 28 April 2026, raising the facility size from ₹495 crore.[2][13] Most of those facilities are bank guarantees, which clients ask for on large orders, so the rating supports order intake more than it supports borrowing. The promoters, who hold 59.43 percent, have pledged no shares.[13][14][15]

Where the business is weaker than its reputation

The first weakness is margin. Interarch is the largest listed PEB maker but not the most profitable. DAM Capital puts FY26 EBITDA margins at 11.2 percent for M&B Engineering, 10.5 percent for Epack Prefab and 9.3 percent for Interarch, and puts Kirby, an unlisted peer, at 10.8 percent.[24] On the August 2025 call an analyst asked whether the earlier goal of a margin of 10 percent or more was still possible, and management answered yes. The company then delivered 9.3 percent for FY26 and 8.6 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[8][2] A leader with a mid-pack margin is a company where scale is not yet turning into pricing power.

The second weakness is cash. Kirby, which runs on customers' advances, shows negative working capital of 50 days in DAM's table. Interarch ran at 51 days positive in FY26 and reported negative operating cash flow of ₹18.8 crore on a profit of ₹134.5 crore.[24][2] Management says it is tightening payment terms on large orders so that milestones are not delayed.[11] Until the numbers show it, this is a stated intention.

The third weakness is that entry barriers are low. ICICI Securities lists minimal entry barriers, steel price swings and dependence on private capital spending as the key risks.[26] DAM expects the top eight players to raise installed capacity from about 1.13 million tonnes to about 1.54 million tonnes, an increase of about 36 percent, while the market grows about 10 percent a year.[24] If demand slips for even a few quarters, this much new capacity can push prices down.

The fourth weakness is concentration. The five largest customer groups were 44 percent of FY26 revenue against 27 percent in FY25 and 25 percent in FY24, and the repeat-order share fell from 82 to 72 percent.[2] Management says these are blue-chip customers and that it has had no bad debts in the last two to three years, which is credible. It also means a single delayed payment from one of them is large relative to a quarter's cash flow.[11]

What the evidence says about the moat

A moat is something that keeps profits high when a rival tries to take your customers. Here the moat is real but narrow: it is a long customer list, integrated plants and erection crews, not a patent or a cost advantage in steel. The evidence for it is the 72 percent repeat-order share and the fact that new-age clients (data centres, semiconductors, batteries, renewables) were about 35 percent of the order book at the end of July 2026, with DAM Capital saying such clients are open to paying a premium for speed and reliability.[2][25] The evidence against it is the flat margin band and the fact that peers earn more on each rupee of sales.[24]

Our reading is therefore a good business with a repeatable franchise, selling into a market that is growing, but whose economics are not yet strong enough to excuse a weak cash cycle or a broken chart. Chapter 2B scores the five numbers that decide that.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management walk-the-talk means checking what the company promised one to two years ago against what it delivered. The record on growth is strong. It is weaker on margin and on funding, and mixed on cash.

On growth, management set a 17.5 percent revenue growth target for FY26 and 20 percent a year after that.[9] FY26 revenue grew 30.6 percent to ₹1,898.0 crore.[2] The FY28 revenue target was first given as ₹2,400 to ₹2,500 crore in May 2025, repeated as ₹2,500 crore in February 2026, and lifted to ₹2,700 crore in August 2026.[7][10][12] For FY27 the guide is ₹2,150 to ₹2,200 crore, and Q1 grew 20.7 percent.[11][2] The company has beaten its revenue targets and raised them, which is a good sign about execution.

On margin the record is weaker. A 10 percent or better EBITDA margin was confirmed on the August 2025 call and 9.3 percent was delivered.[8][2] On funding, the company said in February 2026 that it had planned to pay for the new plants from its own money, and that the ₹100 crore fund-raising it was asking shareholders to approve was meant to bring the plants forward.[10] Six months later it asked for ₹250 crore, citing faster expansion and working-capital needs.[1][12] That is not a broken promise, but it is a change of plan that dilutes holders by about 9 percent at today's price.

5. What could happen next

Every share price carries a story about the future. A reverse discounted cash flow (Reverse DCF) works backwards from today's price to find that story: it asks what growth rate over ten years makes a company's future profits, discounted at 12 percent a year and valued at 20 times profit at the end, worth exactly today's market value.[31] For Interarch the ledger solves for 6.76 percent a year on trailing profit of about ₹133.7 crore, which it derives from the market value of ₹2,692.07 crore and the trailing price-to-earnings ratio of 20.14.[29] The method uses profit because the company's own free cash flow is negative or not published for the trailing year.

Six point eight percent a year is a modest growth expectation for a company that has grown profit 18 percent a year for three years. The price is therefore not asking for much, and that is why the stock is not clearly overvalued. The next question is whether the profit forecasts behind the Street's more optimistic story are intact, and the ledger says they are slipping.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER. The rule is that a current holding in Stage 4 with no base, or one that trips a red line on solvency, governance or cash conversion, is a candidate for replacement. Interarch trips two red lines: the chart is in Stage 4 with no base, and three-year cash conversion is 0.35, below the 0.40 line, with only one quarter of evidence for a turnaround.[29][31][2] The share closed at ₹1,600.80, above the stop at ₹1,485.77, so the verdict does not come from a stop being hit. It comes from the structure of the chart and the cash flow.

Your position is 250 shares bought at an average of ₹1,759.25 for a total of ₹4,39,813.59. At ₹1,600.80 it is worth ₹4,00,200 and shows an unrealised loss of ₹39,613.59, or 9.01 percent, and it is 8.75 percent of your net worth of ₹45,73,985.12.[30] The market does not know your purchase price, and the rule does not use it. What matters is the size: the largest weight the risk rule would allow for a name at the lowest conviction tier (TIER_4) is 2 percent, so this position is more than four times that ceiling. If the stop at ₹1,485.77 were hit, you would lose about ₹28,757, or 0.63 percent of net worth, which is nearly twice the 0.35 percent risk budget for the tier.[31]

We would carry out the decision in two steps rather than one. Step 1: sell 125 shares now. Use a limit order on any bounce toward the 20-day average of ₹1,673.62, because volume is thin and RSI is 33, and if that level is not reached within five trading sessions sell at the market. This is a sequence of price levels and dates, not a forecast, and it reduces the chance of selling everything at the low.[29]

Step 2 is the other 125 shares, and it is a temporary exception to the size rule. At ₹1,600.80 they are 4.37 percent of net worth (125 x ₹1,600.80 / ₹45,73,985.12), above the 2.0 percent ceiling for TIER_4, which equals about 57 shares. We accept the overweight for a short time because the loss to the hard stop on 125 shares is about ₹14,379, or 0.31 percent of net worth, inside the 0.35 percent risk budget for the tier.[31] The exception ends at the close of 16 November 2026, the first session after the 14 November filing deadline, whether or not the results have been filed.

On the day the September-quarter results are filed, the second tranche follows one table of outcomes. If the cash test and the chart test are both met (the reversal condition in Trigger 3), cancel the sale of the second tranche and re-score the name. Even then we would not add shares, because the position would still be above the 2 percent ceiling. If the cash test is met but the chart is still below ₹1,697.61 or still in Stage 4, cut the holding to no more than 57 shares and keep only that until the chart test is also met. If the cash test is not met, is only partly met (for example operating cash flow between zero and ₹50 crore), or cannot be read because the filing is late or lacks the figures, sell the rest. A chart recovery before the results only pauses the sale. It never cancels it, and it never authorises buying more.

The replacement itself is advisory. The ₹4.0 lakh that would be freed is 8.75 percent of net worth and should go to the best-ranked challenger in the shortlist that passes Layer 1, meaning a Stage 2 chart or a base breakout, a cash-conversion ratio above 0.70 and a reward-to-risk of at least 3.0. We do not name the challenger here because the displacement workflow ranks them across the whole shortlist.[31]

Practical points that do not change the decision. After the five-for-one split is effective, your 250 shares become 1,250 and your average cost becomes ₹351.85, with no change in value.[1][18] The final dividend of ₹12.50 per share had a record date of 3 September 2026 and has already been decided.[1][4] A sale at a loss can be set against gains elsewhere in the portfolio, and the tax treatment depends on the holding period, which the portfolio record does not give, so check it with a tax adviser. The rule decides the action. The tax point only affects how you execute it.
