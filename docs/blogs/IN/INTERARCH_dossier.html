1. What the company does

A pre-engineered building is a steel building made like a kit. Interarch designs the frame on computers, cuts and welds the steel parts in its own factories, trucks them to the customer's site in the order they will be needed, and sends approved builders to bolt them together. The customer gets one company that is answerable for the whole steel structure, and the company says the building goes up in 40% to 50% less time than a poured-concrete one[2]. Interarch says it does only the steel frame, roof and walls. It does not do the civil, electrical or plumbing work[6].

Interarch earns money by signing fixed-price contracts, building the steel parts, and recognising revenue as the work is done. In FY26 (the year to March 2026) revenue was ₹1,898 crore. Of that, ₹1,618 crore came from full turnkey PEB contracts, ₹258 crore from selling building components without erection, and ₹22 crore from other operating income[2]. A typical order is delivered in four to nine months, which is why management says it cannot take orders for delivery a year or more away[5].

The customers are mostly industrial. In FY26 about 87% of revenue came from industrial and manufacturing buildings, about 11% from infrastructure such as warehouses, and about 1% from other buildings[2]. That is changing. In the June 2026 quarter, buildings such as offices, hotels and a data centre were 10% of revenue[2][6]. Named long-standing customers include Reliance Industries, the Tata group and the Aditya Birla group[8]. In FY26, 72% of revenue came from customers who had ordered before[2].

Here is where each ₹100 of FY26 revenue went. About ₹60.5 paid for steel and bought-in parts, ₹8.9 for staff, ₹9.3 for site erection by approved builders, ₹3.6 for outside job work and ₹8.4 for other costs. That left about ₹9.3 of EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), the profit before interest, tax and the accounting charge for wear on assets[2]. Steel is the swing factor. The rating agency notes that the company has not always been able to pass steel-price rises on to customers, so the margin moves around more than the sales do[8].

The company is also a capital spender. It has six plants, including the first phase of a new plant at Kheda in Gujarat that started on 9 July 2026, with installed capacity of 221,000 tonnes a year[2][6]. A heavy-steel plant in Andhra Pradesh, for the thicker beams that data centres and tall buildings need, was already in trial production at the time of the August 2026 call[6]. A proposed 76:24 joint venture with ER Steel Inc of Canada would make open-web steel joists for the US and Canada[2]. Each of these needs cash before it earns any, which matters for the cash story in chapter 3.

How an order moves through the company

Every order starts with an estimate. The customer sends architectural drawings and a design brief, and Interarch's engineers work out the structure and the weight of steel needed, then quote a price per tonne. Management says each building has to be estimated, quoted, won and only then detailed, because no consultant hands over a finished structural design[6]. A wrong estimate on a large order can cost more than a year of small gains, which is one reason the company refuses orders that do not suit its delivery schedules or payment terms[6].

Once an order is won, the customer usually pays an advance. At 31 March 2026 customers had paid ₹183.2 crore ahead of delivery, up from ₹164.1 crore a year earlier[2]. Later payments depend on billing milestones and on the customer approving the work. When a company bills a large contract but has not yet been paid, the amount sits in receivables. That is the main way this business can show a profit and still run short of cash, and it is what happened in FY26.

The business is also seasonal. The first two quarters of the financial year are usually the weakest, because monsoon rains and site clearances delay dispatches, and management expects revenue to pick up from the October quarter onward[6]. Revenue in the June 2026 quarter was ₹459.6 crore, below the ₹503.6 crore of the March quarter[3]. This matters for judging any single quarter, including the September 2026 result that is due next.

The four legs of the business

The Managing Director describes the company as a four-legged animal: sales and marketing, design and engineering, project management with approved builders, and the factory. A factory alone does not make a pre-engineered building company, he says[6]. The company has 155 or more design engineers and detailers, over 90 project managers and a network of 65 approved builders and erectors[2]. The builders are outside contractors, so site manpower is a risk Interarch does not fully control, and management named it as a challenge in the August 2026 call[6].

The legs are what customers pay for. A paint plant or battery plant costs far more than its building, yet the building is on the critical path, so a late delivery delays the whole plant. That is why large customers pick a few trusted suppliers. Management says only two or three companies can take on the very large orders in electric-vehicle and battery plants, which is its case for steadier pricing on big jobs[6].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Interarch's strength is scale in a business where scale counts. It has the second-largest installed capacity among integrated PEB makers, 221,000 tonnes a year, and a market share the company puts at 6.5% of the integrated players[2]. The organised players, led by six large firms, hold an estimated 40% to 45% of the market and are growing faster than the unorganised makers, according to the company's industry data[2]. In FY26 it shipped 162,000 tonnes. Against the 200,000 tonnes in service during the year that is about 81%. Against today's 221,000 tonnes, which includes the Kheda plant opened in July 2026, it is about 73%[6][2]. A full factory is good for margins, but it also means growth now depends on new capacity.

The balance sheet is a second strength. Total borrowings were ₹15.0 crore at 31 March 2026 against equity of ₹881.1 crore, so debt-to-equity is about 0.02[2][1]. Crisil rates the bank facilities Crisil A/Stable and Crisil A1, reaffirmed on 28 April 2026, and says the interest cover was about 16.5 times in FY25[8]. The rating agency lists strong liquidity, but it also names the main weakness: the business is cyclical and raw-material prices make the operating margin volatile[8]. A rating of A is solid but not top-tier, which fits a mid-sized industrial company.

Track record is a third. Management raised its FY26 revenue projection once and still beat it: the first projection was ₹1,720 crore, the second ₹1,850 crore and the result ₹1,898 crore[5]. The first plant of the Gujarat expansion opened in July 2026, in the month management had named in May[5][6]. Chapter 2B tests this more closely.

The weaknesses are real. First, margins are thin and exposed to steel. EBITDA margin was 8.6% in the June 2026 quarter, 10.5% in the March quarter and 9.3% for FY26[2]. Crisil would reconsider the rating if the margin fell below 8% on a sustained basis[8]. Second, receivables are growing faster than sales, which is why cash from operations was negative in FY26 and is analysed in chapter 3[1]. Third, customers are becoming more concentrated: the five largest customer groups were 44% of FY26 revenue, against 25% in FY24[2].

Governance needs a separate note. The Income Tax Department searched Interarch's offices, factories and some executives' homes between 18 and 22 August 2025 and took documents and data backups[1]. The company says no demand has been raised, it filed a block assessment return on 26 February 2026, and it sees no need for adjustments to its accounts[1]. The auditor drew attention to the matter in an emphasis-of-matter paragraph and said it may have an adverse effect on the functioning of the company[1]. In October 2026 the president of corporate finance and strategy retired[11]. None of this proves wrongdoing, but the outcome is not known, and that uncertainty belongs in the price.

Competition and the size of the prize

Management says the industry has grown from about three companies in the early 2000s to around 300 today, and names Zamil and Kirby as other large players[6]. The company's own prospectus data put the PEB market at about ₹195 billion in FY24 and expect ₹330 to 340 billion by FY29, with PEBs still only 3% to 5% of Indian construction[2]. A rising market lets several firms win, but it also invites new capacity from rivals. EPACK Prefab, a listed rival, grew revenue 34.5% in FY26 and has 147,122 tonnes of capacity[20].

Interarch's edge shows up in repeat business and in the very large, complex orders. It won a ₹165 crore order in Gujarat for an energy company in the June 2026 quarter and booked ₹609 crore of orders between May and July 2026[2][6]. The limits of the edge show up in price. The company says it sometimes takes a large order at a lower margin to spread fixed costs, and it cannot demand any price it wants because other firms can also do the work[6].

What the moat is and is not

A moat is a lasting advantage over rivals. Interarch's advantages are integration, engineering depth, customer trust and a factory network in north and south India that, Crisil says, spreads its orders across regions[2][8]. These are real, but they are advantages of execution rather than of patents or contracts. A customer can switch supplier on the next order, and the company's own repeat-order share fell from 82% of FY25 revenue to 72% in FY26[2].

The honest summary is that this is a good operating business with a clean balance sheet and a modest moat, and that the debate is about cash, governance and price, not about whether it can build steel buildings. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means checking what management promised a year or two ago against what happened. Interarch gives numeric guidance on its calls, so the check is possible. The record is mostly good on sales and plants, and weaker on cash. Management describes its own projections as cautious and said in August that this year's might be beaten, and the FY26 result fits that claim[6][5].

The one clear miss is working capital. In February 2026 the Chief Executive said net working capital was about 34 days and the company would try to keep it in that range[7]. Three months later the audited cash flow showed ₹164.6 crore absorbed by working capital and operating cash flow of −₹18.8 crore[2]. Management explained that large work-contract orders had been billed and not yet collected, and that stocks had been built up because steel prices were rising and some suppliers had to be paid in advance[6]. These explanations may be correct. They still mean the 34-day claim did not hold for the year.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above rests on assumptions, and the weakest one in each is named below with the evidence that would prove it wrong and the date we would know. The research standard also asks for a red-team bear case, which is a deliberate attempt to argue against our own conclusion. Our conclusion is to replace the holding. So the strongest counter-argument is the case for keeping it, and we make that case first.

The strongest case against our own verdict

The case for keeping the stock is simple and not weak. The business is growing about 18% a year, has almost no debt, holds a record order book, and is opening two plants into strong demand[2][6]. The price is 45% below its high and RSI is 22.6, a reading that is called oversold. Three analysts see ₹2,140 to ₹2,889[14]. We still recommend the exit for three reasons. The rule that governs is a hard red line, and the rules were written to stop us from holding on in a falling market because the story sounds good. The expected return in chapter 5, 4.3%, is below our hurdle. And the evidence that would justify holding, positive cash from operations that collects the FY26 receivables, is one unaudited quarter and not yet a trend[6].

The hinge claim

The claim everything depends on is that profit will turn into cash. If H1 FY27 operating cash flow is at least 70% of profit, the cash-conversion KPI moves from FAIL to WATCH and the reverse DCF is about 5.9%, not 22%. That removes the cash reason for replacing the holding, but the Stage 4 red line still stands until the chart clears, so the turnaround reading becomes credible only when both happen. If it is not, the stock is a profit story without cash, and the bear case becomes the base. The half-year cash flow statement is published with the September 2026 results, which the company has not yet dated. One third-party estimate is 28 October 2026[15][16]. Until then, the share price will move on the chart and on news such as the share sale, not on this evidence. That is why we treat the September filing as the one scheduled event that can change the verdict, and why chapter 7 attaches a trigger to it.
