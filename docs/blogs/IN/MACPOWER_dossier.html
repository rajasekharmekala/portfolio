1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Operational Cycles

Core Investor Question: How did a 2003 Rajkot lathe workshop evolve into a 2,500-machine CNC manufacturer, and what do its past revenue and margin cycles reveal about its structural resilience?

Macpower CNC Machines Limited was incorporated in 2003 at Metoda GIDC near Rajkot, Gujarat. First-generation entrepreneur Rupesh J. Mehta founded the firm alongside co-promoters and family members [13], [14]. Rajkot's industrial belt—known for diesel engines, castings, bearings, and auto-ancillary shops—gave Macpower both local buyers and skilled machinists. For its first decade, Macpower built conventional and entry-level CNC lathes under the 'Turner' brand [13], [15]. It served MSMEs with a modest capacity of roughly 600 machines per year [13], [15]. During that pre-listing phase, the company operated like a classic assembly shop. It bought sheet-metal enclosures, spindles, and control panels from outside vendors. As a result, operating profit margins (OPM) stayed in the low single digits: 2.5% in FY15, 3.4% in FY16, and 2.4% in FY17 on flat sales of ₹64–68 Cr [1], [15].

The structural shift began with Macpower's 2018 Initial Public Offering on the NSE Emerge platform [13]. Many SME issuers used IPO cash to pay promoter debt or plug working capital gaps. Instead, Macpower put almost all of its ₹39 Cr net inflow into backward integration and capacity doubling at its 8-acre Metoda plant [1], [5], [13]. Between FY18 and FY21, management built in-house sheet-metal fabrication, powder-coating lines, and precision spindle machining [5], [15]. Those additions lifted annual capacity from 600 machines to roughly 1,300 machines [5], [15]. The financial payoff came fast. Operating margins jumped from 2.4% in FY17 to 11% in FY18 and 12% in FY19, while revenue more than doubled from ₹68 Cr to ₹140 Cr [1].

To judge Macpower without looking at its stock quote, we must study how it performed across three industrial cycles from FY15 to H1 FY27 [1], [15]. The first test was the FY18–FY20 domestic auto and NBFC slowdown, followed by the March 2020 COVID-19 shock. Revenue fell 42% from ₹140 Cr in FY19 to ₹82 Cr in FY20 [1]. Operating profit dropped from ₹17 Cr to ₹5 Cr as OPM narrowed from 12% to 6%, and ROCE sank from 30% to 6% [1]. Yet Macpower never posted an annual operating loss or net loss. Net profit stayed positive at ₹3 Cr in FY20, and debt remained tiny at ₹1 Cr because management had avoided debt during the 2018 expansion [1].

The second cycle (FY21–FY23) tested Macpower's post-COVID rebound and supply-chain resilience. Revenue recovered 38% to ₹113 Cr in FY21 and surged 68% to ₹190 Cr in FY22 [1]. Growth then slowed to 6% in FY23 (₹202 Cr) as global chip and controller shortages at FANUC and Siemens delayed machine dispatches [1]. Even so, operating margins held steady in an 8%–11% band across FY21–FY23 [1]. That stability proved that the 2018 backward integration had permanently raised Macpower's trough margin floor above its pre-IPO 2.5%–3.4% range [1]. During this cycle, Macpower also launched its 'NEXA' vertical. NEXA is a dedicated sales and engineering arm for Vertical Machining Centers (VMCs), Horizontal Machining Centers (HMCs), Vertical Turret Lathes (VTLs), and Double Column Machines aimed at replacing imports [4], [8], [15].

The third and ongoing cycle (FY24–H1 FY27) marks Macpower's shift from a cyclical shop supplier to a multi-vertical precision platform [5], [15]. Revenue grew 19% to ₹241 Cr in FY24, 9% to ₹262 Cr in FY25, and 27% to ₹333 Cr in FY26 [1], [2]. Operating margins expanded to 15% in FY24 and 16% in both FY25 and FY26 [1], [2]. In Q2 FY27 (ended September 2026), quarterly revenue hit a record ₹120.33 Cr (+40.4% YoY), lifting H1 FY27 revenue to ₹215.56 Cr [4], [15]. In November 2025, a 40,000 sq ft assembly addition raised Metoda capacity by 500 units to 2,500 machines, which now runs near 95% utilization [4], [9], [15]. Meanwhile, the pending order book grew 2.7x in three years to ₹476.7 Cr by September 2026, with NEXA making up 40% of the backlog [4], [15].

2. What the Company Does: Segments, Revenue Mix & Concrete Examples

Core Investor Question: What exact machines does Macpower build, how do its product families and end-markets split, and how does a customer actually use them on a factory floor?

Macpower CNC Machines Limited operates as a single Ind-AS segment: CNC metal-cutting machine tools [2], [15]. At its 8-acre Metoda plant, it builds over 388 models and variants across six core machine types [4], [15]. These include Turning Centers, Vertical Machining Centers (VMCs), Horizontal Machining Centers (HMCs), Vertical Turret Lathes (VTLs), Double Column Machines (DCMs), and 4+1 / 5-axis units [4], [8], [15]. It also builds proprietary 'Macatrol' controllers and robotic or gantry automation cells [4], [15]. Because Macpower does not publish annual segment revenue splits, its FY23 audited mix serves as our baseline, while order-book data shows how fast the mix is upgrading [15], [18].

While Turning Centers historically anchored ~64% of sales, average machine realization has climbed to ₹20.36 lakh in Q2 FY27 because NEXA large-format machines now represent 40% of the ₹476.7 Cr order book [4], [15].

To see why customers buy across these families, it helps to separate the physics of metal cutting. A CNC Turning Center spins a steel bar or forged blank at high RPM against a stationary cutting tool [15]. This setup works best for round parts like transmission shafts, wheel hubs, hydraulic fittings, and pump impellers [15]. Macpower's Turning Center line spans basic 2-axis lathes to twin-spindle units that cut two parts at once [15]. Newer Turn-Mill centers, like the TOM 200 launched in Q1 FY27, add a Y-axis milling head to drill off-center holes in a single clamping [15].

A Machining Center flips the physics. The workpiece stays clamped on a precision table while a fast-spinning tool moves across X, Y, and Z axes [15]. Vertical Machining Centers (VMCs) hold the spindle upright and serve die-and-mould shops, EV battery housings, and electronics casings [15]. Horizontal Machining Centers (HMCs) mount the spindle sideways with a rotary pallet changer [15]. That layout lets a factory machine four faces of a heavy casting—such as a tractor gearbox or armoured-vehicle engine block—in one setup [15].

At the top of Macpower's lineup sits the NEXA vertical, built to replace heavy imported machines [4], [15]. NEXA covers Vertical Turret Lathes (VTLs) for large rings and valve bodies, plus bridge-style Double Column Machines (DCMs) [15]. Examples include the 30-tonne DCM 4222 (priced near ₹2 Cr) and the 5-metre DCM 5227 launched in Q2 FY27 for railway bogies, aerospace tooling, and large moulds [15]. Macpower has also proven 5-axis capability and won a ₹2 Cr single 5-axis order, with a new aerospace/defence model slated for IMTEX January 2027 [6], [15]. Still, CMD Rupesh Mehta calls simultaneous 5-axis a niche 'Ferrari' market in India of only 150–200 units per year [6], [15]. It also faces heavy 300-page German BAFA export-control rules on Siemens controllers [6], [15].

3. Distribution, Supply Chain & Unit Economics

Core Investor Question: How does Macpower manufacture, distribute, and service its machines—and what does every ₹100 of revenue yield in profit and cash?

Macpower's go-to-market model blends direct technical sales with selective dealers for standard machines [11], [14], [15]. As of Q2 FY27, the company covers 39 Indian cities through 9 branch offices and 9 technology centers [4], [15]. Four more tech centers in Kolhapur, Mumbai, Chennai, and Aurangabad are set to open in Q3 FY27, backed by over 995 employees [4], [15]. Direct sales engineers handle custom NEXA machines, large accounts (Hero, Kirloskar, Graphite India), and government or defence tenders [11], [15]. Dealers stock fast-moving catalog lathes on 30–60 day credit for MSMEs that need quick delivery [11], [15]. Management plans to double its sales and service team by late FY27 ahead of the 13-acre plant launch [4], [15].

Manufacturing runs at the 8-acre Metoda GIDC site in Rajkot. In November 2025, a 40,000 sq ft rented unit lifted annual capacity from 2,000 to 2,500 machines [9], [15]. Backward integration drives Macpower's cost edge. The firm makes 40%–45% of its component count in-house, including sheet-metal covers, powder coating, control panels, and 1,700–1,800 spindles per year [6], [15]. Key precision parts—ball screws, linear guideways, bearings, and CNC controllers (FANUC, Siemens, Mitsubishi)—are still imported. Those imports drove ₹24.14 Cr of forex outgo in FY26 [2], [6], [15]. Management aims to lift in-house component share to 75%–80% once the 60-acre backward-integration site is built [6], [7].

Payment terms vary sharply by sales channel. MSME buyers pay an advance at booking and settle the balance upon bank-loan payout [15]. In H1 FY27, Macpower tightened credit by stopping dispatches against third-party finance assurance letters. That shift cut receivables from ₹46.27 Cr in March 2026 to ₹22.89 Cr in September 2026 [15], [16]. Large corporates pay 80%–90% before dispatch and 10%–20% after installation [10], [15]. Government and defence tenders carry 90-day post-commissioning terms, and March inspection cutoffs often push Q4 revenue into Q1 [10], [11], [15].

The waterfall highlights the core tension in Macpower's business model. On the income statement, unit economics look strong: a ~38% gross margin (62% raw material cost) and ~3x–5x asset turnover potential [1], [15]. With tiny interest costs, the firm earns a 10.2% net margin and 29% ROCE [1], [15]. On the cash-flow statement, stocking 20,000+ part types and a year-long buffer of imported controllers and ball screws ties up cash [1], [7]. As a result, FY26 Free Cash Flow was just ₹3 Cr despite ₹34 Cr of Net Profit [1].

Analysts must also account for steep quarterly seasonality and fixed-cost leverage. Historically, Q1 brings only 15%–20% of annual sales as MSMEs pause after March budgets and April–May bank lending slows [6], [10]. Q2 and Q3 each bring 20%–25%, while Q4 delivers 30%–35% of annual revenue [6], [10]. Because ~90% of non-material costs—plant overhead, branch rents, and salaried staff—are fixed, EBITDA margins swing widely with quarterly volume [15], [17]. Recent list-price hikes (enacted 2–3 months before October 2026) are only starting to reach Q3 FY27 billing as older backlog clears [15].

Operating Leverage & Pricing Lag Mechanics

In Q2 FY27, employee costs rose 25% YoY to ₹11.58 Cr while sales jumped 40.4% to ₹120.33 Cr, showing strong fixed-cost absorption [15]. Even so, EBITDA margin stayed flat at 16.6% (vs 16.5% YoY) because other expenses rose 58% to ₹14.62 Cr on new branches, trade shows, and sales hiring [15].

Because Q2 FY27 dispatches came from older order-book pricing and existing stock, recent price hikes should start lifting gross margins from Q3 FY27 onward [15].

4. Industry Structure, Competitive Moat & External Learning Hub

Core Investor Question: How wide is Macpower's moat against Jyoti CNC, LMW, Ace Micromatic, and foreign imports—and what do industry twins teach us about scaling and failure?

India's machine-tool market has a wide gap between local supply and domestic demand. Per the Indian Machine Tool Manufacturers' Association (IMTMA), local production reached ₹16,478 Cr against annual demand of ₹38,000–40,000 Cr [15], [19]. That leaves nearly 60% of India's demand by value met by imports from Japan, Germany, Taiwan, and China [15], [16], [19], [20]. IMTMA expects domestic demand to pass ₹50,000 Cr by 2030 as EV localization, electronics manufacturing (EMS), railways, and defence expand [15], [19]. Metal-cutting CNC machines make up ~75% of this market [19]. At ₹333 Cr in FY26 sales, Macpower holds just ~2% of domestic output and under 1% of Indian demand—leaving a long import-substitution runway [2], [19].

Macpower sits in the value-engineered mid-market tier below global leaders (DMG Mori, Mazak, Okuma, Makino) and domestic leader Jyoti CNC Automation [15], [16]. It competes directly with Ace Micromatic (Ace Designers), Lakshmi Machine Works (LMW MTD), Bharat Fritz Werner (BFW), and Lokesh Machines [15], [16]. Its moat rests on three pillars. First, a 39-city sales and service network built for MSME uptime [4], [15]. Second, an in-house 388+ model catalog free of foreign royalties [4], [15]. Third, 40%–45% in-house component manufacturing that drives 16.2% EBITDA margins with near-zero debt [4], [6], [15]. Defence pre-qualification adds a second barrier. With 200+ machines across 35 defence units and 100+ machines across 6 aerospace units (including HAL and ISRO), rivals need years of trial audits to match its record [15].
