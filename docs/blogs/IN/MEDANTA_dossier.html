Before you read

Global Health Limited runs the Medanta hospital chain, with 3,737 beds in six hospitals across north and east India[2]. The business is strong: sales doubled in four years, cash from operations covered profit 1.29 times over three years, and the company holds net cash[19][4]. This report rejects the stock for now, because the price sits in a base, which is a flat resting zone, and it fails all three momentum tests[20]. The stock is 19.7% below its August high after the Supreme Court questioned medicine mark-ups at private hospitals[25]. The call changes only on a fresh review, either when the chart turns up and every gate condition is re-checked or when a new base forms near ₹1,037 or lower, and it would be wrong if price caps spread beyond cancer drugs[27].

1. What the company does

Medanta is a group of large private hospitals for serious illness. Doctors call this tertiary and quaternary care. It covers heart surgery, cancer treatment, brain and spine surgery, and liver and kidney transplants. Global Health Limited, the listed company, was founded by the heart surgeon Dr Naresh Trehan. He still holds 33.00% of the shares and has pledged none of them.[11] On 30 June 2026 the group had 3,737 installed beds in six hospitals. Gurugram has 1,440, Lucknow 778, Patna 601, Ranchi 310, Indore 175 and Noida 433.[2] The group works with 2,480 doctors, 8,180 nurses and paramedics, and 14,325 full-time and retainer staff.[2]

Patients, insurers and governments pay the bills. In Q1 FY27, 55% of inpatient revenue came from cash-paying patients. Insurers, through third-party administrators, paid 26%. Government schemes such as CGHS, the central government health scheme for civil servants and pensioners, paid 11%. Public-sector and corporate contracts paid 5% and others 3%.[2] International patients brought in ₹782 million. That is about 6.0% of the ₹13,041 million revenue from operations, on our division of two cited figures.[1] The cash share matters later in this report. Insurers and government schemes pay pre-agreed package rates. Cash patients pay closer to list price, so they feel medicine price caps more.[27]

The money mechanics are simple. A hospital sells bed days. Revenue is roughly beds times occupancy times the revenue earned per occupied bed per day. That last figure is ARPOB, average revenue per occupied bed. It was ₹70,244 in Q1 FY27, up 5.5%.[1] The company ran 3,037 census beds on average, which are the beds used for overnight stays. It filled 173,064 bed days.[2] A patient stayed 2.87 days on average, down from 3.03 days a year earlier. The same bed therefore treats more people.[1] Revenue per patient, ARPP, was ₹201,891 against ₹201,795. Growth came from more patients, not from higher prices per patient.[1]

Costs are mostly people and supplies. In Q1 FY27 employee cost was ₹5,136 million, or 39.4% of revenue from operations. Materials were ₹2,944 million (22.6%) and other expenses ₹2,029 million (15.6%). The percentages are our division of the cited lines.[1] Employee cost grew 33.3% while revenue grew 26.5%. That shows the cost of hiring doctors for new hospitals.[1] The profit measure the company reports is EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) before ESOP expense. It was ₹3,153 million, a 23.8% margin.[1] Below that line, depreciation rose 52.9% to ₹690 million and finance cost rose 93.5% to ₹267 million. Noida and new beds came onto the books. Profit after tax slipped 1.1% to ₹1,573 million even as sales rose 26.2%.[1]

How a new hospital ramps, and why Noida matters

A new hospital loses money at first. Doctors, nurses and equipment are paid for before patients fill the beds. Noida shows this. In FY26 it earned revenue of ₹906 million and lost ₹783 million at the EBITDA level.[4] The quarterly EBITDA loss ran ₹30 million, ₹197 million, ₹320 million and ₹236 million through FY26. It then fell to ₹49 million in Q1 FY27.[2] Noida had 141 census beds on 30 September 2025 and 323 on 30 June 2026, out of 550 planned beds.[2] Management says occupancy there is 30% to 40%. It also says breakeven should arrive earlier than first expected.[3] CRISIL noted that Lucknow and Patna both broke even in their first year. Investors hope Noida repeats that pattern.[13]

Customer concentration, geography and cycles

No single payer dominates, but one hospital does. Cluster 1 produced ₹7,715 million of the ₹13,262 million group total, about 58% on our division. Gurugram is the largest part of it.[2] CRISIL has long listed this concentration as a weakness. It also lists competition from new hospitals in Delhi, the National Capital Region and Uttar Pradesh.[13] Hospital demand is steady through the economic cycle. The swings come from regulation, from international patients when flights or neighbouring countries are disrupted, and from seasonal infections.[7] Management said Q4 international revenue was marginally down in sequence because of flight disruptions.[4]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ARPOB: average revenue per occupied bed per day. It uses hospital revenue excluding outpatient pharmacy and other income, divided by occupied bed days.[2]
  • ARPP: average revenue per inpatient admission, on the same revenue base.[2]
  • ALOS: average length of stay, the days an admitted patient stays in hospital.
  • Census beds and installed beds: census beds are used for overnight stays. Installed beds also include day-care beds.[2]
  • Cluster 1 and Cluster 2: the company grouping of its hospitals. Cluster 1 is Gurugram, Indore and Ranchi. Cluster 2 is Lucknow, Patna and Noida.[3]
  • PAT and OPM: profit after tax, and the operating profit margin as Screener defines it, which is operating profit divided by sales.[19]
  • CFO and TTM: cash flow from operations — cash actually collected by core operations —, and the trailing twelve months.
  • FCF: free cash flow — operating cash left after capital spending —, the cash left after running the business and buying assets.
  • NPPA and DPCO: the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority, and the Drugs (Prices Control) Order 2013 under which it caps medicine prices.[33]
  • TPA and CGHS: third-party administrators who settle insurance claims, and the central government health scheme.[2]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position rests on clinical reputation, scale in under-served regions and a doctor network that is hard to copy. Medanta Gurugram has ranked among the world's top 150 hospitals for seven years running. The Lucknow hospital became the first in Uttar Pradesh to win JCI accreditation.[4] The Group CEO said several Noida department heads moved from Gurugram. That lets Noida borrow a trained team and a brand on day one.[3] Management also said senior doctor attrition is very negligible to none. That is a statement, not a measured rate, so we treat it as unverified.[3] The only published turnover figure covers all permanent staff. It was 32% in FY26, 35% in FY25 and 34% in FY24, and nurses and junior staff dominate it.[10]

Geography is the second source of strength. Lucknow serves Eastern Uttar Pradesh, which management puts at more than 100 million people. Patna serves Bihar.[3] Lucknow grew from 200 beds at launch in November 2019 to 778 beds. Volume growth in Lucknow and Patna is running above 20% a year.[3][35] The Guwahati project, now 650 beds, extends the same playbook to the North East. Management says the announcement alone has raised patient flow to Gurugram from that region.[3] The company has not raised prices much. It says it has not really taken a tariff increase, and ARPP was flat.[3][1] That supports growth in volume. It also means pricing power is not yet proven in this cycle.

Financial strength supports the franchise. CRISIL upgraded the long-term rating on ₹1,500 crore of bank facilities to AA/Stable from AA-/Positive on 16 December 2025.[12] Promoter pledge is nil. The auditor, Walker Chandiok and Co LLP, gave an unmodified opinion on internal financial controls.[11][10] Standalone claims not acknowledged as debts were ₹317.8 million of tax matters and ₹671.1 million of other cases. That is about 2.5% of equity of ₹40,125.7 million.[10] The company reports no material related-party transactions for FY26.[10] Crisil ESG rated the company 64, and its core ESG score 70, for FY2026.[14]

The weaknesses are real. First, regulation. The company's own annual report lists price caps on stents and knee implants, and warns that wider price controls could hurt profit.[10] Second, competition. New hospitals in the National Capital Region and Uttar Pradesh will sharpen it, as CRISIL said.[13] Third, execution. About ₹4,850 crore of capex is still to be spent over five years, and free cash flow was minus ₹123 crore in FY26.[3][19] Fourth, the share register. Foreign portfolio holders cut their stake from 12.94% in June 2024 to 9.46% in June 2026. Domestic institutions raised theirs from 10.57% to 16.89%.[19]

Specialty mix and why complex cases support the margin

Medanta describes itself as a destination for complex care. The CEO said cancer was 14.4% of healthcare services revenue in Q1 FY27, up from 13.7% a year earlier. He added that cancers of the kidney, urinary tract and gut are counted in other departments, so the cancer line understates the oncology work.[3] Kidney and urology rose to 8.2% of revenue from 7.6% a year earlier.[3][2] This matters for the price-cap story. The government cap announced on 9 October covers non-scheduled anti-cancer drugs. A hospital with a large cancer unit has more drug revenue exposed.[32][30] Medanta's exposure looks moderate, not low. Outpatient pharmacy revenue was ₹609 million, about 4.7% of revenue from operations on our division. Materials consumed were 22.6%.[1]

Evidence on ownership and governance

The 33.00% promoter holding is one individual, Dr Naresh Trehan. It has not moved from 33.01% in the last four quarters.[11][23] The next large holders are Dunearn Investments (Mauritius), the Temasek-linked holder, at 14.84%, and co-founder Sunil Sachdeva at 10.04%. Mr Sachdeva held 11.16% at December 2025.[23][24] Mutual funds hold 16.03% in June 2026, up from 10.66% a year earlier. Fund-house data for September 2026 show Invesco at 5.194% against 5.297% in June, a small trim.[23][24] The September 2026 shareholding pattern was not yet filed on 18 October 2026. The latest official promoter and pledge data are therefore dated 30 June 2026.[11]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of reported numbers show a company that doubled sales. Consolidated sales rose from ₹2,167 crore in FY22 to ₹4,410 crore in FY26, a 19.4% yearly rate on our working. Net profit rose from ₹196 crore to ₹554 crore.[19] The operating profit margin peaked at 25% in FY24. It fell to 21% in FY26 as Noida costs arrived ahead of Noida revenue.[19]

Three-year growth is slower than the five-year figure suggests, because FY22 was a recovery year. From FY23 to FY26, sales grew 17.6% a year and profit after tax grew 19.3% a year. These are our workings on cited rows: (4,410 / 2,710) and (554 / 326), each raised to the power of one-third.[19] Earnings per share, EPS, rose from ₹7.75 to ₹20.70 over four years. The twelve months to June 2026 show ₹20.69, which is flat on FY26.[19] The pause comes from depreciation and interest on new assets. They grew faster than operating profit in Q1 FY27 and held profit flat.[1]

Cash tells a more careful story. Cash from operations rose from ₹311 crore to ₹714 crore. The FY26 figure ties to the audited consolidated statement of ₹7,143.91 million.[19][10] Capital spending was ₹837 crore in FY26 against ₹277 crore in FY24, taken as cash from operations less free cash flow per Screener. Free cash flow therefore turned to minus ₹123 crore.[19] That is the price of building Noida and adding beds at Patna and Lucknow. It will repeat while capex runs at ₹800 to ₹900 crore a year.[6]

The number

Three-year cash conversion is 1.29. For every ₹1.00 of profit after tax over FY24 to FY26, the company produced ₹1.29 of cash from operations.

How it was calculated

We added cash from operations for FY24, FY25 and FY26 (612 + 624 + 714 = 1,950). We divided by profit after tax for the same years (478 + 481 + 554 = 1,513). The result is 1.289.[19]

The inputs

Cash from operations of ₹612, ₹624 and ₹714 crore, and profit after tax of ₹478, ₹481 and ₹554 crore, from the Screener consolidated tables.[19] The FY26 cash figure matches the audited consolidated statement at ₹7,143.91 million. FY25 matches at ₹6,237.59 million.[10]

The range

Swapping in the audited FY25 and FY26 cash flows (₹623.8 and ₹714.4 crore) for Screener's rounded figures leaves the ratio at 1.29. A bear case takes cash from operations 10% lower in each year. That gives 1.16, still above the 0.70 floor.

If the number is off

A ratio under 0.70 would fail the floor. The risk is rising debtor days, which went from 24 to 34. We watch that line each quarter.

The number

Return on capital employed, ROCE, is 17.4% for FY26. It is the profit before interest and tax earned on each rupee of capital the business uses, measured on average capital employed.

How it was calculated

Profit before tax of ₹715 crore plus interest of ₹90 crore gives ₹805 crore. Capital employed is net worth plus borrowings. It was ₹5,152 crore at March 2026 and ₹4,105 crore at March 2025, an average of ₹4,628.5 crore. 805 / 4,628.5 = 17.4%.[19]

The inputs

Profit before tax, interest, reserves, equity capital and borrowings from the Screener consolidated tables. Screener also prints ROCE of 17.4%.[19]

The range

On year-end capital of ₹5,152 crore, ROCE is 15.6%, just above the 15% floor. StockAnalysis uses a different definition that counts cash and shows 13.30% for FY26, which would fail the floor.[21] We pass the floor on the Screener basis and flag the definition risk.

If the number is off

If ROCE keeps falling about one point a year while Noida ramps, it would sit near the floor by FY28. A rise in Noida profit would reverse that.
