1. What the company does

In plain words: Websol makes the thin silicon cells that turn sunlight into electricity, and solar panels built from those cells, at one factory in West Bengal. This chapter says what it sells, who buys it, and which government rules and prices decide whether the sales happen.

Websol was founded in 1990 and works from one site, the Falta Special Economic Zone in West Bengal [3] [22]. It runs two Mono PERC cell lines of 600 MW each, so cell capacity is 1,200 MW, plus a 550 MW panel line [2] [3]. The second cell line was commissioned in September 2025, and management says it was paid for entirely from internal accruals, meaning cash the business earned [5]. Average cell efficiency is about 23.3 percent [3].

In the June 2026 quarter the plant made 259 MW of cells (92 percent of capacity) and 103 MW of panels (81 percent) [2]. Revenue from operations was ₹372.60 crore, 70 percent above the year before, and operating profit before depreciation was ₹125.58 crore [2]. Profit after tax was ₹77.79 crore, up 16 percent, so profit grew much more slowly than sales [2]. For FY26 (the year to 31 March 2026) revenue was ₹1,049.44 crore, up 82.4 percent, and profit after tax was ₹303.00 crore [1].

Cells go two ways. About 153 MW of the 259 MW made in June was sold to other panel makers, and the rest went into Websol's own panels [4]. Management says its products are “supplied to mostly DCR projects, which is PM-Surya Ghar and PM-KUSUM” [4]. A domestic content requirement (DCR) means a government scheme pays only when the panels use Indian-made cells, which keeps imported cells out of those schemes. Two customers each bought more than 10 percent of FY26 revenue and together bought 22.62 percent; in FY25 three customers bought 48.13 percent [1]. The filings do not name them.

The order book was ₹1,278 crore at 30 June 2026, up from ₹1,161 crore at 31 March [2] [3]. In the quarter ₹490 crore of new orders came in and ₹373 crore was delivered, so orders received were 1.31 times revenue billed [3]. By value the book is ₹665 crore of cells and ₹613 crore of panels [3]. Management counts only written, firm orders and says many customers order without a fixed contract, so the book understates repeat business [4].

The two incentive rows are not published, so no figure in this book includes or excludes them, and the filings do not let us size them. The revenue line is therefore partly set by policy, not only by customers. The next dated policy point is 31 December 2026, when a third-party guide says the net-metering exemption ends. What decides the price per panel and per cell

Management says panels sold at about ₹20.50 per watt-peak in the June quarter, and that cell prices were soft in that quarter and had risen a little by August [4]. It gives three reasons for the lower margin: more panels in the mix, softer cell prices and a higher cost of silver paste [4]. The cell price figure in the transcript is garbled, so we do not use it. Management also says it cut silver use by 20 percent in FY26 and targets a further 10 percent [4]. Chapter 2B tests whether the margin promise held.

Why does this matter? The schemes need Indian cells, so the price of a cell is set inside India. Management says the market price “is driven by the industry here rather than what the pricing is in China” [5]. That is why more listed cell capacity matters more to Websol's margin than world prices do. It also means a delay in the approved-list rule weakens cell prices, because imported cells can compete for longer. Chapter 2 weighs these forces against the strength of the plant.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

In plain words: Websol is strong because it sells scarce Indian-made cells at high margins and runs its lines full, and weak because margins are falling, cell supply is growing and the owners have a history of borrowing against their shares. This chapter weighs both sides with the filed evidence.

Where the strength is

The plant runs close to its limit: 92 percent of cell capacity and 81 percent of panel capacity in June 2026 [2]. Margins are high. FY26 EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), which this book also calls operating profit before depreciation, was ₹428.5 crore on revenue of ₹1,049.44 crore, a 40.8 percent margin [1]. Four listed peers earned 12 to 34 percent in FY26 on Screener's figures [27] [28] [29] [30].

The balance sheet moved from borrower to net saver. At 31 March 2026 borrowings were ₹117.97 crore and cash, bank deposits and liquid funds were ₹152.44 crore, so on our sum net cash was ₹34.47 crore [1]. On 4 August 2026 the company also repaid the whole IREDA term loan of ₹110 crore from internal accruals [2]. IREDA is the Indian Renewable Energy Development Agency, the lender named in the filing.

Return on capital is very high. ROCE (return on capital employed) is operating profit before interest and one-off items divided by average capital employed. Ours is ₹376.83 crore over ₹603.3 crore, or 62.5 percent, a recompute from the balance sheets [1]. The company quotes 65.7 percent [3] and Screener shows 63.2 percent [12]. The gap comes from the capital base each uses; all three are far above the 15 percent standard.

Where the weakness is

The margin is falling. Operating margin was 47.31 percent in June 2025, 40.78 percent in December 2025 and 33.70 percent in June 2026 [11]. In the June quarter revenue fell 7 percent from March and operating profit fell 14 percent [3]. Management gives the mix, soft cell prices and silver costs as the causes [4]. We did not find a filing that explains the 56 percent share price fall from the November 2025 high, so we do not state a cause for it.

Cell supply is growing. About 30 GW of cell capacity was listed on the approved list by April 2026 [17], and Websol's 1.2 GW is 4 percent of that. Our inference, not a filed fact, is that the scarcity behind the high margins shrinks as more lines list. The company has one site and one product family, so a line stoppage or a tender delay hits all sales.

Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, net of supplier credit. It has grown fast. Inventories rose from ₹34.27 crore to ₹159.86 crore in FY26, and management says they rose another 7 percent in the June quarter [1] [4]. Management also says customers' liquidity was strained for a time and orders were delayed, not cancelled [5]. Chapter 3 measures the effect.

Earnings are young. Profit after tax was a loss of ₹23.69 crore in FY23 and ₹120.96 crore in FY24 [8]. The FY24 loss included ₹105.34 crore of one-off items, mainly ₹96.86 crore for discarding or impairing old equipment [8]. Two profitable years are a short record for a business that sells into a price-driven market.

Governance and ownership

Promoters hold 12,90,37,310 shares, shown as 29.72 percent on the balance-sheet share count [10]. The 14 October 2026 filing prints the same shares as 29.32 percent, but its other three percentages (26.57, 21.92 and 4.65) all imply a base of about 43.4 crore shares, so 29.32 is probably a misprint; no gate depends on it [9] [1]. Public holders own about 66 percent, and the company's own call shows shareholders pressing management on communication and the change of plant location [4].

The IREDA loan carried the managing director's personal guarantee and corporate guarantees from two promoter companies, plus a pledge of 9,51,72,110 promoter shares [1]. The company also gave a letter of comfort of ₹5.29 crore for a related party [1]. Management says the CRISIL rating of BBB+ stable was unchanged in August 2026 [4]. One director declined reappointment on 22 September 2026; the record gives no reason [13].
