1. What the company does

Manorama Industries Limited (NSE: MANORAMA, BSE: 541974) turns the seeds of forest trees into specialty fats. It buys sal seeds and mango kernels from tribal collectors in Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra, and shea nuts and shea butter from West Africa.[9] A single plant at Birkoni in Chhattisgarh crushes the seeds, extracts and refines the oil, and then splits it into hard and soft parts, a step called fractionation.[11][3] The hard part, called stearin, and a cocoa butter equivalent (CBE) made from it are the main products.

CBE is a plant fat that melts and sets like cocoa butter. Chocolate makers replace a share of their cocoa butter with it to cut cost and to stop bars melting in hot countries. The company says the European Union, India and several other markets allow up to 5 percent CBE in chocolate that is still sold as chocolate.[3] Stearin and CBE together were 71.4 percent of FY26 revenue. The rest is olein, the soft part, plus shea, sal, mango and other butters sold to cosmetics and personal-care firms.[3]

The buyers are some of the largest food names in the world. The investor deck lists Ferrero, Mondelez, Mars, Hershey, Nestlé, Barry Callebaut, Bunge Loders Croklaan and AAK among food customers, and L'Oréal, The Body Shop and Lush among personal-care customers.[3] CARE Ratings notes that a supplier usually needs three to four years of approval before orders start.[9] That makes customers slow to win and slow to lose. It also means few customers carry a lot of weight: the top five were 55.34 percent of FY26 revenue from operations, up from 33.32 percent a year earlier.[11] Exports were 60 percent of Q1 FY27 sales.[3]

The company earns money from the gap between what it pays for seeds and butter and what it charges for the fractions. Contracts run 9 to 12 months, and the company says its selling prices follow its cost model and have stayed largely stable.[4] Consolidated revenue from operations was ₹1,366.74 crore in FY26, up 77 percent, and profit after tax was ₹224.92 crore.[1] The de-oiled cake left after extraction is sold as cattle feed and adds a little to margin.[3]

Capacity is the growth lever. The fractionation plants could process 47,500 tonnes a year at March 2026, after a 25,000-tonne plant started in July 2024 and 7,500 tonnes came from debottlenecking, which means removing small bottlenecks in an existing plant.[9][3] Another 4,500 tonnes from debottlenecking is expected in the third quarter of FY27, taking the total to 52,000 tonnes.[4] A further plan of about ₹460 crore over two to three years adds a refinery, a third fractionation plant, a cocoa butter alternative plant at Raipur and a shea processing factory in Burkina Faso, with the main projects targeted for the third quarter of FY28.[3][9][4]

Four things changed in the last four months. The company raised ₹500 crore in a qualified institutions placement (QIP), a sale of new shares to large institutions, at ₹1,470 a share on 2 July 2026.[11][17] Q1 FY27 revenue grew 39.5 percent and profit after tax grew 67.6 percent.[3] The company paid ₹20.64 crore after a customs inquiry on 29 July and admitted that it reported the matter late.[14] And in September the board approved up to ₹105 crore of new capital for nine overseas subsidiaries.[12][13] Chapter 2 weighs these facts.

Where the raw material comes from

Seeds are gathered by hand. Tribal women pick sal seeds and mango kernels in the forest, clean them in the village and sell them to collection centres that send them to Birkoni.[3] The seasons are short. Sal seeds arrive from May to June, mango kernels from May to July and shea nuts from October to November, so the company must buy a year of stock in a few weeks.[9] Shea nuts bought in West Africa take about 60 days to reach India, which adds to the stock sitting in warehouses.[9]

The company has ten overseas subsidiaries, eight of them in Africa for sourcing, plus units in the UAE and Brazil.[3] In Q1 FY27 it formed a Chad subsidiary and bought about 10 hectares in Burkina Faso for a shea and mango nut processing site, with regulatory approvals still pending.[4][3] Management says the African subsidiaries are cost vehicles with no sales of their own, so they add expense before they add profit.[4]

Who owns the company and who runs it

The company was incorporated in 2005, and Ashish Saraf is chairman and managing director.[9] The investor deck lists Vinita Saraf as vice chairperson and whole-time director, and Shrey Saraf, Gautam Pal and Ashok Jain, the chief financial officer, as whole-time directors.[3] CARE records that the Saraf family of Raipur founded the company.[9] The promoters held 54.32 percent until June 2026 and 51.38 percent after the QIP. Their share count did not change at 3,24,35,406, so the fall of 2.94 points comes from new shares being issued, not from selling.[16][17][23] Foreign funds held 4.16 percent and domestic funds 6.40 percent at September 2026, up from 3.22 and 2.63 percent in June.[23]

On 4 September 2026 the board appointed Dr. Rohini Tiwari as an independent director for five years and recorded that Mudit Kumar Singh's term ended on 5 September.[12] The company has no promoter pledge and has declared no encumbrance from 1 April 2026.[16]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • CBE (cocoa butter equivalent): a plant fat that can stand in for cocoa butter in chocolate. It costs less and resists heat better.
  • Stearin and olein: the hard and soft parts of an oil after it is split. Stearin is the main raw material for CBE.
  • Fractionation: the step that splits an oil into fractions by cooling it and filtering the solid part from the liquid part.
  • Tonnes a year (tpa): a plant's yearly processing capacity. Utilisation is actual output divided by this number.
  • Debottlenecking: removing small constraints in an existing plant to raise output without building a new one.
  • QIP (qualified institutions placement): a sale of new shares to large institutions such as mutual funds and sovereign funds.
  • Shea, sal, mango: tree seeds and nuts that give hard butters with a melting profile close to cocoa butter.
  • Hedging: a contract that fixes a future exchange rate. The company hedges about half of its net foreign-currency exposure.
  • Mark to market: restating a hedge contract at today's exchange rate, which can create a paper gain or loss before the contract ends.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Manorama's strength is a sourcing and customer system that is hard to copy, and its weakness is that the same system is narrow. The company earns about 35 percent on its capital, grows fast, and holds an A+ rating from CARE.[23][9] But a few customers buy most of its output, one plant makes it, and its stock needs a lot of cash. This chapter lists the evidence on each side and ends with what it means for an owner.

What the strengths are made of

The first strength is the supply chain. Millions of small collectors, a village-level network and ten overseas subsidiaries give the company direct access to seeds that rivals buy through traders.[9][3] CARE calls the procurement network a competitive edge.[9] The second strength is the customer lock-in. The placement document says relationships involve qualification cycles, co-developed formulations and plant-level trials, which create high switching barriers.[11] A chocolate maker that has tested a fat in its recipe will not change supplier lightly.

The third strength is returns. Screener shows a return on capital employed (ROCE), the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses, of 34.9 percent and a return on equity of 39.3 percent.[23] The company reports ROCE of 46.2 percent on its own basis and an asset turnover of 7.4 times for the standalone business in FY26, meaning each rupee of plant produced ₹7.4 of sales.[3] Debt fell: consolidated borrowings went from ₹482 crore to ₹356 crore and the debt-to-equity ratio, which is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, from 1.04 to 0.51.[23][1] CARE raised the long-term rating from A- to A in January 2025 and from A to A+ on 27 March 2026.[10][9]

The fourth strength is the cost advantage that CBE gives chocolate makers when cocoa is dear. Cocoa futures hit a record US$12,931 a tonne in December 2024, and chocolate makers shrank bars and used more cocoa butter equivalents.[31][32] Management says Q1 FY27 growth was volume-led, and the company reports stable selling prices.[4] The comparison with the global leader is useful but uneven. AAK, the Swedish specialty-fats group and a named customer of Manorama, earned a 13.5 percent operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, on its chocolate and confectionery fats over the last twelve months (SEK 2,072 million on SEK 15,315 million of sales), and its group return on capital was 20.1 percent.[29][9] Manorama's operating margin after depreciation was about 24.6 percent in FY26, so it earns more on each rupee of sales, but it sells a narrower, less finished product.[3]

What the weaknesses are made of

The first weakness is concentration. The top five customers were 37.87 percent of revenue in FY24 and 33.32 percent in FY25, then jumped to 55.34 percent in FY26, and the top ten were 69.66 percent.[11] The placement document says the company has not had a material loss of key customers in FY24, FY25 or FY26.[11] The rise came as the new 25,000-tonne plant filled up with large orders. But the cost of losing one account is now far larger than it was.

The second weakness is a single plant and a loose raw-material chain. All manufacturing is at Birkoni, and the company has no formal long-term contracts with the collector networks that supply it.[11] Nigeria banned raw shea nut exports from 26 February 2026 to 25 February 2027. Management calls it one of 22 shea countries and says its sourcing is not materially hurt.[30][4] We accept that for now, since the company has subsidiaries in ten African regions, but the ban shows how quickly a government can change the supply picture.

The third weakness is a related-party supply link. Manorama Africa Limited, a company in which the directors have significant influence and which the placement document calls a promoter-group entity, sold the company ₹191.75 crore of goods in FY26, ₹229.70 crore in FY25, ₹195.86 crore in FY24 and ₹76.84 crore in FY23.[1][2][11] The FY26 figure is 14.0 percent of consolidated revenue from operations and about 26 percent of the ₹733.35 crore cost of goods sold.[1][3] The company plans to turn this entity into a wholly owned subsidiary.[11] Until it does, a quarter of the cost of goods is bought from a firm the family influences, at prices the outside shareholder cannot test.

The fourth weakness is compliance and pay. A customs office in Indore summoned the company on 24 July 2026 over palm mid fraction imported from the UAE under the India-UAE trade pact. The company paid ₹20,64,17,893 on 29 July, issued a debit note to the supplier on 10 August, and told BSE that its disclosure was later than the 24-hour limit.[14][15] No show-cause notice had been issued, and how the cost will be booked is not published. Separately, performance bonuses to four members of the Saraf family rose to ₹11.66 crore in FY26 from ₹2.51 crore, which is 5.2 percent of profit.[1] Neither item is large on its own, but together they say governance deserves a watch.

The fifth weakness is demand risk from cocoa itself. Cocoa fell to about US$2,888 in February 2026 and was near US$5,600 in mid-October 2026.[31] When cocoa butter gets cheaper, the cost gap that favours CBE narrows. European grindings, a measure of chocolate demand, fell 7.8 percent to a decade low.[32] The company's own answer is that its products are formulated to customer recipes and prices have held.[4] We cannot test that with a published price per tonne, because the company does not give one.

Market share

No source we found publishes Manorama's share of the global CBE or specialty-fats market, so we make no market-share claim. The only comparison we can make from filings is with AAK, which is a much larger and more diversified group and also a customer.[29][9]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management guides often, so its record is easy to check. The pattern is clear. It beats what it controls, such as revenue and margin, and it misses or delays what depends on timing, such as capacity, working capital and disclosure.

FY26 revenue guidance was raised three times: from ₹1,050 crore plus in April 2025 to ₹1,150 crore plus in October and to ₹1,300 crore in January 2026. Actual revenue was ₹1,366.74 crore consolidated.[6][7][8][1] Guidance that is raised three times was set low, and we treat it that way. The EBITDA margin guide of 23% to 25% was beaten with 26.4%.[6][3]

The misses are about cash and timing. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could be wrong. This chapter takes each estimate in turn, names its weakest assumption and says what evidence would show the error and when we will see it. It then sets out the case against our own conclusion and the single claim on which the report hangs.

Weakest assumption behind the cash-conversion estimate

The estimate of ₹130 crore of operating cash flow assumes that inventory days hold near 362 as sales grow 25 percent. The history argues against it. Inventory rose in each of the last three years, by about ₹232 crore in FY24, ₹161 crore in FY25 and ₹177 crore in FY26, and management said in April 2025 that the working-capital peak was behind it.[2][1][6] The FY26 relief also leaned on trade payables rising ₹76 crore. The evidence that would show us wrong is a September half-year cash flow below zero. We will see it with the September 2026 results, which are due by 14 November 2026.[19]

Weakest assumption behind the EPS estimate

The base case assumes a 26 percent margin and 17 percent revenue growth in FY28. The weakest part is revenue. A 17 percent rise needs about 85 percent utilisation of 52,000 tonnes at stable prices, but management says 80 percent is the number to use, and the Q1 call walked back an 85 to 90 percent target on the new capacity.[4][3] The evidence that would show us wrong is Q3 FY27 utilisation under 80 percent after the debottlenecking, or a margin below 25 percent. We will see this in the December 2026 quarter results, due about February 2027.

Weakest assumption behind the target multiple

The base value assumes the market keeps paying 36 times FY28 profit. The data window is short. Screener's series runs only from 2 May 2025 and swings between 33.7 and 84.7 times, and the Indian processors trade at 15 to 21 times.[25][26][27][28] If the market decides that Manorama belongs with food ingredients and not with growth stocks, 27 times is not extreme. The evidence that would show us wrong is a sustained close above ₹2,149.50 with rising estimates, which would mean the market is rating the stock on a different scale from ours.

Weakest assumption behind the probability weights

The weights of 25, 45 and 30 percent are judgement, not measurement. The weakest is the 45 percent on the base case, because that case needs growth to slow from 77 percent to 25 percent without any shock to customers, cash or compliance. Moving ten points of weight between bear and bull moves the weighted value by about ₹190 either way. The next two results releases will change the weights more than any forecast.

The case against our conclusion: the red-team view

The sceptic's case is that the numbers are better than the business. Five customers bring 55 percent of sales, up 22 points in a year, and the filings do not name them.[11] A quarter of the raw material is bought from a firm the promoters influence. Three-year cash conversion is 0.11, and the company has just raised ₹400 crore of equity for working capital, which is the cash that its own operations did not supply. The customs payment shows weak control over imports and a late disclosure, and family bonuses jumped fourfold.[14][1] Cocoa has fallen from US$12,931 to about US$5,600, although it has roughly doubled from its February 2026 low, so the reason chocolate makers use CBE is weaker than it was and could weaken again.[31][32]

In that story the price falls to about ₹961, a 50 percent drop. Your 55 shares would lose about ₹52,300, which is 1.14 percent of net worth. The stop at ₹1,475.32 would cap the loss at about ₹23,973 on a closing basis, but a gap on results could pass through it. We take the case seriously and it is why we do not add. We do not accept it as the base case, because margins have held for six quarters, debt has fallen and the company has just been rated A+.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is KEEP_COMPOUNDING, used as a labelled exception, and the instruction is to hold the 55 shares and not add at ₹1,911.20. The strict conditions for that label are not met: gates are partial, only 2 of 5 operating indicators pass, and reward-to-risk is 0.22 against a floor of 3.0. The other labels do not fit either. TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING needs an extended price or an RSI above 75; the RSI is 46.79 and the price is 0.13 ATR above support. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP needs all gates to pass. REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER needs a broken support level, two failed operating indicators or a broken thesis, and we have one FAIL, a support that holds and a thesis that is intact on earnings.[33]

This differs from the earlier clean-slate note, which reached REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER. We read the rule more narrowly: it fires on a breakdown, on two failed indicators or on impairment. Our count is one FAIL and two WATCH, so the rule does not fire. The cash-conversion failure and the weak reward-to-risk are the reasons not to add, and they are different from the reasons to sell.

The position is small and at cost. You bought 55 shares between 3 and 16 September 2026 at ₹1,885.70 to ₹2,013.30, for ₹1,05,267.60 or ₹1,913.96 a share, and the position is worth ₹1,05,116.00, 2.30 percent of net worth.[34] A stop-out at ₹1,475.32 would cost about ₹23,973 from today, 0.52 percent of net worth. The TIER_3 risk-unit size for today's stop distance is about 2.19 percent of net worth, or about 52 shares, so you hold about three shares more than that size, which is too small a gap to act on. TIER_3 is the portfolio's label for a lower-conviction name, with an allowed 2 to 3.5 percent of net worth, and a risk-unit size is the largest holding whose loss at the stop stays inside the 50 basis point risk budget.

When would we add? Only at or below about ₹1,609, where the ₹2,009 target gives 3 to 1 against the ₹1,475.32 stop, and only if cash flow and customs tests are not failing by then. The 3.0 core floor sets that price, since the holding sits in the CORE_ALPHA bucket and no archetype qualifies; the turnaround archetype's 3.5 floor would put it near ₹1,594. That price is near the 63-day base low of ₹1,562.10 and the 200-day average of ₹1,542.04, so it is also a structural floor. At that price the stop distance is only 8.3 percent and the position could be rebuilt toward 3.5 percent of net worth, about 99 shares. The entry zone in the Layer 3 table is that add zone, not the current price.

What would make us exit? A close below ₹1,475.32 or a Stage 4 reading, which means sell what is left. The let-winners-run rule, which lets a strong holding grow to 22 percent of net worth, does not apply here, because it is meant for stocks with intact fundamentals and rising estimates in a larger position, and this holding is 2.3 percent of net worth with no gain. Estimates are rising, but cash conversion is not intact. Any swap into a challenger would be a separate, advisory decision, and no challenger has been tested in this report.
