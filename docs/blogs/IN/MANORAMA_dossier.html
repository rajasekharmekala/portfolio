1. What the company does

Manorama Industries buys the seeds and nuts of tropical trees, mainly sal, shea and mango, and turns them into hard fats and butters. Chocolate makers use these fats instead of, or alongside, cocoa butter, and the best-known product is called a cocoa butter equivalent, or CBE. The company calls its model “waste to wealth” because the seeds fall from forest trees that nobody plants[2]. It was founded in 2005 and is listed on both the Bombay and National stock exchanges[15].

The people who pay are food and beauty manufacturers. Chocolate and confectionery makers take about 60 percent of global demand for specialty fats and cosmetics about 20 percent, according to an industry study quoted in the company’s 2026 share-sale document[15]. In the quarter to June 2026 sales split 60 percent export and 40 percent domestic[4]. Supply contracts run nine to twelve months and are renewed through the year, so no single quarter carries most of the renewals[5]. A few buyers matter a great deal: the five largest took 55.34 percent of FY26 revenue, up from 33.32 percent a year earlier[15].

The money is made in the factory. Raw seeds and imported butters are cooked, pressed and then split by a solvent process called fractionation into a liquid part (olein) and a hard part (stearin). Blending those parts gives the CBE and other recipes that customers have tested and approved. Management says value-added products were 70 to 75 percent of FY26 sales and CBE alone about 30 percent[10]. Raw material is about half of sales, and the gross margin, the share of sales left after paying for raw material, moves in a 45 to 50 percent range[5]. Operating profit before depreciation and interest, called EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), was 26.3 percent of sales in the June 2026 quarter[4].

Plant size sets the ceiling on sales. Fractionation capacity was 15,000 tonnes a year until mid-2024, rose to 40,000 when a second plant opened[9], and reached 47,500 in FY26 after that plant was stretched by 30 percent[6]. Management plans another 4,500 tonnes around the third quarter of FY27, taking the total to 52,000, and guides to about 80 percent use of that capacity this year[5]. A larger programme of about ₹460 crore over two to three years adds a third fractionation plant, a refinery, a cocoa butter alternative plant and a seed-processing plant in Burkina Faso[7][5].

Two money ideas run through the rest of this report. The first is working capital, the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what suppliers have not yet been paid. Seeds are bought in the harvest season and kept for months, so inventory sat at ₹710.40 crore at March 2026, which Screener puts at 356 days of sales[10][1]. The second is dilution, the fall in each shareholder’s slice when new shares are issued: in July 2026 the company sold 34,01,360 new shares at ₹1,470 to raise ₹500 crore, lifting the share count from 5,97,08,530 to 6,31,09,890, or by 5.7 percent[15]. About ₹400 crore of the net proceeds is meant for working capital[15].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong where it is hard to copy. Raw material is the scarce part: sal and shea seeds grow wild across India and West Africa and must be collected, dried and moved, which favours a company with buying offices on the ground. The annual report counts ten operating subsidiaries across West Africa, the UAE and Brazil[10], and the company has now added Chad to the list and bought about 10 hectares in Burkina Faso for a seed plant that still awaits approvals[4]. A second barrier is customer approval. Chocolate and cosmetics makers test a fat across plant trials and co-developed recipes, which, in the company’s own words, creates high switching barriers[15].

The company also claims scale in its niche. The industry study quoted in the share-sale document names it India’s largest exporter of tree-borne specialty fats, sal fat and CBE for FY25 by value and volume[15]. That is a statement inside the company’s own offer document, so we treat it as a claim from the company’s side, not an independent ranking. What can be checked is the result: sales rose from ₹457 crore in FY24 to ₹1,358 crore in FY26 and the operating profit margin rose from 16 percent to 27 percent[1].

The business is weak where it depends on a few things going right. Five buyers now take 55.34 percent of sales[15]. A customs inquiry into imports of palm mid fraction under the India–UAE trade pact led the company to pay ₹20.64 crore on its own initiative in July and August 2026[16]. Nigeria, a large shea grower, has barred raw shea nut exports until 25 February 2027[27]. Management says its sourcing across many countries limits the damage[5], and the June 2026 quarter results show no sign of an effect[4].

Competitors and the price of cocoa

The offer document lists AAK AB, Cargill, Wilmar, Bunge, Mewah and 3F Industries among the larger makers of specialty fats[15]. AAK of Sweden is the closest listed comparison; its operating profit margin was 10.26 percent in 2025 against Manorama’s 26 to 27 percent[26][1]. The gap shows how much of Manorama’s margin comes from a narrow, specialist range and a low-cost seed supply, and it also shows how little room the company has to relax if rivals push into the same recipes.

Cocoa prices are the outside force that matters most. Cocoa fell about 70 percent from the December 2024 peak of $12,931 a tonne by April 2026, and makers that had swapped cocoa butter for vegetable fats during the spike had not reversed that change[28]. If cocoa keeps falling, real cocoa butter becomes cheaper and the price advantage of an equivalent narrows. We treat that as a slow, real risk to volumes and prices, not as an event that has appeared in the filed results; the June 2026 quarter was volume-led, and management called its prices stable[5].

The same industry study sizes the global specialty fats and butter market at $4,547 million in 2025, rising to $6,547 million by 2030 at about 7.6 percent a year[15]. That market size looks small beside AAK’s own 2025 sales of SEK 46,021 million[26], so the figure probably describes a narrower product class than AAK’s business. We use it only to say that demand is expected to grow in the high single digits, not to size Manorama’s share.

What this means for an owner

For an owner, the strengths are real but narrow. The company has a supply network of seed buyers that is hard to copy, recipes that customers have tested and approved, and a margin that stayed high through a period of very fast growth. The weaknesses are concentration, outside rules and the price of a competing commodity. Five buyers carry more than half of sales, so one lost contract would be felt at once. Customs and export rules are set by governments, not by the company. And if cocoa stays cheap, the price gap that makes an equivalent attractive could narrow. None of these has yet shown up in the filed results, which is why we score them as items to watch and not as failures. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 has a weakest assumption. This chapter names each one, says what evidence would show it wrong and when that evidence will arrive. The date of the next quarterly results has not been announced as of 23 October 2026. The listing rules normally require results within 45 days of a quarter end, which would mean about 14 November 2026 for the September quarter, but that is our expectation and not a confirmed date.

Central profit: the weakest assumption is margin and plant ramp

Our central path needs quarterly sales to rise to about ₹430 crore in the September quarter and to keep rising with the plant stretch, at a 26 percent margin. The June 2026 quarter was ₹404 crore, only 3.2 percent above the March quarter[4]. Freight and container costs are already lifting other expenses[5], and management itself would only commit to a 25 to 27 percent range[7]. If September-quarter sales come in below ₹415 crore or the EBITDA margin below 25 percent, our central path is too high.

This matters because every other number in the estimate follows from it. If FY27 sales growth were ten points lower, FY28 revenue would be about ₹1,870 crore, and with a 25 percent margin FY28 profit per share would be about ₹48.84 and the value at 40 times about ₹1,953. The September-quarter results will be the first real test, and the date is not yet announced, so we state it as expected and not confirmed.

The multiple: the weakest assumption is that 40 times survives slower growth

We apply 40.0 times to FY28 profit that grows only 14 percent from FY27. The stock traded at 31.2 times in March 2026 when the broad market fell and at 46.8 times in October 2025. If investors cap the stock near 30 times as growth slows, the central value falls to about ₹1,677 even with profit on plan. The evidence would be a month-end reading below 31 times on trailing profit.

The multiple matters as much as the profit. At 36.17 times the reward-to-risk at the close falls to 2.5, which is the floor for a turnaround name, and at 31.23 times the central value equals today’s price. Because the stock has traded between 31.2 and 46.8 times over the last twelve month-ends, a fall to the bottom of that range is not remote. We therefore set the position at a small size, and we treat a month-end reading below 31 times as a signal to re-underwrite the target.

The stresses are one variable at a time and then together. A multiple of 31.2 on on-plan profit gives about −0.1 percent, the bear profit at 40 times gives about −4.3 percent, and the two together give about −25.4 percent, below the stop. The stop defines the trading loss we plan for, which is 6.33 percent from the close. It is not the fundamental downside, which is the larger number, and it is the reason the position stays small and may not be added to on the chart alone.

Cash conversion: the weakest assumption is that FY26 repeats

FY26 operating cash flow of ₹259 crore was helped by trade payables rising by about ₹65 crore and receivables falling by about ₹45 crore, neither of which can repeat[10]. Inventory still grew by about ₹161 crore[10], and in the June 2026 quarter the stock build was ₹139.91 crore[3]. In the half-year to September 2025 operating cash flow was ₹189.07 crore, on a basis the call does not state[8]. The half-year to September 2026, whose cash flow statement date is not announced, will be read as evidence only. It triggers no automatic sale, because stock-building is seasonal and the annual durability test is the decider.

This is the hinge of the case, and it is why the cash test sits in chapter 7 as a sell trigger and not just a comment. A business that must raise ₹400 crore of new equity for working capital in a year when it reports ₹259 crore of operating cash flow has not yet shown that growth pays for itself. Management says the new forward-integration plants need only one to three months of working capital, against five to six months today, which would help if it proves true[7].

A rolling ratio can also pass for the wrong reason. With zero FY27 operating cash flow the consolidated three-year ratio would be 0.30, only because FY24’s loss drops out of the window. The durability test in chapter 7 therefore asks the FY27 cash flow itself to be positive and at least 0.40 of FY27 profit, about ₹124 crore at our central profit, against the 0.64 share (about ₹149 crore) that looks repeatable in FY26. Capital spending is separate: it is an investing outflow of ₹225 to 250 crore for FY27, funded from the share-sale money, bank lines and operating cash, and it adds funding risk but does not change operating cash flow itself.

Customers and compliance: the weakest assumption is that nothing breaks

Five buyers carry 55.34 percent of sales[15], and the offer document admits it may not reduce that share[15]. The customs inquiry has not produced a show-cause notice, and management expects no material harm beyond the ₹20.64 crore already paid[16]. The company disclosed it on 10 August after receiving the summons on 24 July, and the exchange asked it to explain[16][17]. The ₹20.64 crore equals 9.2 percent of FY26 consolidated profit but only 0.19 percent of the market value, so the money is small and the signal about controls and supplier checks is what matters. A second inquiry, a notice with a larger demand or the loss of a top-five buyer would show this assumption wrong.

The red-team bear case

The strongest case against owning the stock is this. The company grew sales 76 percent in a year and paid for it with inventory: three years of audited figures show ₹47 crore of cash for ₹385 crore of profit[12][11][10]. It then raised ₹500 crore of new equity at ₹1,470, mostly to fund working capital[15], which suggests growth will keep absorbing cash. Its customers are few, its main competitor commodity has lost 70 percent of its price, and a customs matter has already cost ₹20.64 crore. At 42.9 times trailing profit there is little room for any of this to go wrong. We weight this at 30 percent and size the position accordingly. The red team’s sharpest point is that a failed three-year ratio, one partly financed year of recovery and a 28 percent bear case are not outweighed by a nearby stop. We accept that the exception is weak, which is why it is time-limited, why an add is blocked until the FY27 cash tests pass and why no chart signal alone can reopen adds.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, and for the 55 shares you hold it means hold, add none, as a time-limited exception. The rule behind it is the technical routing rule: a company that clears the other layers but whose chart passes none of the momentum tests is held back from buying and not rejected. On the cash test, the plain ratio test is failed (0.12); the red line is treated as not tripped only under the working-capital turnaround clause, on our judgment (readings table, chapter 3). Without that clause a failed cash ratio would mean REJECTED, and we do not describe the failure as cleared. The exception lapses at the FY27 annual results (about May 2027, not confirmed) unless Trigger 2 passes, and a better chart alone never renews it.
