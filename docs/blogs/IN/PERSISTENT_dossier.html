Before you read

Persistent Systems is a Pune-based company that builds and rebuilds software for large clients, mostly in the United States. The business is excellent, but the shares are not yet priced for an attractive entry, so our verdict is to wait: no order is placed today, and a buy comes only after a fall to between ₹5,350 and ₹5,500 and a refreshed set of checks, not at today’s ₹5,832.50. Three things weigh on the price: a pending €1.27 billion purchase of Germany’s Nagarro, a profit drop caused by deal costs and a weaker margin, and falling analyst estimates. Waiting would prove too cautious if the deal closes on time and the margin returns to about 16 percent before the price falls into that zone. The idea fails on a close below ₹5,063.93.

1. What the company does

Persistent Systems writes, modernises and runs software for other companies. A bank, an insurer, a hospital group or a software maker hires Persistent’s engineers instead of building a large team itself. The company was founded in 1990 in Pune by Dr. Anand Deshpande, who is still its chairman and managing director, and his family holds about 30 percent of the shares.[6][20] On 30 September 2026 it employed 29,598 people, and about 84 percent of the hours worked were delivered from India.[2]

The clients pay for engineers’ time and, increasingly, for results. In the second quarter of FY27 (July to September 2026) Persistent earned $487.6 million, or ₹4,664 crore, a rise of 20.0 percent in dollars on a year earlier.[1][2] The most recent wins show what the work looks like: moving a large U.S. insurer’s policy system to a modern platform, running the technology of a U.S. cancer centre, and supplying engineers to a hyperscaler’s own team of ‘forward deployed’ engineers.[1] A hyperscaler is one of the very large cloud-computing companies. A forward deployed engineer works inside the client’s team rather than at the vendor’s office.

Three industry groups buy most of the work. Software, hi-tech and emerging industries are 42.8 percent of revenue, banking and insurance 33.7 percent, and healthcare and life sciences 23.5 percent.[2] By geography, North America is 81.4 percent, India 9.2 percent, Europe 7.1 percent and the rest of the world 2.3 percent.[2] The company counts 218 clients paying it more than $1 million a year, and four clients paying more than $75 million.[2] The five largest clients were 38.1 percent of revenue in the latest quarter and the ten largest 46.8 percent.[2]

Revenue comes from people, so the cost is also people. Pay and related costs were 68.8 percent of revenue in FY26.[4] That is why three numbers matter for this business: how many engineers it can keep busy, what each hour sells for, and how fast it can add staff without wasting money. The company earned an EBIT margin of 15.0 percent in the September quarter.[1] EBIT is operating profit after depreciation and amortization, and the margin is that profit as a share of revenue. Auditors list revenue on fixed-price contracts as a key audit matter, which means they spent extra time testing how profit is counted on jobs where Persistent bears the cost overrun risk.[4]

The Nagarro purchase in plain words

In June 2026 Persistent offered €81 in cash for every share of Nagarro SE, a Munich-based engineering firm with about €1 billion of yearly revenue and about 18,500 staff.[6] The offer was about 140 percent above Nagarro’s share price on 25 June 2026, the last day before the news.[6] News reports put the whole deal at about €1.27 billion.[25] By 6 October, holders of about 94.04 percent of Nagarro’s shares had accepted.[2]

The deal is not finished. Persistent is waiting for approval from the Reserve Bank of India and from investment regulators in Austria and Spain, and management expects to close in the fourth quarter of calendar 2026 or the first quarter of 2027.[3][2] If it closes, Persistent’s European share of revenue would rise to about 22 percent from about 9 percent in FY26 (the figure in the June deal material; the latest quarter was 7.1 percent), and the combined yearly revenue would be about $2.9 billion.[6] Persistent says that debt after closing should stay within conservative limits and come down over two years.[6]

The money comes from a mix of shares and debt. The plan caps new shares at $450 million of a total of $1,250 million.[2] The share part was done in October: Persistent opened a share sale to institutions on 21 October with a floor price of ₹5,550 and room for a discount of up to 5 percent, and on 26 October it allotted 7,683,981 new shares at ₹5,550 each, raising about ₹4,265 crore.[18][19][21] That adds about 4.9 percent to the share count.[2] The rest will be bonds and loans, and the rating agencies are watching them.[15][17]

What this report does with that information

A company that is about to buy something this big is two companies for an investor: the one that exists today, and the one that will exist after the deal. This report values both, and it keeps them apart whenever the numbers allow. The next chapter says why the business is strong, the chapter after that tests five operating numbers, and the later chapters put a price on the result.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • earnings per share (EPS) — the company’s profit divided by the number of shares, so it shows profit for one share.
  • EBITDA — earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating cash profit; we use the plainer EBIT margin in this report.
  • return on capital employed (ROCE) — yearly operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business; this report writes it as ROCE, short for return on capital employed.
  • free cash flow (FCF) — cash left after running the business and paying for equipment and software it must keep buying.
  • cash flow from operations — the cash a business earns from its normal work before it pays for investments or raises money.
  • price-to-earnings — the share price divided by a year of profit per share, so it is the number of years of today’s profit the market is paying for; it is written P/E.
  • debt-to-equity — borrowings divided by the money shareholders have put in and left in the business.
  • net debt — borrowings minus the cash the company holds.
  • gross margin — revenue minus the direct cost of delivering the service, as a share of revenue.
  • operating margin — revenue minus all running costs, as a share of revenue.
  • net margin — profit after everything, including tax, as a share of revenue.
  • working capital — the cash tied up in day-to-day operations, mostly money clients have not yet paid.
  • capital expenditure — money spent on buildings, computers and other long-lived assets.
  • dilution — the fall in each existing shareholder’s slice when a company issues new shares.
  • buyback — a company buying its own shares back from the market, which shrinks the share count.
  • guidance — the company’s own forecast for a coming period.
  • consensus estimate — the average of professional analysts’ forecasts for a number.
  • book-to-bill — new orders signed divided by revenue recognised; above 1 means the order book is growing.
  • relative strength index (RSI) — a 0 to 100 meter of how fast a price has been rising or falling; this report writes it as RSI, the relative strength index.
  • average true range (ATR) — the typical daily price swing in rupees; this report writes it as ATR, the average true range.
  • 20-day moving average — the average closing price of the last 20 trading days. The 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the same idea over 50 and 200 days; they are slow lines that prices tend to bounce off.
  • Stage 2 — the confirmed uptrend phase of the Weinstein stage scale, where the price sits above a rising 50-day average. Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend, where the price sits below falling averages.
  • base — a sideways stretch in the price, like a platform, built before a larger move. The pivot is the top of that platform. A breakout is a close above the pivot on heavy trading, and a retest is the pullback that checks the pivot from above.
  • stop loss — the price at which the reason for owning the stock is broken and the position is sold.
  • limit order — an order that fills only at the stated price or better, so it never chases the market. The entry zone is the band of prices where we would be willing to buy.
  • asymmetry — the ratio of possible gain to possible loss, measured from the entry price: (target − entry) divided by (entry − stop). It is also called reward-to-risk.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Persistent’s strength starts with growth that has lasted. Revenue in dollars has grown for 26 quarters in a row, from $131.0 million in the first quarter of FY21 to $487.6 million in the second quarter of FY27.[2] Over the five years from FY21 to FY26, dollar revenue grew 23.9 percent a year, and the latest quarter ran at 20.0 percent on a year earlier, or 20.8 percent after removing currency effects.[2][1] That pace is the main reason the market pays a high price for the shares.

The second strength is how much profit each rupee of capital earns. Screener reports return on capital employed (ROCE) of 34.4 percent and return on equity of 27.2 percent.[21] The company’s own figure for ROCE, which removes cash from the capital base, was 45.4 percent in the September quarter.[2] The audited annual report uses a stricter after-tax measure and shows 29.3 percent for FY26.[4] The three numbers differ because each defines capital and profit differently, but all three are far above the 15 percent that we treat as the quality floor.

The third strength is the balance sheet before the deal. Borrowings were nil at 31 March 2026, and the only debt-like item was lease obligations of about ₹477 crore, against shareholders’ funds of about ₹7,838 crore.[4][21] Cash and investments stood at ₹2,999.7 crore on 30 September 2026.[2] Cash collection is also good: cash flow from operations over the last twelve months was 96.6 percent of profit, up from 76.2 percent three months earlier, and the number of days clients take to pay fell from 61 to 50.[2]

The fourth strength is who owns the company. The promoter family holds 30.29 percent, and none of it is pledged to lenders in any of the last 12 quarters.[20][21] Institutional investors, meaning mutual funds, foreign funds and insurers, hold about 52.75 percent, so professional investors own much of the stock.[20] The company paid ₹40 a share in dividends for FY26, including an ₹18 final dividend.[4]

Where the evidence is thinner

Client concentration is rising. The five largest clients were 38.1 percent of revenue in the latest quarter, against 32.9 percent a year earlier, and the ten largest were 46.8 percent, against 43.2 percent.[2] In FY26 the single largest client was 9.3 percent of revenue.[4] Management said in April 2026 that the top five include two financial-services clients, one technology client and one healthcare client.[10] The large technology contract signed in the June quarter is worth more than $650 million over 6.5 years.[3] That contract is good news for revenue, but it also ties Persistent’s growth to one client’s budget.

Margins are under pressure. The EBIT margin was 15.0 percent in the September quarter, 130 basis points lower than a year earlier, and 100 basis points lower than in June.[3][2] A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. Management lists the causes: the yearly pay rise cost 180 basis points before a 40-point offset from lower share-based pay, the large contract’s software and cloud costs took 90 basis points, and lower use of staff took 20 basis points.[3] Staff use fell because the company hired ahead of the ramp-up: utilisation was 85.8 percent, against 88.2 percent a year earlier.[2]

Currency and treasury items cut reported profit. Persistent lost ₹455.8 million on foreign exchange in the quarter and ₹1,508.4 million over the half year.[3][2] Part of the loss comes from hedges, which are contracts to sell dollars at a fixed rate; Persistent held $500 million of them at an average of ₹95.6 per dollar.[3] The company discloses neither the share of revenue that comes from artificial-intelligence work nor the share that comes from hyperscaler partners, so those two numbers cannot be checked.

How Persistent compares with the sector

The sector is growing slowly, and Persistent is not. In the same quarter Infosys grew 1.5 percent and TCS grew 2.8 percent in constant currency, and Infosys cut its FY27 revenue growth forecast to between 1 and 2 percent from a range of 1.5 to 3.0 percent.[28] LTM, formerly LTIMindtree, grew 5.1 percent in constant currency, and Coforge’s dollar revenue rose 40.1 percent on a year earlier, with 3.8 percent organic growth on the quarter.[27][26] Persistent’s 20.8 percent is therefore among the fastest, and a share of it came from one very large contract.

The risk behind the slowdown is that artificial intelligence lets clients buy the same software work with fewer engineers. Persistent’s answer is to take on outcome-based contracts, where it promises a cost saving and shares in the revenue, and to sell its own AI tools.[3] That answer is untested at scale: the large contract that carries it lowered the margin by 90 basis points in its first full quarter.[3] The stock market has noticed. Over the year to 30 September 2026 Persistent shares rose 9.5 percent, while the Nifty IT index fell 17.7 percent and the Nifty 50 fell 8.1 percent.[2]

What this means for an owner: the business earns excellent returns and grows faster than most of its large listed peers, but the quality is already in the price. The shares trade at 49.5 times trailing earnings, against 21.5 times for LTM, 25.9 times for Mphasis and 40.8 times for Coforge.[21][30][31][29] The next chapter tests whether the operating numbers justify that premium.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

An engineering-services company is not judged like a factory or a bank. It sells people’s time, so the numbers that govern it are how fast revenue grows once currency is removed, how much new work has been signed, how much profit each rupee of revenue keeps, how dependent it is on a few clients, and whether it can hire and keep staff without wasting money. These five were chosen because each one can fail on its own. A company can grow quickly on one client, or grow without earning a profit, or sign work that it cannot staff.

Each measure is scored PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). The rule in our scoring file is that a company qualifies when the total is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 and no more than one measure fails.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

The test of management is whether it did what it said it would do. We looked at promises made one to two years ago and compared them with what the company reported. The record is mostly good, with one soft spot: the largest promise has been re-described since it was made.

The clearest example is the USD 2 billion revenue goal. In April 2025 the company wrote that it was optimistic about reaching “$2 billion in annual revenue by FY27”.[12] In January 2026 it said it was on track for “$2 billion by March 2027”.[11] In April 2026 the chief executive described it as “a run rate”. When an analyst asked whether that meant a quarterly rate, annualised, rather than full-year revenue, he said “Correct” and put the exit rate in the March 2027 quarter “in the range of $500 million or so”.[10] Quarterly revenue was already $487.6 million in September 2026, so the run-rate version is nearly met.[1] Our model puts organic revenue for the full year at about $1.97 billion, just under $2 billion, so the original wording is met only if Nagarro joins the numbers before March 2027.

The margin promise is weaker. In January 2025 CNBC-TV18 reported that the company expected about 14.5 percent for the year and aimed to improve it by 200 to 300 basis points by FY27, which implies about 16.5 to 17.5 percent.[13] FY26 came in at 15.6 percent, the first quarter of FY27 at 16.0 percent and the second quarter at 15.0 percent.[9][2] In April 2026 the chief financial officer said, “we have achieved whatever we had said” and called 16 to 17 percent “an aspiration”, not guidance.[10] We read that as a lower bar than the one set in January 2025.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter attacks our own work. For each estimate it names the weakest assumption, what evidence would show we are wrong, and when that evidence will arrive. Then it gives the strongest case against buying, and it names the one claim on which the whole view rests.

The FY27 and FY28 profit estimates

The weakest assumption is the margin. We assume an EBIT margin of 15.5 percent in the December quarter, 16.0 percent in the March quarter and 16.0 percent through FY28. The September quarter was 15.0 percent, and three costs were in it: the yearly pay rise, the lower use of staff, and about $4 million a quarter of software and cloud amortization from the large contract.[3] Management says the last cost will fall as the contract scales and as it finds savings, but it has not said by when.

The evidence that would show us wrong is a December-quarter margin below 15.0 percent, or amortization that stays above ₹1,500 million a quarter. We will know when the third-quarter results arrive, on the proposed board dates of 19 and 20 January 2027.[14]

The 5 percent gain from Nagarro

The weakest assumption is that Persistent’s own illustration is a fair guide. It assumes $1,498 million of debt, ₹93 per dollar, $1.14 per euro and a tax rate of 25 percent, and it assumes that 30 percent of the price is booked as assets and written off over eight years.[5] The final allocation is not known, and the real financing is a mix of $450 million of shares and bonds. Nagarro’s own profit also includes items that Persistent adds back: Nagarro’s reported EBITDA for CY25 was €118.7 million, but after adjustments it was €138.2 million, and with a foreign-exchange add-back €153.7 million.[5]

We would be wrong if Nagarro’s EBIT margin stays near its reported H1 2026 level of 9.8 percent (our arithmetic: €49.2 million over €500.5 million), because the illustration uses an adjusted 11.9 percent, or if the written-off asset charge is much larger than the illustration.[7][5] The first test arrives with the April 2027 results, which are proposed for 20 to 22 April, and a clear view needs the first full quarter after closing.[14]

The reward-to-risk ratio at ₹5,500 would fall to 1.82 to 1, because (6,292 − 5,500) ÷ (5,500 − 5,063.93) is 792 ÷ 436.07. To keep 2.5 to 1 at ₹5,500 the target must be at least ₹6,590.18, which at 40 times needs profit per share of at least ₹164.75. Our ₹6,600 clears that bar by only ₹9.82, so the entry depends on the Nagarro gain being real. That is why a later promotion to a buy needs two refreshed facts: the half-year cash conversion to September 2026, and an accretion calculation built on the financing actually raised, not on the all-debt illustration.

The target multiple of 40

The weakest assumption is that the market keeps paying 40 times a year of profit for a company whose forecasts are falling and whose sector is cutting its own. Infosys lowered its FY27 growth forecast to between 1 and 2 percent, and TCS grew 2.8 percent.[28] If Persistent’s premium to peers shrinks, the multiple could fall to 30 and the price to about ₹4,950 on our FY28 profit. That is 15.1 percent below today’s ₹5,832.50 and 10.0 percent below the ₹5,500 top of the entry zone, against a planned loss of 7.9 percent at the stop. A gap to that level would therefore pass through the stop. The multiple of 30 is the bear-case multiple that Chapter 5 weights at 35 percent, applied here to unchanged base profit per share of ₹165.

The evidence would be a close below ₹5,063.93, or a sharp drop on the day the third-quarter results are published. Both would be visible within days of the January 2027 results.

The stop loss and the position size

A stop loss is a price at which we sell, but it is only as good as the market on the day. Our stop is based on a close, and a bad piece of news, such as a failed deal or a downgrade, could open the stock well below it. If the stock gapped to the bear value of ₹4,440 after a buy at ₹5,500, the loss would be 19.3 percent. On a position of 3.5 percent of the portfolio that is about 0.67 percent of the portfolio, against a planned loss of 0.28 percent at the stop and a risk budget of 0.50 percent.

That is why the initial purchase is half-size, about 1.75 percent, and the second half waits for the third-quarter results. A half-sized gap loss would be about 0.34 percent.

The strongest case against buying

A sceptic would say five things. One, Persistent is paying about 140 percent over Nagarro’s pre-deal price for a firm that grows by single digits, and it is using leverage and a ratings watch to do it.[6][15] Two, reported profit per share will fall in FY27, from ₹119.74 (audited basic) to about ₹110 on our numbers, because of one-time costs and amortization.[4] Three, the top five clients are 38.1 percent of revenue and one contract is worth more than $650 million, so a single client’s budget decision could move the numbers.[2][3] Four, the price already assumes 19 to 21 percent growth in cash flow for ten years, and the sector has just cut its forecasts.[28][34] Five, the share sale at ₹5,550 means the newest big holders bought almost 5 percent below today’s price, and they may sell into strength.[21]

Our answer is that points one, two and four are in the price, so we ask for a lower price, and that points three and five are risks we size for, not reasons to avoid the business. We would be more worried if the margin fell in January or if the deal were delayed beyond March 2027.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, and the rule behind it is the distance to support. The price is 3.42 ATR above the 20-day swing low of ₹5,162.00, which is beyond the 1.6 ATR limit in our support ladder, so the rule says do not buy at market. The reward-to-risk ratio from today’s ₹5,832.50 to our ₹6,600 target is 1.00 to 1, well below the floor of 2.5. The stock is not currently held in the portfolio, so there is nothing to audit or trim.

No resting limit orders are placed today. The plan below applies only after three conditions are met together, and a fall to ₹5,500 on its own does not trigger a buy. The conditions are: first, a fresh momentum and support test; second, an updated target that still gives at least 2.5 to 1 at the entry price; and third, the refreshed half-year cash conversion and an accretion bridge built on the financing actually raised, as described in Chapter 6. If the price touches ₹5,500 before those are done, the reader does nothing except start that refresh, and buys only if all three conditions pass.

Once the conditions pass, the order plan is two limit orders. A limit order fills only at its price or better. We would place half of the initial size at ₹5,500 and half at ₹5,430, the midpoint of the ₹5,358 zone floor and ₹5,500. The initial size is half of the planned 3.5 percent weight, so 1.75 percent of the portfolio in total. We cancel any unfilled order if the Nagarro offer lapses or the stock closes below ₹5,350 on heavy trading before the orders fill. The second half is bought only after the third-quarter results of 19 and 20 January 2027 show an EBIT margin of at least 15.5 percent, and only at or below ₹5,500. A fill before those results carries result-day risk, and the half-size is there to limit it.

The size follows from the risk budget. TIER_3 gives a budget of 50 basis points, which means we are willing to lose half of one percent of the portfolio if the stop is hit. The stop is 7.93 percent below ₹5,500. Dividing 0.50 by 7.93 gives 6.3 percent, which is above the tier cap of 3.5 percent, so the cap of 3.5 percent applies. A full position stopped out at ₹5,063.93 loses about 0.28 percent of the portfolio, and the half-sized first position loses about 0.14 percent.

Once we own the stock, the let-winners-run rule applies. We do not sell a rising stock only because its RSI passes 70 or because its weight passes 15 percent. We raise the stop under the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average and keep the position until the trend or the business breaks. The weight ceiling for a winner is 22 percent.
