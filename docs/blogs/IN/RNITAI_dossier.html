1. What the company does

RNIT AI Solutions Limited is a software company based in Hyderabad. It sells software as a service — software that a customer rents and pays for by usage instead of buying outright — to state governments and, in smaller amounts, to companies. Its best-known product is a facial recognition system (FRS) that checks who a person is by comparing a live photo with a stored record. State education, welfare and skills departments use it to mark attendance for teachers, students, hostel residents and field staff. The company says it has processed more than 3 billion facial verifications and can handle about 15 million a day[6][7]. These are the company's own figures, and no outside audit of them was found.

The company on the stock exchange today is not the company that began life in 1985. It was listed as Autopal Industries, a Jaipur-based company classed as an auto-parts maker[1], and it went through a corporate insolvency process. The National Company Law Tribunal in Jaipur approved a resolution plan on 23 September 2024. Under that plan the old promoters' shares were cancelled with no payout, the public's holding was cut to one share for every four held, and RNIT Solutions and Services, the software business run by Raja Srinivas Nandigam, was merged in[4]. The name changed to RNIT AI Solutions in February 2025 and trading resumed on the BSE main board on 24 October 2025[6]. For that reason, almost none of the older history on the stock screens describes the business you own.

Who pays? Mostly state departments and state agencies, often through empanelled technology agencies such as APCFSS, APTS, RailTel and Goa Electronics, which the company lists as channels[6]. The investor deck names Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Goa, Meghalaya, Lakshadweep and other regions as customers, and it describes five ways of earning: pay-per-use SaaS, long-term government contracts, one-off platform builds, maintenance and support, and company subscriptions[7]. The auditor's report adds that contracts can be time-and-material, fixed-price, milestone-based or subscription, and it flags revenue recognition as a key audit matter because the company must estimate how much of a fixed-price job is finished[5].

The economics are unusual for a software company of this size. The company had 76 employees at the end of March 2026[5], so revenue per employee was about ₹68 lakh. Employee cost took only 15.4% of revenue, but a line called consultancy charges, which means outside developers and contractors, took 28.4%[2]. The business therefore leans on outsourced delivery. Operating profit before depreciation, which is the company's EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), was ₹20.68 crore in FY26, a margin of about 40%[2][1]. That margin is before the cost of building the product, which the company capitalises: it added ₹18.81 crore of product-development assets in FY26, almost as much as its pre-tax profit[5]. How an order becomes cash

A state order usually starts as a pilot and grows into a statewide rollout. The company's own wording is pilot-to-scale[7]. The newest example is the Notification of Award from Andhra Pradesh State Skill Development Corporation on 22 September 2026. It covers a multilingual interview platform for up to 20 lakh users, billed per minute of use, over a five-year agreement. The company classes it as above ₹25 crore including tax, its largest order to date[8][20]. Payment depends on actual usage and satisfactory delivery[8].

That usage-linked design is the heart of the bull case, because a platform that is already built can bill for every minute a student speaks to it. It is also the heart of the risk. Usage billing starts only after go-live, and the filing says set-up is planned within 15 working days of the award, subject to approvals[8]. Until the first bills are raised, revenue does not change. Government payers also settle slowly: RNIT's receivables equalled about 146 days of sales at March 2026[1][2]. For you, an order headline is a promise of future revenue and not yet cash, and the next three result dates will show how fast the promise converts.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A competitive position is the reason customers keep paying, and for RNIT the honest answer has two parts: some strengths are visible in the filings, and several important ones cannot be checked. The company calls itself the largest AI-based FRS software-as-a-service player in the Indian government sector and cites a national e-governance award in 2023 and a Prime Minister's award for public administration in 2024[6]. Those are company statements about a product called Siksha Setu, and the filings give no independent market-share figure. Treat the claim as a description of ambition. It does not prove a moat.

What can be checked is growth and margin. Revenue rose from ₹32.23 crore to ₹51.50 crore, profit after tax from ₹7.21 crore to ₹12.02 crore, and the operating margin — the share of revenue left after running costs — went from about 32% to about 40%[2]. The balance sheet holds more cash and fixed deposits (₹50.10 crore) than borrowings (₹18.04 crore), and the promoters have pledged none of their shares[5][13][30]. Interest cover, which is operating profit divided by interest cost, was 20.55 times[5]. Those are real strengths for a company of this size, and they explain why the stock attracted buyers.

The weaknesses sit in the cash records and the structure of the business. Customers owed the company ₹20.63 crore at March 2026, and ₹4.49 crore of that had been outstanding for one to two years[5]. Contract assets, which are work done but not yet billed, added ₹5.15 crore[5]. The company obtained no credit rating in FY26[15] and we found no analyst coverage, and it says it has held no analyst or investor meeting[7]. For an owner of 23% of the portfolio, this means there is no outside party checking the numbers except the auditor, who changed in FY26[5].

Competition and technology risk are named in the annual report but not measured. The management discussion lists fast technology change, cyber and data-protection risk, strong competition from established firms and start-ups, and changing AI and privacy rules[5]. Facial recognition used on citizens is a sensitive area, and a rule change in one state could stop a rollout. The filings do not say how many contracts are exclusive or how long they run, apart from the five-year Andhra Pradesh skills contract[8]. That gap is why the verdict leans on price and position size, not on a belief in a lasting moat.

What looks strong

Three things look strong. The first is speed of growth: FY26 revenue grew 59.8% and the first quarter of FY27 grew 25.7% on the year, so the trend is up even after the sharp seasonal fall[2][3]. The second is order flow. Since February 2026 the company has announced work or pilots in Meghalaya, Lakshadweep, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh, its FY26 wins include Goa[14][7], in December 2025 it rolled out its first project for the Andhra Pradesh Department of Legal Metrology[9], and in August it signed its first financial-technology development agreement, with 1FC Technology[14][22]. The third is the capital position: ₹47.16 crore of fresh equity in six months, and the company repaid most of its loans from the managing director, which fell from ₹7.50 crore to ₹1.22 crore[5].

Why it matters to you: these strengths support the idea that the business could keep growing at 25% to 35% a year. They do not prove it will, because the orders have no published rupee value except the Andhra skills award and one 'above ₹25 crore' class[8]. The strengths also do not make the price cheap. What looks weak

Four things look weak. Cash conversion is poor: over the last three fiscal years the company produced ₹4.31 crore of operating cash flow against ₹18.91 crore of profit, which is 0.23 rupees of cash for each rupee of profit[2][4]. Receivables are slow, at 146 days of sales, and 21.8% of them are more than a year old[1][5]. The company spends about as much on building product as it earns before tax, so profit does not turn into free cash: free cash flow (FCF), which is the cash left after buying assets, was negative ₹7.74 crore in FY26 and ₹23.81 crore in FY25[2][4]. Finally, revenue is lumpy. The June 2026 quarter was 55% below the March quarter, a seasonal pattern that also appeared a year earlier[3][1].

Governance carries smaller flags. The secretarial auditor noted that the board and its committees did not meet the independent-director rules until 28 May 2025, which the company blames on the insolvency process[5]. The managing director and a family member lent the company ₹2.93 crore during FY26 interest-free and ₹1.97 crore was still outstanding at year end[15][31]. The statutory auditor was replaced during FY26[5]. Three promoter-group members holding 0.07% in total asked to be reclassified as public, and BSE agreed on 6 October 2026[3][12]. That is a small change, and it lowers the stated promoter holding only slightly. When the exchange asked about the price movement in August, the company replied on 20 August that the move was purely market-driven and that no price-sensitive news was pending[11][14]. None of these is a fraud finding. Together they mean the company is still building the control record that larger listed companies already have.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report rests on assumptions, and a good report says which assumption is weakest and how we would find out. This chapter does that for three of them, gives the strongest argument against our own conclusion, and names the one claim on which the decision hinges. It matters because the decision involves 23% of your portfolio: a mistake in a large position costs more than a mistake in a small one.

Our growth forecast may be too low or too high

The weakest assumption in the earnings estimate is that revenue in the next three quarters grows 34% from the same quarters of FY26. The first quarter grew 26%, FY26 grew 60%, and nothing in the filings says which pace is more likely, because the company gives no guidance and publishes no order book[3][2][5]. We picked a midpoint. If growth is only 12% in those quarters, FY27 revenue would be about ₹58.5 crore, and the stock would be priced at about 86 times that year's profit.

What would show us wrong is the second-quarter revenue. Anything below ₹15.4 crore, which is 12% above last year's ₹13.72 crore, says the bear case is on track. Anything above ₹18.4 crore, which is 34% above, says our base is too cautious[1]. The result is expected in the first half of November, but this is not confirmed and the company has not announced the board date. Listing rules normally give 45 days from quarter end, which would be 14 November 2026. We recalled that rule and did not fetch it, so treat the date as an expected outer limit and not a confirmed one.

The cash-flow adjustment may overstate the problem

We say the three-year cash figure is 0.23 as reported and lower once loan money is removed. The filed cash-flow statement itself includes the change in borrowings inside operations[2], so that placement is the company's presentation and not an error we can prove. Banks sometimes treat an overdraft used for daily needs as part of working capital, and some companies follow that approach. The tax line is also untidy: ₹4.18 crore is shown as paid while the balance sheet still lists the same amount as owed[2]. On the tax-line reading, which treats that ₹4.18 crore as unpaid and leaves the overdraft inside operations, the three-year ratio is about 0.45, which is above the 0.25 line. The red-line fail therefore follows the as-reported 0.23 and is sensitive to the tax treatment. The loan-excluded figures are our own regrouping and not filed numbers.

What would show us wrong is the half-year cash flow statement that should come with the Q2 result (expected in November, not confirmed; board date not announced). If operating cash flow excluding loan lines is at least ₹2.5 crore for the six months, which is roughly half of the profit we expect, the shortfall looks like timing. If it is below ₹1 crore, the pattern is structural. Either way the weight rule requires a trim, so this uncertainty changes the size of the second step and not the first.

The locked-price reading is inferred, and two data points conflict
