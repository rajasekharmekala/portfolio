1. What the company does

This chapter explains the business in plain words, so that the numbers in later chapters have something to attach to. RNIT AI Solutions Limited is listed on the BSE under code 517286, and its trading symbol is RNITAI. The company describes itself as a technology firm that sells enterprise and government digital-transformation solutions, and it is based in Hyderabad.[1] Its main claim is that it is the largest AI-based facial recognition system (FRS) software-as-a-service provider in the Indian government sector. In other words, it sells software that identifies people from their faces, and it bills for that software as a subscription or per use rather than as a one-off product.

The business in one paragraph

Think of the company as a software utility that governments rent. An agency signs up, the company's identity software verifies citizens against records, and the company earns money on the volume of checks it runs. The company says it has processed billions of identity verifications across more than 300 government departments, and it describes that as 3 billion-plus.[1] That figure is the company's own claim and we have not audited it. The annual report describes the group as an AI-first technology firm built on four pillars: artificial intelligence, trusted digital identity, digital governance platforms, and AI-powered software-as-a-service products. It also names a conversational AI assistant called NIA and an education product called Faceify Edu.[8] What this means for an owner is that the revenue is a mix of government projects, a subscription or usage stream, and newer contracts in financial technology and training.

Where the revenue comes from

Recent contract news shows the mix. Second, the rating document is describing an older company, so it should be read as evidence about the company's disclosure habits rather than about its current product.

The terms behind the numbers

Several terms come up repeatedly. Return on capital employed (ROCE) measures how much operating profit the business earns on the money tied up in it. It is like asking how much rent a building earns on what it cost to build, and a floor of 15 percent means the business must earn at least that before tax and interest. Cash from operations (CFO) is the cash the business actually generates from its trading. Profit after tax (PAT) is the accounting profit after tax. Comparing the two over three years tells us whether reported profit is turning into cash. A ratio of 0.70 or more is the floor. Debt to equity (D/E) compares borrowings with the shareholders' funds, and the floor is below 0.70.

On the price side, the 50-day and 200-day moving averages are the average closing price over the last 50 and 200 sessions. A stock trading above both, with both sloping upward, is in what Stage Analysis calls Stage 2, the markup phase. Average true range (ATR) is a measure of how far the price usually moves in a day, here 14 days averaged. Distance from support is measured in ATR, so a stock 1.0 ATR above its 20-day average has moved about one normal day's range above that level. RSI, the relative strength index, is a 14-day momentum gauge from 0 to 100, and readings above 70 are called overbought. The asymmetry ratio compares how far the price could rise to a target with how far it could fall to a stop. Where a figure could not be verified from a cited source, this report writes N/A rather than estimating it.

What this means for an owner: the company is a small government-technology business that has changed shape, and its track record under the new model is only a few years long. That is the first thing to hold in mind before reading the gate table.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Strength and weakness here are judged against the gates, not against the story. A business can be growing fast and still fail the test of whether it earns enough on its capital and turns that profit into cash. This chapter sets out both sides so that the verdict is not decided by one number.

Revenue from operations rose from ₹32.23 crore in FY25 to ₹51.5 crore in FY26, and profit after tax rose from ₹7.21 crore to ₹12.02 crore.[7] Working that out, revenue grew by about 60 percent and profit by about 67 percent. Return on capital is the weak point

The return numbers do not match the profit story. On the audited FY26 basis, ROCE is 12.02 percent and return on equity is 8.64 percent.[3] The reason is that the company's equity has grown fast. A small debt load and no pledged shares

Revenue is lumpy

What this means for an owner: the business is improving on profit and cash flow in the latest year, but it has not yet shown that the improvement is durable, and the return on capital is still below the bar that the framework sets.

The framework's formula is (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). Read it as a fraction: the top is how far the price could rise to the target, and the bottom is how far it could fall to the stop. Each line below shows a figure used in the calculation and where it comes from.

The inputs

  1. Price (ledger close, 12 October 2026): ₹108.00.[4]
  2. Stop, tactical rule: lower of the 20-day swing low ₹85.69 and the 20-day and 50-day averages, which is ₹85.69, minus 0.5 times ATR of ₹4.24, which is ₹2.12. Stop equals ₹83.57 (ledger-derived).
  3. Downside to stop per share: ₹108.00 minus ₹83.57 = ₹24.43, which is 22.6 percent of the price, or 5.8 ATR.
  4. Central target: N/A. The framework permits forward EPS (earnings per share, which is profit divided by the number of shares) times a three-year median P/E, or a measured move. A measured move is a target set by adding the height of an earlier price range to the level where the price moved above it. Forward EPS is not published (Trendlyne shows no estimates), the three-year median P/E is not in our cached sources, and no prior high could be verified from the ledger's 63-session price history.[4]
  5. Target bull: N/A, for the same reason.
  6. Upside to target: not computable. The stored target of ₹125.00 in the prior run was not verified in this run and is not used.
  7. Asymmetry ratio: N/A. Required floor for Tactical Momentum is 3.5 to 1. The gate result is FAIL_ASYMMETRY on the target test.

The range

The range has one known end and one unknown end. The downside end is known: the price could fall ₹24.43 per share to the stop. The upside end is unknown, so no range can be drawn yet. For the ratio to reach the 3.5 floor, the target would have to sit more than three and a half times that downside above the price.

If the number is off

If a verifiable target is later published, the ratio would be recalculated from the same price and stop, and the gate would be re-run. If the ledger price or stop changed, the downside end would move with it. The verdict does not rest on this test alone, because the return and cash gates also fail.

What this means: the downside arithmetic is clear and the upside arithmetic cannot be done honestly. Until a target is anchored, the position cannot be tested for asymmetry, and the framework does not allow a guessed target to stand in its place.
