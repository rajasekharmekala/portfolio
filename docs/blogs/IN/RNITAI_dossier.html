1. What the company does

RNIT AI Solutions Limited builds software that lets a government check, with a face scan, that the right person turned up. A teacher marks attendance by looking at a phone camera instead of signing a register. A welfare department confirms that a beneficiary is a real person before money moves. The company calls the core technology a facial recognition system, which means software that matches a face on a camera to a stored record. Its corporate office is in Hyderabad and its managing director is Raja Srinivas Nandigam.[15][24]

The annual report describes four product families: artificial intelligence, trusted digital identity, digital governance platforms and AI software sold by subscription to small and mid-sized firms.[15] The newest product is NIA, a conversational AI engine that runs mock job interviews in English, Hindi and Telugu.[30] The company also sells Faceify Edu for schools and Faceify Corp for staff attendance and visitor control.[15] It reports one business segment, so the accounts do not split revenue by product.[16]

Almost every customer is a state government body. The Q4 FY26 investor presentation lists work in nine states and union territories: Andhra Pradesh, Assam, Bihar, Goa, Lakshadweep, Meghalaya, Odisha, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh.[27] Many contracts come through state technology agencies, such as Andhra Pradesh Technology Services and Telangana Technology Services, which buy on behalf of departments.[28][33] Export sales were ₹17.67 crore in FY26, or 34.3% of revenue, but the filing does not name the countries or customers.[7]

Three pricing models matter. The first is a paid pilot, followed by a statewide rollout. The second is pay-per-use, where the state pays for each scan or each minute of use. The third is a maintenance and support contract.[24] Pay-per-use sounds attractive because revenue keeps arriving as long as people use the system. It also means a headline contract value is a ceiling, not a promise. The January 2026 award from Andhra Pradesh says the project starts in the lowest tier, up to ₹50 lakh, and moves up only as usage rises.[28]

RNIT did not list through an offering of shares. The listed company was Autopal Industries, an auto-parts maker pushed into insolvency when an operational creditor, Tack Innovations, filed an application over unpaid dues. On 23 September 2024 the National Company Law Tribunal in Jaipur approved a plan by Raja Srinivas Nandigam and Vivek Kumar Ratakonda. The plan cancelled the old promoters’ shares, cut public shareholders’ holdings by 75% and merged the unlisted RNIT Solutions and Services Limited into the shell, with 1 April 2023 as the effective date.[18] Owners of RNIT Solutions received five new shares for each share they held, 7,13,74,990 shares in all.[19] The company took its present name on 17 February 2025.[18]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Facial recognition system (FRS): software that matches a face on a camera to a stored record.
  • SaaS (software as a service): software rented by subscription or by usage instead of sold once.
  • NCLT: the National Company Law Tribunal, the court that approves insolvency plans in India.
  • Resolution plan: the rescue plan for a bankrupt company that the court approved in September 2024.
  • Goodwill: the value booked for a purchase above the assets acquired; it is not cash and is not written off each year.
  • Preferential allotment: new shares sold to chosen investors at a negotiated price, outside a public offer.
  • Receivable days: how many days of sales are still unpaid on the balance sheet.
  • Crore and lakh: ₹1 crore is ₹100 lakh, or ₹1,00,00,000.
  • Net worth: shareholders’ money, equity capital plus reserves.
  • Reverse DCF: a calculation that starts from today’s price and solves for the growth rate the price assumes.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share, which is the company’s profit divided by its number of shares.
  • ROCE stands for return on capital employed, which is operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow, which is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment and software.
  • Operating margin is the share of each rupee of sales left after the costs of running the business.
  • Debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders’ money.
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in customer bills, advances and stock, less what the company owes suppliers.
  • Guidance is the company’s own published forecast for a coming period.
  • RSI stands for relative strength index, a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been moving; above 70 is called overbought.
  • ATR stands for average true range, which is the average daily price swing in rupees.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend phase on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • Base is a sideways stretch of trading that a price builds before a move, and a pivot is the top of that base, the level the price must clear to move up.
  • Breakout is a move that takes the price above a base or pivot on heavy volume.
  • Measured move is the height of a base projected above its pivot to give a price target.
  • Entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Support is a price level where buyers have stepped in before, so a fall often pauses there.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business looks strong on its own reported numbers and weaker once you ask how much of the profit has become cash. This chapter separates what is hard to copy from what is hard to trust. Each claim carries its source, and where the source is the company’s own marketing, the text says so.

What looks strong

Margins are high. In FY26 profit before other income, interest and depreciation was 40.2% of revenue. The company has few staff, 76 at 31 March 2026, and buys outside help, ₹14.60 crore of consultancy charges, only when a project needs it.[3][9][7] Revenue per employee was about ₹67.8 lakh (5,149.88 divided by 76). That is the arithmetic of reused software, not of hours billed.

Reuse is the second strength. The same matching engine is set up for school attendance in Telangana, panchayat staff in Goa and education staff in Lakshadweep.[24] Each new state should cost less than the first. The company also won the Andhra Pradesh interview platform as the lowest bidder, called L1, in an open competition run by a state agency, and it holds an extra work order on the same skills portal for resume-building and job-discovery modules.[28][29] Winning at the lowest price shows the product is competitive. It also shows the buyer is shopping on price.

The balance sheet is the third strength. Debt-to-equity is 0.13 and interest is covered 20.55 times. Cash plus long fixed deposits of ₹46.79 crore exceed borrowings of ₹18.04 crore.[4][9] No promoter shares are pledged. The managing director holds 50.1% of the company, promoters together hold 63.2%, and 21.45% of the shares are locked in.[36][37] A company that must wait months for government payments needs exactly this cushion.

What is unproven

The annual report names its own threats: established technology firms, niche suppliers and start-ups, price pressure and fast-moving technology.[16] Because niche suppliers and start-ups are on that list, the moat cannot be the matching algorithm alone. It is the relationship with state agencies, the references from earlier rollouts and speed of delivery. Those are real, but they are hard to measure from outside. The company cites a National e-Governance Award and a Prime Minister’s Award for Excellence in Public Administration 2024.[24] We could not verify either award in a primary government source, so we treat both as company claims.

The company says it has processed more than 3 billion facial verifications, serves more than 300 departments and corporate ecosystems, and can handle up to 50 million identifications a day.[20][24] These are unaudited operating claims. They show reach, not revenue. FY26 revenue of ₹51.50 crore spread across 300 customers would average about ₹17 lakh each (5,149.88 divided by 300), so the revenue probably sits with a handful of large departments. The company does not disclose which ones.

Revenue quality and cash

Receivables are the weak spot. Customers owed the company ₹20.63 crore at 31 March 2026, equal to 146 days of sales. Of that, ₹6.22 crore was more than six months old and ₹4.49 crore was between one and two years old. No loss allowance is recorded.[6] Add ₹12.51 crore of advances, including ₹5.15 crore of contract assets (work done but not yet billed), and ₹16.32 crore of other current assets. About ₹49.46 crore, or 96% of a year’s revenue, sits in money the company is waiting to receive or recover.[6]

Capitalised development is the second concern. Intangible assets, mostly product development, grew by ₹18.81 crore in FY26 against total employee cost of ₹7.95 crore.[8][7] The filing does not split those additions between salaries, consultancy and other costs, so we cannot see how much spending bypassed the profit and loss account. Goodwill of ₹39.98 crore and intangibles of ₹29.15 crore together equal 49.7% of net worth.[4] Goodwill arose from the share swap in the 2024 merger. It is tested for impairment each year, but it is not cash and cannot be sold.[19]

Governance and funding

Several signals call for care. The statutory auditor changed from Nitender Kumar and Associates to BRAND and Associates LLP in March 2025, and then to M S P R and Co. for FY26, two changes in about thirteen months.[21][14] The FY26 opinion is unmodified.[14] Loans from the managing director stood at about ₹7.5 crore at 31 March 2025 and ₹1.22 crore a year later, after ₹5.66 crore of repayments in a year when the company raised ₹47.16 crore of new equity from outside investors.[12][10] The Axis Bank term loan is secured on properties owned by directors.[13]

The exchange has also queried the share price. BSE sent a surveillance query on 4 February 2026 about price movement, and the company answered that the move was market-driven.[34] A similar clarification followed in August 2026.[32] Neither answer is a finding of wrongdoing. Both are reminders that this is a stock with no analyst coverage that moves sharply on small volumes.

Rules for face data

India has no dedicated biometric law. Face data falls under older information-technology rules that do not apply to the State, and under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act of 2023, whose Rules were notified on 14 November 2025 with an eighteen-month phase-in.[44][43] Government bodies can process personal data for services and benefits under the Act’s State exemptions, which helps today’s customers. A later rule that limits face matching for staff or student attendance would hit the company’s main product. We treat that as a slow-moving risk, not a near-term one.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a weak joint. This chapter names the weakest assumption for each estimate, then gives the red-team bear case, which is the strongest honest argument against our own verdict, and the single hinge claim on which the whole decision turns.

Weakest assumption in each estimate

The FY27 estimate assumes that Q1 is always the same share of the year. Two data points do not prove a seasonal pattern. Q1 was 12.1% of FY26 revenue, but FY26 itself includes the first ramp of new contracts, and Q4 FY26 revenue of ₹17.62 crore was 34% of the year. The measured-move target treats the move from the ₹55.68 base low to the ₹130.20 high as a template for what comes next. The expected value assumes our probabilities. They are judgement. We cannot test them, so we state the sensitivity instead: a shift of five points of probability from the base case to the bear case lowers the expected price by about ₹5.0.

The strongest case against holding is this.
