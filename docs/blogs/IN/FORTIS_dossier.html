1. What the company does

Fortis Healthcare Limited is a listed Indian hospital operator with a diagnostics business attached. On 30 June 2026 it ran 36 healthcare facilities in 12 states, with over 6,150 operational beds (this count includes beds it manages for other owners) and about 400 diagnostic labs.[1] Patients and their insurers pay it for inpatient stays such as heart, brain, kidney, bone and cancer treatment, and for outpatient visits and tests. Hospitals produced ₹2,187 crore of the ₹2,545 crore group revenue in the June 2026 quarter, which is about 86 percent; the rest came from the Agilus lab chain.[1]

The number that drives a hospital is the occupied bed. Revenue per occupied bed per year is called ARPOB (average revenue per occupied bed), and it was ₹2.71 crore in the June quarter, while 69 percent of beds had a patient in them.[1] Revenue therefore follows three things: how many beds the group has, how full they are, and how much each full bed earns. In the quarter the number of occupied beds rose 16.7 percent to 3,418, but earnings per occupied bed rose only 2.6 percent, so almost all of the growth came from adding beds, either by building them or by buying hospitals.[1][2]

Who pays matters as much as how many patients come. Some pay themselves, some are covered by private insurers and the outside firms that process their claims (called third-party administrators, or TPAs), and some belong to government schemes: CGHS for central government staff and ECHS for ex-servicemen.[2] The government schemes set their own price lists. On the June call the chief executive said that a 30 percent discount to maximum retail price on chemotherapy drugs under these schemes cut cancer-revenue growth from about 23 to 24 percent to about 5 percent.[2] International patients are a smaller but richer stream: ₹174 crore in the June quarter, about 8 percent of hospital revenue.[2]

Control sits with IHH Healthcare, whose subsidiary Northern TK Venture holds 31.17 percent and is the only promoter.[16][15] IHH put in ₹4,000 crore in November 2018 for roughly 31 percent, after the earlier promoters, the Singh brothers, had left the board in March 2018.[21][18] In a statement filed on 28 September 2026, three days after the Supreme Court order, IHH said it plans to raise its stake to 51 percent over three to five years and to take Fortis and its own Gleneagles hospitals to about 10,000 beds by 2031.[21] Fortis already manages five Gleneagles India hospitals for a fee of 3 percent of their revenue, which brought in about ₹6 crore in the June quarter.[2][13]

Growth is built three ways. The first is brownfield expansion, which means adding floors or towers to hospitals that already run at high occupancy, and management plans about 1,800 such beds over four years.[4][1] The second is buying hospitals: it paid ₹462 crore for Shrimann Hospital in Jalandhar, bought People Tree Hospital in Bengaluru, took a long lease on a Greater Noida hospital, and bought out the private-equity holders of Agilus for about ₹1,778 crore.[13][3][11] The third is management contracts, such as a new 300-bed hospital in Cuttack where Fortis operates without putting in money.[1] Spending on buildings and equipment is guided at about ₹900 crore a year, of which 60 percent is upkeep.[4]

How one hospital earns its profit

A hospital has large fixed costs: the building, the machines, the nurses and the doctors on salary or contract. Once those are covered, each extra patient adds profit at a high rate. That is why occupancy matters so much. Fortis says 14 of its facilities earned a margin above 20 percent in the June quarter and together made 70 percent of hospital revenue; a year earlier 13 did.[2] FMRI, the flagship Fortis Memorial Research Institute hospital in Gurgaon, ran at about 85 percent occupancy in November 2025, and the chief financial officer puts its margin near 25 percent.[7][2]

The weaker end of the group is where the upside and the risk sit. Manesar has 187 operating beds at about 60 percent occupancy, and both Manesar and Greater Noida earn margins of about 10 percent or less; management says both should reach the mid-teens by March 2027.[2][29] The BG Road hospital in Bengaluru and Mulund in Mumbai have low occupancy that management expects to improve.[2] Newly bought or opened units pulled the group margin down by about 0.4 points in the quarter, and management hopes they will add about 1 point once they mature.[2]

The people cost: doctors and the new share-option plan

Doctors are the scarce input in a hospital. In April 2026 Fortis introduced an employee share-option plan (ESOP) for roughly 55 to 60 percent of its doctors and administrative staff.[2] The charge was about ₹31 crore in the June quarter and management expects about ₹40 crore a quarter next, falling to about ₹30 crore and then ₹25 crore.[2][1] Management says the plan was designed over about 18 months to tie doctors' interests to the company, not as a reaction to rivals poaching doctors in Delhi NCR.[2] For an owner the plan has two sides: it lowers the risk that star doctors leave, and it adds a cost that holds back reported margin for about three years.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is the profit a business makes from its operations before financing costs and accounting write-offs; Fortis reports it as operating EBITDA, which leaves out other income.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is the yearly profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses; Fortis shows 13.4 percent, and the portfolio's quality floor for a compounder is 15 percent.[15][47]
  • EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided across all its shares; Fortis earned ₹13.80 in FY26.[15]
  • FCF or free cash flow is the cash left after running the business and paying for buildings and equipment.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been moving; below 30 is called oversold, which means the price fell unusually fast, and above 70 is called overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the average daily price swing in rupees, used here as a ruler for how far the price sits from its floor.
  • Stage 2 is the rising phase of the four-stage trend scale used in this portfolio, while Stage 4, the falling phase, is where the share sits today.
  • A base is a flat shelf of prices a share builds before a new move, and the pivot is the top of that shelf; a breakout is a close above the pivot on strong volume.
  • A stop loss is the price at which the idea is judged wrong and the position is cut; a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better; the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss: the target minus the price, divided by the price minus the stop.
  • Net debt is borrowings minus cash, and debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money; the first shows what is owed after using the cash, the second how much of the business is funded by lenders.
  • Guidance is the company's own published forecast, and a consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for the same number.
  • Capital expenditure is spending on buildings, machines and equipment, working capital is the cash tied up in day-to-day operations, and cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before financing.
  • Operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue, and price-to-earnings is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence for the business is the margin climb. Operating profit was 17.5 percent of revenue in FY23 and 22.8 percent in FY26, on revenue that rose from ₹6,298 crore to ₹9,128 crore.[15][3] Hospital margin alone went from 20.5 percent in FY25 to 22.2 percent in FY26, and Crisil, the rating agency, expects the group margin to stay at 22 to 25 percent over the medium term.[3][13] That is the pattern of a chain filling its beds and spreading fixed costs over more patients, and the margin stayed between 21 and 24 percent in each of the last five quarters.[15]

The second strength is financial safety. Crisil upgraded the long-term rating to AA+ with a stable outlook in September 2025 and reaffirmed it in January 2026.[14][13] Net debt was ₹2,233 crore at 30 June 2026, which is 1.01 times a year of operating profit and 0.21 times shareholders' money.[1] Cash from operations was ₹1,601 crore in FY26 against ₹945 crore spent on buildings and equipment.[10] A parent with deep pockets adds to this: IHH's chief executive has spoken of fresh equity of about ₹10,000 crore for growth, as the Fortis chief financial officer told analysts in May.[4]

The weaknesses are about returns, rules and reputation. Returns first: ROCE is 13.4 percent and return on equity 11.2 percent, below the 15 percent the portfolio asks of a compounder, because the group has spent heavily on acquisitions and the capital has not yet earned its keep.[15] Borrowings in the accounts rose from ₹1,155 crore in March 2024 to ₹3,473 crore in March 2026, and interest cost rose from ₹131 crore to ₹314 crore over the same two years.[15] Rules next: government schemes and insurers set prices for a large share of patients, and in October 2026 the pricing authority approved in principle a cap on trade margins for non-scheduled cancer drugs at 30 percent of maximum retail price.[31][32][2]

Reputation is the third weakness, and it is specific to Fortis. The auditor draws attention in both the standalone and the consolidated accounts to an ongoing investigation by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office into transactions that date from before the board changed in 2018, and to a 2022 Supreme Court order that let the Delhi High Court consider appointing forensic auditors to examine dealings between Fortis and RHT Health Trust.[12] The group also lists ₹2,909.6 crore of contingent liabilities, which are claims it disputes and has not booked; ₹1,670.6 crore of that is income tax.[11] None of this has stopped the hospitals operating, and Crisil says the pending litigation may not have any material implication, but it limits how highly the market will value the profits.[13]

How strong is the competitive position?

Fortis competes with Max Healthcare, Apollo, Medanta, Narayana Health and Rainbow in a market where licences, doctors' reputations and the cost of building a tertiary hospital keep newcomers out. Its edge is a network of clinically led hospitals clustered in Delhi NCR, Punjab, Mumbai, Bengaluru and Kolkata, where it can share doctors and marketing.[2] The edge is real but not unique: Max earns a higher operating margin (25 percent in its latest quarter against 21 percent for Fortis) on a similar return on capital, and Medanta earns a higher return on capital (17.4 percent).[36][38][15] Management itself says that when new hospitals open in a micro-market, as happened in Noida, local doctors move and margins wobble.[2]

There is also a quality edge that cannot be measured from outside. Four facilities are accredited by the Joint Commission International and 34 by India's NABH (the national hospital accreditation board).[15] The brand itself was bought back for ₹200 crore in April 2025, which ends a provision of 0.25 percent of revenue for a brand licence fee and makes the brand an owned asset.[13] Overall the moat is moderate: strong in the 14 large hospitals, still being built in the newer ones, and exposed to payers and regulators who can change prices.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management here has a mixed record, good on cost and leverage and weaker on timing. The test is simple: take what was said one to two years ago and set it beside what happened. Where the company gave a number, the number is used; where only an analyst's summary of a plan is on record, the table says so.

The margin promise is the clearest hit. At the start of FY26 management said it expected about 2 points of margin improvement, and by November 2025 the guided hospital range was 20.5 to 22.5 percent.[8][7] The hospital delivered 22.2 percent, which is 0.3 point short of the top of the range but 1.7 points above FY25, and diagnostics guided at 22 to 23 percent delivered 23.6 percent.[3][8] The group margin rose 2.4 points to 22.8 percent.[3]

Revenue per occupied bed is the clearest miss. In November 2025 the chief financial officer expected ARPOB growth of 5 to 6 percent in the second half; in February 2026 he said 4 to 5 percent.[7][6] Reported growth was 4.5 percent in the December quarter, about 2 percent in March (₹2.56 crore against ₹2.51 crore) and 2.6 percent in June.[6][3][1] Timing is the other soft spot. The new FMRI tower, which the chief financial officer said was due in the last quarter of FY26, was to open 'within weeks' on 25 May 2026 and was still awaiting its occupancy certificate on 7 August.[4][2] An analyst also noted that FY28 bed additions fell from 465 in the January presentation to 173 in the May one, and the chief financial officer confirmed a delay at the Shalimar Bagh expansion in Delhi while approvals are sorted out.[4]

On capital allocation, the group spent about ₹2,900 crore over two years on acquisitions and the brand: ₹1,778 crore for the Agilus minority, ₹462 crore for Shrimann, ₹200 crore for the brand and about ₹430 crore for People Tree, with another ₹400 crore of capital planned for that site.[11][13] Net debt rose from ₹1,869 crore in June 2025 to ₹2,233 crore in June 2026, yet leverage stayed at 1.01 times operating profit, within the 1.5 times ceiling Crisil set.[9][1][13] Return on capital has moved from 10 percent to 13 percent, which is a good start and not yet the 15 percent that would show the purchases paying off.[15] The dividend is ₹1 a share, about 7 percent of profit.[3][15]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in chapter 5 rests on an assumption, and each one can be wrong. This chapter names the weakest assumption in each estimate, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when we will know. It also states the red-team case against our own verdict, because a rejection can be a mistake too.

Weakest assumption 1: the margin climbs from 21 percent to 23 percent by FY28

Our base case needs the group margin to rise from 21.1 percent in the June quarter to 23.2 percent in FY28, about 2 points, even after the share-option charge. Three things could stop it. Oncology revenue growth has fallen from 23 or 24 percent to about 5 percent because of lower chemotherapy prices for government patients, and the new cap on trade margins for cancer drugs could widen that.[2][31] Jefferies estimates a hit of 2 to 5 percent to EBITDA if hospitals cannot pass on the cuts, while noting that stent and implant price caps in 2017 were absorbed within about a year.[23] Finally the newer hospitals, Manesar and Greater Noida, must reach mid-teens margins, and Manesar's oncology unit is due by November.[2]

The evidence that we are wrong would be a hospital margin before the share-option charge of at least 22 percent in the September quarter, ARPOB growth of 4 percent or better and occupancy of 70 percent or more. We will know when the September-quarter results are filed, which the rules require by 14 November 2026, though the board-meeting date had not been announced when we searched.[30]

Weakest assumption 2: the legal overhang is a headline, not a payout

The Delhi High Court ordered the audit on 31 August 2026, and the Supreme Court on 25 September allowed it to proceed while calling the High Court's observations 'tentative'.[19][18] Fortis says it was never a party to the arbitration, is neither a judgment debtor nor a garnishee, received no money from the sale of the former promoters' shares, and that no liability, penalty or fine has been imposed on it.[18] The audit, by S Ramanand Aiyar & Co, will cover all Fortis share and fund movements since 24 May 2016, including ₹4,666 crore transferred to RHT Health Trust.[27] A news report puts Daiichi's outstanding claim at about ₹5,300 crore and the audit timeline at six months.[20]

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, and one rule alone produces it: the technical routing rule. A Stage 4 breakdown with Test C failing is the only chart condition that the rulebook treats as disqualifying, however good the business is.[47][46] The financial red lines all pass, the Layer 2 scorecard is 3.5 of 5.0 and qualifies, and the balance sheet is sound. Two secondary facts explain why a market purchase would be wrong even after the chart test is passed. Reward-to-risk at today's price is 1.71 against a Core Alpha floor of 2.0, using our ₹830 base value and a stop at ₹681.79, and the probability-weighted return is only 8.7 percent.[47] The decision would move to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT if the share closes at or above ₹759.39 with a positive two-week change, which passes Test C.

If Test C passes, the buy zone is ₹704.95 to ₹718.85, and a limit order there is acceptable where a market order is not. The lower edge is the 63-day low, which is also the 20-day swing low and the 52-week low. The upper edge is 0.6 average daily swings above it, the top of the full-size band of the support ladder.[46][47] At the top of the zone, reward-to-risk is 3.0 (₹111.15 of gain against ₹37.06 of risk), and at the bottom it is 5.4; both clear the 2.0 floor. The weighted value of ₹800.50 is about 11 percent above the top of the zone and 14 percent above the bottom, so the return is moderate but the risk is small and defined. The stop is the 63-day low minus one average daily swing, ₹704.95 - ₹23.16 = ₹681.79, which is the rule for a Stage 1 or turnaround base.[47][46] Buying at market after a Test C pass would be wrong, because at ₹759 reward-to-risk would be about 0.9.

The table that follows sizes the position, so four terms need plain words first. A basis point (bps) is one hundredth of one percent, so 50 bps is half of one percent of the whole portfolio. The risk budget is the most the portfolio accepts to lose if the stop is hit: 50 bps for a TIER_3 idea, and none for TIER_0. Portfolio heat is the total of those risk budgets across all open positions, capped at 12 percent of the portfolio. The tier cap is the largest position size allowed for the tier, 3.5 percent for TIER_3.[47] The position size is the smaller of the tier cap and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. Today the tier is TIER_0 and the size is zero. If the name were later re-underwritten as TIER_3, the stop distance from ₹718.85 would be 5.16 percent, the 50 bps budget would allow 9.7 percent, and the 3.5 percent tier cap would bind.[47] Once a position exists, the let-winners-run rule applies. Do not trim only because the relative strength index passes 70. Instead, trail the stop up under the 21-day exponential average (a moving average that weights recent days more) or the 50-day simple average (the plain mean of the last 50 closes), whichever the price is respecting, while the trend and the fundamentals hold. The portfolio lets a winner grow to as much as 22 percent of its value before it must be trimmed.[47]
