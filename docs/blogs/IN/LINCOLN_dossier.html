1. What the company does

Lincoln Pharmaceuticals makes finished medicines, which the industry calls formulations: tablets, capsules, dry syrups, liquid vials, ointments and sprays. The company began in 1979 as a partnership in Ahmedabad and listed in the 1990s. It still runs from two plants in Gujarat. One is at Khatraj near Ahmedabad and one is at Mehsana, which makes cephalosporin antibiotics[2][4][1]. It holds about 1,700 registered products, has developed more than 600 formulations across 15 treatment areas, and employs roughly 1,850 people[2]. The group reports a single business segment, pharmaceuticals, and its only subsidiary is a tiny trading partnership that earns no sales[1][4].

It earns money in two ways, and they behave very differently. In India it sells branded generic medicines. These are copies of off-patent drugs sold under Lincoln's own brand names, promoted by a field force that visits doctors. FY26 domestic sales were ₹278.5 crore, up 18.0 percent[4]. Abroad it sells mostly to importers and distributors in countries with lighter regulation. This is called B2B, short for business to business. The company has no distribution network of its own because it wants to avoid local-currency and collection problems[10]. FY26 export sales were ₹392.6 crore, up only 1.4 percent, so exports made 58.5 percent of the year's sales[4][6].

The export side is concentrated. Management gives a rough split of exports. About 40 percent goes to Africa, about 25 percent to Latin America and South-East Asia together, and about 15 percent to tenders from the United Nations, UNICEF and similar buyers. Canada makes up most of the rest[10]. ICRA, the rating agency, says African markets supply more than 25 percent of exports. It adds that some African customers still owe overdue amounts, although collections improved this year[7]. The mix swings widely from quarter to quarter. Exports were 50.9 percent of sales in the June 2025 quarter, 73.3 percent in December 2025 and 63.7 percent in June 2026[15][18][2]. A reader should therefore treat any single quarter of export or domestic growth as noise until a second quarter confirms it.

Lincoln pays for its plants and its field force from the gap between selling price and cost. Its operating margin is operating profit divided by sales, before interest, tax and other income. It was about 14.6 percent in FY26 on our calculation from the filed numbers[3][4]. Customers pay slowly, so Lincoln needs a lot of working capital. This is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills between buying raw materials and being paid. ICRA puts it at 34 percent of income[7]. On top of the drug business sits a large pool of treasury assets. These are loans to other companies, mutual funds, shares and cash. Together they equal about 39 percent of the stock-market value of the company. The interest, gains and currency effects on them flow into reported profit as other income[4][5][7].

What Lincoln sells and where it is trying to go

Anti-infectives are medicines that fight bacteria, fungi and parasites. They make up the largest share of sales, at 30.6 percent of the June 2026 quarter, up from 26.5 percent a year earlier. The muscle-and-joint range fell from 15.1 to 10.8 percent. The anti-parasite range fell from 8.3 to 4.1 percent[2]. Management wants to move toward what it calls lifestyle and chronic medicines, especially women's health and skin care. It names heart, diabetes, skin and ear-nose-throat products as the growth leaders. Its ear brand Tinnex is one example[2][10]. A new plant for cephalosporins, a family of antibiotics, is the biggest planned addition. Management says it made about ₹45 crore of sales in FY26. It hopes to reach ₹90 to ₹100 crore next year and ₹150 crore later[10]. A small plant for bulk drug ingredients has also started, with approval for 10 products and about ₹4 crore invested[2]. These moves matter because Lincoln's goal of ₹1,000 crore of annual revenue needs sales to grow about 50 percent from the FY26 level. Management says the existing plants can only stretch another 15 to 20 percent[10][2].

Who owns and runs it

A promoter group led by managing director Mahendra Patel runs the company. Three other whole-time directors sit beside him, so four of the nine directors are executives[1][4]. The promoter group holds 49.78 percent of the shares, a figure that has not moved since June 2024. The September 2026 shareholding filing states that none of those shares are pledged as security for loans[9][25][26]. Foreign portfolio investors hold 4.55 percent and domestic funds hold almost nothing. No brokerage publishes forecasts for the company, so the share price is set by a thin crowd of about 29,000 holders[9][23]. The auditor is Samir M. Shah & Associates, an Ahmedabad firm of chartered accountants, and its report flags the existence and recoverability of trade receivables as the key audit matter[4]. Promoter control, no pledge and no institutional following are common among Indian small caps. They lower the risk of forced selling but also mean few outside analysts check the numbers.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Formulation: a finished medicine in its final form, such as a tablet or an injection, as opposed to the raw drug ingredient.
  • Branded generic: a copy of an off-patent drug that is sold under the maker's own brand name and promoted to doctors.
  • DPCO (Drug Price Control Order): the government rule that caps the selling price of listed medicines in India. ICRA says about 20 percent of Lincoln's products fall under it[7].
  • Inter-corporate deposit (ICD): a loan from one company to another; Lincoln had ₹187.8 crore of such loans at March 2026[7].
  • CDMO (contract development and manufacturing organisation): a firm that develops and makes medicines for another company's brand; Lincoln does this for Canada[10].
  • Other income: profit that does not come from selling medicines, here mainly interest, investment gains and foreign-exchange gains.
  • EU-GMP, WHO-GMP and TGA: quality certificates from European, World Health Organization and Australian inspectors that allow sales into regulated or semi-regulated markets[2][4].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Lincoln's strongest asset is its balance sheet. Total borrowings were ₹4.4 crore at March 2026, against about ₹758 crore of shareholders' money. Profit covered interest 129 times. The company holds roughly ₹429 crore of loans, funds and cash[5][4]. ICRA has held its A rating on the bank facilities, reaffirmed in April 2024, June 2025 and September 2026, and calls the liquidity strong. CRISIL reaffirmed its A rating with a stable outlook on January 9, 2026[28][27][7][8][29]. No shares are pledged by the promoters[9]. These facts mean the company is very unlikely to fail from debt, which is the first thing to rule out in a small drug maker.

The business itself is solid but not distinctive. Lincoln's advantages are its registrations in 60-plus countries and its long relationships with distributors. It also holds plant approvals from WHO-GMP, EU-GMP and Australia's TGA, and six production lines were cleared by Health Canada in January 2026[2][10]. These approvals take years to earn and keep some competitors out of tenders. But they are not patents. A rival with the same certificates can bid for the same orders. Meanwhile about 20 percent of the product range is under price control. Competition in India's generic market is intense. ICRA says scale is only moderate at ₹693 crore of revenue[7]. We would call the moat narrow, built on paperwork and relationships rather than technology or brand.

The weaknesses show up in the numbers rather than in the story. Sales grew 7.2 percent in FY25 and 7.7 percent in FY26 against a stated goal of 15 to 18 percent a year[3][11]. The operating margin fell from 20 percent in FY22 to 15 percent in FY26 and has now been flat for five quarters[3][1]. Return on the money invested in the business fell from 24 percent to 16 percent over the same four years. One reason is that the company keeps lending and investing surplus cash at modest returns instead of paying it out[3][4]. Compare Caplin Point, a rival that also sells to Latin America and Africa but runs its own distribution. It earns a 35 percent operating margin and a 24 percent return on capital, and the market pays about 27 times its profit[31][10]. Lincoln's lower margin is the price of a B2B model that keeps its risk low. The market pays it about 11 times profit for that trade-off.

Evidence on pricing power and cost control

A company with pricing power can raise prices faster than its costs rise, and the result is a steady or rising margin. Lincoln's margin on its own drug sales was 15.5 percent in the June 2026 quarter and 15.5 percent in the June 2025 quarter. This is our calculation from the filed quarterly statements. The slide has paused but not reversed[1][3]. Revenue from operations rose 15.1 percent in that quarter, and operating profit before other income rose 15.2 percent. Costs grew in line with sales, so nothing extra reached the bottom line from operations[1]. Raw materials and packing consumed about 36 percent of sales in FY26. ICRA names swings in the price of active ingredients and in currency rates as the main profit risks[4][7]. Management says a 15 percent margin is the safe floor and that it may reach 18 percent. It declined to promise more, saying it did not want to overcommit[10]. The margin has not improved in five years, while sales grew from ₹472 crore to ₹671 crore. The extra sales came at ordinary prices, not premium ones.

Evidence on balance-sheet strength and what it is used for

A strong balance sheet is only valuable if the money is put to good use. Lincoln's loans to other companies and individuals rose from ₹141.3 crore to ₹187.8 crore in FY26. That is 24.8 percent of net worth. ICRA says there have been no bad debts so far but that timely recovery is a key point to monitor[7]. The accounts classify these loans as unsecured and 'considered good'. Management said on the February 2026 call that they are backed by mortgages. The two descriptions do not match, and we cannot settle the point from public documents[4][10]. Management also explained that some of the money is lent to suppliers who give discounts for cash payment, and that the loans earn 10 to 12 percent[10]. The interest income reported on all loans and deposits was only ₹7.6 crore in FY26. That is low for a ₹188 crore loan book if the stated returns were being earned in cash[4]. That gap is the reason we treat the treasury assets at book value at best and do not give them a premium.

A buyback is when a company buys its own shares to shrink the share count, and Lincoln has said it does not plan one[10]. Lincoln holds ₹429 crore in loans, funds and cash and earns little on it. 3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter shows five years of revenue, profit, cash and debt. It then asks the Layer 1 question. How good is the business at today's level, and which way are the numbers moving? Three figures recur. Earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares, here about 20.03 million shares[9]. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is the profit earned before interest and tax on the money invested in the business, shown as a percentage. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is operating profit before those four items. Lincoln's own EBITDA figure includes other income, so it runs about ₹33 crore higher than the operating profit we use[6][3].

Every figure in the tables is in ₹ crore unless marked, and one crore is ten million. The yearly numbers are consolidated, which here equals the standalone company because the only subsidiary earns no sales[3][4]. Financial years end in March, so FY26 is April 2025 to March 2026. The last column of the profit table is the trailing twelve months to June 2026, which is the newest period filed. The next result is due on October 29, 2026[24].

Three more terms matter for the cash and debt discussion. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates from running itself, after tax and working capital but before spending on plant. Free cash flow (FCF) is that cash minus spending on plant and equipment. Net debt is borrowings minus cash and liquid investments, and for Lincoln it is negative, meaning the company holds more than it owes[3][5]. A price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by a year of earnings per share. A low one means the market pays little for each rupee of profit. Layer 1 has to test the reasons for a low one.

Our variant perception

A variant perception is a view that differs from the market's and that we can defend with evidence. The market's view of Lincoln, as the price and the free screening sites show it, is that this is a cheap, debt-free, family-run exporter: Trendlyne labels it a 'value stock, under radar', and Screener's automatic summary lists 'almost debt free' as its only strength[23][3]. At 11.4 times earnings and 1.45 times book value, that view looks reasonable.

Our view is different on one point and agrees on the rest. We think the shares are fairly priced, not cheap, because the 11.4 times figure rests on ₹42 crore of other income that includes unusual currency gains. On normal earnings the multiple is 13.2 times, which is the three-year median. We also think the market is right to pay less than peers: margin, growth and returns are all lower, and management's targets have been missed twice. Where we agree with the market is on safety: the balance sheet, the pledge-free promoters and the A ratings from two agencies make a permanent loss from failure unlikely[7][8][9].

The edge, if any, lies in timing rather than in valuation. The June 2026 quarter showed 15 percent growth in sales and in operating profit, the first time since March 2025 that quarterly sales growth reached the company's own range[3][1]. If the September quarter, due on October 29, repeats that with the margin at 15 percent or better and a more normal other income, the market would have the first evidence that the growth plan is working, and a 12 to 13 times multiple on rising operating profit would lift the shares. If operating profit is flat again and the quarter is carried by other income, the shares have little to lift them. We cannot know which, so the sensible stance is to wait for the number.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption more than the others. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show it is wrong, and gives the date on which the evidence will arrive. It also sets out the strongest honest case against our own conclusion. Here that is a case that the shares are worth less than we say. A rejection is easy to defend by listing risks, but harder to defend by pricing them. We take each weak point in turn, then bring them together in one table with dates.

Weakest assumption behind the earnings estimate: other income settles at about ₹26 crore

Our FY28 earnings of ₹49.2 per share include ₹26 crore of other income. That is 20 percent of the profit before tax in that year. The number is a judgement, not an observation. Other income was ₹22 crore in FY25 and ₹33 crore in FY26, and it was ₹24 crore in a single quarter of FY27. Over five quarters it ranged from minus ₹4 crore to plus ₹24 crore[3][4][1]. The largest part is currency gains. They depend on the rupee, on African and Latin American currencies, and on how much of the export bill the company leaves unhedged. Management described its policy as 'we don't hedge fully'[10]. If the rupee strengthens or the hedging policy changes, ₹26 crore could easily be ₹12 crore. That is our bear-case figure, and on its own it cuts about ₹5 from annual earnings per share, or 11 percent. The September quarter result on October 29, 2026 will give one more data point. The December quarter result in February 2027 will give a second. Two more quarters would make a pattern[24].

Weakest assumption behind the price estimate: the multiple

Our central price uses 11.4 times earnings. That is the lower of the trailing multiple and the three-year median of 13.5 times, as the scoring rules direct. The choice matters a great deal. At 13.5 times, the same FY28 earnings give about ₹665. The asymmetry from today's price then rises from 0.14 to 1.5. From the top of the lowest-risk buying range it rises from 1.44 to 4.3[21]. We chose the lower multiple for two reasons. The 11.4 times already contains the unusual gains, and a company with falling margins has not earned a re-rating. A reader who treats the three-year median as the right anchor would reach a different conclusion on price. The conclusion on quality would not change, because Layer 2 fails on its own. Two things would move us. One is two quarters in a row with a margin of 16 percent or more, which would show the margin is rising rather than flat. The other is a rise in institutional ownership above the current 4.6 percent, which would show professional buyers agree[9].

The red-team bear case: what the shares are worth if the weak points arrive together

The strongest case against Lincoln is not a collapse but a slow slide in earnings quality. The audited accounts classify ₹17.5 crore of trade receivables as credit-impaired and set aside ₹1.0 crore against them[4]. If the rest had to be written off, the pre-tax charge would be ₹16.5 crore. That is about ₹6.2 per share after tax, or 14 percent of FY26's net profit. ICRA lists overdue African receivables and the ₹187.8 crore loan book among its monitoring points. It says a significant rise in third-party loans, or any delinquency, could lead to a downgrade[7]. Now add four more problems. Currency gains reverse. Two senior quality-assurance managers resigned within a month. A plant re-inspection that management expected by June cannot be confirmed. An acquisition uses up the cash pile[22][10][3]. In that case the shares could trade at 9 to 10 times earnings of about ₹36, which is about ₹325 to ₹360. That is 35 to 40 percent below today's price, and it is the case that would hurt a holder.

7. What we would do, and when

Our decision is REJECTED for now. Lincoln does not go onto the shortlist and we place no buy order. The rule behind the verdict has three parts. First, Layer 2 scores 2.5 out of 5.0, below the 3.0 hurdle. Second, none of the compounder, turnaround or tactical archetypes qualifies. Third, the reward-to-risk ratio is 0.14 to 1 at the ₹550.05 close and 1.44 to 1 at ₹508.6, against a floor of 2.0. This is a valuation and quality rejection, not a technical one. The technical red line is not met as written, because the 200-day average is still rising, so the Stage 4 label is a weak, unconfirmed downtrend[39].

Layer 3 asks where the price stands against support, using a ladder measured in ATR, the average daily swing. A price within 0.6 ATR of support, up to ₹508.6, earns a full-size position. Within 1.2 ATR, up to ₹522.3, earns a standard position. Within 1.6 ATR, up to ₹531.4, allows a starter position through a limit order, which is an order to buy only at a stated price or lower. Anything further away is NO_BUY. Lincoln at ₹550.05 is 2.42 ATR above its ₹495 support, so it sits in the NO_BUY band[39]. The lowest band, ₹495 to ₹508.6, is where we would place a limit order if the verdict improved. We show it in the table below as the zone we would reconsider, not as an active entry. The NO_BUY reading explains why no order is placed today. It is not a reason for the rejection, which rests on Layer 2, the archetype tests and asymmetry.

Position size follows from the stop. The formula takes the lower of two numbers: the conviction tier's maximum weight, and the tier's risk budget divided by the distance to the stop. A rejected name has conviction TIER_0 and a weight of zero. For reference, suppose Lincoln had qualified as a low-conviction name with a 35 basis-point risk budget. The ratio improves only because the entry price falls toward a stop that sits 6 percent below it. Layer 2 would still fail.
