CHAPTER I

Forty-One Years of Brick, Salt-Pan Land & Cycle Survival

"To understand a real estate company's future margins, do not start with this quarter's glossy brochure. Start forty years ago and ask how the founders bought the dirt and how they survived the last credit freeze."
— Sam Zell, Equity Group Investments — Principles of Real Estate Cycle Survival

If you want to understand why no modern private-equity fund can replicate Ajmera Realty's cost structure in Mumbai, you and I have to travel back to the 1970s and 1980s. Incorporated in 1985 and listed on the Bombay Stock Exchange under scrip code 513349 and the National Stock Exchange under the ticker AJMERA, Ajmera Realty and Infra India Limited is the public flagship of the Ajmera Group, an engineering and construction house founded by the late Chhotubhai Ajmera and his seven brothers across Gujarat and Maharashtra. Back when most Mumbai builders were fighting over tiny single-building plots in South Mumbai or Bandra, the Ajmera brothers practiced a completely different craft. They walked out to the marshy, unglamorous edges of Mumbai's commuter rail corridors. Places that polite society dismissed as salt pans, industrial wasteland, or remote suburbs. And bought contiguous tracts of fifty to a hundred acres at agricultural or industrial prices.

Buying raw peripheral earth was only step one. The family understood that land only becomes valuable when families can actually live on it. So they laid down asphalt roads, piped in municipal water, wired electrical substations, planted twenty-acre public parks. Built schools and temples before selling apartment towers in multi-decade phases. That pioneering township playbook created famous Mumbai residential neighborhoods: Shastri Nagar in Andheri West, Yogi Nagar in Borivali West, Ajmera Nagari. The crown jewel that anchors our investment thesis today. The 100-acre Bhakti Park and Ajmera i-Land township in Wadala East, alongside the contiguous 55-to-66-acre land reserve in Kanjurmarg East. In the 1990s and 2000s, they carried that same peripheral-township blueprint south to Bengaluru's Electronic City technology corridor. Meanwhile, experimenting with projects in Ahmedabad, Pune, Rajkot, Surat, Bahrain, and the United Kingdom.

When you and I open the twelve-year consolidated financial archive on Screener from March 2015 through September 2026, we can see how the company evolved across three sharply defined corporate eras. During Era One, from fiscal 2015 through fiscal 2018, Ajmera operated as a conservative, slow-turning traditional builder monetizing incremental phases at Wadala and Bengaluru. Consolidated revenue climbed from 165 crore rupees in fiscal 2015 to 370 crore rupees in fiscal 2018, operating profit rose from 45 crore rupees at a 27 percent margin to 138 crore rupees at a 37 percent margin. Net profit reached a pre-pandemic peak of 84 crore rupees, or 4.64 rupees per split-adjusted share. This lifts return on capital employed from 6 percent to 14 percent. Yet beneath those fat operating margins, capital velocity was glacially slow: inventory days exceeded 14,000 to 18,000 days in fiscal 2017 and 2018 as land and finished luxury apartments sat on the books. Meanwhile, total borrowings nearly doubled from 268 crore rupees to 515 crore rupees.

Then came Era Two, from fiscal 2019 through fiscal 2021. The three-year winter that broke half of Mumbai's real estate industry. In September 2018, the default of IL&FS triggered a systemic freeze across India's non-banking financial companies, shutting off the shadow-bank construction loans that developers relied upon. Almost simultaneously, the Real Estate Regulation and Development Act, known as RERA, required builders to ring-fence 70 percent of customer collections inside dedicated project escrow accounts. As Ajmera borrowed to fund upfront project approvals and construction before launches could scale, consolidated debt surged to 863 crore rupees in fiscal 2019 and peaked at 968 crore rupees in March 2020 against just 638 crore rupees of shareholder net worth. A heavy 1.52 times debt-to-equity ratio. When COVID-19 lockdowns froze construction sites in fiscal 2020 and fiscal 2021, annual sales stalled at 344 crore and 347 crore rupees, interest expense devoured 66 crore and 58 crore rupees per year, operating margins compressed to 28 percent. Consolidated net profit plunged 63 percent from 84 crore rupees down to a trough of 31 crore rupees in fiscal 2021, dragging return on capital employed back down to 6 percent.

Possession. Under India's RERA law, 70 percent of every rupee collected from customers must go straight into a ring-fenced, project-specific bank escrow account.
