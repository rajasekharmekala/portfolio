Before you read

Union Bank of India is a government-owned bank and the fifth largest state-run bank by business[14]. Its quarterly profit rose 30 percent, bad loans are falling, and the shares cost about 6.4 times a year of earnings[1][31]. We still say wait. At ₹184.60 the likely gain to our ₹200 target is smaller than the 12 percent fall to our ₹162.16 exit price, so we would buy only between ₹165 and ₹172. The view is wrong if deposits keep lagging loans, if rising interest rates cut bond values faster than they lift lending margins, or if the new loan-loss rules cost well above the ₹5,500 crore the bank expects, with ₹7,000 crore as our rejection line[4].

1. What the company does

A bank is a business that borrows from many people and lends to others at a higher price. Union Bank of India does this at very large scale. At 30 September 2026 it held ₹13.19 lakh crore of deposits and had lent ₹11.56 lakh crore, so its total business was ₹24.75 lakh crore[3][1]. It runs 8,713 branches, 8,815 cash machines and 28,654 business-correspondent outlets run by agents, and it employs 74,106 people[1]. The bank in its present form came together on 1 April 2020, when Andhra Bank and Corporation Bank were merged into it[14].

The Government of India owns 74.76 percent of the shares and has held that exact share for eleven straight quarters[31][2]. The next largest holder is Life Insurance Corporation of India with 5.36 percent at March 2026[11]. Foreign investors hold 9.40 percent and the public holds 4.71 percent[1]. Government ownership matters in two ways. It gives lenders and depositors a strong safety net, which is why the rating agency CARE rates the bank's Tier-II and infrastructure bonds AAA[14]. It also means the bank is asked to carry out public schemes, such as loans to street vendors and small farmers[1].

The bank's profit comes mostly from the gap between what it earns on loans and what it pays on deposits. That gap, in rupees, is called net interest income. In the September 2026 quarter it was ₹10,583 crore out of total operating income of ₹15,234 crore, about 69 percent[1]. The rest is fees such as loan processing and insurance sales (₹3,210 crore), gains from trading bonds (₹701 crore) and cash recovered from loans that were written off earlier (₹740 crore)[1]. The last two are less steady than fees, and we return to that point in chapter 2.

The loan book has three retail-style arms and one large corporate arm. Retail loans (mainly home and vehicle loans) were ₹2.65 lakh crore, farm loans ₹1.90 lakh crore and loans to small businesses ₹1.80 lakh crore. Together these three, which the bank calls RAM, are 57.47 percent of its domestic loans[3][1]. The corporate arm, which includes lending to power, roads and finance companies, was ₹5.20 lakh crore and grew 23.63 percent in a year[1]. Loans to non-bank finance companies and housing finance firms were ₹1.76 lakh crore, or 15.94 percent of domestic loans, up from 13.64 percent a year earlier[1].

On the funding side, a bank wants cheap money. The cheapest is the current and savings account balance that customers leave with it. Union Bank's CASA share, which stands for current and savings account deposits and is the part of deposits that costs the bank little or nothing in interest, was 34.93 percent of domestic deposits, up from 32.56 percent a year earlier[1]. The more expensive money comes from large 'bulk' term deposits, which the bank has been cutting on purpose: they fell 9.29 percent in a year[1]. Because it cut them while lending grew 18.53 percent, total deposits rose only 6.87 percent[1]. That gap is the main weakness we study in chapters 2 and 2B.

Three ratios describe how well a bank earns. The net interest margin (NIM) is the spread between what the bank earns on its assets and what it pays for its funds, shown as a percent of assets; Union Bank's was 2.86 percent in the quarter[1]. The gross non-performing assets (GNPA) ratio is the share of all loans on which borrowers have stopped paying for 90 days or more; it was 2.42 percent[1]. The net non-performing assets (NNPA) ratio is the same share after the bank has set aside money against those loans; it was 0.42 percent[1]. Falling ratios mean fewer bad loans and a safer bank.

Two valuation terms appear throughout. Earnings per share (EPS) is the bank's profit divided by its number of shares; for the six months to September 2026 it was ₹14.24 on the bank's own accounts[2]. The price-to-earnings ratio, also called P/E, is the share price divided by a year of earnings per share; it tells you how many years of today's profit the market is paying for. At ₹184.60 and trailing profit of ₹27.77 per share (the last four quarters, our sum of three cited periods[2][1]), the P/E is 6.65. The exchange-based figure the ledger carries, 6.41, uses consolidated profit[38][31].

How the bank would be used by a customer

A salaried person opens a savings account, gets a home loan and buys an insurance policy through the same branch. A farmer takes a crop loan. A small shopkeeper takes a business loan and sends payments through the bank's current account. A government department keeps its balances with the bank and uses it to pay out subsidies. Each of these relationships earns the bank a little spread or fee, and most of them stay for years[1].

The government is also an important customer. The bank is a distribution channel for public schemes: it has sanctioned ₹31,860 crore under the emergency credit line scheme and ₹6,174 crore of Mudra loans in the September quarter alone[1]. Such loans carry government guarantees or priority-sector status, which supports volumes but not always high margins. Plain-English glossary for this report

  • NII: net interest income, the interest the bank earns on loans and investments minus the interest it pays on deposits and borrowings.
  • RAM: the bank's label for retail, agriculture and micro, small and medium enterprise loans taken together.
  • Credit cost: the money set aside each year for bad loans, as a percent of average loans; 0.21 percent in the September 2026 quarter[1].
  • Provision coverage ratio (PCR): the share of bad loans already covered by set-aside money; 95.20 percent[1].
  • CRAR and CET1: capital adequacy ratios. CRAR is total capital as a percent of risk-weighted assets; CET1 counts only the highest-quality capital, mostly shareholders' equity. Union Bank reports 17.94 percent and 16.05 percent[1].
  • ROA and ROE: return on assets and return on equity, the profit the bank earns on each rupee of assets and of shareholders' money. They were 1.38 percent and 17.21 percent in the quarter[1].
  • Core ROA: the bank's own ROA after removing trading gains and recoveries on written-off loans; 1.03 percent[1].
  • ECL: expected credit loss, the new RBI method that makes banks set money aside before a loan turns bad; it starts on 1 April 2027[17].
  • SMA: special mention accounts, loans that are overdue but not yet bad; they were ₹2,282 crore at September 2026[1].
  • FCNR(B): foreign-currency deposits from non-resident Indians; the RBI's swap window made them a new funding source in 2026[20][16].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The honest summary is that Union Bank has become a better bank than it was three years ago, but it is still a middle-ranked one. The improvement is visible in every quarter: net profit rose from ₹4,249 crore to ₹5,537 crore in a year, bad-loan ratios fell by about a quarter, and the capital cushion grew[1]. The middle ranking shows up when it is set against peers on deposits and bad-loan levels, which we do in the peer table in chapter 3.

The first strength is the safety net. The Government of India owns 74.76 percent, and the rating agencies treat that support as central to their ratings. CARE rates the bank's Tier-II and infrastructure bonds AAA with a stable outlook, and says it would downgrade if government ownership fell below 51 percent or if net bad loans stayed above 3 percent for long[14]. Net bad loans are 0.42 percent today[1]. ICRA, a second agency, would reassess if return on assets fell below 0.3 percent or the capital cushion above the regulatory minimum shrank below 100 basis points[15]. Return on assets is 1.38 percent[1].

The second strength is capital. The bank's total capital ratio is 17.94 percent and its highest-quality (CET1) ratio is 16.05 percent, up from 14.37 percent a year ago[1]. Almost all of its capital is the best kind. Management says the bank can pay the whole estimated ₹5,500 crore cost of the new loan-loss rules from one quarter's profit and would lose about 70 basis points of CET1 if it took the hit at once[4]. That is a comfortable position for a bank growing loans at 18.5 percent.

The third strength is a turnaround in loan quality that is backed by numbers, not slogans. Bad loans fell from ₹32,085 crore to ₹27,916 crore in a year, new bad loans in the quarter were ₹1,856 crore, and the stock of overdue-but-not-yet-bad loans of more than ₹5 crore fell to ₹2,282 crore from ₹5,634 crore a year earlier[1]. The CEO says the whole stress book, meaning the ₹2,282 crore of overdue loans plus ₹3,701 crore and ₹2,790 crore of restructured loans under two frameworks, is the lowest in five years[21].

Where the bank is weaker than its peers

The weakest link is deposits. Deposits grew 6.87 percent in the year to September 2026, while loans grew 18.53 percent and the banking system's deposits grew 17.8 percent[1][16]. The bank's global credit-to-deposit ratio reached 88.13 percent, up from 79.67 percent a year earlier[1]. CARE notes that the bank's CASA share is lower than at the largest state banks, and that its bad-loan levels, although better, are still only moderate compared with peer state banks[14]. Canara Bank reports a gross bad-loan ratio of 1.33 percent and Bank of India 1.60 percent, against Union Bank's 2.42 percent[29][30].

The bank is aware of the gap and has a plan: shift to retail and savings deposits, use foreign-currency deposits through the RBI window (the bank raised about $2.25 billion, around 70 percent of it on what the bank describes as a leveraged basis), and issue bonds[20][24]. But each fix has a cost or a limit. The CEO says about 70 percent of the foreign-currency money is for five years, which helps, but bonds usually cost more than savings balances, and an 88 percent credit-to-deposit ratio leaves less room for a second year of faster loan growth[4].

Earnings quality is the next concern. The bank reports a return on assets of 1.38 percent but a 'core' return of 1.03 percent once trading gains and recoveries on written-off loans are removed[1]. Those two items were ₹701 crore and ₹740 crore in the quarter, about 18 percent of operating profit[1]. Trading gains fell 34.6 percent in a year and will probably stay low while interest rates rise, because rising rates cut bond prices[1][20].

Concentration is a third point. The 20 largest borrowers made up 12.94 percent of advances at March 2026, up from 11.75 percent a year earlier[11]. The 20 largest depositors were 5.19 percent of deposits, down from 6.65 percent[11]. Neither number is alarming, but the loan side is moving the wrong way for a bank that wants to look safer.

How we weigh the evidence

Strengths and weaknesses do not carry the same weight. Government support and capital strength protect against a catastrophe, but they are already priced into a share that trades near its accounting book value. The weaknesses (deposits, earnings quality, bond-price risk) decide whether profit rises or falls from here, so they matter more for the price. For that reason the Layer 2 scorecard in chapter 2B gives deposits and earnings quality only a half mark each, and the verdict in chapter 7 relies on entry price rather than on the strengths.

What this means for an owner: you hold a lender with an almost certain safety net, a strong capital cushion (CET1 16.05 percent), and profit that is currently benefiting from low provisions and fast loan growth. You do not hold a franchise that attracts deposits as easily as the largest state bank, and you should not expect the multiple to rise just because quarterly profit did. The next chapter turns these claims into five measured indicators with a pass-or-fail rule for each.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management guidance is a numerical forecast a company gives about its own future results, and a company that meets its guidance is one whose other promises deserve more weight. We checked the bank's yearly targets against what it later reported, going back to the year ended March 2024. The sources are the bank's own presentations and call transcripts[6][5][7][8][9][4]. We count each promise once. The pattern is clear. On quality and profit measures (margin, bad loans, return on assets) the bank has met most of its goals. The exceptions are small: recoveries and slippages missed their FY25 targets by about 5 to 6 percent, and the March 2026 quarter margin fell short of the level it said it would defend. On growth measures it fell short in the year to March 2025: it targeted loan growth of 11 to 13 percent and deposit growth of 9 to 11 percent, and delivered 8.62 and 7.22 percent[5]. It also gave no growth guidance for the year to March 2026, a year in which loans grew 9.74 percent and deposits only 2.72 percent[10][7]. The system grew about 14.5 percent in loans and 11.5 percent in deposits that year, so the bank lost share[14].

The current year is different. In April 2026 the bank said it expected loan growth of 13 to 14 percent, and by September it was at 18.53 percent[9][1]. It said it would defend and then improve its margin, and the margin went from 2.64 to 2.80 to 2.86 percent in two quarters[1][4]. It said the cost of the new loan-loss rules would be about ₹5,500 crore and has set aside ₹1,000 crore in three steps of ₹700 crore, ₹100 crore and ₹200 crore[1][4]. One caution applies. The new managing director, Asheesh Pandey, completed his first year in the bank in October 2026. These promises come from a team that has been tested for only four or five quarters[4].

3. The numbers, in plain words

The numbers in this chapter come from two views of the same bank. Screener, a data site, gives five years of consolidated accounts, which add the bank's subsidiaries and joint ventures[31]. The bank's own presentation gives standalone figures, which count only the bank itself and are the ones that bank-specific ratios use[1]. The two profit lines are close: the quarter's consolidated profit was ₹5,781 crore and standalone ₹5,537 crore[2][23]. We say which view each table uses, because neither view alone tells the whole story.

A bank is not a factory. Its accounts need a different reading. Revenue includes the interest it earns, and 'interest paid' is the main cost, not a financing choice. Screener's return on capital employed, called ROCE for short and meaning return on capital employed, is 6.30 percent for this bank[31]. That figure is not a sensible test for a lender because borrowed money is its raw material, and we use return on equity (ROE) instead. That is the usual yardstick for banks. A bank's cash flow from operations is the cash its day-to-day business brings in or uses, and for a bank it is dominated by changes in loans and deposits. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after paying for the assets a business needs, and it has no clear meaning for a lender either. So the usual cash-conversion test cannot be applied as written, and we explain our substitute in the Layer 1 section below.

Over five years the pattern is a strong profit recovery. Profit is the story. Consolidated net profit rose from ₹5,265 crore in the year to March 2022 to ₹19,430 crore in the year to March 2026, and the last twelve months to September 2026 reached ₹21,999 crore[31]. Revenue grew far more slowly, 10 percent a year over three years, so most of the gain came from lower provisions and a better margin, not from selling more[31]. Deposits were ₹13.22 lakh crore at September 2026 against ₹10.34 lakh crore in March 2022, a rise of 28 percent over four and a half years[31].

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks whether the bank clears the hard red lines in the scoring file and which of three profiles it fits. Four red lines apply. The first is a debt-to-equity ratio, which is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, of 1.20 or more, or interest cover below 2; neither suits a bank. The second is a pledge of 20 percent or more of the promoter's shares. The third is a low ratio of cash from operations to profit. The fourth is a price chart in Stage 4, which is the confirmed downtrend in which the price is below a falling 50-day and a falling 200-day average. The scoring file recognises three profiles: a compounder, a turnaround and a breakout idea, which is a stock that moves through a price ceiling that has held before. Beyond the red lines, a candidate must show an asymmetry ratio, the gain to the target divided by the loss to the exit price, which is also called the reward-to-risk ratio, of at least 2.5 to 1 for a turnaround or tactical idea.

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on future profit. A reverse discounted cash flow, or reverse DCF, works backwards: it asks what growth the current price assumes. The ledger's version solves for the yearly profit growth over ten years that justifies today's price, using a 12 percent discount rate and a final-year value of 20 times profit. For this bank it returns minus 8.75 percent a year[38]. Read literally, the market expects the bank's profit to shrink by almost 9 percent a year for ten years.

We do not rely on that number, and the ledger itself labels its base as a stand-in: it is trailing net profit of ₹21,983 crore backed out from the market value and the P/E, and it is not a cash flow. For a bank the proxy is wrong in kind. A lender must keep most of its profit as new capital to support loan growth, so profit is not cash that owners can take out, and free cash flow, which is cash left after running the business and buying assets, is not a meaningful figure; the ledger shows no yield for it. The right question for a bank is what return on shareholders' money the price assumes, and we answer it in the first estimate below.

The second reference point is what analysts expect. The consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who cover a stock. The ledger carries no profit-per-share consensus and no revision trend for this bank, so we mark those fields N/A. It does carry price targets from 17 analysts, ranging from ₹175 to ₹235 with a mean of ₹205.94[38]. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report can be wrong. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each one, builds the strongest case against our own conclusion, picks the single claim on which everything else depends, and says when the evidence will arrive. It uses three tools: the bank's own calendar, the bank's own disclosures and the RBI's rule dates. The bank has said its next results come by 31 January 2027, and full-year results by 30 April 2027[13].

The weakest assumption behind each estimate

For the FY27E profit of ₹27.96 per share the weakest assumption is that provisions other than tax run at ₹1,100 crore a quarter. The bank spent ₹979 crore in the June quarter and ₹701 crore in September, and its credit cost of 0.21 percent was helped by recoveries exceeding new bad loans, as the CEO said[2][20]. If recoveries thin out, provisions could reach ₹1,800 crore and profit per share fall to about ₹26.0. The evidence will come with the December-quarter results, due by 31 January 2027.

For FY28E at ₹28.8 the weakest assumption is that net interest income grows 12 to 14 percent. That needs loans to keep growing at 13 to 14 percent, which needs funding. The bank's deposits grew 6.87 percent while loans grew 18.53 percent, and the credit-to-deposit ratio is 88.13 percent. Management calls the domestic ratio of about 84 percent comfortable within an 80 to 85 percent range[1][24]. If deposit growth stays low, either loan growth slows or the bank pays up for money. Either would show in the margin by the March 2027 quarter.

For the central price target of ₹200 the weakest assumption is a P/E of 7.0, half a turn above the three-year median. The first test is whether the stock holds above its 50-day average of ₹179.57. A close below the 200-day average of ₹175.99 would be an early warning.

The red-team bear case

A skeptic would say the stock has already had its re-rating. It rose about 10 percent in three weeks on a quarter that the brokers describe as a beat caused mainly by low provisions[27][26]. The profit before tax rose 36.7 percent but profit after tax rose 30.3 percent, because the tax rate rose from 21.6 to 25.2 percent[2]. A tax rate near 25 percent is probably the new normal, so some of the growth is already behind us.

The skeptic would add that the interest-rate cycle has turned against banks that fund themselves with deposits. The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50 percent on 7 October and changed its stance to 'calibrated tightening', which means further rises are possible and cuts are off the table for now[18][19]. System deposits grew 17.8 percent, helped by a one-off RBI window for foreign-currency deposits whose liquidity is now shrinking; Union Bank grew deposits 6.87 percent[16][1]. If competition for deposits intensifies as liquidity tightens, the bank's cost of deposits (5.01 percent) could rise before its loan yields (7.90 percent) catch up. About 53 to 55 percent of its loans are linked to the repo rate and reprice quickly, while the rest reprice more slowly[1][4].

The skeptic would finish with the loan-loss rules. The bank estimates ₹5,500 crore; Indian Bank, a peer, put its own at ₹3,500 crore[20]. The bank says the sum is about one quarter's profit and 70 basis points of CET1 capital and that the RBI has given 'multiple years' to absorb it[4][17]. But the rules also raise the standing cost of provisions on good loans, and the bank's own CEO said this part of the effect is still being worked out industry-wide[24]. A bear who believed the full ₹4,500 crore of remaining cost would be charged in one year would value the stock at about ₹135. A last risk is dilution, which is a fall in each existing share's claim on profit when a company issues new shares. The board has approved ₹8,000 crore of capital raising for FY27 across equity, AT-1 and Tier-II instruments[1][14].

Our answer to the skeptic is the price we ask. At ₹172 or below, the stock has already given back most of its three-week rise and sits near the 20-day average; the exit price of ₹162.16 is only 5.7 percent below. At ₹184.60 we agree with the skeptic and do not buy.

The single hinge claim and when we will know

The claim on which everything depends is this: quarterly profit stays at ₹5,200 crore or more in the December 2026 and March 2027 quarters, which requires the margin to stay at 2.85 percent or higher (between 2.75 and 2.85 percent we keep the orders but do not add the second half, and below 2.75 percent we cancel) and provisions other than tax to stay at or below about ₹1,100 crore a quarter. If that holds, FY27E profit per share of about ₹28 is safe and the ₹200 target is reasonable. If it fails, the central case becomes the bear case. We will know from the December-quarter results, due by 31 January 2027, and from the full-year results, due by 30 April 2027[13]. The December RBI policy meeting adds a second reading on rates.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is to wait, and our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. We do not buy at ₹184.60. We place a limit order, which is an order that buys only at a stated price or lower, so that we pay no more than ₹172. The entry zone is the price range in which we would be willing to buy: ₹165.00 to ₹172.00. The stop loss is the price at which we accept the idea is wrong and sell: ₹162.16, which is the 20-day swing low of ₹164.81 less half the typical daily swing (₹2.65), applied to closing prices. What would raise the buy range is set out in the triggers below. Even then, at ₹184.60 the stock would still be a wait.

The rule behind the verdict is the reward-to-risk floor for turnaround ideas, 2.5 to 1. At ₹184.60 the ratio is 0.69 to 1, because the central target of ₹200 gains ₹15.40 while the stop risks ₹22.44. At the top of the zone, ₹172, it is 2.85 to 1, with ₹28.00 to gain and ₹9.84 to lose. The ladder of distance to support alone would put the stock in the 'standard' band (0.95 ATR from the 50-day average) and point to accumulating on dips, but the floor decides. The ₹172 top of the range depends on the central valuation holding. If the multiple compresses to 6.0 on the same profit, the target falls to ₹172.80 and the reward-to-risk ratio at ₹172 drops to 0.08 to 1, so the zone would then be useful only below about ₹165.20. The zone is anchored to three levels: just below the 20-day average (₹174.13), near the pre-result close of ₹168.10 on 5 October, and just above the 20-day swing low (₹164.81)[38][26].

The order plan is as follows. Our risk budget for a tier-three idea is 50 basis points of the portfolio, and the stop distance from ₹172 is 5.72 percent, so the formula would allow 8.7 percent, but the tier cap of 3.5 percent applies. We start with half, 1.75 percent, as two equal limit orders at ₹172 and ₹166. The second half is bought only if all four conditions hold after the December-quarter results: quarterly profit of at least ₹5,200 crore; net interest margin of at least 2.85 percent (between 2.75 and 2.85 percent we keep the orders but add nothing, and below 2.75 percent we cancel); the deposit shortfall condition in chapter 6 is not triggered (deposit growth below 8 percent together with a credit-to-deposit ratio above 90 percent); and the price is at or below ₹178. All unfilled orders are cancelled on 31 January 2027 unless the results are published and pass the hinge test. If the first half is filled at ₹172 and the stop is hit, the loss is about 0.10 percent of the portfolio.

This bank is not a current holding, so no holding audit applies. If the position fills and the stock rises, the let-winners-run rule applies: we do not trim just because the RSI passes 70 or the weight grows, and we trail the stop to the rising 50-day average while profit and the margin hold. The weight may grow to the 22 percent runaway cap for a winner. The decision is the same for every reader of this report: the price is the issue, not the bank. For a bank that makes ₹21,000 crore a year and trades at 6.4 times earnings, a wait of a few weeks costs little if the entry is better.
