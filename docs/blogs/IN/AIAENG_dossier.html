1. What the company does

A grinding mill is a large rotating steel drum that crushes rock into powder. Inside, thousands of steel balls tumble and smash the ore, and thick steel plates called liners protect the drum wall. Both wear away and must be replaced, and in management's own example a liner lasts about six months, so a mine or cement plant buys them again and again. AIA Engineering sells exactly these parts. In its own words it makes high-chrome grinding media, liners and diaphragms, collectively called mill internals, for cement, power-plant, quarry and mining customers[19]. A bag of cement and a gram of gold both need rock ground fine, which is why the same company can serve both.

Mining is the larger and more important customer. Of the 258,002 tonnes the group sold in FY26, mining took 159,813 tonnes (62%) and cement, thermal power and quarries took 98,189 tonnes (38%)[8]. Sales are mostly exports: ₹2,807 crore of the ₹4,355 crore product sales came from outside India and ₹1,548 crore from India[3]. The company reaches customers through its own marketing arm, Vega Industries, which carries stock in warehouses close to mines and is the only customer that crosses 10% of standalone revenue, a sign that it is the group's own distribution company and not an outside buyer[3]. Production sits in India, where management says the cost, skills and ports still make it the best place to make this product[14].

The money is made on weight, not on units. Product sales of ₹4,355.48 crore on 258,002 tonnes work out to about ₹168.8 for each kilogram (our arithmetic from the filed figures)[8]. Management asks investors to plan on about ₹165 a kilogram, because the price moves with six things: the product mix, the cost of the raw materials ferrochrome and steel scrap, the dollar, competition and shipping[13][14]. It also says the gross margin, the share of each sale left after the cost of materials, is consistently around 60% to 61%[14]. The business is therefore a spread business: AIA buys metal at one price, shapes it with heat, design and testing into a part that lasts longer in the mill, and keeps about 28% of sales as operating profit before other income (our arithmetic from the filed FY26 statements)[1].

The product competes with a cheaper alternative. Most mines still use forged steel balls, which are hard, simple and sold by distributors, including some twenty forging companies in China[14]. A broker's 2023 estimate puts AIA at about 7% of the roughly 2.5 million tonnes of grinding media that gold, copper and iron-ore mines use each year, and says high-chrome cast media has only 20% to 25% of the market, so most of the growth case is persuading mines to convert from forged to high-chrome[23]. That conversion is slow because each mine is different and the supplier must run trials that last from three months to two years[14]. The long-run opportunity management quotes is about 1.5 million tonnes in gold, copper and iron ore[14], against sales of 258,002 tonnes today.

How a new customer is won, and why it takes years

A mine does not switch grinding media on a price list. The supplier first studies the mill, proposes a design and alloy, supplies a trial lot, measures throughput, power use and the share of metal recovered, and only then discusses price and volume. Management calls this a solution sale, not a part sale, and says a conventional liner maker is 'just a vendor' in comparison[14]. The profit pool follows that logic: the operating margin (operating profit divided by sales) of about 28% is more than three times the 9% EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) margin that Magotteaux, the other large high-chrome maker, reported for 2025[1][24].

The newest product is a new generation discharge system, a set of plates at the exit of the mill, which management says raised throughput and cut power use in a very large South American mill after two years of work, and which led the customer to order a second mine conversion[13]. It cannot be sold alone: it is sold with liners and grinding media as one package, so a win adds all three parts to volume[14]. No tonnage has been disclosed for this win, which is why the market is waiting and the share price is not.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Return on capital employed (ROCE) is operating profit before interest divided by the money tied up in the business; a higher number means each rupee invested earns more.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a rough measure of the cash profit from running the business before financing and tax choices.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash the business generates from operations minus the cash spent on plant and equipment; it is what an owner could take out without starving the business.
  • Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills; when it rises faster than sales, profit shows up on paper before it shows up in the bank.
  • Operating margin is operating profit divided by sales; it shows how many paise of each rupee of sales are left after the costs of running the business.
  • Earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit divided by its number of shares; the stock price divided by EPS is the price-to-earnings ratio, which is the number of years of current profit the market is paying for.
  • Guidance is a forecast that management gives for its own results; AIA gives none for volume at present.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' funds; zero means the company owes banks nothing.
  • A consensus estimate is the average of the profit forecasts that professional analysts publish.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong in four ways, and each has evidence. First, the margin gap to its closest rival is large: AIA's operating margin before other income was about 28.4% in FY26 and 26.4% in the June 2026 quarter, while Magotteaux, which sold 608,000 tonnes and US$1,031 million of product in 2025, made an EBITDA margin of 9%[1][6][24]. A rival with more than twice the tonnage and one-third the margin is the best sign of a pricing edge that is not about scale. Second, the balance sheet is a fortress. Borrowings of ₹485 crore were repaid in FY26. Interest cover was 32.9 times. The company holds about ₹4,300 crore of investments and cash, roughly 13% of the market value[1][2][13]. Third, returns are high on any reading. Return on capital employed was 21.4% on our calculation and 21.1% on Screener. It is about 33% if the cash pile is taken out of capital employed (Chapter 3 shows the working)[19]. Fourth, the company is spending on the next step. It spent ₹104 crore on capital items in FY26. It has 436,000 tonnes of installed capacity against 258,002 tonnes sold, and it says it can run at 70% to 75% utilisation without new plants[2][13][14].

The business is weak in four other ways. Volume has not grown: sales were 291,342 tonnes in FY23, 297,345 in FY24, 255,443 in FY25 and 258,002 in FY26, so the three-year sales line fell 3.4% a year in rupees (FY23 ₹4,909 crore to FY26 ₹4,420 crore on the filed figures)[2][1][19]. Trade barriers hold back some markets: competitors in several countries file anti-dumping cases against AIA's imports, the United States finalised anti-dumping duties on Indian high-chrome grinding media in April 2025, and management says the United States anti-dumping rate was about 10% on top of the 50% metals tariff that applied in August 2025[2][12][27]. Profit growth since FY23 has come from interest on cash and foreign exchange gains, not from selling more: other income was ₹474 crore in FY26 against ₹332 crore in FY25 and made up about 29.8% of pre-tax profit[1]. And the growth story depends on a handful of mine trials whose results management will not share[14]. One more oddity is the capacity figure: the stated installed capacity is 460,000 tonnes in the FY25, first-quarter FY26 and half-year FY26 decks and 436,000 tonnes from the nine-month FY26 deck onward, and the filings we read do not say why[11][38][10][9].

The seed idea for this name called it a global duopoly with Magotteaux in high-chrome grinding media and mill liners[35]. That is half right. In high-chrome cast media the two are the largest suppliers, but high-chrome is only 20% to 25% of mining grinding media, forged balls from distributors and Chinese makers fill the rest, and AIA's share of the whole mining market is about 7%[23]. So AIA is a leader in a niche that is trying to take share from a larger incumbent product, not a duopolist in a closed market. That matters for the price: the moat protects margin on what AIA sells today, but it does not by itself create growth.

Cyclicality enters through the customers' own spending. Mining volume fell 3.8% in FY26 even as copper and gold prices were high, because large mines buy in annual cycles, change product mix, and delay trials, and management says it does not track quarterly run rates[8][14]. Gold averaged US$4,506 an ounce in the June 2026 quarter, 37% above a year before, and mine production rose 2%, so miners are healthy and not short of cash[26]. Management says gold and copper mines face worsening ore grades and falling metal output, which means mills must grind more rock for the same metal, and that is the problem its trials aim to solve[14]. The unit economics are favourable and the demand is durable, but the timing of volume is in the customers' hands, and the stock has been de-rated because that timing has slipped for three years.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record is mixed in an informative way. On things it controls, such as capital spending, margin and debt, it delivered or beat what it said a year ago. Operating profit before interest is profit before tax of ₹1,591.77 crore plus finance costs of ₹36.51 crore, or ₹1,628.28 crore. 1,628.28 / 7,606.18 = 21.4%. For the core business, we take EBITDA of ₹1,744.26 crore, remove other income of ₹474.05 crore and the joint-venture share of ₹13.92 crore, and subtract depreciation of ₹112.69 crore, which gives ₹1,143.60 crore. We divide by average capital employed after removing investments (₹3,864.09 crore and ₹3,127.65 crore, average ₹3,495.87 crore): 1,143.60 / 3,495.87 = 32.7%.

Our variant perception

Where we differ from the market, and why. The market's view, as the consensus numbers express it, is that FY27 profit per share grows only about 2% (₹138.80 against ₹136.11 reported for FY26) and FY28 grows 10%, so the benefit of the new conversions is expected in FY28. We also differ from the sceptics. The company is not in trouble: it has no debt, high returns, a margin three times its main rival's, and real proof of concept at a very large mine[13][24]. How it was calculated

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 attacks our own numbers. Each estimate has one assumption it cannot survive without, a fact that would show it wrong, and a date when that fact arrives. Weakest assumption of the reverse DCF: which cash flow is the right base

The reverse DCF gives 15.7% a year on three-year average free cash flow of ₹734.85 crore, but between 10.8% and 21.2% depending on the base year[1][4][5]. The weakest point is that FY26's cash flow was depressed by a ₹576 crore build in receivables and stock, which could be timing or could be slower-paying customers. Evidence to watch is receivable days, which were 74 at March 2025, 84 at March 2026 and 90 at June 2026[8][6]. Weakest assumption of the earnings estimate: 290,000 tonnes and a 28% margin in FY28

Our FY28 earnings per share of ₹137.6 needs volume to rise 12% from FY26 and the operating margin to hold at 28%. If volume is 270,000 tonnes instead, earnings per share is about ₹129.7; if it is 320,000 tonnes, it is about ₹149.3 (our sensitivity at 28% margin). A reader who disagrees would have a defensible case for the opposite verdict, and should look at the earnings estimate first, because the two assumptions together decide the result.

Red-team bear case: why the stock could be worth much less

The strongest bear case has five parts. First, the quality of earnings: other income was 29.8% of FY26 pre-tax profit, exchange gains and treasury returns are not operating income, and a falling rupee or lower deposit rates would remove some of it[1][8]. Second, the moat: AIA has about 7% of the mining grinding media market, Chinese forged ball makers are numerous and cheap, and high-chrome conversion has been promised since at least 2023 without a tonnage figure; a 2023 broker note already described a 20% to 25% penetration headroom[23][14]. Third, trade barriers: the United States finalised anti-dumping duties on Indian high-chrome grinding media in April 2025, the August 2025 call described an anti-dumping rate near 10% on top of a 50% metals tariff, and Brazil and Canada have duty actions with an incumbent that benefits[12][14][27]. Fourth, capital allocation: ₹4,300 crore sits in investments, the dividend is 11.8% of profit, and the company has chosen to spend ₹170 crore to ₹200 crore on land for a corporate house while volume is 13% below its peak[14][1]. Fifth, governance housekeeping: the secretarial audit reports that the board and audit committee were not correctly composed at 31 March 2025 and that fines were paid to the exchanges, and the chairman changed in April 2026[16][2]. On this view the stock is a good business at a fair price with no catalyst, and the fair price could fall toward ₹2,528 if conversions stall.

The bear case has two counterweights. Magotteaux's 9% margin shows that the niche is hard to enter profitably, and the Chile mine win is the first evidence that a very large mine will pay for the whole solution[24][13]. We keep the bear case at 30% for this reason.
