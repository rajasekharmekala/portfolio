1. What the company does

Man Industries makes large steel pipes, the kind laid in trenches to move oil, gas and water across hundreds of kilometres. It runs three plants. Pithampur in Madhya Pradesh and Anjar in Gujarat together have more than 1.2 million tonnes a year of capacity. Since 21 May 2026 a third plant in Saudi Arabia adds 430,000 tonnes a year, which lifts the group to about 1.6 million tonnes[4][8]. A pipe is made by shaping a steel plate or coil into a tube and welding the seam. The company uses three methods. LSAW (longitudinal submerged arc welded) presses a plate into a round tube and welds one straight seam. HSAW (helical, or spiral) winds a coil into a spiral and welds the spiral seam. ERW (electric resistance welded) is used for smaller pipe[4][8]. Management says 80% to 85% of pipes are coated with plastic layers that stop rust, and that more than 80% of the India order book is for export[3].

The customer is a pipeline owner or a contractor that has won a tender. The company quotes a price per tonne, buys the steel, turns it into pipe, coats it and ships it. Steel is the largest cost and is passed through to the buyer, so sales move with steel prices. In FY26 steel prices were about 25% lower than a year earlier, yet standalone sales still rose[7]. What matters is the profit left after steel and plant costs, so this report follows EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), which is operating profit before finance and accounting charges, and the operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales. Consolidated EBITDA margin was 13.0% in FY26 and 14.6% in Q1 FY27, against 7.8% in FY23[5][6][1].

The way the company sells has changed. In FY26 more than 70% of volume moved on delivered-duty-paid (DDP) terms, where the company pays freight, duty and clearing and charges the buyer for them[7]. That lifts both sales and other expenses, and it distorts the gross margin, which is sales minus the direct cost of materials as a share of sales. On the Q1 FY27 call an analyst said gross margin fell from 53% in Q4 to 35% in Q1. The finance chief put it down to product mix and promised a fuller answer, which the transcript does not contain[3]. Because of this, the report leans on EBITDA and cash flow rather than on gross margin.

A pipe plant also ties up working capital, which is the cash locked in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company itself owes suppliers. Inventory days rose from 98 in FY24 to 251 in FY26, debtor days from 41 to 103, and payable days from 76 to 241[14]. The company is therefore carrying far more stock and far more credit on both sides of its ledger, and Chapter 3 shows how much of its reported cash flow rests on supplier credit. Management gives guidance, which means its own forecast of sales and margin for the year ahead. The latest is revenue of about ₹5,000 crore in FY27 at an EBITDA margin of 13% to 15%[4].

Customers, countries and how the orders arrive

Orders arrive one tender at a time, which makes sales lumpy. Exports are the bulk of the India order book, at 80% or more, and many go to Taiwan, Central Asia, the Gulf and Southeast Asia[3][7]. The Taiwan branch alone booked ₹321 crore of revenue in Q1 FY27, which is 32% of standalone revenue on our division of the two filed figures. The auditor did not review that branch's accounts and notes they are management-certified[2]. NPC sells to Saudi Aramco, the Saudi Water Authority, Qatar Petroleum and contractors such as Larsen and Toubro, Saipem and Subsea7[10]. About 20% to 25% of the India shipments in the 2026 regional conflict were headed toward the Strait of Hormuz, and two ships waited for clearance in the spring[7]. What this means for you: a single large order, a shipping closure or a customer's delay can move a quarter, and the profit line tells you less than the order book does.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is stronger than it was, but it is not yet a compounder. Operating profit as a share of sales climbed from 6% in FY23 to 14% on the trailing twelve months, and net profit rose from ₹67 crore to ₹204 crore[14]. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses. Screener shows 16.2% now, against 10% in FY23, and the company's own FY26 figure is 18.4%, because the two sources define capital differently. Margins differ for a similar reason: Screener's operating profit leaves out other income, which gives 6% for FY23 and 12% for FY26, while the company's EBITDA includes it, which gives 7.8% and 13.0%[14][1]. Return on equity is weaker at 9.2%, because the share count grew 15.8% in FY26 and net profit is only 4.7% of sales[1]. CRISIL, the rating agency, raised the long-term rating to A+/Stable from A/Stable on 1 June 2026 and named the NPC deal, better operating results and wider geography as reasons[11]. A rating upgrade tells you the lenders are more comfortable. It does not tell you the shares are cheap.

The moat is made of approvals and relationships, not patents. A pipe maker needs a customer's approved-vendor status before it can bid, and that takes years of plant audits. NPC has held Saudi Aramco's approved-vendor status since 2005, and Man India was added to the Qatar Energy LNG vendor list in 2025[8][18]. The group has supplied more than 20,000 km of pipe to 30-plus countries[1]. Management says demand for large pipe is ahead of supply and will stay so for three to four years, helped by Saudi gas and water programmes, and Aramco's Master Gas System expansion is a US$10 billion project split into 17 packages, 15 of them for pipeline laying[3][26]. Chapter 6 tests the other side of that claim.

The weak spots are visible in the same sources. The India plants ran at only 50% to 60% of capacity in Q1 FY27, which management attributes to the mix of order sizes rather than a lack of room[3]. The unexecuted order book was ₹4,600 crore in January 2026, fell to about ₹3,000 crore for India in May, and stood at ₹3,600 crore in August with the Saudi plant included, of which ₹2,200 crore to ₹2,300 crore is India[21][7][3]. Orders of about ₹1,000 crore on 18 June, ₹300 crore of it for India and ₹700 crore for NPC, had lifted the consolidated book to about ₹4,100 crore[22]. After a quarter of deliveries it stood at ₹3,600 crore on 12 August, and a ₹600 crore order from domestic and international customers on 10 September took it back to about ₹4,100 crore[23]. Competitors are adding Saudi capacity too: the company's own deck lists about 1.05 million tonnes arriving in 2027 to 2028 from Group Five, Welspun Corp and Jindal Saw, on top of East Pipes' 500,000 tonnes finished in March 2026[8].

What was bought in Saudi Arabia, and why it was cheap

NPC cost US$102 million for 100% of the shares, funded by US$70 million of debt taken inside the Saudi subsidiary and US$32 million of the group's own cash[8][7]. The seller was a group in which Nippon Steel and Sumitomo held 52%. Management says they wanted to exit to focus on steel, and that NPC lost business because the Japanese owners bought Japanese steel instead of cheaper Chinese plate[7]. NPC's own figures for 2025 are strong. Revenue was SAR 792.7 million, EBITDA SAR 196.7 million (24.8%) and profit SAR 143.5 million (18.1%). Net worth was US$158.6 million, so the price was 0.6 times book value on our division of US$102 million by US$158.6 million, which the company's slide prints as 0.7 times, and 2.7 times profit, against 9.0 to 10.4 times EBITDA for four Saudi-listed peers[8]. The company calls NPC debt-free with US$83 million of cash and liquid assets. The slide breaks that sum into US$38.0 million of cash, US$13.0 million of receivables and US$32.0 million of finished-goods stock, so only about 46% of it is cash[8].

The price looks too low for a plant earning 25% margins, and the report does not take the explanation at face value. Management points to a motivated seller and to a plant that ran below capacity under Japanese ownership. The same management cut its FY27 sales forecast for NPC from ₹1,500 crore to ₹2,000 crore in May to about ₹1,200 crore in August, and in May said the first quarter would be similar to or slightly below the year before[7][3]. The company converts NPC's 2025 revenue to about ₹1,899 crore, so the new forecast is about 37% below that full-year figure, or about 26% below it after allowing for ten months of ownership instead of twelve (our arithmetic)[8]. Our reading is that the 2025 margins came from a large Aramco programme that may not repeat, which is why Chapter 5 assumes a 15% to 17% margin and not 25%.

The accounting is the part we cannot yet see. The segment note in the June 2026 results shows capital employed, which is the segments' assets less their liabilities, of ₹2,679 crore, up from ₹2,087 crore at 31 March 2026[2]. That is a rise of ₹592 crore in a quarter when profit was ₹61 crore[2]. Estimate. We think the jump is mostly the difference between NPC's net worth of US$158.6 million and the US$102 million paid. At the roughly ₹94 per US dollar implied by the company's ₹960 crore for US$102 million, that gap is about ₹533 crore[8][1]. If so, the deal produced an accounting gain on buying below book value, booked in equity and not in the profit line. It would make the balance sheet look stronger and book value per share higher, and it would not add a rupee to earnings. The filings we fetched do not state this, and the full balance sheet arrives with the Q2 FY27 results.

The regulator's order, and what it means for governance

On 29 September 2025 SEBI, the market regulator, issued a 73-page order against the company, its chairman, its managing director and its chief financial officer[35]. A complaint had alleged that funds were moved to subsidiaries and that a subsidiary's results were left out of the group accounts to hide losses. A forensic audit covering FY2015 to FY2021 followed[35]. SEBI found that the company did not consolidate its subsidiary Merino Shelters Private Limited for FY2016 to FY2021, and that this broke the listing rules. It also found failures to disclose related-party dealings[35]. The company argued that consolidation would have contradicted a Bombay High Court order and that it had explained the position in its annual reports. SEBI did not accept that argument[35].

SEBI barred all four from accessing the securities market for two years, froze their existing holdings during the ban, and fined each ₹25 lakh, which is ₹1 crore in total[35]. The shares fell 10.7% in a session on 30 September 2025[37]. On 10 October 2025 the Securities Appellate Tribunal admitted the company's appeal and stayed the ban, on condition that half the penalty be deposited in a fixed deposit with a lien in SEBI's favour. It listed the case for 20 January 2026[36]. We could not find what happened after that date, so the appeal is treated as pending.

What this means for you: the order concerns years that ended in 2021 and the ban is stayed, so it does not stop trading today. It does mean that the chairman, who holds all of the pledged shares, is the subject of an open regulatory appeal. If the tribunal lifted the stay, the freeze on existing holdings could apply for the rest of a two-year period that began on 29 September 2025. It is also a reason to read the Merino Shelters real-estate income and the Taiwan branch figures with care. We treat the matter as a governance warning, which our India red lines do not score on their own, and we weigh it with the pledge in Chapter 7.

Quality of earnings: three checks

First, standalone profit and consolidated profit differ. In Q1 FY27 standalone profit was ₹78 crore and consolidated profit ₹61 crore. In FY26 the figures were ₹196 crore and ₹171 crore[1]. Management says the standalone company earns interest and guarantee fees from its subsidiaries, which cancel on consolidation, and that a ₹25 crore currency loss on imported Jammu equipment sits in the subsidiary[3][7]. Intercompany deposits carry about 8% interest[7]. We therefore use consolidated numbers throughout.

Second, cash flow from operations, which is the cash the trading business produces before it pays for plants and machines, depends on suppliers. Trade payables rose from ₹503 crore in FY24 to ₹1,471 crore in FY26, and other financial liabilities jumped from ₹30 crore to ₹580 crore in FY26[1]. Net debt is borrowings minus cash, and a negative figure is called net cash. The company reports net cash of ₹157.5 crore for March 2026. It also reports free cash flow (FCF), which is the cash left after spending on plants and machines, of ₹132 crore for FY26[5]. Both figures are true on the balance sheet date. A large part of the cash was also funded by creditors who must be paid.

Third, audit coverage is thinner than the headline suggests. The auditor's limited review report says the Dubai branch and the Saudi, UAE and real-estate subsidiaries were reviewed by other auditors, and that the Taiwan branch and two UAE subsidiaries were management-certified and not reviewed. Its conclusion is unmodified[2]. That is acceptable under the rules, and it is a reason to want the audited year-end accounts for FY27 before relying on the branch figures.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A pipe maker is judged on whether it has work booked, whether the work earns a decent margin, whether the cash comes in, whether the new plants arrive on time, and whether an acquisition delivers. These five measures are chosen for that reason, and each has a pass mark stated before we look at the number. The standard is that at least four of five must pass. A bank would be judged on loan quality, and a software company on repeat revenue, so these five would be wrong for them.

The same measures are the ones management itself talks about on every call, which makes them fair. The order book and the margin are on the first slide of each results deck, and the plant dates are repeated in each press release[4][1]. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has a mixed record on forecasts. On 13 May 2025 it said it was confident of 20% sales growth in FY26, with standalone sales near ₹4,000 crore, and it repeated the 20% figure on 14 November 2025[18][20]. Standalone sales were ₹3,455 crore, up 10.8% and about 14% short of the ₹4,000 crore mark[1]. Margins did better than promised. Management had expected a gain of 50 to 100 basis points in margin, and the consolidated EBITDA margin rose by 290 basis points[18][5].

The hardest miss is the outlook for FY27. In November 2025 the finance chief said the company would touch about ₹7,000 crore of revenue in FY27. In May 2026 the guide was ₹5,000 crore to ₹5,500 crore, and in August it was about ₹5,000 crore, a cut of 29% from the November figure, even though the new figure now includes NPC[20][7][4]. The plants slipped as well. In May 2025 the Saudi and Jammu plants were to be running by Q3 FY26, in August 2025 the Saudi mill was a 300,000-tonne plant costing about ₹630 crore, and in November Jammu was to start on 1 April 2026[18][19][20]. The Saudi mill was scrapped when NPC was bought, and Jammu now targets March 2027[7][4].

There are things management did deliver. It raised the money it said it needed: the June 2025 plan of ₹300 crore of fresh capital was completed in July and August 2025, and the monitoring report shows no deviation in how the funds were used[25][24]. It closed the NPC deal after, by its own account, talks that ran on and off for at least nine months, and it reduced the promoters' pledge from 27.2% in mid-2024 to 20.05% by May 2025[7][13]. The pledge then stopped falling and has stayed at 20.05% at every reading since May 2025[13].

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of numbers show a company that has roughly doubled its profit while borrowing more. Consolidated sales rose from ₹2,139 crore in FY22 to ₹3,564 crore in FY26 and ₹3,875 crore over the twelve months to June 2026. Net profit rose from ₹102 crore to ₹170 crore and then to ₹204 crore[14]. EPS (earnings per share) is net profit divided by the number of shares. It rose from ₹17.16 to ₹27.24 on the trailing twelve months. It fell slightly in FY26 to ₹22.73, because the 15.8% increase in shares spread a bigger profit across more holders[14][12].

The sales path hides a one-off. FY25 sales of ₹3,505 crore include ₹369 crore of real estate revenue from the Merino Shelters land assignment in the last quarter, so FY26 sales of ₹3,564 crore are up only 1.7% as reported. Taking the one-off out of FY25, which is our arithmetic, FY26 sales grew about 13.6%[5][1]. Margins did the real work. Operating profit rose from ₹137 crore in FY23 to ₹439 crore in FY26, which is a margin move from 6% to 12%, and to ₹532 crore at 14% over the latest twelve months[14].

Borrowings and interest rose with the expansion. Borrowings, which include lease liabilities in the Screener series, grew from ₹57 crore in FY22 to ₹628 crore in FY26, and interest cost from ₹37 crore to ₹152 crore[14]. Shareholders' equity rose from about ₹943 crore to ₹2,087 crore, helped by retained profit and the 2025 share issue, so debt stayed small against equity at 0.30 times[14][1]. 5. What could happen next

A reverse DCF works backward from the price. An ordinary discounted cash flow model adds up the profit a company will earn in future years and shrinks each year by a discount rate. Here we start from the market value of ₹6,491 crore instead, and ask what profit growth for ten years would justify it. The rules use a 12% discount rate and a terminal multiple of 20 times profit for India. The answer is growth of 12.75% a year from trailing profit of ₹204 crore[14][16].

In plain words the price assumes profit grows from ₹204 crore to about ₹677 crore by FY36, which is our arithmetic on the ledger's 12.75%. Is that realistic? Against our own forecast it is not demanding. Our FY28 profit is about ₹456 crore, and growing from there to ₹677 crore over eight years needs only about 5% a year. The trailing profit also contains just 40 days of NPC, so the starting point understates the earning power of the group as it now stands. The valuation, in other words, is not what rejects the stock, although the upside depends on the margin step-up arriving. The risk is whether the FY28 profit arrives, and whether it stays when steel, freight and pipe prices move.

Analyst coverage is thin. MarketScreener shows one analyst with a target of ₹800, which is 7.7% below the price[16]. The ledger shows that analyst's EPS forecast for FY27 at ₹47.0, up 42.9% in 30 days but down 25.5% over 90 days, and for FY28 at ₹75.3, down 11.2% over 90 days, which the ledger labels FALLING_ESTIMATES. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report rests on an assumption that could be wrong, and this chapter names the weakest one for each. For the EPS estimate it is that NPC earns ₹300 to ₹500 crore of revenue a quarter at a 15% to 17% margin from Q2 FY27. For the debt estimate it is that Jammu and the Dammam coating plant finish by March 2027 and working capital stays under control. For the cash estimate it is that suppliers do not ask to be paid faster. For the target it is that the market keeps paying 19.6 times profit while the Nifty 50 falls. Each is a single point of failure, and the table later in this chapter sets out what would show it and when we will know.

A red-team exercise asks the opposite question to the one the rest of the report answers. Instead of asking what has to go right, it asks what would have to go wrong for the stock to lose half its value. Three scenarios do that. None is a forecast, and each uses facts already in the filings.

Fatal flaw 1: the India order book does not refill

The India order book fell from ₹4,600 crore in January to ₹2,200 to ₹2,300 crore in August, and the India plants ran at 50% to 60% of capacity[21][3]. Management's FY27 India sales target of about ₹3,800 crore needs about ₹2,790 crore of sales in the last nine months, which is our arithmetic on the ₹3,800 crore guide less ₹1,010 crore booked in Q1[3][2]. Management said some orders were already won but not announced because it only announces orders above ₹500 crore, and the ₹600 crore order of 10 September is consistent with that[3][23]. If the autumn passes without a refill, India sales could slip toward ₹3,300 crore and the FY27 guide of about ₹5,000 crore becomes about ₹4,500 crore. The stock has already priced a forecast near ₹5,000 crore, so a miss would hit the price twice, through the profit and through the multiple.

Fatal flaw 2: NPC earns less than the story, and Saudi prices soften

NPC's 24.8% EBITDA margin in 2025 came from an Aramco-heavy order mix, and management now guides 15% to 18%[8][7]. New Saudi capacity arrives: about 1.05 million tonnes from Group Five, Welspun Corp and Jindal Saw in 2027 and 2028, on top of 500,000 tonnes from East Pipes in March 2026, against a Saudi base of about 2.5 million tonnes[8]. Management itself says there may be price pressure, though not a price war[3]. The Saudi plant also depends on one customer. In Q1 the 2026 regional conflict closed or slowed shipping lanes, and Aramco work is what carries the margin. Measured against the Chapter 3 base, where NPC earns ₹1,093 crore of revenue at the group's 14.5% margin, which is about ₹158 crore of EBITDA, NPC revenue of ₹1,000 crore at a 13% margin would give ₹130 crore. That is about ₹28 crore less EBITDA and takes FY27 EPS from about ₹37 to about ₹34 (our arithmetic).

Fatal flaw 3: creditors, lenders and the regulator call time

Debt rises to about ₹1,600 crore at the peak, with US$70 million at NPC, ₹389 crore for Jammu and US$25 million for the coating plant[3]. Trade payables have risen to ₹1,471 crore and other financial liabilities to ₹580 crore, so a large part of the cash was funded by suppliers and letters of credit[1]. If those creditors shorten their terms, the net cash of ₹157.5 crore becomes net debt quickly. The promoter pledge adds a separate trigger: the pledged 6,500,000 shares are worth about ₹562 crore at today's price, and a fall to the 200-day average of ₹547.58 would cut that to about ₹356 crore, which is our arithmetic and which may prompt a lender to ask for more security[12][29]. Finally, a third of Q1 standalone revenue came through a Taiwan branch whose accounts were management-certified and not reviewed by the auditor[2]. An error or a delay there would hit sentiment at once. The SEBI order adds a legal strand. If the tribunal lifted its stay, the two-year restraint and the freeze on holdings of the company, the chairman and the managing director would apply, at a time when the chairman has pledged shares and the group needs to roll over loans[35][36].

Four labels in the table below need plain words. A support band sorts a price by how far it sits above its nearest floor, measured in ATR.
