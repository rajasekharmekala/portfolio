1. What the company does

Man Industries (India) Limited makes large-diameter steel pipes, called line pipes, that carry oil, gas and water over long distances. The company was founded in 1988, and its first long-seam pipe plant opened in 1994 at Pithampur in Madhya Pradesh [23]. It now has plants at Pithampur and Anjar in Gujarat [6] (secondary), and since May 2026 a third in Saudi Arabia [22] (secondary). A long-seam (LSAW) pipe is a flat steel plate bent into a tube and welded along its length. A spiral (HSAW) pipe is steel coil wound in a spiral and welded along the seam. The Indian plants can make 11.75 lakh tonnes a year, split equally between the two types (one lakh is one hundred thousand), and the Saudi plant adds 430,000 tonnes [17] (secondary)[22].

The buyers are oil and gas companies, pipeline contractors and water authorities. Exports were 79.10 percent of FY26 turnover [20] (secondary), and management says more than 80 percent of the India order book is for export [5]. The Saudi plant's customers include Saudi Aramco, the Saudi Water Authority and contractors such as Larsen & Toubro [22] (secondary). Each pipe is made to a customer's order, so sales follow the order book rather than a catalogue. That is why this book tracks the order book first and the share price second.

Two smaller units matter. A subsidiary is building a stainless seamless pipe plant at Jammu for ₹600 crore, with production expected by March 2027 [5]. Merino Shelters Private Limited, a wholly owned subsidiary, granted development rights on about six acres in Navi Mumbai to the Paradise Group on 31 March 2025 and is entitled to 30 percent of the developed area [10]. Management expects ₹35 to ₹50 crore of cash from it in FY27 [6] (secondary). The same subsidiary sits at the centre of the SEBI order described in chapter 3 [14].

How one order becomes profit

A buyer awards a project. The company buys steel plate or coil from mills, forms and welds the pipe, tests it, and often coats it. Management says it fixes the steel cost as soon as an order is confirmed [8]. Profit is therefore the gap between the agreed pipe price and the locked steel cost, plus the extra charged for coating and bends. That gap moves with the mix: management noted that a pipe of the same diameter can sell for USD 2,000 or USD 1,000 depending on grade [5]. In FY26 more orders were delivered to the customer's site, a term called delivered duty paid, which moves freight into costs; management says it recovers freight and more [7][5].

Two consequences follow. First, the steel cost is protected only on orders already booked, and steel prices are at a four-year high in October 2026 [25] (secondary), so new bids start from a higher cost. Second, the profit per tonne is not a fixed number, which is why chapter 2B scores margin durability and not just the margin.

Reading guide to the measures used in this report

Each term is explained once, here, and used in the same sense afterwards. The list is short on purpose, so that a reader with no finance training can follow the tables.

Why the list is long. The report uses many measures because a pipe maker is judged on several things at once: how fast it grows, how well it turns steel into profit, how much cash it ties up, and how the share price is behaving. A reader does not need to remember every term. Each table carries a sentence before it that says what to look for, and a paragraph after it that says what the numbers mean for an owner. Where a figure is our own calculation and not a filed number, the text says so, and the run notes at the end list every such item. The terms that matter most for the verdict are the pledge, the ratio of possible gain to possible loss, the exit price and the scorecard.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit before interest and tax divided by the money tied up in the business. It shows how well each rupee of capital is used.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges. Management counts other income inside it, and we say so where it matters.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit after tax divided by the number of shares. price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share; a figure of 27 means investors pay 27 rupees for each rupee of yearly profit.
  • free cash flow (FCF for short) is the cash a business generates from operations minus what it spends on plant and equipment. cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates from its day-to-day work after tax is paid, before spending on plant.
  • capital expenditure is money spent on plant and equipment. working capital is the money tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers; a business with long collection and stock periods needs more of it.
  • debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' funds; below 1 means shareholders fund more than lenders do. net debt is borrowings minus cash and similar assets.
  • operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales. gross margin is sales minus the cost of materials, as a share of sales.
  • guidance is what management says it expects to deliver. dilution is the fall in each existing owner's share of the company when new shares are issued.
  • consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who cover a stock.
  • RSI, the relative strength index, is a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has moved; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought. ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing, which we use to measure distances on a chart.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing price over the last 20, 50 and 200 sessions. They smooth the price into a trend line.
  • Stage 2 is a rising trend: the price is above a rising 50-day average that is above the 200-day average. Stage 4 is the reverse: the price is below a falling 50-day average that is below a falling 200-day average.
  • base is a flat stretch of trading after a decline, from which a stock may start rising; its pivot is the price at the top of that stretch, and a breakout is a close above the pivot on higher volume.
  • retest is a pullback to a level the price just broke through, to see whether buyers defend it. stop loss is the price at which we would sell to limit a loss.
  • limit order is an order to buy at a stated price or lower, never higher. entry zone is the price range in which we would be willing to buy.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop. A ratio of 2.5 means 2.5 rupees of possible gain for each rupee at risk; the house floor for this strategy is 2.5.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong where it is hard to copy and weak where it is cyclical. The hard-to-copy part is approval: oil majors buy pipe only from mills they have qualified, and qualification takes years. The cyclical part is that orders arrive in lumps, steel is the main cost, and the customer list is concentrated in export projects. The three subsections below separate what is proven from what management claims.

Competitive position: what is proven and what is not

Proven: the Indian LSAW plants hold API licences and approvals from oil and gas majors [24]. The Saudi plant gives Aramco-approved vendor status, which management calls a move from exporter to in-kingdom manufacturer [5]. The company became a listed vendor of Qatar Energy LNG in 2025 [10]. CRISIL, a rating agency, upgraded the long-term rating to A+ with a stable outlook on 1 June 2026, citing the Saudi deal and a better operating record [18] (secondary).

Not proven: management says there is a shortfall of pipe supply against demand for the next three to four years, and also accepts that new Saudi capacity, with players such as Welspun, East Pipes and Arabian Pipes named by an analyst, may bring some pressure on price [5]. Those are company statements about the future. India utilisation of 50 to 60 percent shows the Indian plants are not full today [5].

Pricing power and steel cost

The profit margin has risen, but its history is mixed. CRISIL puts the operating margin at 12.3 percent in FY26 against 8 to 10 percent in the past [18] (secondary). An analyst on the August 2026 call noted that gross margin rose from about 22 percent in earlier years to nearly 38 percent in FY26; management credited delivered-duty-paid orders and coating [5]. The same call showed a quarter-on-quarter fall from 53 percent to 35 percent, and the finance chief said he would come back with an answer; none is on the record we read [5].

Pass-through of steel cost works through the lock-in policy [8]. It protects booked orders but not the next bid. With flat-steel prices at a four-year high and hot-rolled coil at ₹64,100 a tonne in early October 2026 [25] (secondary), the margin on orders won from now on depends on how much higher steel can be recovered in price. We could not test that from any filing, so it is a watch item and not a finding.

Where the strength is thin

CRISIL names the working-capital-intensive nature of the business and its exposure to end-user cycles and steel and currency swings as the main weaknesses [17] (secondary). Screener's own checklist flags debtor days rising from 79 to 103 [1] (secondary aggregator). Governance is the other thin point: SEBI barred the company and three executives from the securities market in September 2025 for misstated accounts from FY16 to FY21, the appeal tribunal stayed the order on 10 October 2025, and the appeal is still pending [14][15] (secondary)[20] (secondary). The promoters also pledge 6,500,000 shares as security for company bank loans [3]. Chapter 3 tests both items against the hard red lines. What this means for an owner: the weaknesses are manageable alone, but the pledge and the court record together are what move this stock out of the bucket of names that can be bought on a chart signal.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions, and some of them are weak. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, the evidence that would show it wrong, and the date we would know. The red-team view is that the stock is a good business at a price that already assumes the good news; we state the strongest case against that view as well.

Earnings: the Saudi ramp and the margin

Our central FY28 profit per share of ₹38.90 assumes 15 percent revenue growth and a 13.5 percent margin. Management guides 25 to 30 percent growth and 14 to 16 percent margin for three to five years but says it cannot commit [5]. If the Saudi plant reaches ₹400 to ₹500 crore a quarter at 20 percent or better, we are too low. The Saudi plant was run before the takeover by a company that management says wasted material and could not make the wide spiral pipe that water orders need [5], so the improvement is plausible but unproven. Only about 20 days of Saudi sales are in the reported numbers [5]. What this means for an owner: the earnings estimate is the one most likely to be moved by a single quarterly report.

Multiple: 17.435 times may be too low for a changed business

The median comes from a period when the company was smaller, earned 6 to 10 percent margins and carried the SEBI case. A bigger, higher-margin business with a Saudi platform could deserve more. The peer set argues the same way: Welspun Corp trades at 27.3 times and Ratnamani at 37.0 times [27] (secondary)[29] (secondary). Against that, the range of our ten readings is 14.0 to 24.2 times, the stock is at 27.4 times, and the market has never paid this multiple for it in our series. The bull case already uses 20.0 times. What this means for an owner: a wrong multiple moves the value as much as a wrong earnings figure, because at 38.90 of earnings each point of multiple is worth about ₹39 a share. The risk is two-sided, but the market is already at the top of the range.

Steel cost and pass-through

The lock-in policy protects booked orders [8], and flat steel is at a four-year high [25] (secondary). If the company has to quote new bids at higher steel cost and competitors with Saudi capacity cut price, the margin on the next ₹3,000 crore of orders will be lower than on the current book. We found no filing that splits gross margin by order, so this stays a watch item. The unexplained fall in gross margin from 53 to 35 percent between the last two quarters is the specific piece of evidence we are missing [5]. What this means for an owner: the margin is the most sensitive input in the model. Each percentage point of margin moves FY28 profit per share by about ₹5.2, which at the median multiple is about ₹90 a share.

Cash flow and funding: debt rising to ₹1,600 crore

Debt is planned to rise from about ₹628 crore to as much as ₹1,600 crore once Jammu and Dammam draw their loans, with finance costs near ₹190 crore in FY27 [1] (secondary aggregator)[5]. On our FY27 central profit, that is about 0.7 times shareholders' funds at March 2027, close to the 0.70 compounder bar but far from the 1.20 red line. Receivables are rising faster than sales, and the Saudi plant needs working capital of USD 100 to 125 million on USD 200 million of sales [5]. A delay in either plant would add interest before it adds revenue. What this means for an owner: financing risk is moderate and rating-supported, since CRISIL expects net cash accruals of over ₹450 crore against repayments of ₹240 to ₹250 crore [18] (secondary), but it limits how much the company can absorb a delay without cutting its plans.

Governance and legal: a stay is not a final ruling

The 2025 SEBI order found accounts misstated for six years and was stayed on a condition [14][15] (secondary). The company says the issues are legacy and that it has consolidated since FY24 [19] (secondary). If the tribunal confirms the order, the restraint on the company and its chairman and managing director would take effect and the multiple would likely fall. The pledge of 6,500,000 shares secures company loans [3]; a lender could in principle invoke it. We assign no probability to these events and treat them as the reason the pledge limb is a hard line and not a judgment. What this means for an owner: this is the risk with no timetable, and it is the one that could change the label from a pricing question to a trust question for a long-term holder.
