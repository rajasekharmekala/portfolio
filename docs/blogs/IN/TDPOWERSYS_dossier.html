1. What the company does

TD Power Systems Limited, based near Bengaluru, designs and builds alternating-current generators and large electric motors.[7] A generator is the machine that turns the spin of an engine or turbine into electricity. The range covers generators for steam and gas turbines up to 250 MVA (a unit of electrical size), hydro generators up to 45 MVA, generators for gas and diesel engines up to 25 MVA, and induction, synchronous and traction motors.[7] The managing director told a television interviewer that about 95 percent of current business is in machines below 60 MW, the mid-sized end of the market.[19] On 30 June 2026 the company had installed 8,375 generators in 113 countries.[7]

The buyers are mostly other equipment makers, not the final power-plant owners. A turbine or engine maker needs a generator to pair with its machine, buys it from TD Power, and ships the complete set to the project. Management lists gas turbines, gas engines, steam turbines for steel, cement and sugar plants, and hydro projects as the main order sources.[7][8] In the quarter to 30 June 2026, exports and deemed exports (sales to Indian firms that re-export) were 93 percent of new orders: ₹6,836 million of ₹7,341 million.[7] The order book of ₹22,073 million on that date was 69.5 percent exports, 17.9 percent domestic, 7.6 percent Indian locomotive motors, 2.1 percent Turkey, 1.9 percent railway exports and 1.0 percent spares.[7] The filings do not name the largest customers or give their share of sales, so customer concentration is not published and cannot be measured here.[13]

Each machine is built to order, so revenue arrives as machines ship. Many contracts carry price-variation clauses, which pass metal cost changes on to the customer.[8] The gross margin — sales minus the direct cost of materials, shown as a share of sales — was 34.7 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[7] After staff and other running costs, EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit before accounting charges) was 19.0 percent of sales.[7] Management says it steers margin with price rises, cost cuts, product mix and exchange rates, and aims to stay within about one percentage point of its target gross contribution.[8] Spares and service are only 5 to 6 percent of sales, because a well-built generator needs almost no service in its first ten years.[8]

Three forces drive demand. First, data centres need power now and cannot wait for the grid, so they install gas-fired power on site, and TD Power's turbine and engine customers carry some of the longest order books in the industry.[8][26] Trade press reports that GE Vernova alone has 116 gigawatts of gas turbine backlog, with deliveries scheduled out to 2031 (secondary sources).[25][26] Second, grids need stabilising equipment, and renewable projects need hydro, geothermal and waste-to-energy generators.[8] Third, power equipment is a small part of a data-centre budget, which management put at under 5 percent of project cost, so buyers are not very sensitive to price.[8] The same management says Indian domestic demand is subdued, growing about 10 to 12 percent a year, and that a hyperscale data-centre build in India is unlikely without gas and water supply.[8]

Several things changed over the past twelve months. A third factory was declared operational on 18 December 2025.[10] Secondary sources report that the company signed a ten-year build-to-print agreement with Siemens Energy dated 13 August 2026 to make large two-pole generators to Siemens Energy's own designs; no minimum volume has been disclosed.[20][21] Management says a new plant for machines above 100 MW would be ready around mid-2028, with meaningful revenue around 2030.[19] It takes no fresh railway orders and will review that segment at the end of this year.[8] As reported by secondary sources, on 12 October 2026 it raised ₹600 crore from institutions at ₹775 a share, issuing 77,41,935 new shares, after splitting each ₹2 share into two ₹1 shares in August and after a ₹75 crore promoter issue at ₹600 a share; the exchange notices behind these items were not fetched.[16][17][18] The next chapter asks how strong this business really is.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • MW and MVA are units of electrical size; one MVA is roughly one MW for a well-run generator. A 100 MW generator can power a town of several hundred thousand people.
  • Order book is the value of signed orders that have not yet been delivered, in rupees. It tells you how much work is already secured.
  • Prime mover is the engine, steam turbine, gas turbine or water turbine that spins the generator.
  • OEM stands for original equipment manufacturer: the firm that builds the turbine or engine and sells the complete set.
  • Behind-the-meter power is electricity made on the customer's own site rather than bought from the grid.
  • Build-to-print means the customer supplies the design and the manufacturer builds exactly to it.
  • QIP stands for qualified institutions placement: a share sale to large institutions at a price set by a regulator's formula. Chapter 2 turns from what the company does to how strong it is.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence for this business is growth that arrived as signed orders, not only as promises. Consolidated sales rose from ₹797 crore in the year to March 2022 to ₹1,856 crore in the year to March 2026, and profit after tax rose from ₹70 crore to ₹239 crore.[4] Manufacturing order inflow was ₹2,238 crore in FY26 against ₹1,478 crore a year earlier, and the pending order book on 1 April 2026 was ₹1,973 crore against ₹1,368 crore a year before.[9][13] In the June 2026 quarter new orders of ₹734 crore were 1.15 times sales of ₹640 crore. That ratio is called book-to-bill: new orders divided by sales, where a figure above 1 means the order book is growing.[7]

Where the business is strong

Returns on money invested are high and rising. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses, and it rose from 16 percent in March 2022 to 34 percent in March 2026.[4] That means each extra rupee put into factories and stock has been earning well above the 15 percent floor this framework sets.

The balance sheet carries almost no debt. Debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' money; here borrowings were ₹18 crore against reserves of ₹1,041 crore in March 2026, a ratio of about 0.02, and the company states that its standalone borrowing at year-end was nil.[4][13] CRISIL, a credit rating agency, upgraded the company's bank-loan rating to AA-/Stable on 22 June 2026.[22] Because growth is being funded without borrowing, a sudden rise in interest rates or a lender's change of mind is not a direct threat to the case.

Access to the right customers took years to build and is hard to copy quickly. The company's own history lists a technology licence from Toyo Denki of Japan in 1999 to 2001, a long-term gas-engine generator supply agreement with INNIO signed in 2009, a licence from Siemens in 2012 for two-pole generators up to 250 MVA, and a long-term traction-motor agreement with Alstom in 2017.[7] Engine and turbine makers qualify each supplier through testing and long track records, so a supplier already approved has a head start when the makers add capacity. We infer this advantage from the history and the order flow; the company does not publish a market-share figure.[8]

Where the business is weak

Cash collection lags profit. In the June 2026 quarter trade receivables were ₹785 crore against ₹240 crore of cash, and an analyst asked why the company raised funds while holding that much unpaid customer money.[7][8] Management replied that payment terms are fixed with customers and cannot be changed much without losing business. Debtor days, the average time customers take to pay, rose from 112 in March 2024 to 146 in March 2026.[4][8] The consequence is that profit turns into cash slowly, which chapter 3 measures.

The company depends on a small group of large customers and on one theme. Exports were 93 percent of June-quarter orders, management says it has no major client additions coming, and the gas turbine and engine demand is tied to the pace of US data-centre building.[7][8] The annual report also lists product concentration, especially in steam turbine generators, as a stated risk.[13] In build-to-print work the customer owns the design, which usually leaves the buyer with more bargaining power over price; that is our inference, not a disclosed term.

Some past choices were costly. In the quarter to March 2026 a delayed shipment to a customer of the Turkish unit led to a large penalty for late delivery, which cut consolidated margin; management called it a one-off.[9] The Turkish unit now has only 3 to 3.5 million euros of orders to execute this year and is kept as a service back-up.[8] Railway traction motors, which were promised to ship to four markets as late as February 2026, are now being run down, with no fresh orders taken.[8][10] The promoters' share of the company fell from 34.3 percent in December 2024 to 26.9 percent by June 2025; the fetched filings do not state the reason, so we do not offer one.[4]

How we weigh them

We weigh the order flow and the returns more heavily than the cash gap. The order flow is signed work, and the cash gap is partly a timing matter that customer advances help to cover.[8] We weigh the cash gap more heavily than management does. Three years of cash flow is a cleaner test than one good quarter, and that test is not yet passed.

The scorecard in chapter 2B and the red-line tests in chapter 3 turn this weighing into numbers. The price work in chapters 4 and 5 then shows why a strong business can still be a hold and not a buy. Strength in the business and value in the price are two separate questions, and the second is where this report finds the doubt.

The multiple of 48.5 may not apply

The three-year median of 48.5 comes from a period of 30 to 40 percent growth. If growth slows to the 26 percent the analysts imply for the FY28 profit step, the market may pay less. If the data-centre theme stays hot, it may pay more, as the 91.5 reading of September 2026 shows.[5][23]

The signal to watch is the trailing multiple itself. A fall toward the 32 low with orders still rising would mean the market is repricing the theme and not the company. We use the median for the reference target because it is the only multiple with a published source and a three-year record, not because it is the likeliest.

Data-centre demand may turn

Management says demand is firm because its OEM customers hold non-refundable advances, and that power equipment is under 5 percent of a data-centre budget.[8] The other side is real. An analyst on the call noted that much of the multi-year backlog at global turbine makers is slot reservations and not firm orders. Evidence of a turn would be orders falling below sales for a quarter, or an OEM cutting its slots. The first signal is the order inflow in the September-quarter results. The strong order book gives about 3.4 quarters of cover, so a turn would show first in new orders and only later in sales.

Cash conversion and the QIP proceeds

A company that raises ₹600 crore while holding ₹785 crore of receivables and ₹240 crore of cash is telling us that growth costs more than internal cash provides.[8] The interview says the funds are mainly for the new large-generator programme, whose cost has not been filed. Red-team bear case
