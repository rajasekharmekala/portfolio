Before you read

TD Power Systems, near Bengaluru, builds big electric generators and motors for power plants, and 93% of its latest quarterly orders came from exports.[2] Sales rose from ₹797 Cr in FY22 to ₹1,856 Cr in FY26 and the return on capital is 34%, which is excellent.[1] We still reject it for new money because only 48 paise of each rupee of three-year profit became operating cash, the stock trades at 90.6 times earnings, and a ₹600 Cr share sale is under way.[1][14] We would be wrong if unpaid customer bills turn into cash soon and the price first falls back to its rising 50-day average. For the shares we already hold, the rule-based posture is to exit in an orderly way and swap the money into a shortlist name that passes the gates; chapter 7 gives the levels.

What the company does

TD Power Systems Limited was incorporated in Bangalore on 16 April 1999 as a private company and became a listed public company later.[21] It began with a technology tie-up with Toyo Denki of Japan and chose to build custom, made-to-order alternating-current generators instead of standard units.[22] The two founders still lead it: Mohib Khericha is chairman and Nikhil Kumar is managing director.[22] Mr Kumar is an engineer from NIT Surathkal who earlier worked as a general manager at Kirloskar Electric.[23]

A generator turns spinning motion into electricity. A steam turbine, a gas turbine, a water turbine or a gas engine supplies the motion, and a generator bolted to it makes the power. TD Power sells that generator, up to 200 MW in size, plus industrial motors, railway traction motors, spares and repair work.[18] Its website says it has more than 42 GW of installed capacity and over 6,300 installations.[18] The Q1 FY27 deck counts 8,375 units in 113 countries, with Bahamas added in the quarter.[2]

The customers are mostly the makers of the turbines and engines, not the final power-plant owners. These makers order a generator for each turbine they sell, and some pay non-refundable advances.[3] That makes TD Power a supplier to global equipment brands. When data centres, gas power plants and hydro refurbishment grow, the turbine makers add capacity and order more generators.[6] The MD said on the May 2026 call that gas turbine and gas engine demand "continues to roll on without pause" and that the order flow already extends into FY28.[6]

The five product lines

Steam turbine generators serve sugar mills, paper mills, biomass and waste-heat plants, and the MD described that market as steady, growing 10% to 12% a year.[6] Gas turbine and gas engine generators are the fast-growing line and include a project for SpaceX named on the call.[6] Hydro generators are lumpy but 2026 was set to be one of the best years, with refurbishment work in India and abroad.[6] Motors had been de-prioritised to free capacity for generators and are now getting separate manufacturing lines.[6] Railway traction motors have an order book of ₹211 Cr and no fresh orders, so that capacity will move to generators and motors.[3] Services and spares are 5% to 6% of sales.[3]

Management has a clear plan for size. Debottlenecking costing about ₹50 Cr in FY27 and FY28 should lift capacity to roughly ₹3,200 Cr of annual sales in FY28, and a larger plant is planned for FY29 and FY30 to reach beyond ₹4,000 Cr.[3] Generators above 100 MW have an 18 to 20 month lead time, so they will add no revenue before FY28.[3] The people running this are the MD Nikhil Kumar, CEO Deepak Kumar Sinha, who previously headed L&T-MHI Power Turbine Generators, and CFO M N Varalakshmi.[2]

Glossary of terms used in this report

EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges.

EPS means earnings per share, which is profit divided by the number of shares.

ROCE means return on capital employed, the operating profit earned each year per rupee of capital tied up in the business.

RSI means relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast the price has moved lately; above 70 is hot and below 30 is weak.

ATR means average true range, the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions, used here as a ruler for distances and stops.

A 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 sessions, a short-term trend line.

A 50-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 50 sessions; the middle trend line most funds watch.

A 200-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 200 sessions; the long-term trend line.

Stage 2 is the markup phase in Stan Weinstein's four-stage chart model: price above rising 50-day and 200-day averages.

Stage 4 is the markdown phase: price below falling averages.

A base is a flat resting period after a rise, where the price moves sideways while buyers and sellers reset.

A pivot is the price at the top of a base; a close above it with heavy volume is the usual buy signal.

A breakout is a move through the pivot or another resistance level.

A retest is a pullback to a level the price just broke, to see whether it now holds as support.

A stop loss is a pre-set price at which you sell to cap a loss.

A limit order is an order to buy or sell only at a stated price or better.

An entry zone is the price range in which we would be willing to buy.

Reward-to-risk is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop loss; this report also calls it asymmetry, and the portfolio floor is 3.5 for tactical holdings and 3.0 for core holdings.

Gross margin is sales minus the direct cost of materials and production, as a share of sales.

Operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales, before interest and tax.

Net margin is final profit after tax as a share of sales.

Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' funds.

Net debt is borrowings minus cash; a negative number means the firm holds more cash than debt.

Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by the earnings per share; it says how many years of current profit the price pays for.

Free cash flow is cash from operations minus the money spent on plant and equipment.

Cash flow from operations is the cash the business generated from its daily trading, after tax and after changes in receivables and stock.

Guidance is the forecast that management gives to investors.

A measured move is a price target found by adding the height of a base to its breakout level.

Book-to-bill is new orders received divided by sales in the same period; above 1.0 means the order book is growing.

Dilution is the fall in each existing shareholder's ownership share when a firm issues new shares.

Capital expenditure is money spent on plant, machines and buildings that last more than a year.

Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the firm owes its own suppliers.

A consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who cover a stock; this report found none for TD Power in the sources it could read, so it builds its own.

Why the business is strong or weak

The strengths are easy to see. Sales grew 45% in FY26 to ₹1,856 Cr and 72% in the June 2026 quarter to ₹640 Cr.[1][3] Profit after tax rose 37% in FY26 to ₹239 Cr, and in Q1 FY27 it rose 72% to ₹86 Cr.[1][3] The operating margin has sat between 17% and 19% for three years, and the Q1 FY27 figure was 19.0%.[1][3] Return on capital employed climbed each year, from 16% in FY22 to 34% in FY26.[1] Borrowings are only ₹18 Cr against ₹1,072 Cr of shareholders' funds, so the debt-to-equity ratio is about 0.02.[1]

The credit rating agency agrees. CRISIL lifted the long-term rating to AA-/Stable from A+/Positive on 22 June 2026 and the short-term rating to A1+ from A1, covering ₹488 Cr of bank facilities.[15][16][17] A better rating usually means cheaper working-capital loans and more trust from foreign customers placing large orders.

The moat: custom engineering and long relationships

A custom generator must fit a particular turbine, shaft speed, cooling method and grid code. Customers qualify a supplier over years and rarely switch a working design in the middle of a project. The Substack research note we cite describes TD Power as sitting between the global giants such as GE Vernova and Siemens and small local makers such as Kirloskar Electric, and as a partner to turbine makers rather than a rival.[22] That is a view, not an audited fact, so treat the moat as plausible and not proven. The best hard evidence is the order book of ₹2,207 Cr and the fact that 93% of Q1 orders came from exports.[2]

This is a debt-free company whose working capital is large. It builds machines to order, ships them abroad, and waits for the last payment tranche. The cost is real: when sales are growing at 45% to 72%, every extra rupee of sales ties up about 30 paise of working capital (109 days of 365) before cash returns. Fast growth therefore drains cash even when profits are high, which is why growth and free cash flow of just ₹19 Cr in FY26 coexist.[1] The fix is faster collection or customer advances; management says OEM customers pay non-refundable advances, but we could not confirm what share of orders carry them.[3]

What could happen next

The company has told investors what it expects. For FY27 it raised its revenue outlook to ₹2,600 Cr and said it expects about ₹700 Cr of new orders each quarter, which is roughly ₹2,800 Cr for the year.[3][4] It guides an operating margin of 18% to 19%, and capacity near ₹3,200 Cr in FY28.[3] A third-party summary of the May 2026 call assigns 60% to a base case of ₹2,400 Cr FY27 revenue, 20% to a bear case and 20% to a bull case above ₹2,800 Cr; that was before the outlook was raised, so we use it only as a sense of the spread.[7] We build three estimates from the guidance: earnings per share, a twelve-month price, and dilution. Each is laid out with its working so that you can change an input and redo the sum.

Estimate 1: earnings per share for FY27 and FY28

The number

We expect FY27 earnings per share of about ₹10.9 before the new shares and ₹10.7 after them, and FY28 earnings per share of about ₹12.9 on roughly 32.3 crore shares.

How it was calculated

FY27 revenue of ₹2,600 Cr, the company's own outlook, times a net margin of 13.2% gives profit of ₹343 Cr. Divide by 31.4 crore shares for ₹10.9. For FY28 we take revenue of ₹3,200 Cr, which is management's capacity figure, times 13.0% for ₹416 Cr, divided by 32.3 crore shares (31.4 crore plus about 3% from the two share issues) for ₹12.9.

Why these estimates might be wrong

The weakest link in the earnings estimate is the revenue outlook itself. Management raised it after one strong quarter, and a single large contract slipping a quarter would move FY27 revenue by hundreds of crores. The Turkish contract showed the risk: shipping delays on one lot led to a high late-delivery penalty and cut the consolidated margin.[6] The first piece of evidence that would show the revenue estimate wrong is the September 2026 quarter; the board meeting date for those results is N/A in every source we read, and the trading window stays shut until 48 hours after it.[19]

The weakest link in the price estimate is the multiple. We chose 55 times by judgment. If investors keep rewarding the data-centre story, the stock can stay near 70 times, and our central target would be ₹903. We cannot rule this out, and we do not have a data-driven median multiple, so we show the range and avoid pretending to precision.

Red-team: the strongest case against our own conclusion

A red-team pass attacks the verdict. If we are too negative, the bear case runs like this. Cash conversion is poor because the company is growing 45% to 72%, and fast growers always carry large receivables; the ratio will mend as growth normalises. The company is debt-free, rated AA-, and holds ₹240 Cr in cash with another ₹600 Cr coming.[3][15] Return on capital is 34%. Strength versus the Nifty 50 is the highest in its group. A rule that rejects this stock for a ratio of 0.48 may reject the best compounder on the exchange.

We take that seriously and keep the verdict for three reasons. The gate is a rule set before the research, so that we do not talk ourselves into exceptions. The ratio was below 0.70 in three of the last five years (FY22, FY25 and FY26), not just one. And the valuation leaves no room for error: even if cash conversion mends, the central price estimate is below the current price. The single hinge claim is that receivables of ₹785 Cr convert to cash on normal terms; if that happens by the March 2027 balance sheet, the rule can pass again within two years.

Under the portfolio rules a holding that fails Layer 1 has a fundamental thesis impairment, which maps to REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER: exit in an orderly way and swap the money into the best shortlist challenger that passes Layer 1 and the 3.5 reward-to-risk floor. The stock is still in an uptrend, so the exit can be staged: sell about half of the 245 shares at the next sessions into strength, and sell the rest on a close below the 50-day line at ₹729.48. If the Layer 1 gate still fails at the next session, we start the exit by selling about half of the 245 shares (about 122) into the current strength and keep the other half only until the 50-day support line or the stop is hit.

If the price closes below ₹729.48, the 50-day average, we sell all remaining shares, because the trend support and the cash gate would both be failing.

If the price closes below ₹660, the stop, any shares still held are sold the next session; a gap below the stop is sold at the open. This is the backstop, not the plan.

If the Q2 FY27 results or the annual report show operating cash of at least ₹361 Cr for FY27 (so the three-year ratio reaches 0.70 once FY27 replaces FY24), we re-run the full underwriting, and until then no new money goes in at any price.

If the price falls to ₹729 to ₹750 on quiet volume while the QIP is priced near ₹737 to ₹775, we would look again at the chart posture only, and we would still not buy back or add while the gate fails; this zone exists so that a re-qualified name has a defined entry.

If the price closes above ₹856.10, the 52-week high, on volume of at least 1.0 times average, the stock enters a new high with price discovery; this would be a better price to exit into, not a reason to add, and any shares still held would have the stop raised to about ₹709 (₹729.48 minus half an ATR).

What changes the verdict

Only cash can reverse this verdict. The FY27 operating cash target of ₹361 Cr, about 105% of expected profit, is a tall order for a business growing this fast. The more realistic path is FY28: if operating cash over FY27 and FY28 totals about ₹570 Cr against expected profit of ₹759 Cr, the three-year ratio would reach 0.70. The next filings that could show progress are the September 2026 quarter, the March 2027 annual numbers, and the receivables figure in each investor deck.[3]
