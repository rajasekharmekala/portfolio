You and I should begin where the company itself begins: with a capability transfer, not a consumer brand. TD Power Systems was incorporated in 1999, and the company’s FY2026 timeline says its Toyo Denki technology acquisition established the engineering base for its generator business. That origin matters because a generator is engineered around a prime mover, voltage, frequency, operating environment and the customer’s plant layout; the product is a project-specific machine, not a catalog item picked from a shelf [1].

From that base, TDPS built a specialized exporter. The company’s own milestones point to global customer access through sales entities: a United States subsidiary formed in 2013, the German entity acquired in 2016, and Turkish generator manufacturing beginning in 2017. The annual report also records motors entering the portfolio in 2021, its global footprint expanding to 105 countries in 2024, and a third Indian manufacturing site at Tumkur commencing operations in FY2026 [1]. This is a sequence of adjacency bets: bring generators closer to customers, add motor products that use overlapping electrical-engineering skills, and add physical throughput when the order base supports it.

The financial record shows why this is not a straight-line compounder story. The price-blind statement history in the supplied Screener fundamentals shows revenue at ₹597 crore in FY2015, ₹380 crore in FY2017 and ₹435 crore in FY2018; profit after tax was negative in FY2016–FY2018. Revenue then recovered to ₹594 crore in FY2021, ₹872 crore in FY2023, ₹1,001 crore in FY2024, ₹1,279 crore in FY2025 and ₹1,856 crore in FY2026. The company has moved from a low-utilization, low-margin trough into a high-growth phase, but the record spans a full industrial cycle and carries two distinct regimes: losses before FY2019, and rising profitability after FY2020 [1].

The weak years are analytically useful. FY2017 and FY2018 combined revenue contraction with thin operating margins of roughly 2.8% and 2.4%; FY2019 still produced only 7% operating margin. By FY2021 the margin had recovered to 12%, FY2023 to 15%, and FY2025–FY2026 to 18%. That step-up is evidence of scale, mix and process leverage, but it is not proof that the margin floor is permanently 18%. The end markets—steel, sugar, cement, hydrocarbons, utilities, data centres and rail—buy capital equipment when their own investment programs allow it [1][9].

The business geography has also changed. CRISIL reports overseas markets at about 70% of FY2026 sales compared with 40–50% around five years earlier, and TDPS now supplies generators across more than 100 countries. International reach can diversify the timing of domestic orders and deepen OEM relationships; it also brings currency, local qualification, logistics and counterparty exposure. In FY2026, consolidated exports and overseas branch/subsidiary sales together were material, while domestic and deemed-export sales remained meaningful [9][9].

The operating cycle has not simply disappeared as scale improved. The fundamentals file shows debtor days rising from 95 in FY2015 to 182 in FY2019, easing to 100 in FY2021, then moving back to 146 in FY2026. Cash conversion cycle rose from 21 days in FY2015 to 189 days in FY2025 before easing to 173 in FY2026. That is a large capital commitment for an engineered-to-order manufacturer. Even during record reported growth, accounts receivable and inventory grew faster than cash earnings in FY2026 [1][1].

Management’s FY2026 call described “the strongest financial and operational performance” as the result of higher order inflows and execution, and the annual report extends that optimism into a ₹2,400 crore-plus FY2027 revenue expectation. The two-sided reading is straightforward: order momentum and a larger global installed base support the next leg, while the long history says demand is cyclical and cash conversion can strain when deliveries ramp [1][4]. The best historical evidence is therefore not the guidance alone; it is whether each new capacity addition yields customer-qualified shipments, stable margins and operating cash.

You should also account for the balance-sheet actions around the FY2027 ramp. The September 2026 results disclose a ₹75 crore preferential allotment to promoters and a ₹600 crore QIP, both following the 2026 stock split. These transactions expand the equity base and fund an ambitious capacity and product expansion period. Their consequence belongs in per-share analysis later; in this blind pass, the key business question is whether the cash is translated into productive assets and durable customer programs [2].

TDPS reports one operating segment under Ind AS 108: manufacturing AC generators and electric motors. That accounting choice means the company does not publish segment revenue, segment profit or invested capital for steam, gas, hydro, motors, rail and aftermarket separately. We can describe product lines and name their disclosed customer situations, but we cannot assign a precise revenue or profit share to each. That missing split is itself a diligence limitation: external readers cannot independently identify which product economics are carrying the consolidated margin [1][1].

The largest disclosed product bucket is AC generators. These machines convert mechanical rotation from a turbine, engine, hydro turbine or other prime mover into electricity at customer-specific output and operating conditions. The annual report disaggregates FY2026 standalone revenue from operations at ₹1,716.7 crore, with AC generator sales of ₹1,449.2 crore, or about 84.4% of that total. Generator spares and components contributed ₹197.2 crore, aftermarket spares in domestic and overseas branches added ₹33.8 crore, and services were ₹26.1 crore. The accounting notes also show sales of scrap and inter-branch eliminations, so categories do not perfectly map to product lines and should not be confused with profit segments [1].

Steam turbine generators serve industrial plants that have process steam or captive power needs. In FY2026, TDPS disclosed ongoing orders for 30–60 MW machines, including 45–60 MW, 11 kV generators supplied through leading turbine OEMs for Indian steel installations; a 1 × 60 MW, 11 kV order was identified as its largest four-pole order in that segment. An end-to-end example is an iron-and-steel project that needs dependable electricity from a steam turbine: the turbine OEM integrates a TDPS generator with its turbine and plant package, TDPS engineers winding, cooling, insulation, voltage and mechanical interfaces against the project specification, manufactures and tests the machine at its plant, then ships to the project site for integration and commissioning. Price is contract-specific; no list price or realized margin is disclosed [1][3][8].

Gas-engine and gas-turbine generators are the strongest recent growth driver according to the FY2026 annual report, with demand linked to U.S. data centres, distributed power and industrial applications. Here the paying customer may be an engine or turbine OEM rather than the data-centre operator itself. A plausible disclosed-chain example is a U.S. data-centre power package: the engine maker qualifies TDPS as generator supplier; TDPS designs a synchronous generator to match the engine speed, voltage, frequency and redundancy architecture; the OEM packages and sells the system to an operator that needs dispatchable on-site power while grid connections are constrained. FY2026 materials cite significant volume and prototype orders from global OEMs, but with customer names generally withheld; therefore we cannot claim a named data-centre customer, exact unit count or unit price [1][1][3].

Hydro generators serve new small and mid-sized hydropower builds as well as refurbishment. The annual report cites 2 × 7.7 MW, 6.3 kV, 600 rpm horizontal hydro turbine generators for Vietnamese projects and package orders in India and Nepal. The customer chain runs from a hydro developer or EPC selecting a turbine package, to a turbine OEM matching the generator to water flow, head and rotor speed, to TDPS engineering and manufacturing the generator and completing factory acceptance testing, transport and site commissioning support. The economic price is negotiated per machine and package; disclosed reports provide order examples but not a product-line sales or profit split [1][3].

Motors are a developing adjacency: induction motors up to 20 MW, synchronous motors up to 50 MW and traction motors up to 1,250 kW are described in the FY2026 annual report. In an industrial example, a mine, water system or cement plant requires a high-power motor with specified torque, duty cycle and operating environment. TDPS engineers around the mechanical load and electrical supply, builds and tests the motor, and sells directly or through industrial/OEM relationships. In rail, TDPS supplies stators and rotors for traction motors under long-term arrangements; the locomotive or traction-equipment customer validates components against its platform and maintenance requirements. The company says U.S. and European qualification work is ongoing; volume production follows approvals, so we should not count it as established revenue until it appears in shipments [1][1][3].

Aftermarket parts, refurbishment and services monetize the installed base. TDPS reported 6,297 generators installed through FY2024. Its FY2026 annual report later reported 8,055 generators and motors supplied worldwide, including 105 motors, and 68 service centres [1][8]. A plant operator facing a worn bearing, damaged winding or performance issue can source compatible spares or refurbishment from TDPS; the company can identify the model and serial history, dispatch components, send a technician or coordinate a local service partner. This channel potentially has shorter selling cycles and better repeat economics than a new capital order, but the reported FY2026 service and aftermarket sales remain small relative to generator sales; the company does not disclose product gross margins [8][1].

Geographic information is more transparent than product mix. Consolidated FY2026 sales were ₹1,856.2 crore: domestic sales including deemed exports were ₹734.4 crore (39.6%); export sales were ₹968.6 crore (52.2%); and overseas branch/subsidiary sales, net of intercompany eliminations, were ₹153.3 crore (8.3%). CRISIL’s group view puts overseas exposure at roughly 70% when direct exports and overseas entities are combined, a useful reminder that the legal-entity tables define the channels differently [1][9]. Export breadth protects against a single-country capex pause, but it does not eliminate end-market or customer concentration. CRISIL says the top ten customers represent 60–70% of core generator-manufacturing sales [9].

TDPS sells engineered equipment to other businesses. Its main path is direct technical selling and approval by turbine and engine OEMs, plant builders, factories and railway suppliers. A generator is one part of a larger power package. Before approval, TDPS must prove that it fits the turbine or engine, meets the buyer’s quality rules, passes tests and can ship on time. The FY2026 call and CRISIL name Siemens, INNIO Jenbacher and Triveni Turbine among major customers [4][9].

The company serves overseas buyers through factories in India and Turkey and local customer teams. TDPS says it has three Indian sites: two in Bengaluru and one in Tumkur. It also has subsidiaries and branches in the United States, Europe, Japan and Turkey. The FY2026 annual report lists 68 service centres around the world. These teams help with product approval, service and delivery. Public filings do not show sales by office or service-centre use [1][8].

An order starts with the buyer’s needs. The buyer sets the power output, voltage, speed, cooling and site limits. TDPS engineers turn those needs into a design and parts list. The factory winds coils, machines parts, assembles the unit and tests it. After factory acceptance, the generator ships to the project. The customer connects it to a turbine, engine or other prime mover, then commissions the system. This custom work can build customer loyalty. It also makes scheduling and inventory harder than for standard parts [1].

Pricing is set for each contract. The quote reflects size, design, metal costs, testing, delivery and warranty. TDPS records equipment sales when control of the goods transfers; it records service sales over time. At FY2026 year-end, standalone receivables were ₹708.7 crore and customer advances were ₹172.1 crore. Advances fund part of a project, but they do not cover all receivables and inventory. Debtor days were 146 and inventory days were 148 [9][1].

Working capital is the main cash risk. Operating profit before working-capital changes was ₹318.10 crore. Receivables used ₹207.54 crore; inventory used ₹165.36 crore; other receivables used ₹5.55 crore. Higher trade payables added ₹154.24 crore and other payables or provisions added ₹82.27 crore. Cash from operations was ₹176.16 crore before tax. These numbers do not prove bad debts. They show the cash needed to deliver more projects while customers pay later. Watch whether collections catch up after shipment and whether new orders bring customer advances [1].

The Tumkur facility should be judged by output and returns. CRISIL says the roughly ₹140 crore plant opened in December 2025 and was funded with internal cash. TDPS plans ₹60–₹80 crore of annual capital spending for debottlenecking and automation. The company has not disclosed a public utilization rate or rated unit capacity. For now, track order inflow, backlog, shipments, margin and the extra cash tied up in working capital [9].

Distribution also shapes the economics of each order. The engineer and sales lead must close the specification before purchasing long-lead materials. A late design change can strand copper, electrical steel or cast parts. Export contracts add packing, freight, currency and warranty needs. Direct customer contact helps TDPS solve those issues, but it does not remove the customer’s ability to delay acceptance or payment. The company’s service staff can help commission a machine and maintain the installed base, yet disclosed spares and service revenue is still modest. For you and me, the lesson is to track working capital by project and customer terms as carefully as order value. The gross margin says what the machine earned. The cash-flow statement says when the customer paid. [1][9]

The limitation is that switching friction is not equivalent to exclusive control. Major engine and turbine OEMs can qualify more than one generator supplier, negotiate pricing, redesign platforms, or shift sourcing. CRISIL estimates that the top ten customers represent 60–70% of TDPS core generator-manufacturing revenue and explicitly flags concentration and end-market cyclicality. A customer relationship can be durable and still be a bargaining-power risk when the customer is larger [9].

Competition arrives from established multinational electrical-equipment makers, engine/turbine OEMs with in-house or preferred generator capabilities, and regional manufacturers competing on cost, lead time and local support. The company’s export reach and ability to handle customized 1–50 MW AC generators are a credible position, but public disclosures do not provide market share by rating class, win rates, bid prices, customer-level concentration or relative delivery lead times. We therefore cannot verify that scale alone gives pricing power. The 17–18% operating margin over FY2025–FY2026 supports current execution strength; raw-material pass-through and customer concentration remain critical tests [9][3].

A second moat-building mechanism is the installed base. The company reported 6,297 generators installed by FY2024; the FY2026 annual report counts 8,055 generators and motors supplied through FY2026, including 105 motors. Spares, service and refurbishment can become repeat business when machines remain in operation for years. Yet reported spares and aftermarket revenue were about ₹33.8 crore in FY2026 standalone versus ₹1,716.7 crore total sales. That is a modest visible stream, not yet evidence that annuity-like aftermarket economics dominate the P&L [1][8].

The growth opportunity is real but cycle-sensitive. Gas-engine and gas-turbine demand tied to U.S. data centres, distributed power and grid support is growing rapidly from a smaller base; hydro refurbishment, larger steam machines, railway components and motor qualifications broaden potential markets. If new customer programs repeat, TDPS can scale a specialized niche without building a utility-scale turbine business. If the current order surge is a one-time customer stocking cycle or if data-centre spending normalizes, new capacity and working capital could arrive ahead of sustainable demand [1][3].

Study the parallels below as mechanism tests rather than direct valuation comparables. The favorable example shows how technical qualification and installed-base service can extend a motor manufacturer into a global franchise. The cautionary example shows how the same broad power-equipment family can face earnings compression when project order flows, raw material costs, execution and payment terms turn at once. TDPS is smaller and has a different product/customer mix, so the relevant lesson is the mechanism, not the absolute outcome [33][34][35].
