1. What the company does

TD Power Systems Limited (TDPS) is a Bengaluru manufacturer of alternating-current (AC) generators, the part of a power plant that turns spinning motion into electricity. A turbine or an engine, called the prime mover, spins a rotor inside a fixed ring of copper coils called the stator, and the movement creates current. TDPS builds these machines from 1 to 250 MVA, for steam, gas, hydro, wind, gas-engine and diesel prime movers.[31] It also makes industrial motors and railway traction motors. By 30 June 2026 it had put 8,375 units into 113 countries.[3]

The buyer is rarely the owner of the power plant. It is usually the company that builds the turbine or engine, known as an original equipment manufacturer (OEM). Crisil, the rating agency, names Siemens, INNIO Jenbacher and Triveni Turbine among TDPS's top customers and says the top 10 customers bring 60-70% of core generator sales.[11] The OEM fits the TDPS generator to its own prime mover and ships the package to the project site. About 70% of FY26 sales were exports, and 93% of the INR 734 crore of orders won in the June 2026 quarter came from exports.[11][3]

The money flows through a simple chain. TDPS designs each machine to the OEM's specification, buys copper, steel and other inputs, builds the stator and rotor in its own plants, tests the machine and ships it.[33] Cost of goods was INR 418 crore on INR 640 crore of June-quarter revenue, so the gross profit margin was 34.7% and the operating profit margin before depreciation, called EBITDA margin (EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), was 19.0%.[3] Contracts carry price-variation clauses that pass raw-material swings to customers, and management says it also uses cost cuts, product mix and exchange-rate gains to hold the margin in its guided band.[2] Spares and after-sales service are only 5% to 6% of sales, because a well-made generator needs almost no service in its first ten years.[2]

The lead that brought this name into the portfolio is its link to artificial-intelligence (AI) data centres, and the record supports it in part. Management names AI data centres, grid stabilisation and renewable power as the demand drivers, and the June 2026 presentation reports large repeat orders from global engine makers for US data-centre applications and significant volume orders from a major US gas-turbine maker in the 14-18 MW range.[2][3] INNIO Jenbacher, a gas-engine maker, is confirmed as a top customer by Crisil.[11] What cannot be checked is the size of the link: management says it does not split orders by application, so the share of data-centre work in the INR 2,207 crore order book is not published.[2][3] India is not part of the story. Management calls domestic demand subdued, at 10% to 12% growth, and sees no meaningful hyperscale data-centre build in India without gas and water supply.[2]

Other lines are smaller. Hydro generators had their largest-ever order in the June quarter, four machines of 16 MW each for a project in Africa, and the company is winning hydro refurbishment work in India.[3][2] Steam-turbine generators serve steel, cement and sugar plants, including a 60 MW unit for a captive power plant.[3] Railway traction motors reach Indian Railways in an indirect way: TDPS supplies stators and rotors to a large corporation that completes the motors.[32] That railway order book is INR 211 crore, or 9.6% of the total, yet management is taking no fresh railway orders and will review the segment at the end of this financial year.[3][2] A Turkish factory in Istanbul is kept alive mainly as a service hub for Europe, with EUR 3 million to EUR 3.5 million of orders to execute this year.[2]

Plants, capacity and the large-generator plan

TDPS declared its third plant operational on 18 December 2025, as it had guided in May 2025, and Crisil puts the cost of the new facility at about INR 140 crore, paid from internal cash.[5][8][11] Management says that INR 50 crore of debottlenecking, meaning the removal of the slowest steps in a production line, is aimed at about INR 3,200 crore of sales capacity for FY28.[2] The transcript is inconsistent on this point: the opening remarks say capacity of about INR 22 billion, while the answers to analysts give INR 32 billion or INR 3,200 crore repeatedly, and the sell-side revenue forecast for FY28 of INR 3,206 crore matches the larger figure, so this report uses INR 3,200 crore.[2][25] The figure is a plus-or-minus indication, not a promise. Management also says another round of spending is needed to reach INR 4,000 crore or more of capacity for FY29 and FY30, with details due after about three months.[2]

The larger bet is generators above 100 MW. The company built the stator line for large machines in 2012 and 2013 but held back on the rotor line when demand fell, and it now says it must invest heavily in rotor manufacture and big machining capacity, up to about 200 MW.[4] A large generator takes 18 to 20 months to make, so none of that work is in the FY27 target.[2] The market leaders there are Siemens, Baker Hughes (which owns Brush), Mitsubishi, Fuji Electric and Toshiba, which the chief executive describes as very large players.[4][2] For the reader this means two things: capacity is the limit on sales through FY28, and the spending needed beyond FY28 is not yet priced or sized by management.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • AC generator (alternator): a machine that turns the spinning motion of a turbine or engine into alternating electric current.
  • Prime mover: the turbine or engine that spins the generator.
  • OEM (original equipment manufacturer): the company that builds the turbine or engine and buys the generator from TDPS.
  • Order book: signed orders not yet delivered. Order inflow: new orders signed in a period. Book-to-bill: order inflow divided by revenue; above 1 means the order book is growing.
  • QIP (qualified institutional placement): a sale of new shares to large institutions only, which increases the share count.
  • Build-to-print: making a product exactly to the customer's drawings and specifications.
  • Debottlenecking: removing the slowest step in a production line to raise output without a new plant.
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer invoices, less what suppliers are owed. Customer advance: cash a customer pays before delivery, which funds working capital.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

TDPS has a strong position in a narrow part of the power-equipment chain. Crisil calls it a leading maker of AC generators in the 1-50 MW range in India, and says it had supplied 8,055 generators up to 31 March 2026, up from 5,280 up to 31 March 2022.[11] That is nearly 2,800 machines in four years, in a field where each machine is built to order. The company makes its own stators and rotors, runs automated varnishing, winding and balancing lines, and has a machine shop that handles parts up to 35 tonnes, so it controls the steps that decide quality and delivery time.[33]

The advantage comes from qualification rather than from patents. An engine or turbine maker must test a generator with its own machine before it will buy hundreds of them, and once a design is qualified, switching suppliers costs months and risks the customer's delivery promise. Management describes the work as deep co-dependence with a small set of customers, and says new customers matter less than "cutting deeper" with the engine and turbine makers it already serves.[2] The Siemens Energy framework agreement is evidence of that trust: Siemens will place individual purchase orders for two-pole generators over ten years, although the filing sets no volume or value.[16][34]

The numbers back the claim of strength. Return on capital employed (ROCE), meaning pre-tax operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business, rose from 16% in FY22 to 34% in FY26.[1] The operating margin, which means revenue less all running costs as a share of revenue, went from 12% to 18% over the same years while sales rose from INR 797 crore to INR 1,856 crore.[1] The balance sheet carries INR 18 crore of borrowings against INR 1,072 crore of equity and reserves, and Crisil upgraded the rating to AA-/Stable in June 2026, citing interest cover of 47 times.[1][9][11]

Three weaknesses sit beside that strength. The first is dependence: 60-70% of core generator sales go to 10 customers, and Crisil flags the risk that any large OEM could change its sourcing.[11] The second is cyclicality. ROCE was 2%, 0%, 1% and -0% in FY15 to FY18, when end-user demand slowed and plants ran below capacity, so the present 34% is not a permanent level.[1][11] The third is cash. Profit is rising faster than cash, and Chapter 3 shows that only about half of each rupee of profit has reached the bank over three years.

What demand evidence says about the AI data-centre claim

TDPS's customers sit at the centre of a very large equipment cycle. GE Vernova reported that its gas equipment backlog and slot reservation agreements, which reserve future turbine production capacity, grew from 100 to 116 gigawatts in the June 2026 quarter and that it now expects at least 125 gigawatts by the end of 2026.[18] Siemens Energy reported record Gas Services orders of EUR 9,967 million in its June 2026 quarter, a book-to-bill ratio of 2.65, a Gas Services backlog of EUR 73 billion, and large orders related to data centres.[19] These two companies sit upstream of TDPS, which makes the generator that couples to the turbines and engines they sell.

TDPS management reports the same demand at the generator level. It says there is a "tremendous shortage of power generation equipment", that its engine and turbine customers have taken significant non-refundable advances from buyers, and that the power plant is less than 5% of a data-centre project's cost, so buyers will pay higher prices rather than cancel.[2] Those are management statements, not audited facts. The honest limits are three. TDPS does not disclose the application split. Management itself says that data-centre projects face execution delays, even though customers still take delivery. An analyst on the call asked whether slot reservations are as firm as orders, and the answer relied on the advances rather than on contract terms.[2]

Where the competitive position is thin

TDPS competes for generator work with large global groups for the biggest machines and with other specialist makers for mid-size units, but it publishes no market share and says no data service tracks one.[2] Its listed Indian peer in alternators, Kirloskar Electric, earns an operating margin near 5% and a ROCE of 14.6%, which shows how much of TDPS's 18% margin comes from its export OEM mix and not from the product type alone.[21][1]

Cost inputs add a second thin spot. Copper trading near $14,000 a tonne raised the cost of the main raw material, and management said customer price increases were approaching double-digit levels in May 2026.[4] Gross margin, which is revenue less the cost of goods as a share of revenue, fell from 35.6% in FY25 to 33.6% in FY26 and recovered to 34.7% in the June 2026 quarter.[3] The pass-through works, but with a lag, and a sharp copper spike is the first thing that would pull the 19% quarterly margin back to 17%.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

The management walk-the-talk audit compares what the company promised one to two years ago with what it delivered. On revenue the record is strong. In May 2025 the initial target for FY26 was INR 1,500 crore, and the company raised it to INR 1,800 crore in October 2025. Consolidated revenue from operations came in at INR 1,856 crore, which is 24% above the first target and 3% above the raised one.[8][6][9] On plants it was also on time: the third plant was promised for the second half of FY26 and was declared operational on 18 December 2025.[8][5]

The FY27 target has been raised four times in 12 months. It was INR 1,800 crore in August 2025, then above INR 2,000 crore, INR 2,200-plus crore, INR 2,400-plus crore and finally INR 2,600 crore in August 2026.[7][6][5][4][2] A manager who keeps raising a target is either cautious or reacting to new orders. Here it is both: new orders beat the pace the company had spoken of, rising from INR 550 crore a quarter in October 2025 to INR 666 crore in the March 2026 quarter and INR 734 crore in the June 2026 quarter.[6][4][3] The caution has a cost for readers: the target stops being a ceiling and becomes a floor, so the sell-side numbers will keep chasing it.

Two promises were not kept in full. Management said in August 2025 that railway business would show a significant uptick in FY27, and repeated in January and May 2026 that it would supply the US, Europe and Russia as well as India. In August 2026 it said it is taking no fresh railway orders and will review the segment at year-end.[7][5][4][2] The second gap is funding. The company has no debt and its rating agency expects no significant borrowing, yet it raised INR 675 crore of equity within two months of a call at which the chief executive declined to discuss funding until the board met two days later, and its cash flow from operations covers less than half of its profit over three years.[11][2][15][9]

The red-team bear case

The strongest honest case against holding any of this stock starts with price. At 83.5 times trailing earnings and 62 times FY27 estimates, the stock needs growth to continue for years, and a company whose cash flow covers 0.475 of profit has just raised INR 675 crore of equity, which says growth is consuming cash.[10][9][12][15] The buyers of the QIP at INR 775 are now 5% under water, and the stock fell 11% in four sessions after the issue.[12][1]

Second, 60% to 70% of core sales come from ten customers, several of them major turbine and engine makers, and the makers in this chain are expanding their own factories: GE Vernova, which is not named as a TDPS customer, says it is on track for 20 gigawatts of annual gas turbine output.[11][18] A customer that builds its own generators, or splits its orders among more suppliers, would hurt TDPS fast. The Siemens agreement is build-to-print, which means TDPS makes to Siemens drawings and does not own the design, and the filing sets no minimum volume or take-or-pay commitment.[16][34]

Third, the stop is too close to the price to give much protection. The stop of 690.32 is 1.1 average true ranges below the close, and the stock moved from 826 to 735 in four sessions in October.[1][26] A normal bad day can reach the stop, which is why this report reduces the number of shares and does not move the stop lower.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is to hold part of the position and cut the rest. The framework's verdict taxonomy has no label for hold-and-cut-to-size, so we use TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING as the nearest label, because it means reduce the position and keep part of it. No profit is being booked, since the position is 1.27% below cost. The cut is driven by two rules: the asymmetry floor of 3.5 to 1 is failed at 0.73 to 1, and the TIER_3 size ceiling of 3.5% of net worth is exceeded by a position that is 3.94%.

The instruction is to sell 28 of the 245 shares with a limit order, which fills only at your price or better, here 735.15 or higher, and keep 217 shares. The entry zone, the band of prices where buying is planned, is 699.00 to 707.00, and it is used only to buy back shares. A bounce toward the 50-day average of 750.72 is an acceptable place to fill if the order does not fill today. The 217 shares are worth about INR 159,500, or 3.49% of net worth. If the stock closes below the stop of 690.32, sell the remaining 217 shares at the next open. That would cost about INR 9,700 from today's value, or 0.21% of net worth, which is small enough to accept.

Selling everything is the wrong answer because the business is excellent, the probability-weighted value is 8.6% above the price, and the stop is close. Holding everything is the wrong answer because the base case offers 0.73 of reward for each 1 of risk, the chart is in Stage 3, three of the five operating KPIs fail to meet the four-of-five bar, and the ledger puts the stock in the NO_MOMENTUM state. The support-distance ladder places the stock in the FULL_SIZE band at 0.58 average true ranges from the swing low of 711.15, but that band applies to names that pass every gate, and this name does not, so no shares are added.

The let-winners-run rule does not apply, because this is not a winner at the portfolio level: it is a 1.27% loser. If the stock later rebuilds a Stage 2 chart, the rule would allow the weight to rise toward 22% without mechanical trimming. That is a long way from where the position sits today.

Here is the arithmetic in short steps. Net worth is INR 4,573,985. The TIER_3 ceiling is 3.5% of that, or INR 160,089. At 735.15 a share, that buys 217 shares. The position holds 245 shares today. The gap is 28 shares. Selling them brings in INR 20,584. The loss on those shares against cost is about INR 266. The stop is 690.32. It comes from the lowest of the swing low, the 20-day average and the 50-day average, which is 711.15, less half of one ATR, which is 20.84. If the stop is hit on the 217 shares, the loss from today's value is INR 9,728. That is 0.21% of net worth.

Here is why the tier is TIER_3. TIER_2 is for names that pass every gate, and this one does not. TIER_4 is for names that fail on the business as well as the price. This business passes on orders, margin and capacity. So the middle tier fits. The risk-unit formula gives a larger weight than the ceiling. The ceiling therefore sets the size.

Four things can change the decision. The first is the chart, because a close under 690.32 ends the holding. The second is the September 2026 results, which will show cash flow, orders and margin. The third is a return above the 20-day line on volume, which would reopen the question of adding. The fourth is a new target. If it rises enough, the 3.5 floor could be met at a higher entry.
