Terms used in this report

The framework uses a small set of market terms. ROCE is return on capital employed, which measures the profit a business earns on the money tied up in it. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of sessions, like a smoothed trend line; the 50-day and 200-day averages are the two the rules use. Stage is the Weinstein picture of a stock's season: Stage 1 is a flat period after a fall, Stage 2 is a rising markup, Stage 3 is a top or pullback where the rise has stalled, and Stage 4 is a decline. A base is a period of sideways trading that comes before a new move, and a pivot is the price level above which a base is considered to have broken out. A breakout is a close through that level on enough volume to be meaningful.

RSI, the relative strength index, is a 14-day reading of how fast a price has risen or fallen; values above 70 are read as stretched and below 30 as oversold. ATR, the average true range, is the typical daily swing of the stock over 14 sessions, and the rules use it as a ruler for distances. Asymmetry is the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss, measured against a verified target and a stop. An operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales, before interest and tax.

The momentum gate is a code check that runs on the same price file for every name. Test A asks whether the stock is in an upward trend now, and Test B asks whether a new uptrend is starting from a base. A name that fails both gets no allocation, however good the business.

Fatal-flaw red team

The red team asks what would make the case for the company wrong, not what makes it right. Three flaws are worth stating.

First flaw: the business is tied to market activity. Fees and distribution income rise and fall with investor flows and the level of markets. A fall in markets would reduce income and could pull the growth rate well below the three-year figures of 30 percent for sales and 24 percent for profit.

Second flaw: the valuation leaves little room. A price-to-earnings multiple, which is the share price divided by earnings per share, of 53.7 is high. If the earnings growth slows, the multiple is more exposed than a lower-rated company would be. The stock has already fallen 22.64 percent from its 52-week high, which shows that the market has already reduced the multiple once.

Third flaw: the trend has not yet confirmed. The 50-day average has risen while the price has stayed beneath it. That pattern can persist for weeks. Until the price closes above the average, the rising average is not yet a signal of strength.

What this means for you: none of these flaws decides the verdict alone. Together they support the rule. The company is worth watching, and the trend has to answer the flaws before a purchase makes sense.
