1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Cycles

Core Investor Question: How did a 1983 Hyderabad industrial entity transform after 2008 into a qualified Tier-1 supplier of rotating airfoils for global turbine and aerospace OEMs, and what capital cycles shaped its current balance sheet?

From 1983 Incorporation to Rakesh Chopdar's 2008 Pivot

Azad Engineering Limited was incorporated in 1983 [1][2]. Its modern industrial identity began in 2008. First-generation entrepreneur Rakesh Chopdar took the helm in Hyderabad and pivoted toward ultra-precision, life-critical rotating components [2]. At that time, most Indian precision shops chased low-complexity, high-volume automotive or general industrial job work. Switching costs were minimal, and domestic price competition capped margins. Chopdar spotted a structural gap in the global supply chain. Western and Japanese original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) in power generation, oil and gas, and aerospace faced aging machining capacity and rising labor costs. They also struggled with multi-year lead times for complex 3D airfoils and turbine blades [2][5].

Entering that supply chain required more than buying 5-axis CNC machines and bidding on tenders. Global turbine and aero-engine OEMs put prospective vendors through 3-to-5-year qualification cycles. These audits test metallurgical integrity, destructive and non-destructive testing (NDT), AS9100 quality systems, and NADCAP special-process accreditations [5]. Early units in IDA Jeedimetla (Hyderabad) served as Azad's proving ground. There, the team built its engineering bench and mastered exotic alloys like Inconel, Waspaloy, titanium, and specialized martensitic stainless steels [2][5]. Over the next fifteen years, Azad qualified more than 1,700 distinct part numbers. That intangible asset cannot be replicated quickly by a well-funded entrant [5].

The Global OEM Qualification Ladder (2010–2023)

Between 2010 and 2023, Azad climbed the qualification ladder across four global sectors. These are thermal/gas/nuclear energy turbines, oil and gas equipment, commercial/defense aerospace, and domestic defense propulsion [2][4][5]. Management avoided relying on a single anchor client. Instead, it built overlapping Long-Term Contract and Price Agreements (LTCPAs) with Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI), GE Vernova (and legacy GE Steam Power), and Siemens Energy. The roster also includes Baker Hughes, Rolls-Royce, Honeywell, Eaton, and Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) [4][10][11][12]. By FY21, consolidated revenue reached ₹123 Cr with a 23% operating profit margin (OPM) [1]. Qualified part families then shifted from first-article inspection into serial production. Revenue jumped to ₹194 Cr in FY22 (32% OPM) and ₹252 Cr in FY23 (29% OPM) [1].

That pre-IPO growth phase exposed the financing tension of precision aerospace and turbine manufacturing. Exotic superalloy bar stock and forgings must be procured months ahead of delivery. Global OEM receivables also carry long export credit terms. As a result, Azad's borrowings climbed from ₹88 Cr in Mar 2021 to ₹197 Cr in Mar 2022 and ₹301 Cr in Mar 2023 [1]. Interest expense surged from ₹5 Cr in FY21 to ₹52 Cr in FY23. That interest burden compressed FY23 net profit to ₹8 Cr despite ₹72 Cr in operating profit [1]. Early institutional backers—including Piramal Structured Credit Opportunities Fund and DMI Finance—funded this private phase. Pre-IPO angel investors such as Sachin Tendulkar (who invested roughly ₹5 Cr in early 2023), VVS Laxman, Saina Nehwal, and PV Sindhu also joined ahead of listing [9].

The Two Capital Raises: Dec 2023 IPO and Mar 2025 QIP

In December 2023, Azad Engineering completed a ₹740 Cr Initial Public Offering (IPO). It comprised a ₹240 Cr fresh issue and a ₹500 Cr Offer for Sale (OFS) by Piramal, DMI Finance, and promoter Rakesh Chopdar [9]. Of the fresh proceeds, ₹138.19 Cr went to debt repayment and ₹60.40 Cr went to capital expenditure. Another ₹24.17 Cr funded general corporate purposes [9]. Deleveraging was immediate. Total borrowings dropped from ₹301 Cr in Mar 2023 to ₹39 Cr in Mar 2024. Interest expense eased to ₹18 Cr by FY25. Consolidated revenue accelerated to ₹341 Cr in FY24 (+35% YoY) and ₹457 Cr in FY25 (+34% YoY) [1].

Soon after the IPO deleveraged the balance sheet, a wave of mega-contracts arrived. Cumulative multi-year OEM agreements topped ₹3,200 Cr, and the rolling order book reached ~₹6,500 Cr [3][10]. Fulfilling that backlog required a step-change in dedicated plant capacity. Between February 25 and March 3, 2025, Azad executed a ₹700 Cr Qualified Institutional Placement (QIP). It issued 5,468,750 shares at ₹1,280 per share [8]. The QIP allocated ₹525.00 Cr to capital expenditure, ₹156.21 Cr to general corporate purposes, and ₹18.79 Cr to issue expenses [8]. From Dec 2023 to Sep 2026, promoter shareholding stepped down from 65.90% to 55.84% via OFS and QIP dilution. Over the same span, FII ownership rose from 4.68% to 12.70%, and DII ownership grew from 5.71% to 10.40% [1].

That structural lag explains why operating profit surged 8x between FY21 (₹28 Cr) and FY26 (₹225 Cr) even as free cash flow remained deeply negative throughout the buildout.

Segment 1: Energy & Oil & Gas (~79–80% of Revenue) — Concrete End-to-End Example

The Energy & Oil & Gas unit supplies rotating and stationary airfoils, buckets, nozzle vanes, and combustion parts. These serve heavy-duty gas turbines, steam turbines, nuclear power islands, and oilfield drilling systems [4][11][12]. Consider Azad's single-source 8-year Long-Term Contract and Price Agreement (LTCPA) with Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI), Japan. It covers hot-section nozzle vane segments and advanced gas/thermal turbine airfoils. That deal sits within a cumulative MHI relationship topping $156 million / ~₹1,387 Cr [12].

The Customer Problem: MHI's high-efficiency gas turbines run at firing temperatures above 1,600°C. That heat maximizes combined-cycle plant output for global grid and data-center power demand. At those temperatures, hot-section nozzle vanes guide supersonic, corrosive combustion gases onto rotating blades. Even a 10-micron profile error or micro-crack cuts aerodynamic efficiency or causes blade-tip rub. MHI needed a dedicated non-Japanese Tier-1 partner that could hold tight tolerances without single-region bottlenecks [12].

From Raw Alloy to Qualified Part: Azad buys certified nickel-based superalloy forgings or bar stock, such as Inconel 718 / Waspaloy. Those alloys resist heat and deformation, so they work-harden and wear down standard cutting tools. Inside its dedicated 7,200 sq. m. MHI facility at Tuniki Bollaram (commissioned March 2025) [16], blanks enter simultaneous 5-axis CNC milling. Single-setup milling carves 3D twisted airfoil contours without fixture stack-up errors, holding tolerances to ±0.005 mm (5 microns). Parts then pass through NADCAP-compliant stress-relief heat treatment and precision surface finishing. Next come coordinate measuring machine (CMM) laser scans and ultrasonic/fluorescent penetrant non-destructive testing (NDT). Multi-year rate contracts lock in pricing with raw-material pass-through clauses, yielding mid-30s segment EBITDA margins [12][15]. A similar model serves Siemens Energy (€90.1 million / ~₹811 Cr, 6-year deal for combustion commodities, cold blades, and vanes) [11]. It also covers GE Vernova (three exclusive 7,600 sq. m. plants for steam/nuclear/industrial airfoils) [16][17] and Baker Hughes (7,600 sq. m. plant commissioned April 2026 for complex oilfield parts) [16].

Segment 2: Aerospace & Defence (~17–20% of Revenue) — Concrete End-to-End Example

Azad's Aerospace & Defence unit makes precision-forged and 5-axis machined rotating engine parts and flight-critical airframe hardware. These parts fly on Boeing 737/777, Airbus A320/A350, and Gulfstream jets for Rolls-Royce, Honeywell, and Eaton. Azad also serves domestic defence programs with HAL and DRDO [10][13]. For years, Azad acted strictly as a build-to-print component supplier. In 2025–2026, it made a structural leap with India's Gas Turbine Research Establishment (GTRE) under DRDO [13].

Concrete End-to-End Example — The Indigenous ATGG Expendable Turbojet Engine: India's cruise missiles, loitering munitions, and target drones once relied on imported small-thrust turbojet engines. That reliance created strategic and export-control risks. GTRE designed an indigenous Advanced Turbo Gas Generator (ATGG) expendable turbojet engine in the 350 kgf (3.4 kN) thrust class. It picked Azad Engineering as its private-sector manufacturing and integration partner [13]. Unlike a single turbine blade order, this program required Azad to machine high-speed compressor and turbine rotors from titanium (Ti-6Al-4V) and superalloys. Azad also balanced the spool assembly to sub-micron runout limits, integrated fuel-control and ignition subsystems, and delivered a flight-ready propulsion unit [13].

Azad built, assembled, and delivered the first indigenous ATGG turbojet engine to GTRE/DRDO [13]. Early defence engine deliveries add modest initial revenue next to the ₹603 Cr consolidated base. Yet the strategic shift is clear: Azad proved it can move from a parts vendor to a complete propulsion system integrator. Once weapon-testing and airworthiness checks conclude, serial builds for domestic tactical missiles and UAVs can diversify Azad's 93% export mix [13].

Segment Economics: Why 37% Operating Margins Exist

Why Rotating Parts Differ from Static Castings or Airframes

Investors comparing Indian precision firms should note the physical stress profile of Azad's parts. Static airframe brackets or hydraulic manifolds face predictable structural loads. By contrast, 3D rotating airfoils and turbine buckets spin at thousands of RPM inside hot, corrosive gas or steam flows [2][5]. Azad's value-add lies in multi-axis toolpath engineering, residual-stress control, and zero-defect metrology across 1,700+ qualified SKUs [5]. Two new 7,600 sq. m. exclusive GE Vernova plants opened in September 2026 [17], alongside the April 2026 Baker Hughes oilfield plant [16]. Together, these launches show OEMs are expanding wallet share across steam, gas, nuclear, and downhole Oil & Gas hardware. That multi-plant commitment reduces single-customer ramp risk over time. Each dedicated bay locks in multi-year tooling, metrology, and engineering routines.

3. Distribution, Supply Chain & Unit Economics

Direct B2B OEM Distribution & Long-Term Supply Agreements

Azad Engineering has zero distributor or retail layers. Distribution is 100% direct B2B under multi-year Long-Term Contract and Price Agreements (LTCPAs). Global OEMs across Europe, Japan, and North America drive ~93% of sales, while domestic defence and power PSUs add ~7% [3][4][5]. Rather than bidding on spot tenders each quarter, Azad works under 4-to-8-year rate contracts. These agreements set part numbers, quality rules, annual volume bands, and raw-material indexation clauses upfront [5][12]. Between March 2025 and September 2026, Azad opened 44,800 sq. m. of customer-exclusive bays at Tuniki Bollaram [16][17]. Once a part is qualified in a dedicated bay, purchase orders flow from the OEM's global ERP straight into Azad's schedule.

Raw Material Sourcing, Hyderabad Footprint & The DSO/Inventory Cycle

Specialized raw materials come mostly from OEM-approved overseas mills [6]. These include certified nickel-based superalloys (Inconel, Waspaloy), titanium bar stock, and precision forgings. That creates a long supply-chain pipeline. Stock moves through ocean transit, customs clearance, and incoming lab checks before 5-axis machining. Parts then undergo NADCAP heat treatment, NDT, and final OEM sign-off before export dispatch. On the collection side, global OEMs enforce long export credit terms. Standalone debtor days sit in the 170–189 day range, reaching 189 days in FY26 [1][6]. Inventory days also spiked from 1,085 days in FY25 to 2,067 days in FY26. Azad stocked work-in-progress (WIP) and raw material across six new plants undergoing delta qualification [1][7]. To pull debtor days toward ~90 days, management is using bill discounting facilities (via ICICI Bank and DP World). It is also negotiating 50% advance payments on select OEM orders, though that raises finance costs [6][18].
