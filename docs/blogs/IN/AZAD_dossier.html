1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Stock Cycles

Core Investor Question: How did a 1983 Hyderabad precision-machining workshop become a listed global OEM supplier, and what do its past earnings and price cycles tell us about the current 22% drawdown?

Azad Engineering Limited (NSE: AZAD) does not begin on a stock exchange; it begins in a Hyderabad machine shop in 1983[25]. For four decades the company ground mission-critical rotating components for global original equipment manufacturers before Dalal Street noticed. It makes airfoils, 3-D blades, vanes, blisks and impellers that spin inside jet engines and industrial gas turbines, surviving temperatures beyond 1,000°C under extreme centrifugal load[10]. Its customer list reads like an industrial roll-call: GE, GE Vernova, Rolls-Royce, Safran, Pratt & Whitney, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, Siemens Energy, Honeywell and Baker Hughes[3][6][13]. The equity has lived a far more dramatic life than the factory.

1983: The Hyderabad Founding and the Precision-Machining Foundation

Azad Engineering was incorporated in 1983 as a Hyderabad-based manufacturer of precision mission-critical components for global OEMs in energy, oil and gas, and aerospace and defence[25]. The model that emerged was never volume machining; it was qualification machining. To sell a single hot-section nozzle vane into a gas turbine, a supplier must clear years of metallurgical testing, fatigue-cycle certification and process audits before the first commercial order ships. Chairman and CEO Rakesh Chopdar built the firm around that discipline, and it is why the company reports under two segments while serving three end-markets: the annual report states it serves energy, aerospace and defence, and oil and gas, reported under Aerospace & Defence and Energy and Oil & Gas[24]. CFO Ronak Jajoo and Whole-Time Director Vishnu Malpani, reappointed through 2031, run that structure[3][5].

The December 2023 IPO at ₹524 and the Promoter Stake Reset

The company came to the public market in December 2023. The initial public offering priced at ₹524 per share — an offer of up to ₹740 Cr combining a fresh issue with an offer for sale — opened on 20 December 2023 and closed on 22 December 2023[14]. That listing marked the moment a forty-year-old private machine shop became a listed industrial asset. The float also reset the ownership structure. Promoter holding stood at 65.90% in December 2023 and fell to 55.84% by September 2026[18]. Over the same window, foreign institutional investors moved from 4.68% to 12.70% while domestic institutions rose from 5.71% to 10.40%, leaving the public at 21.06% across 1,49,134 shareholders[18]. Nomura Asset Management later trimmed its position, disposing of 1,321,713 shares between June 2025 and April 2026[19].

Six OEM-Dedicated Facilities and the ₹700 Cr QIP Build-Out

Azad's industrial strategy is visible in its bricks. The company operates six OEM-dedicated lean facilities across 44,800 square metres, each aligned to a specific customer: Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, GE Vernova, Siemens Energy and Baker Hughes[3]. Dedicated lines exist because a certified vane or blade cannot simply be moulded and shipped; it must be produced under a frozen process that the OEM has itself audited. To fund that expansion Azad raised ₹700 Cr through a qualified institutional placement in March 2025[9]. By 30 June 2026 it had utilised ₹589.42 Cr, leaving ₹107.88 Cr unutilised, and it extended the deployment timeline from 31 March 2026 to 31 March 2027[9]. A further ₹250 Cr was capitalised into plant and machinery within nine months, with ₹700 Cr slated for deployment across FY27–28[7].

The build-out translates into a stated capacity ceiling. Installation supports an annual revenue run-rate above ₹1,300 Cr by the end of FY28 — more than twice the FY26 base — with management reiterating over-25% long-term annual growth[3][20]. The order book that must feed those lines stands above ₹6,500 Cr, roughly 10.8 times FY26 revenue, split Energy ₹3,860 Cr (largest block) and Aerospace & Defence ₹1,930 Cr, with the balance in Oil & Gas, and delivery visibility of five to six years[6][4]. Capacity, not order intake, is the binding constraint management is spending against.

Primer: Why Qualification Machining Is Both a Moat and a Cash Trap

Winning an OEM qualification is extraordinarily hard and therefore defensible. A single hot-section nozzle vane must survive above 1,000°C under extreme centrifugal load, and single-source relationships such as the Mitsubishi Heavy Industries hot-section nozzle vane programme run for years[6][10].

The same certification that locks in the customer also locks in the cash. Long qualification cycles, deep safety inventory and extended payment terms mean a growing backlog absorbs working capital rather than releasing it. Azad's FY26 standalone operating cash flow was -₹123.26 Cr against a PAT of ₹132.16 Cr, with working capital absorbing ₹300.30 Cr[4]. The moat and the cash trap are the same object viewed from opposite sides.

2. What the Company Does: Segments, Revenue Mix & Concrete Examples

Core Investor Question: What exactly does Azad Engineering sell, how does revenue split across its segments, and which named components and customers generate that money?

Azad Engineering sells one thing extremely well: certified rotating components and airfoils for machines that cannot be allowed to fail. That single capability is reported through two segments, but it serves three end-markets. The FY2025-26 annual report is explicit that the company serves energy, aerospace and defence, and oil and gas, while reporting under Aerospace & Defence and Energy and Oil & Gas[24]. Energy & Oil/Gas: The 81.5% Engine, Told Through Three Components

Most investors assume an energy-transition company sells turbines. Azad's energy and oil/gas revenue is more specific: it makes the internals that let those turbines and compressors survive. A gas-turbine airfoil, whether destined for a GE Vernova frame or a Siemens Energy unit, is a casting that must hold its shape while combustion gases sweep past it at extreme temperature and pressure. The rolling order book illustrates the depth of these relationships. Energy orders stand at ₹3,860 Cr, the largest single block of the ₹6,500 Cr-plus backlog, with delivery visibility of five to six years[6][4]. Baker Hughes, a name synonymous with oil-and-gas compression, extended its agreement with Azad to 2030[3].

Three customers anchor this segment, each illustrating a different link in the chain. Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, covered in detail below, buys single-source hot-section nozzle vanes[6]. GE Vernova buys gas-turbine airfoils for power generation, a market tied to the global build-out of flexible thermal capacity. Baker Hughes buys components that sit inside oil-and-gas rotating equipment, tying Azad to the compressor and pumping infrastructure that moves hydrocarbons[3]. One segment, three end-demand drivers, and a backlog that outlives any single order cycle.

Concrete Example: A Mitsubishi Heavy Industries Hot-Section Nozzle Vane

Take one object and follow it end to end. A Mitsubishi Heavy Industries heavy-duty gas turbine needs a ring of stationary nozzle vanes sitting immediately downstream of the combustor. These hot-section parts face the most brutal conditions in the entire machine, because the flame exits the combustor directly onto them. MHI does not want two suppliers for that part, since qualifying a second source would repeat years of metallurgical validation. So it signed a long-term component purchase agreement with Azad. Phase two alone is worth ₹651 Cr, and the full agreement totals ₹1,387 Cr over five years — roughly ₹81 Cr of revenue a year, on a single-source hot-section nozzle vane programme[6].

Follow the cash, too. That ₹81 Cr a year arrives on a delivery schedule inside a five-to-six-year visibility window, not a spot order[6]. This is what an OEM-dedicated facility is for: Azad runs a lean line specifically serving MHI among its six customer-aligned facilities across 44,800 sqm[3]. The vane is a tiny part; the contract wrapped around it is the actual asset.

GE Vernova Airfoils and Baker Hughes Components in the Same Segment

Mitsubishi Heavy Industries is not the whole energy segment. Follow a GE Vernova gas-turbine airfoil instead. GE Vernova builds the heavy-duty turbines that utilities install to balance intermittent renewables, and every one of those machines needs rows of compressor and turbine airfoils. Azad machines and finishes these airfoils for GE Vernova inside a dedicated lean facility, one of the six OEM-specific lines the company runs across 44,800 sqm[3]. The airfoil is a shaped piece of high-temperature alloy that must hold precise aerodynamic geometry while the rotor spins at full speed, and it is the sort of part that a utility cannot source from an uncertified workshop.

Then follow a Baker Hughes component. Baker Hughes operates across oil and gas, where rotating compressors and pumps move hydrocarbons through pipelines and processing plants. Azad supplies precision parts into that equipment, and in 2026 the two companies extended their agreement to 2030[3]. This is the third distinct demand driver inside a single reporting segment. MHI pulls Azad toward power generation, GE Vernova toward the utility turbine fleet, and Baker Hughes toward upstream and midstream oil-and-gas infrastructure. Different primes, different end-markets, the same underlying capability: certified rotating components that a global OEM trusts with its most demanding duty.

Aerospace & Defence: 17.2% of Revenue, Most of the Story

The smaller segment carries the louder narrative. Aerospace & Defence contributed ₹101.26 Cr, or 17.2% of FY26 standalone revenue, and its first-half FY27 run-rate of ₹60.4 Cr was up 28.3%[4][3]. Its products are jet-engine airfoils, 3-D blades, vanes, blisks and impellers, sold to the same elite set of engine makers that dominate the global market: GE, Rolls-Royce, Safran and Pratt & Whitney[10]. In February 2025 Azad signed a long-term agreement with Rolls-Royce to supply complex machined parts for the life of a civil aircraft engine programme[13][26]. Defence adds a domestic layer: a two-year rate contract with BHEL worth about ₹52.68 Cr for gas-turbine work[3][20].

Two features distinguish this segment. First, the deals are long-dated by design, mirroring the multi-decade lives of the engine programmes they serve. Second, the defence work is politically and strategically visible, which raises its profile far above its 17% revenue share. The domestic turbojet is the clearest illustration of that gap between revenue size and strategic weight.

Concrete Example: India's First Indigenous 350-kgf Turbojet for DRDO

On 22 July 2026, Azad delivered India's first fully indigenous 350-kilogram-thrust class expendable turbojet engine to the DRDO's Gas Turbine Research Establishment (GTRE)[8][27][29]. Read the structure of that programme carefully, because it is a template. Azad has been GTRE's sole industrial partner since May 2024, and it moved from contract to hardware delivery in just 26 months[8]. GTRE designed the engine; Azad manufactured and assembled it[28]. A second-source round followed, awarding six engines split three-and-three between two partners on a 36-month schedule[8]. Weapon-platform testing was expected to take four to six weeks and was explicitly non-contractual[8].

The revenue here is modest, and it should be. What matters is the qualification. A company that has passed DRDO's scrutiny for a complete engine, not merely a component, has moved from making parts to integrating systems. That is a different rung on the industrial ladder, and it is why the segment draws attention disproportionate to its ₹60.4 Cr half-year run-rate[3].

Case Note: From Part-Maker to Engine Integrator

For decades Azad's identity was component supply: a blade here, a vane there, each certified and each individually replaceable. The DRDO turbojet changes that category. Delivering a complete 350-kgf expendable engine means Azad assembled, tested and handed over a functioning propulsion system, with 26 months from contract to delivery[8].

The strategic value is not the six-engine second-source round; it is the reference. When a defence establishment trusts a supplier with an entire engine, that supplier joins a much smaller club. India's push toward indigenous defence content makes such references scarce, and therefore valuable, even when the near-term rupees are small[27][29].

3. Distribution, Supply Chain & Unit Economics

Core Investor Question: Does winning single-source OEM contracts actually turn a reported profit into free cash for the shareholder?

Azad Engineering does not reach the market through distributors, catalogs or an aftermarket network. Revenue is won part by part, by qualifying a specific rotating component into a specific engine or turbine programme owned by a named OEM, then holding that qualification for as long as the programme runs. The buyer list reads like a roll-call of the global energy and aerospace primes: GE, GE Vernova, Rolls-Royce, Safran, Pratt & Whitney, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, Siemens Energy, Honeywell and Baker Hughes [3][6][13]. Qualification is the real product. Before the first part ships, it must clear the OEM's own metallurgical, dimensional and endurance protocols, and once cleared it is locked to that programme — the reason these contracts are described as single-source or sole-source rather than competitive. Replacing a qualified supplier of a hot-section component is not a purchasing decision an OEM makes casually; it reopens years of re-certification risk. Roughly 93% of FY26 standalone revenue was exported [4], so almost the entire book is billed in hard currency to buyers who judge a supplier on process capability before price. That is the distribution model in one line: direct to the OEM, rupee-cost base, dollar-revenue book.

Dedicated Capacity, Built Cell by Cell for One Customer

Delivery is organised around dedicated capacity rather than a shared shop floor. Azad runs six customer-dedicated lean manufacturing facilities spanning 44,800 sqm, purpose-built for Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, GE Vernova, Siemens Energy and Baker Hughes [3]. A dedicated cell means the tooling, fixtures, inspection routines and often the workforce are aligned to one customer's part family, which shortens qualification cycles and gives the OEM audit confidence. The installed base is sized for materially more volume than today: management frames installed capacity as supporting a run-rate above ₹1,300 Cr of annual revenue by end FY28, more than double the FY26 level [3]. Capex follows the same logic, with ₹250 Cr capitalised into plant and machinery in nine months [7] and the ₹700 Cr QIP earmarked across FY27–FY28 for infrastructure and equipment [9]. The distribution channel is therefore not a salesforce; it is a physical footprint that is hard for a rival to clone customer by customer.

Sole-Source Contracts and the Clock That Governs Delivery

Revenue visibility comes from contracts that behave like annuities, but the cash arrives on the OEM's clock, not Azad's. The Mitsubishi Heavy Industries long-term contract is single-source for hot-section nozzle vanes, with ₹651 Cr in phase two and ₹1,387 Cr in total across five years — roughly ₹81 Cr a year [6]. The Rolls-Royce agreement, signed February 2025, commits Azad to complex machined civil aircraft engine parts for the life of the programme [13][26]. The booking of a rolling order book above ₹6,500 Cr, roughly 10.8x FY26 revenue, is spread over five to six years of delivery visibility [6][4]. What unites these contracts is time: the gap between winning an order and shipping it is measured in years. The DRDO GTRE turbojet programme ran 26 months from contract to delivery of India's first fully indigenous 350-kgf expendable turbojet [8][29]. Long cycles protect the incumbent but tie up capital for the whole of the wait.

Supply-Chain Risk: Superalloys, Single Sources and the Inventory They Force

The inputs are as specialised as the outputs. Components that sit above 1,000°C under extreme centrifugal load need superalloys built on nickel, cobalt and titanium, and those upstream materials are themselves often single-sourced and price-volatile [10]. When a qualified part fails inspection it cannot simply be reworked and re-billed; it waits, and the working capital waits with it. The balance sheet carries the consequence directly: consolidated inventory days rose from 1,085 in FY25 to 2,067 in FY26, and the cash conversion cycle stretched from 804 to 1,720 days [15]. Management reports a tighter presentation basis, citing inventory days of 158 and receivable days of 164 and noting that 96% of inventory is under one year old [4]. The divergence between the two bases is itself the risk: the difference lies in slow-moving long-cycle raw material and work-in-progress accumulated to service years of OEM deliveries. For a business that must always be ready to feed a sole-source programme, inventory is not waste — it is the price of being the supplier of record.

Logistics, Currency and the Export Book

Selling direct to global OEMs makes Azad an export business in everything but name. Exports were 93% of FY26 standalone revenue [4], and in H1 FY27 domestic revenue was only ₹43 Cr, about 12% of the total [3]. That skew shapes the operational model in three ways. First, earnings are denominated in dollars and euros while the cost base — labour, power, machining and the growing plant at Hyderabad — sits in rupees, so currency movement feeds straight into reported margins. Second, physical logistics carry unusual weight: finished hot-section parts must move under controlled packaging to assembly lines in Europe, the United States and Japan, and a shipment delay on a sole-source part can stall a customer's production line, which raises the OEM's stake in Azad's reliability and reinforces the relationship. Third, the thin domestic channel leaves little local buffer when a foreign programme pauses. The export orientation is the source of the pricing power and the reason the working-capital cycle is long: distant customers on long-cycle programmes pay on long-cycle terms.

4. Industry Structure, Competitive Moat & External Learning Hub

Core Investor Question: Is Azad's qualification moat strong enough to protect it from concentrated buyers and faster-moving rivals?

Azad's competitive advantage is not a patent, a brand or a cost breakthrough. It is a stack of qualifications. Its products — airfoils, 3D blades, vanes, blisks and impellers for jet engines and industrial gas turbines — must operate above 1,000°C under extreme centrifugal load [10]. Few manufacturers in the world can hold that envelope, and fewer still can do it at the cost base an Indian plant offers. The advantage compounds because it is proven to the customer, on the customer's terms, in the customer's own laboratories. Yet the same structure that creates the moat also concentrates the risk: the number of buyers capable of awarding such work is tiny, and those buyers control the timing of every rupee that flows. This chapter separates the genuine moat from the vulnerabilities that surround it.

The Certification Moat and Its Switching Costs

The moat is built the moment an OEM qualifies Azad for a specific part on a specific programme. Once a hot-section vane or a blisk is qualified, the OEM faces the classic switching-cost trap: to move the work it must re-qualify a new supplier through the same metallurgical, dimensional and endurance gauntlet, absorb the supply risk during the transition, and do so on a component where failure carries outsized consequences. That is why so much of the book is single-source. Mitsubishi Heavy Industries awards single-source hot-section nozzle vanes under a long-term contract worth ₹651 Cr in phase two and ₹1,387 Cr in total over five years [6]. Rolls-Royce signed a long-term agreement in February 2025 for complex machined civil aircraft engine parts for the life of the programme [13][26], and the Baker Hughes agreement was extended to 2030 [3]. These are not annual tenders; they are qualifications with a decade-scale horizon, and each one raises the cost of ever switching away.

Switching Costs Are Real — So Is Concentration Risk

Switch the perspective and the same moat looks like a vulnerability. Azad's customer base is the narrow set of global primes that build jet engines and industrial gas turbines — GE, GE Vernova, Rolls-Royce, Safran, Pratt & Whitney, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, Siemens Energy, Honeywell and Baker Hughes [3][6][13]. It operates six customer-dedicated lean facilities across 44,800 sqm for Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, GE Vernova, Siemens Energy and Baker Hughes [3], and about 93% of FY26 standalone revenue is exported [4]. When a handful of buyers control the volume calendar, a supplier cannot dictate when orders accelerate. Azad is a price-taker not on price per part — its margins are strong — but on volume timing. A programme delay or a customer inventory correction lands directly on its revenue and its working capital, with no diversified domestic channel to absorb the swing. The moat protects Azad's position inside a programme; it does not protect Azad from the programme's own rhythm.

MTAR, Howmet and the Shape of the Competitive Set

The closest listed Indian comparable is MTAR Technologies, and the contrast is instructive. MTAR carries an order book of ₹2,580 Cr that converts in 12–36 months, guides FY27 growth of 50–80%, and runs a tighter working-capital cycle of 172 days [10]. Azad's backlog is larger in revenue multiple — above ₹6,500 Cr, roughly 10.7–10.8x FY26 revenue — but converts over five to six years, and its margin profile is close to double MTAR's [10][6]. Read together, the comparison says Azad wins richer, longer contracts and MTAR turns the wheel faster. Globally, the competitive set is made of the aerospace component majors such as Howmet and Precision Castparts in the United States and MTU Aero Engines in Germany — all larger, all holding the same qualification-based moat, and all facing the same concentrated buyer set. Azad's distinctiveness is not that it has invented a new moat; it is that it holds this moat from a lower-cost base than the Western majors.

China+1, the Energy Transition and the Demand Pool

Two structural forces widen the demand pool. The first is China+1: as Western OEMs and their governments diversify supply chains away from a single geographic concentration, qualified manufacturers outside China gain a seat at the table. A Hyderabad plant with single-source contracts for Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, GE Vernova, Siemens Energy and Baker Hughes is precisely the kind of supplier that benefits when sourcing is deliberately spread [3]. The second is the energy transition. As power systems lean harder on gas turbines for balancing intermittent renewables, demand for hot-section components — the parts Azad already makes — rises with the installed base, and the same prime movers that build those turbines are Azad's customers. Defence adds a third channel, most vividly in the delivered DRDO GTRE 350-kgf expendable turbojet, India's first fully indigenous engine of its class, delivered on 22 July 2026 [8][29]. China+1 and the energy transition do not remove the concentration risk, but they enlarge the number and urgency of programmes chasing a small universe of qualified suppliers.

What the Moat Does Not Protect

The certification moat protects Azad's position inside a programme; it does not protect Azad from an OEM deciding to slow that programme, or from a customer running down its own inventory. Those are timing risks, and they are outside the company's control.

Rank the moat accordingly: it is a strong structural advantage on pricing and durability, and a weak defence against the calendar. Judge the stock on the volume-timing risk, not on whether the moat is real — the moat is real.

A trailing 115x multiple is a forward statement, not a backward one.
