Chapter 1

Azad Engineering Limited was incorporated in 1983, according to the company profile in the price-blind snapshot. It manufactures aerospace components and turbine parts for original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) in aerospace, defence, energy, and oil and gas. The company’s own homepage describes its products as trusted by global OEMs across 17+ countries [47]. For a long-horizon owner the founding date matters less than the transition it records: an engineering manufacturer became a qualified supplier to large OEMs, and that transition has to be paid for in plant, working capital and time.

The revenue record shows how far that transition has run. Consolidated sales rose from ₹123 crore in FY2021 to ₹194 crore in FY2022, ₹252 crore in FY2023, ₹341 crore in FY2024, ₹457 crore in FY2025 and ₹603 crore in FY2026, with a trailing twelve-month figure of ₹684 crore. That is compounding of roughly 37% a year over five years. Growth has not been smooth, but it has not reversed: the slowest annual rate in the series is 29%, in FY2023.

Margins moved with scale. Operating margin was 23% in FY2021, 32% in FY2022, 29% in FY2023, 34% in FY2024, 35% in FY2025 and 37% in FY2026, and 38% on a trailing basis. Profit did not follow the same smooth path. Net profit was ₹12 crore in FY2021, ₹29 crore in FY2022, only ₹8 crore in FY2023, ₹59 crore in FY2024, ₹87 crore in FY2025 and ₹134 crore in FY2026. Return on capital employed tells a similar story: 22% in FY2022, 16% in FY2023, 21% in FY2024 and 12% in both FY2025 and FY2026, so the newer capacity has not yet earned the return of the earlier base.

FY2023 is the year to study. Operating profit of ₹72 crore was absorbed by ₹52 crore of interest and ₹17 crore of depreciation, leaving profit before tax of ₹13 crore after ₹10 crore of other income. Interest of that scale implies heavy borrowing during the expansion. Capital intensity has risen again since then: depreciation went from ₹29 crore in FY2025 to ₹53 crore in FY2026 and ₹72 crore on a trailing basis, and interest went from ₹18 crore to ₹31 crore over the same span.

The cash record runs the other way. Cash from operations was ₹5 crore in FY2021, ₹21 crore in FY2022, negative ₹10 crore in FY2023, negative ₹7 crore in FY2024, ₹54 crore in FY2025 and negative ₹119 crore in FY2026. In FY2025 the company used ₹918 crore in investing activities and took in ₹877 crore of financing cash, the signature of a capacity build paid for with new capital. Free cash flow was negative in each of the six years, although the snapshot’s free-cash-flow row uses a capex definition that this pass does not rely on (see Chapter 6).

The balance sheet shows where the capital went. Fixed assets rose from ₹436 crore at March 2025 to ₹779 crore at March 2026 and ₹989 crore at September 2026. Capital work in progress stood at ₹80 crore, ₹257 crore and ₹212 crore on the same dates, and borrowings at ₹263 crore, ₹474 crore and ₹578 crore. Reserves rose more slowly, from ₹1,381 crore at March 2025 to ₹1,583 crore at September 2026, against equity capital of ₹13 crore. The company has been building ahead of the cash that the buildout is meant to generate.

The operating cycle did not disappear as the business grew. Debtor days were 156 in FY2021, 182 in FY2024 and 189 in FY2026, the highest in the series, and working-capital days moved from negative 11 in FY2021 to 304 in FY2026. The snapshot also carries inventory-day and cash-conversion-cycle rows that reach 2,067 and 1,720 days in FY2026. Their definitions do not reconcile to the company’s own working-capital figures in the sources read, so this chapter does not rely on them. Chapter 6 uses the debtor and working-capital measures only.

The recent quarters carry the same two signals. The company reported Q1 FY2026 revenue of ₹135 crore, up 36.7% [11], and Q2 FY2026 revenue of ₹143 crore, up 28.1% [9]. The Q4 FY2026 call put revenue at ₹157 crore, up 26.4% [4]. The consolidated quarterly snapshot shows ₹162 crore for March 2026, a difference this pass records rather than resolves. Management planned ₹450 crore of capital expenditure in FY2026 [12] and reported ₹139.3 crore of capex in the first half of FY2027 [20].

The growth rate is also a story of its own. Sales grew 58% in FY2022, the fastest year in the series, and operating margin rose from 23% to 32% in the same year, the first clear sign of operating leverage. Growth then settled into a band of 29% to 35% for four years. The company did not repeat the FY2022 jump, so the next leg depends on whether new capacity fills at the same rate as the old.

Quarter-level growth has been uneven. The call figures show 36.7% growth in Q1 FY2026, 28.1% in Q2, 26.4% in Q4 and 31.6% in Q2 FY2027 on a standalone basis [11][9][4][15]. The quarterly snapshot tells the longer story: sales were ₹83 crore in the September 2023 quarter and ₹191 crore in the September 2026 quarter, more than double in three years. Quarterly operating margin stayed between 32% and 39% across the thirteen quarters to September 2026, a narrower band than the annual swings in the older record would suggest, which points to steadier unit economics than the profit line alone shows.

The most recent half continues both trends. Consolidated revenue for the first half of FY2027 was ₹3,635.34 million, up 28.6% [22]. The company reported its highest-ever quarterly revenue of ₹187.8 crore in Q2 FY2027, up 31.6%, on a standalone basis [15]. Profit after tax grew only 6.6% to ₹67.2 crore over the half [16], and operating cash flow was negative ₹45.8 crore [17]. The company raised a ₹700 crore QIP; ₹621.8 crore was deployed and ₹78.2 crore retained at the reporting date [19].

Interest cover, measured as operating profit divided by interest, shows the same cycle from a different angle. It was 5.6 times in FY2021, 4.4 times in FY2022, 1.4 times in FY2023 and 2.5 times in FY2024. It recovered to 8.9 times in FY2025 and 7.3 times in FY2026, and is 6.3 times on a trailing basis. The FY2023 trough is the period when the expansion leaned most on borrowing, and current cover is strong. Cover is only as good as the operating profit under it, which is why the cash record matters more.

The bull reading of this history is specific. A business that took sales from ₹123 crore to ₹603 crore while its operating margin rose by 14 points, and that reports an order book of about ₹6,500 crore [1], has converted demand into revenue and held its margin in a qualified niche. Qualification takes 30 to 48 months [29], so a new entrant cannot copy the revenue quickly, and the company’s relationships with key customers average over ten years [30]. Those are the strongest facts in the record, and they are not small.

The bear reading is equally specific. Operating cash flow was negative in three of the last six years. Cumulative operating cash flow for FY2021 to FY2026 was negative ₹56 crore, against cumulative net profit of ₹329 crore. Other income supplied ₹46 crore of the ₹187 crore profit before tax in FY2026, a quarter of the pre-tax figure. Working capital, not the factory floor, is where the reported profit has gone so far.

The end markets explain why the order flow is lumpy. Energy and oil-and-gas equipment demand follows customers’ capital budgets, and aerospace follows engine programmes with long lead times. Both drive the timing of orders more than the timing of quarterly sales, which is why the company’s growth has been strong in some quarters and softer in others, and why a year of record revenue can still carry negative operating cash.

Management’s forward statements point further up. The company said it was delivering on top-line growth of approximately 25% or more for the current year [6], and it targets an annual revenue run-rate above ₹13,000 million by the end of FY2028 [36]. These are management targets, not results. Chapter 5 tests them against the record.

The sources read do not document the company’s history before FY2021 beyond its incorporation date, so an earlier operating cycle cannot be traced here, and this pass does not infer one. What the record does show is a sequence of capacity commitments, each financed before its cash conversion appeared. Chapter 2

The company reports two named segments, and they are not equal in weight. Energy and oil and gas is the larger and older of the two. On the company’s call basis, the segment contributed ₹481 crore in FY2026, or 81.5% of revenue [8]. The company described energy as its oldest business and the largest part of its order book [2]. Aerospace and defence contributed ₹102 crore, about 17.2% of revenue [7].

Those two figures sit on a ₹590 crore revenue base from the call, not the ₹603 crore of consolidated sales in the snapshot. The two segments add to about ₹583 crore, which leaves roughly ₹7 crore unexplained in the sources read. This pass treats that residual as a reconciling item rather than a third segment. Profit by segment is not disclosed in any source read: no segment margin, segment EBIT or segment profit share was found [48], so the profit-share column below stays open.

The mix has been steady. In Q2 FY2026 energy and oil and gas contributed ₹117 crore of ₹143 crore of revenue, or 81% [10]. The full-year share of 81.5% [8] is almost identical, so the mix did not shift materially during the year. The sources describe energy customers and turbine parts, but a finer product split inside energy is not disclosed.

The product is a set of qualified process routes rather than a catalogue of items. The business makes super-alloy hot-section components [39], and the qualification condition in the sources is that, for super alloys, OEMs must agree to qualify indigenous suppliers [14]. The company says it has more than 1,700 qualified parts and components across more than 45 qualified manufacturing processes [28]. Raw materials come from approved sources, and the sources name Sunflag and Star Wire as approved for a few grades [13]. Each part number and each process is therefore a separate approval.

Qualification is the commercial gate. The Financial Express profile describes the process as taking 30 to 48 months, which it calls a significant entry barrier [29]; a separate analyst note uses the same 30 to 48 month range for proving each part [40]. The company reports an average relationship of over 10 years with key customers [30]. Management said in Q2 FY2026 that its wallet share across customers in energy, aerospace, defence and oil and gas was about 1 to 1.5% [38]. If that is right, the headroom with existing customers is large in principle, and the real constraint is which parts a customer will move to a new qualified source.

The physical base is six dedicated facilities totalling 44,800 square metres [21]. An analyst note describes nearly 45,000 square metres of production facilities across six dedicated customers [37]. The dedication matters. A facility built around one customer’s parts has a narrow revenue line, so the utilisation risk rises if that customer’s programme slows. The record does not say how much of each facility is committed to which customer.

Energy example one: the MHI agreement. The company signed a Long-Term Contract and Price Agreement for an eight-year term with Mitsubishi Heavy Industries [33]. Read as an end-to-end flow, the agreement sets price and volume expectations before individual orders arrive. Parts for the turbine programme then move through the qualification record already built for those part families, and each shipment is a call-off against the agreement. The sources do not disclose MHI volumes or the share of the order book the agreement covers, so these mechanics are the general way such agreements work, not a disclosed AZAD schedule. What is disclosed is the length: eight years is long enough that capacity plans have to follow the agreement, not the quarter.

Energy example two: the BHEL rate contract. The company won a two-year long-term rate contract worth around ₹52.68 crore [35]. A rate contract fixes prices for a period and lets the buyer order against it as needed. It is much smaller than the MHI agreement. It shows that the energy segment includes domestic power-sector work alongside global OEM programmes, so the segment is not one customer.

The two energy examples differ in scale and in what they show. The MHI agreement runs for eight years, and no value is disclosed for it [33]. The BHEL rate contract is worth about ₹52.68 crore over two years [35], which is roughly ₹26 crore a year, or about 4% of FY2026 consolidated sales. Because the MHI value is not disclosed, the named contracts cannot be sized against revenue as a group. The order book, not any single contract, carries the visibility argument, and the order book is itself a management estimate [1].

Aerospace and defence example. The sources do not name a specific A&D order, so the path shown here is built from the sources and labelled as illustrative. A super-alloy hot-section part starts with a raw-material grade from an approved supplier [13]. The OEM must agree to qualify the company as an indigenous source before production [14]. The proving period for the part family then runs 30 to 48 months [29] before the part moves to series orders. Revenue from that part reaches the income statement only after that gate, which is why A&D revenue of ₹102 crore in FY2026 [7] depends on qualifications started years earlier.

The general sequence for a new hot-section part runs from first-article inspection through process-capability proving to series production. The sources describe the total proving period of 30 to 48 months but not the individual stages, so the sequence here is the general industry pattern, not an AZAD disclosure. What matters for the forensic read is that revenue from a new part family starts only after the last stage, and that a slow OEM qualification delays revenue without any change in the company’s own execution.

The order book is weighted toward energy. In the Q1 FY2026 call the company said energy, its oldest business, constituted the largest part of the order book [2]. The mid-2025 energy book of about ₹3,400 crore [2] is the only segment-level book figure in the record, and it is older than the company-level figure of about ₹6,500 crore at year-end [1]. The sources do not show how the aerospace and defence book has moved, so the segment mix of the order book cannot be updated past mid-2025.

The two named segments carry almost all revenue. Neither segment’s profit share is disclosed, so the forensic chapter cannot say which segment earns the 37% group operating margin.

Customers named in public profiles include large engine and power-equipment groups. A Financial Express headline groups the company with suppliers to GE, Siemens and Rolls-Royce [29]. Those names describe the buyer universe, not disclosed order values. Customer concentration is not disclosed in the sources read: no top-customer or top-five share was found [50]. The MHI and BHEL contracts are the only large relationships named with a term or value, so concentration cannot be measured here.

Energy carries the revenue and the cycle. Oil and gas and power-equipment capital budgets move with their customers’ investment programmes. The bull reading is that a 30-to-48-month qualification base and an eight-year agreement give the energy business visibility well beyond one order. The bear reading is that 81.5% of revenue in one segment, with exports at about 92% of FY2025 revenue [31], means the company’s results follow one customer-investment cycle, even if the customers differ.

Aerospace and defence is smaller and slower. Its bull case is that qualification, once granted, lasts the life of the part programme, and that the A&D share has room to grow as qualifications accumulate. Its bear case is that the segment is only about one-sixth of revenue and depends on approvals that take years, so it cannot offset an energy downturn in the near term.

The segment label itself bundles two end markets. Energy and oil and gas combine power and turbine equipment with hydrocarbon equipment, and their demand drivers differ: power customers follow generation investment, while oil and gas customers follow upstream and downstream spending. The company reports them together, so the record cannot show which of the two sub-markets is carrying the 81.5% share. That is a second reason the segment figure is a summary rather than a driver.

The aerospace and defence segment adds a second source of demand. Defence procurement and engine programme ramps follow their own schedules, separate from the energy capital cycle. The record does not show how much of the A&D revenue of ₹102 crore [7] comes from defence and how much from civil aerospace, so the two cannot be separated, and a slowdown in one would not be visible in the disclosed figures.

The profit split is the missing piece for any segment argument. Without segment margins, the reader cannot tell whether the 37% group operating margin is carried by energy, by A&D, or by a mix of programmes with very different economics. The bull reading assumes the more heavily qualified programmes earn the most; the bear reading assumes the energy programmes, which carry the largest volume, earn the least per rupee. The record supports neither reading, and that is why the profit-share column above stays open [48].

The company’s statements describe a wider footprint than the two segments show. The homepage claims customers across 17+ countries [47], which describes geographic reach. The segment disclosure describes end markets. Neither gives a customer-level revenue split, and the difference matters: a wide geographic footprint can sit on a narrow set of large buyers.

Spread across six facilities, FY2026 sales of ₹603 crore average about ₹100 crore a facility. That average is a rough guide only. Capital work in progress was ₹212 crore at September 2026, so some capacity was still being built and was not yet earning revenue, which means the average understates what a running facility produces.

Export share is the other filter on the book. With exports at about 92% of FY2025 revenue [31], domestic business is a small share, and the BHEL rate contract [35] is one of the few named domestic relationships. Most of the segment book therefore sits with overseas buyers, whose approval and payment rules differ from domestic ones, and that concentrates the commercial risk in a small group of export accounts.

The business is therefore best described as a qualified process route for a small number of technically demanding parts, sold into two end markets, with a long approval gate in front of each. What the record does not show is how profit splits across that route, or how many of the qualified parts carry the revenue. Chapter 3 follows the route through supply, capacity and cash.

Chapter 4

The industry the company serves is precision super-alloy components for aerospace engines, industrial gas turbines and oil and gas equipment. The sources describe the entry barrier rather than the size of the market. The records do not size the market or name the company’s direct competitors, so this chapter describes the structure of the industry, not the company’s share of it.

The moat is qualification. A part family must pass an OEM’s process proving before it earns series orders, and the proving period runs 30 to 48 months [29][40]. The hot section is where qualification is hardest [39]. The company reports an average relationship of over 10 years with key customers [30]. A supplier that has passed the gate keeps the programme for as long as the engine or turbine model stays in production, which is why contract length and relationship length are the core evidence of advantage in the record.

The company describes the scale of its qualification in numbers: more than 1,700 qualified parts and components across more than 45 qualified processes [28], six dedicated facilities [21] and an eight-year price agreement with a major turbine OEM [33]. Those numbers show breadth of qualification. They do not show the margin the moat earns. Margin is in the snapshot: operating margin of 37% in FY2026, up from 23% in FY2021.

Two parallels test the moat from opposite sides. The first is a case where qualification-led scale compounded into a very large business. The second is a case where a similar kind of supplier, with concentrated customers, earned almost no operating income. Both are used as structural twins, not as peer comparisons.

Situational Scaling & Moat Twin: Precision Castparts Corp. (PCC)

United States; 1999 to 2014, acquisition-led scale-up in superalloy castings and forgings

Shared Mechanism: Qualified castings and forgings for jet engines and industrial turbines are slow to approve, so the qualified process chain is the asset. PCC bought qualified capacity, including Wyman-Gordon in 1999 for $721 million plus $104 million of assumed debt, and extended its specialty-metals chain with Special Metals in 2006, so that it controlled more of the qualified route.

Historical Numbers & Outcome: Revenue of US$9.6 billion in FY2014 [41]; Wyman-Gordon purchase of $721 million plus $104 million of debt in May 1999 [42]; Special Metals acquired in 2006 [43].

Direct Implication for AZAD: AZAD has the same type of moat, built organically rather than bought. The test is whether six dedicated facilities turn into qualified output without a fresh capital raise each time, and whether the qualified process chain stays with the company as capacity grows.

Historical Failure-Mode & Base-Rate Twin: SIFCO Industries (United States; 2016 to 2021, aerospace component supplier with concentrated OEM customers)

Shared Vulnerability: A qualified aerospace supplier whose sales sit with a few large engine makers can see its operating result fall close to zero when volume or mix turns against it. The sales base alone does not protect the margin; the operating result is what the customer concentration exposes.

Historical Numbers & Base Rate: Revenue of US$100 million in 2021 and operating income of US$1.1 million, an operating margin of about 1% [44][45]; in 2016 Rolls-Royce was 11% and United Technologies 10% of sales [46].

How AZAD Compares Today: AZAD’s FY2021 operating margin was 23% and its FY2026 margin 37%, so the failure case is not bankruptcy. It is a return toward a thin-margin base while the same working-capital build continues. The downside has to be underwritten before growth is paid for.

Reading the two parallels together, PCC’s scale came partly from buying qualified capacity [42][43]. AZAD is building its capacity organically, six facilities at a time. That is slower but avoids the purchase price. The question is whether each new facility becomes qualified output without another capital raise. The SIFCO parallel is the failure base rate: when a qualified supplier’s sales depend on a few large customers, a modest change in volume or mix can leave almost no operating income [44][45]. AZAD’s own FY2021 operating margin of 23% is the nearest internal base case, a reminder that the 37% margin is a recent level, not an inherited floor.

The bull reading of the moat is concrete. Qualification takes 30 to 48 months [29], relationships run over ten years [30], and the MHI agreement runs eight years [33]. Those are contract-length advantages, the kind that do not disappear in one quarter. The bear reading is that the same barrier protects revenue, not cash. Chapter 6 shows that profit has grown faster than operating cash, and a moat that does not turn profit into cash is weaker than the revenue record suggests.

Customer share is the other limit on the moat. On management’s figure, the company holds about 1 to 1.5% of its customers’ wallet in these categories [38]. That leaves real room to grow, but it also means the company competes for each programme against qualified rivals. The sources do not show how many rivals hold each part family [49], so the strength of the position cannot be ranked against the field.

The parallels show mechanisms rather than forecasts. PCC was a far larger business, and its scale was financed with acquisitions that AZAD has not made. SIFCO’s 2021 revenue in the record, about US$100 million, is larger than AZAD’s FY2026 sales of about ₹603 crore (roughly US$70 million at about ₹85 to the dollar), and its margin was set by its own cost and mix. What transfers is the structure: a qualified process route, a concentrated set of large customers, and a margin that depends on how those customers’ volumes move.

Export geography sets the arena for the moat. With about 92% of FY2025 revenue from exports [31], the company’s qualifications are tested in the processes of overseas OEMs, whose supplier-approval rules decide the outcome. The record does not show how many of the qualifications are shared across OEMs, so the moat may be wider than any single customer relationship, or it may be a set of separate approvals that do not carry over.

The qualification counts need a caution. Over 1,700 parts and over 45 processes [28] are breadth measures. They do not say how many parts carry the revenue, or how many are in production today. A few part families can carry most of the revenue, and the record does not show their share. A moat measured by count can be wide while the revenue depends on a handful of programmes.

The indigenous-supplier condition cuts both ways. When an OEM must qualify indigenous suppliers for super alloys [14], an Indian supplier that has passed the gate gains a structural reason to stay on the programme. The same condition also makes the OEM the decision-maker: the supplier’s position depends on the OEM’s policy, which can change with its sourcing strategy. The record does not show how that policy has evolved for the company’s programmes.

A useful test of the moat is whether the qualified processes are spread across many part families or concentrated in a few. Forty-five processes spread across a broad set of programmes is a wide moat; the same processes feeding one engine family is a narrow one. The record does not show that split, and it is the first thing a fuller disclosure should answer.
