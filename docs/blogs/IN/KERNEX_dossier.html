Terms used in this dossier

Each term listed here is used later in the dossier. Each is explained once here so that the later sections can use them without a break.

ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit a company earns from all the money tied up in it, before interest and tax, divided by that capital. Think of a shop that puts Rs 100 into stock and fittings and earns Rs 48 a year before tax: its ROCE is 48%. The portfolio floor is 15%, and a Core-grade business should clear 18%.

CFO / PAT compares cash from operations (CFO) with the reported profit after tax (PAT). A ratio near 1.0 means the profit shows up as cash in the bank. A ratio below 0.70 over three years means the accounts are reporting profit that the business is not collecting. The floor here is 0.70 across the last three financial years added together.

Stage 1 to Stage 4 is the Weinstein cycle of a stock. Stage 1 is a base after a fall, Stage 2 is a sustained uptrend, Stage 3 is a top where the trend flattens and the stock starts to roll over, and Stage 4 is a downtrend. Only Stage 2 is a buy zone for a momentum holding.

DMA (daily moving average) is the average closing price over a set number of trading days. The 20-day line tracks the short term, the 50-day the medium term and the 200-day the long-term trend. A price above a rising 50-day and 200-day line is the picture of a healthy uptrend.

ATR (average true range, 14 days) is the typical daily price swing. It is the ruler this framework uses to measure distance: a price 2.2 ATR above support has moved about two and a fifth typical days' worth of range away from that level.

RSI (relative strength index, 14 days) is a 0 to 100 gauge of recent momentum. Above 70 is treated as overbought, below 30 as oversold. It describes speed, not direction, so it does not decide a verdict on its own.

Asymmetry is the reward-to-risk ratio: the distance from the current price to a target, divided by the distance from the price down to the stop-loss. The floor is 3.0 for Core alpha and 3.5 for tactical momentum. A target that cannot be traced to a stated method does not count.

Momentum gate is the code test that every candidate must pass. Test A asks whether the stock is already in a healthy uptrend with leading relative strength. Test B asks whether it is entering one from a tight base. Failing both means no allocation, whatever the business quality.

Debt-to-equity compares borrowings with the owners’ stake in the business, which is share capital plus reserves. A ratio of 0.65 means each rupee of owners’ capital is matched by about 65 paise of borrowing. The floor is 0.70.

Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings over the period stated, and it says how many years of current profit a buyer is paying for. A reading of 15.6 means the price is about 15.6 times the trailing year’s earnings.

EPS (earnings per share) is the profit attributed to each share. It is the figure a price-to-earnings ratio is built on, so a forecast EPS is the first input to any target that uses that method.

Measured move is a price target found by adding the height of a base to its breakout level. It is one of the three accepted ways to anchor a target in this framework, alongside forward earnings times a median multiple and a discounted cash-flow value.

How the verdict was reached

The framework has three layers and they run in order. Layer 1 is a set of binary hard gates: a candidate must pass every one, and any single failure ends the analysis with the verdict REJECTED. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five company-specific operating measures, and it needs at least three of four (or four of five) to show a PASS. Layer 3 maps the current price's distance from a verified support level onto a set of entry bands and sets the entry posture. Layer 2 and Layer 3 only run if Layer 1 passes.

The reason for the order is simple. A scorecard can be pleasant to read, and a timing band can say when to buy, but neither matters if the company does not turn profit into cash or if the price trend is broken. So the first question is always the gates. For KERNEX, the cash-conversion gate fails, the technical stage gate fails because the ledger reads Stage 3 rather than Stage 2, and the asymmetry gate fails because no target can be traced to an accepted method. Those are three separate failures, and any one of them would be enough to reject the stock.

The momentum gate is a fourth check that sits beside the three layers. It is computed in code from the frozen price ledger, and it is not recomputed in this dossier. The result for KERNEX is NO_MOMENTUM: it fails both Test A and Test B. Under the portfolio rules, a candidate that fails both tests receives no allocation whatever its business quality, so the momentum result alone would also block an allocation.

For this company, Layer 1 was run in full and failed. Layer 2 was not run, and Layer 3 was not run, because the framework says those layers are evaluated only after Layer 1 passes. Both are described in their own sections below, with the entry posture the ledger band would have given for context.

Layer 2 scorecard

Layer 2 was not run for KERNEX, because Layer 1 failed. The framework says a candidate that fails any Layer 1 gate is not evaluated on operating measures or entry timing. This is a deliberate rule, not an omission. A scorecard that looks healthy on a company whose cash does not reconcile to its profit would make the rejected stock look better than the evidence supports. The scorecard would have tested operating measures such as order-book cover, receivable days and warranty cost. Those are discussed in the business and price sections above. They are reported here as context rather than as scored results.

The rule that would bring Layer 2 into play is a Layer 1 pass. That requires the cash-flow ratio to reach 0.70, the stage to return to Stage 2, and a verified target anchor. Once all three are met, the scorecard would be built on the operating measures that matter for this business. The four measures would be order-book conversion, debtor days, warranty provisions and the cash-conversion trend. Each would be scored PASS, WATCH or FAIL. At least three of the four would need to pass to qualify, which is the count the framework applies when four measures are scored.

Fatal flaws and the red-team case

The strongest honest case against the company is not that its profit is fake. The case is that the profit has not yet become cash, and that the balance sheet is absorbing the gap. Over three years the company generated cumulative operating cash flow of minus Rs 156 crore against cumulative profit of Rs 111 crore. The FY26 year alone shows profit of Rs 88 crore and operating cash flow of minus Rs 100 crore. Receivable days rose from 45 to 268 in a single year, and the other-assets line rose to Rs 885 crore. A bear would say that the revenue is being booked ahead of collection, that the buyers are a small set of railway production units with long payment cycles, and that a warranty provision of Rs 30.05 crore, reported by a secondary source for the June quarter and not checked against the company’s presentation, shows the cost of earlier deliveries is still arriving.

The second flaw is the stage. The stock has fallen 31 percent from its 52-week high, its 50-day average is falling, and the relative-strength reading against the Nifty is negative over three months. The market is paying less for the story than it did a year ago, and the broader market regime is weak, with only 20 percent of the universe above its 50-day average. A stock in that condition can keep falling even when its results are excellent, because the buyers who lifted it have left.

The third flaw is the target. A buy case needs a price that the stock could reach for stated reasons. There is no verified analyst consensus, and the 3-year median price-to-earnings used in the framework is not available from any source reviewed. Without a traceable target, the asymmetry cannot be computed, and an investor is left guessing at upside. The most likely bear outcome is a stock that stays in Stage 3, trades in a range well below its 52-week high, and waits for the cash to arrive.

The bull reply is that the stock has real earning power, that the June 2026 quarter showed Rs 504 crore of sales and Rs 110 crore of profit, and that an order book reported at Rs 3,641 crore would be a large amount of contracted work to deliver. That reply is fair as far as it goes. Its weakness is that the order book figure is management's number reported by a secondary source, and a second summary gives Rs 5,174 crore, so the size of the book is not settled. The bull also has to explain the debtor days and the cash, and the Layer 1 test is the place where that explanation must be shown rather than asserted.

For an owner, the bear case here is specific and has numbers behind it, while the bull case rests on a figure that has not been confirmed. Until the cash conversion turns, the default is to leave the stock out of any allocation.

What would change the verdict

A verdict should say what would move it. The framework names the rule and the number, so the triggers below use the gates' own thresholds and the ledger's own levels. They are listed in the order they are most likely to change, and each one is a condition, not a forecast.

If the three-year CFO-to-PAT ratio on the Screener page reaches 0.70 or more, the cash-flow gate passes. On the FY25 to FY27 window, FY27 operating cash flow would need to be at least about Rs 182 crore plus 70 percent of FY27 profit, which is about Rs 259 crore if FY27 profit were Rs 110 crore. That test would come with the FY27 annual report, which is due only after the year closes on 31 March 2027.

If the ledger shows the stock closing above its 50-day average of Rs 1,881.41 for two sessions with that average turning up, the stage gate moves toward a Stage 2 pass. That is a price trigger that could come in weeks.

If the company or a recognised research house publishes a forward earnings figure and a three-year median price-to-earnings that together give a target at least 3.5 times the distance to the stop, the asymmetry gate can pass. At the current stop of Rs 1,550.40, that target must be at least Rs 2,585.80.

If the price falls to the 63-day base low of Rs 1,593.00 and holds there for two sessions, the stock returns to a base and the support-distance band moves from NO_BUY toward LIMIT_ORDER_REDUCE. That would be the first point at which Layer 3 would matter, and it would still require Layer 1 to pass.

If the close falls below Rs 1,559.56, the 200-day average, for two sessions, the trend breaks and the verdict stays REJECTED on the technical gate with a Stage 4 read added.

If the promoter pledge rises above the 8.87 percent recorded at 30 June 2026, or the debtor days remain above 200 in the September 2026 quarter, the verdict is unchanged but the risk note is strengthened.

What this means for you is that the path back to a buy is long and has several gates. The price levels can change in weeks. The cash ratio cannot pass on one ordinary year, because it is a three-year total and the most recent year (FY26, with operating cash flow of minus Rs 100 crore) still sits inside the window. That is why the verdict is more likely to stay REJECTED through the next two quarterly results than to change. The next quarterly results and the FY27 annual report are the events to check. The 3.5 floor and the momentum gate are both specific rules that a reader can test against the next ledger.
