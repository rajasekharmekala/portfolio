Before you read

Kernex Microsystems is a Hyderabad company that builds Kavach, the train-braking system Indian Railways is fitting across its network[2][12]. Its profit went from a ₹27 crore loss in FY24 to ₹88 crore in FY26, and ₹110 crore in the June 2026 quarter alone[1][2][6]. This report concludes REJECTED. Over three years the business used ₹156 crore of cash in its operations while it reported ₹112 crore of profit[4][5]. The call would be wrong if the half-year cash flow due in November shows unpaid bills and stock turning into cash[4].

1. What the company does

Kernex makes the electronics that stop trains from colliding. Its main product is Kavach, which Kernex's own filings call TCAS (train collision avoidance system). A box in the locomotive reads signals from tags laid along the track and from units at stations. If the driver does not slow down in time, the box applies the brakes. Kernex supplies the locomotive unit, the station unit and the level-crossing unit, and it also does the field work: towers, optical fibre and installation.[2][4] The technology is patented jointly by the state design body RDSO (Research Designs and Standards Organisation) and three approved makers, of which Kernex is one.[2]

The customer is the Indian Railways system: zonal railways, the locomotive factories at Chittaranjan (CLW) and Banaras, and the coach factory at Chennai (ICF). CARE Ratings states that all of Kernex's revenue comes from Indian Railways.[2] Contracts go to the lowest bidder, so Kernex cannot set its own price. Bills can only be raised after installation and certification. Until then, the work sits on the balance sheet as a contract asset, which is revenue earned but not yet billed.[2][4] This is the root of the cash problem examined in Chapter 3: Kernex buys parts and builds units months before a railway milestone lets it send an invoice.

The unit economics look good on paper. In FY26 the company earned a 34.6% margin before interest, lease rentals, depreciation and tax, and PAT (profit after tax) of ₹88 crore on ₹430.54 crore of operating income.[2] The biggest costs were materials of about ₹395 crore, offset by ₹223 crore of unsold stock added to the shelves, and project execution of about ₹54 crore.[4] In the June 2026 quarter, revenue was ₹503.58 crore, operating profit about ₹163 crore and profit ₹109.85 crore, after a ₹30.05 crore provision for warranty claims on equipment already delivered.[6][19] Borrowing costs are rising fast: finance costs were ₹28.15 crore in FY26 and ₹13.23 crore in the June 2026 quarter alone.[4][6]

Customers and cycles matter more than usual here. One order, the 3,024-set CLW contract worth ₹2,465.71 crore, still has about ₹1,870 crore to deliver, which is about 54% of the ₹3,474 crore order book that CARE recorded at 31 May 2026.[2][11] Government spending is the cycle: the Railways have spent ₹3,875 crore on Kavach to June 2026, set aside ₹2,066 crore for FY27, and taken up trackside work on 21,794 route km and fitting on 7,190 locomotives.[12] Revenue arrives in lumps. Quarterly sales were ₹47 crore, ₹73 crore, ₹255 crore and ₹504 crore across the last four reported quarters, so no single quarter is a run rate.[1][21]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Kavach / TCAS: India's automatic train protection system. It brakes the train if the driver fails to.
  • RDSO: the Railways' design and standards body. Its approval is needed before a vendor can ship a new Kavach version.
  • Milestone billing: the customer pays at fixed stages (installation, commissioning), not when parts are bought.
  • Contract asset: revenue recorded for work done but not yet billed.
  • Operating cycle: days from buying inputs to collecting cash from the customer, less the days taken to pay suppliers.
  • CFO/PAT: cash from operations divided by profit after tax. Below 1.0 means profit is not turning into cash.
  • ECL: expected credit loss, the reserve a company sets aside for bills it may not collect.
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company owes its suppliers.
  • ROCE: return on capital employed, which is operating profit divided by the money invested in the business.
  • EPS: earnings per share, which is profit divided by the number of shares.
  • FCF: free cash flow, which is cash from operations less money spent on plant and equipment.
  • Debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' equity.
  • Operating margin is the share of each rupee of sales left after operating costs.
  • Guidance is what management says in advance about orders, dates or results.
  • Consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who cover a stock.
  • RSI: relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of recent buying against selling. Under 30 is oversold and over 70 is overbought.
  • ATR: average true range, the typical size of a day's price swing.
  • Stage 2 and Stage 4: in the Weinstein trend scheme, Stage 2 is the rising markup phase and Stage 4 is the falling markdown phase.
  • Base: a flat price floor where a stock rests before its next move. In the scenario tables the base case is the middle case.
  • Pivot and breakout: the pivot is the price level that, if broken upward, marks a breakout from a base.
  • Entry zone, limit order, stop loss: the entry zone is the price range where we would buy; a limit order buys only at or below a set price; a stop loss is the exit price if the idea is wrong.
  • Asymmetry and reward-to-risk: the expected gain to the target divided by the loss down to the stop. Our floor is 2.5 to 1 for turnaround ideas.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position is real but narrow. Only three firms are authorised to design and make both the hardware and the software of Kavach. Entry barriers are high: complex technical rules, strict safety standards and long validation cycles.[2] RDSO approved version 4.0 for Kernex on 14 October 2025. That let Kernex start deliveries against ₹3,346.35 crore of orders accumulated since April 2024.[9] The listed rival Quadrant Future Tek still shows a train-control segment with almost no revenue and a ₹10.18 crore segment loss in the June 2026 quarter. HBL Engineering is the only other maker already booking large Kavach sales.[24][25][22]

The evidence for strength is the order book and the margin. The order book rose from ₹2,124 crore in March 2025 to ₹3,641 crore in August 2026, even as the company executed work.[8][6] CARE upgraded the bank-facility rating from BBB- to BBB on 12 October 2026.[2] Operating margin moved from 21.6% in FY25 to 34.6% in FY26. CARE credits lower growth in employee costs and better demand.[2] Return on capital employed (ROCE) was 47.8% on Screener's average-capital method. Our own year-end check gives about 35%, because borrowings and equity grew fast.[1][4]

The evidence for weakness is delivery and billing. In March 2025 management said CLW deliveries would start by the end of May 2025 and installation from June 2025.[8] In January 2026 the company told the exchanges it could not finish that 2,500-unit order in time. Certification had been delayed. CLW then refused its request for more time.[10] Kernex won a fresh CLW order for 3,024 sets on 15 January 2026. It plans to use existing inventory for that order.[10][11] Inventory stood at ₹371 crore on the March 2026 balance sheet, against ₹53 crore a year earlier.[4]

Governance is mixed rather than alarming. The statutory auditor resigned on 4 September 2024. It cited other work and staff constraints and said the exit was not due to any failure to get audit evidence.[13] The FY26 audit was signed by NSVR and Associates LLP with four emphasis-of-matter paragraphs. They cover a ₹4.23 crore customer bill more than three years old, ₹2.65 crore of bank guarantees under arbitration, ₹6.38 crore advanced to a joint venture (₹4.03 crore reserved) and the write-down of a US subsidiary.[4][33] Other disclosures are minor: an NSE warning for printing the company's tax number on its letterhead, a report on a breach of the insider-trading code of conduct by one designated employee, and a cyber-security incident in August 2024 that the company said had no major impact on its operations.[14][15][16] The Manthena family runs the company and holds 28.73%, down from 29.14% in December 2023. Of that holding, 8.87% is pledged.[7][1]

Order wins and sector demand

In 2026 came orders from Banaras and Patiala locomotive works (₹91.12 crore and ₹67.53 crore in April) and a new CLW order of ₹475.21 crore in May.[34] Sector demand is large. By 31 July 2026 Kavach 4.0 was commissioned on 2,633 route km, trackside work had been taken up on 21,794 route km, and fitting had begun on 7,190 locomotives and 1,200 electric multiple-unit trains.[12][26]

One cautionary point: the Railways' Kavach budget for FY27 is ₹2,066 crore, which is about the same size as Kernex's own ₹2,100 crore FY27 execution pool from the Q1 presentation.[12][17] We did not find how the loco-factory fitment orders are funded, so we treat the timing of customer payments as unverified. CARE states that collections from Indian Railways are timely.[2]

Other signals in the filings

In October 2025 the exchanges asked about a sharp rise in the share's volume, and the company replied that nothing beyond its Kavach 4.0 approval and ₹3,346.35 crore of orders was pending.[35] The FY25 audited results show how small the business was a year earlier, with revenue of ₹189.77 crore and a profit that included a tax credit.[32][1] The Q1 FY27 results note also shows ₹334.59 lakh of reserve against the ₹422.73 lakh disputed bill, so about 79% is already provided.[6][19]

The number

Three-year cash from operations over profit is -1.40, and Kernex would need about ₹183 crore of operating cash in FY27 for the FY25 to FY27 window to reach the 0.25 floor.[4][5]

How it was calculated

We summed consolidated cash from operations for FY24, FY25 and FY26 (-71.12, +14.55, -99.74 crore) and divided by the summed profit after tax (-26.71, +50.05, +88.24 crore). For the re-entry level we moved the window to FY25 to FY27: FY25 and FY26 cash is -85.19 crore, FY25 and FY26 profit is 138.29 crore, and base-case FY27 profit is about 252 crore, so the window profit is about 390 crore. A 0.25 ratio needs about 98 crore of cash over three years, which means about 183 crore in FY27.

The inputs

FY24 cash flow from the FY25 annual report[5]; FY25 and FY26 cash flow from the FY26 annual report[4]; profit from the CARE rationale and annual report[2][3]; FY27 profit from our Chapter 5 estimate.

The range

Over the two-year window FY25 to FY26 the ratio is -0.62. If FY27 cash from operations were zero, the FY25 to FY27 ratio would be -0.22. At the base-case profit, a cash inflow of ₹183 crore or more clears the floor, and a cash inflow of ₹300 crore would give 0.55.

If the number is off

If the half-year cash flow due in November 2026 shows positive operating cash of about ₹100 crore, the re-entry path is open and we would re-underwrite. If it shows another outflow, the case for the exception weakens further and the rejection stands for at least a full year.

The number

Kernex tied up about ₹228 crore more net working capital in FY26 than a year earlier, and year-end receivables of ₹316 crore are about the size of one quarter of billing.[4]

How it was calculated

Net working capital is inventories plus contract assets plus receivables, minus trade payables, from the consolidated balance sheet: ₹85.5 crore in March 2025 and ₹313.3 crore in March 2026. For receivables we compared ₹316.26 crore with Q4 FY26 revenue of ₹254.58 crore plus 18% GST, which is about ₹300 crore of billing.

The inputs

Balance sheet and cash flow[4]; Q4 revenue[29]; CARE's statement that collections from Indian Railways are timely and bills are raised only after installation.[2]

The range

In the favourable case the receivables are fresh March billing that Railways pays within the normal term, and a large part reverses in the first half of FY27. In the unfavourable case part of the ₹371 crore of stock cannot be installed for months, as the January 2026 CLW delay shows, and borrowing keeps rising. CARE reports bank limits of ₹347.50 crore used at about 69% on average for the 12 months to July 2026 and expects higher reliance on bank borrowing.[2]

If the number is off

If receivables turn out to include older, disputed bills, profit is overstated and the ₹422.73 lakh disputed item in the audit report would be the visible edge of a bigger problem. The test is the September half-year receivables line.

Our variant perception

The market is treating Kernex as a one-off earnings spike with doubtful cash. The shares have fallen from ₹2,580 to ₹1,587 while profit multiplied, and trailing P/E is 14.0 against 27.3 for HBL, which is consistent with that doubt.[1][22]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate. It then gives the red-team case against our own view, and the one claim everything hinges on. We aim the red team at our rejection as well as at the targets, because a rejection can also be wrong.

Weakest assumption in the FY27 profit estimate: revenue conversion

We assumed ₹1,300 crore of FY27 revenue. The weakest link is how fast the backlog turns into billed work. Three dated promises already slipped by 6 to 12 months.[8][4] Evidence that we are wrong would be a quarter with revenue under ₹200 crore, as in December 2025 when it was ₹73 crore. Another sign would be CLW supply stuck near the 45% reported on 13 August 2026.[6][30] The next evidence arrives with the September quarter results, expected by mid-November 2026.

Weakest assumption in the ₹1,800 target: a 12 times multiple

We took a 14% discount to today's trailing multiple. That is a judgment, not a measurement, because no three-year median exists. If cash proves real, the market could pay the 14.0 times multiple or more. HBL trades at 27 times.[22] If cash does not come, the market could pay under 9 times for a supplier that burns cash. The target moves by ₹150 for every one turn of multiple at our EPS.

Weakest assumption in the cash reverse DCF: profit as a proxy for cash

The cash reverse DCF starts from FY27 profit or a share of it. Profit is not cash for this company, and the share that converts is the thing we cannot observe. The model also uses a 12% discount rate and a 20 times exit multiple from the scoring file. A different exit multiple would shift every implied growth rate.

Red-team bear case against the thesis that the earnings are real and will turn into cash

Revenue is booked when units are delivered and installed. The bills are paid later, and suppliers are owed ₹443 crore. The ₹371 crore of inventory is mostly parts bought for the CLW orders. CLW refused to extend the first order, so some of those parts may wait.[4][10] Warranty claims will arrive after the revenue. In Q1 FY27 alone ₹30.05 crore was provided, which is 6% of that quarter's sales.[4][19] A BBB rating means bank money is not cheap. Finance cost rose from ₹6.92 crore in FY25 to ₹28.15 crore in FY26. CARE says the company will rely more on bank borrowing, which pushes cash flow the wrong way for another year.[2][4]

Red-team case against our rejection

The honest counter is that a company growing revenue 20-fold will always burn cash first. The rating agency calls its finances comfortable. Q1 FY27 profit of ₹110 crore is about 70% of the three-year operating cash shortfall of ₹156 crore, although accounting profit neither repays that shortfall nor measures it.[2][6] If the half-year cash flow shows operating cash of ₹100 crore or more, a rejection at 10.6 times forecast profit will call for a fresh underwriting, not an automatic reversal. We accept that risk. A hard rule exists so that a strong story does not override three years of negative cash flow.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED. The rule that decides is the hard red line on three-year cash conversion. Kernex scores -1.40 against a floor of 0.25. The escape clause asks for working-capital or order-book turnaround evidence and sets no numeric bar. We found order-book evidence: ₹3,641 crore, about 8 times sales. We did not find evidence that cash is turning. Reading the clause as needing evidence that cash is turning (Reading A) is our own judgment. Under the looser Reading B the red line is waived, and the rejection still holds, because no archetype qualifies: the compounder standard, the turnaround test and the 2.5 reward floor all fail.[4][6] The level that would change the verdict is operating cash of about ₹183 crore in FY27. An earlier sign would be a half-year statement showing about ₹100 crore positive, with receivables falling from ₹316 crore.

Because the name is rejected, the levels below are for reference only. The position size is zero (TIER_0). The entry zone is ₹1,546 to ₹1,590, from the 20-day swing low to just above the close. The stop is ₹1,460.61. That is the 63-day base low of ₹1,545.70 less one average true range of ₹85.09. The stop sits 8.1% below the top of the entry zone. We are not placing a limit order. The support ladder does not apply, because Layer 1 comes first.
