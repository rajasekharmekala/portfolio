1. What the company does

KEI Industries makes the wires and cables that carry electricity. A cable is a bundle of metal conductors wrapped in insulation. Large cables link power plants, substations, factories and data centres. Small ones, called house wires, run inside walls. The firm began in 1968 as a partnership called Krishna Electrical Industries, listed on the stock exchanges in 1995, and now runs nine plants in Rajasthan, Gujarat and Dadra and Nagar Haveli with about 2,200 employees.[13][12] In the year to March 2026 it earned ₹11,748 crore of revenue, operating profit before other income of ₹1,229 crore, and net profit of ₹918 crore.[1][9] Its earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA), which is the profit from running the business before financing and accounting charges, were ₹1,388 crore, or 11.81% of revenue.[9]

The product mix shows how the money is made. Low-tension power cables, used in buildings and factories, brought in ₹5,063 crore, or 43% of FY26 revenue. House and winding wire brought in ₹3,900 crore (33%), high-tension cables for power distribution ₹1,632 crore (14%), and extra-high-voltage (EHV) cables for transmission grids ₹670 crore (6%).[9] Stainless steel wire added ₹212 crore, and a turnkey electrical contracting arm (engineering, procurement and construction, or EPC) added ₹311 crore.[9] Management is closing the EPC arm because it ties up too much cash; EPC was only 1.27% of sales in the first half of FY27.[4][6] KEI's current EHV plant makes cables up to 400 kilovolts, and a new tower at its Sanand plant is designed for the 500 kilovolt class.[12][11]

Three kinds of customers pay KEI. About 54% of FY26 sales went through dealers and distributors to electricians, builders and small businesses, a share that reached 58.8% in the September 2026 quarter.[9][4] About 28% went directly to institutions such as power utilities, renewable energy developers, railways and large factories, from a list of roughly 2,000 institutional customers.[13] The rest, about 15%, was exports of ₹1,833 crore to more than 60 countries, with established business in the Middle East, Australia and Africa and a growing one in the United States, which is about half of a ₹991 crore export order book.[11][6] No customer matters much on its own: the ten largest were 13% of FY26 sales.[12] KEI says it has more than 25,000 retailers and 2 lakh electricians in its network, and the second-largest power cable business in the country.[6]

The economics are simple to state and hard to master. Copper, aluminium and PVC are the main inputs, and in the September 2026 quarter the cost of goods was 78.2% of revenue.[4] When copper rises, KEI raises prices, so revenue rises without any extra rupee of profit per metre of cable. Copper was up 35% to 40% year on year in September 2026, by JM Financial's estimate, which is why revenue grew 38.5% in the September quarter while the company's own volume growth is not published.[16][4][6] CRISIL Ratings expects industry revenue to grow 28% to 30% in FY27 on volume growth of only about 10%.[24] The operating margin (the share of revenue left after operating costs) has stayed in a narrow band of 10.2% to 10.5% for five years, and the rating agency ICRA says it has stayed within 9% to 11% for a decade.[1][13] The business therefore makes money by growing volume, mixing in higher-margin products such as EHV cable and exports, and managing the cash tied up in metal. That last point decides this report, because the metal is bought first and paid for by customers months later.

The growth plan rests on capacity. KEI has spent ₹1,876 crore of an estimated ₹2,000 crore on a new plant at Sanand in Gujarat, whose first phase began production in December 2025, and which management says can sell ₹6,000 crore to ₹7,000 crore a year once it is full.[6][8][7] The second phase, with the EHV tower, is due by March 2027.[6] Management has also announced a ₹700 crore plant at Salarpur in Rajasthan,[11][7] and plans ₹600 crore to ₹700 crore of capital expenditure (money spent on plants and machinery) each year for three to four more years, and has told investors it targets revenue of ₹25,000 crore by FY30, against ₹11,748 crore in FY26.[11][7][1]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EHV (extra-high-voltage) cable — cable for the high-voltage grid that moves power between cities; it needs type tests and customer approvals, so fewer firms make it.
  • LT and HT cable — low-tension and high-tension cable; they carry electricity inside buildings and across local distribution networks.
  • Order book — signed orders not yet delivered; KEI's pending orders were about ₹5,185 crore at 30 September 2026.
  • QIP (qualified institutional placement) — a share sale to large institutions; KEI raised ₹2,000 crore this way in November 2024 to fund Sanand.
  • LC acceptance — a bank-backed supplier credit: the supplier is paid at a later date and the bank guarantees it. It sits in trade payables but behaves like short-term debt.
  • Natural hedge — matching a price risk with an offsetting position; KEI holds about three months of metal and about three months of institutional orders, so a copper price move works through both.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

KEI's strength is a real but narrow one: it is the number two in a fragmented market, and it earns its place in the hardest corner, high-voltage cable. The Hindu BusinessLine put KEI at 12% of the organised cable market in 2025, second to Polycab, which had nearly a fourth.[25] Polycab holds about 22% of domestic wires and cables, and organised makers as a group rose from about 67% of the market in FY22 to nearly 80% in FY26.[19][26] Revenue grew from ₹5,727 crore in FY22 to ₹11,748 crore in FY26, which is 19% a year over three years and 23% over five.[1] The main outside capital in those years was a ₹2,000 crore share sale to institutions in November 2024, which paid for most of the Sanand plant.[10]

The edge in EHV is approvals and time. A utility will not hang a 220 kilovolt cable on a new supplier without type tests and a track record, and KEI's presentation lists test bodies such as KEMA in the Netherlands and customers such as PGCIL, and its annual report calls it one of the few Indian firms able to make EHV cable beyond 220 kilovolts.[12][11] Domestic EHV sales to institutions rose 82% to ₹559 crore in FY26, and the quarter to September 2026 added ₹217 crore, up 26%.[9][4] Management says its EHV operating margin is close to 15%, against 10.5% for low-tension institutional cable and 11% for retail, and names Universal Cables and imports as its EHV rivals.[7] The CMD adds that new entrants face a long wait before they can make EHV cable.[18] The Sanand tower should add ₹1,300 crore of EHV capacity from March 2027, which would lift the mix if the orders are there.[7]

The second strength is distribution. Dealers and distributors now bring in 58.8% of sales, up from 54.1% a year earlier, and sales through that channel grew 50.5% in the September quarter.[4] The channel matters for two reasons. Retail prices are revised twice a month, so metal costs pass to buyers quickly;[6] and ICRA notes that dealer sales bring better margins and a shorter cash cycle than institutional sales.[13] KEI has 2,146 active dealers, more than 25,000 retailers and a brand supported by television and cricket sponsorship, and it says it prices 3% to 4% below peers in the trade market.[4][6][18] Management admits the dealer count is flat: an analyst on the October call put the year-earlier figure near 2,340, and the CFO said weak dealers are being dropped while stronger ones are added.[6]

The third strength is the balance sheet. ICRA affirmed AA+ (stable) on 23 June 2026, citing interest cover of 19.2 times and total outside liabilities to net worth of 0.34 times.[13] Borrowings were ₹253 crore against equity of about ₹6,665 crore at March 2026, and promoters own 35.00% with none of it pledged.[1][2] The qualification is the letters-of-credit habit: KEI buys metal on supplier credit backed by bank acceptances, which stood at ₹735 crore in March 2026, up from ₹246 crore a year earlier. Counting acceptances, channel finance and leases, ICRA puts total debt at ₹987 crore, so the true gearing is about 0.15 times, not 0.04.[9][13]

What holds the business back

The first weakness is that KEI makes less money per rupee of sales than the best in its group. In the September 2026 quarter its cables and wires segment earned ₹412.91 crore of profit before interest and tax on ₹3,668.52 crore of revenue, an 11.3% margin.[14] Polycab's wires and cables business earned 14.2%.[28] ICRA describes KEI's margin as range-bound for a decade because competition limits pricing power, especially in retail.[13] A fixed gap of about three points means KEI has less room to absorb a price war than the leader.

The second weakness is working capital, which is the cash tied up in stock and customer bills, net of supplier credit. KEI's cash conversion cycle was 102 days in FY26 and inventory days were 100, up from 79 in FY23.[1] In the half-year to September 2026, inventory rose by about ₹1,204 crore on the standalone balance sheet, from ₹2,401 crore to ₹3,604 crore, and operating cash flow was an outflow of ₹489 crore.[4][5] Management attributes ₹600 crore to ₹700 crore of the build to filling the new Sanand plant and the rest to higher copper prices, and says holdings return to about 90 days by March 2027.[6] That is a claim we test in Chapter 6.

The third weakness is execution timing. In May 2025 management said the first phase of Sanand would start by the end of Q1 FY26 and the whole project would finish by the end of FY26. The first phase started in December 2025, about six months late, and the EHV phase is now due in March 2027.[10][8][6] The delays were small against a ₹2,000 crore project, but they pushed the revenue ramp into FY27 and cable capacity utilisation fell to 77% in the first half of FY27 from 84% in FY26, with house wire at 64%.[6][12]

Competition: the Ultravolt question

On 3 September 2026 the Aditya Birla Group's UltraTech launched a wires and cables brand called Ultravolt with ₹1,800 crore of investment, saying it will be the second-largest wire maker by capacity at launch and aims to be in the top two within five years.[16] KEI's stock fell as much as 8.3% the next day, from ₹5,292.50 to a low of ₹4,855.75, and about 22% over September.[36][19] Nuvama warned of a margin impact of 150 to 250 basis points for the sector, up from its earlier 100 to 150, and Motilal Oswal thinks UltraTech could take 5% to 7% share by FY31.[17] ICRA had already flagged in June that two large backward-integrated groups, Adani and Birla, were entering.[13]

The evidence so far is mixed and early. KEI's domestic wires and cables sales grew 45.8% in the quarter to September, and dealer channel sales grew 50.5%, so no share loss is visible seven weeks after launch, and Polycab's own sales grew 53% in the same quarter.[4][27] The CMD says residential wires account for only about 7% of KEI's share of the house-wire market, and that KEI will not cut prices.[18] Jefferies expects Ultravolt to start with wires and low-tension cables, and says EHV and exports can offset retail pressure.[20] Against that, the quarter's EBITDA margin fell to 10.62% from 13.04% in June, and a 150 to 250 basis point margin hit on ₹14,000 crore of annual sales would remove ₹210 crore to ₹350 crore of profit before tax.[4][17] We cannot tell yet which reading is right, so the price in Chapter 5 allows for both.

Governance and tax risk

From 7 to 12 May 2026 the Income Tax Department searched KEI's registered office, plants and other premises, and the annual report says it also covered residences of a few employees.[13][11] No assessment order or demand had been received when the board signed the report on 3 August 2026, and management says it cannot yet quantify any effect.[11] ICRA kept the rating, and said it will monitor the matter.[13] The search is the single largest unknown in the file. A search does not mean wrongdoing, but its outcome cannot be judged from outside, and this one is open. Separately, KEI carries ₹130 crore of excise and GST demands under appeal, unchanged from a year earlier.[11]

The ownership picture is clean. Promoters hold 35.00% with 0.0% pledged for twelve straight quarters, foreign funds hold 26.14% and mutual funds 23.00%.[2] Receivable quality needs watching: FY26 cash flow includes a ₹34.7 crore credit-loss charge and ₹17.7 crore of bad debts written off, against an ₹11.6 crore release of impairment a year earlier.[11]

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A cable maker is judged on a handful of operating facts that sit upstream of profit. We picked five for KEI and scored each as PASS (1.0), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0), as the framework requires. The five are capacity and the Sanand ramp, order book cover, operating margin against guidance (the company's own published forecast), cash conversion, and channel mix. Each was chosen because it explains why the stock fell or why it might recover: capacity drives growth this year, the order book shows how much of next year is already sold, the margin is what the market reacted to on 22 October, cash conversion is where the business is weakest, and channel mix decides margin and working capital over several years.

The score is 2.5 out of 5.0 with one FAIL. The framework needs at least 3.0, so KEI does not qualify and the Layer 2 result is FAIL. We apply the same two-period test to every KPI: where a KPI has a quarter reading and a half-year reading, PASS needs both to pass. We say this plainly because a looser reading of KPI 5 would have given exactly 3.0, and the choice of period would then have decided the verdict. The five scores describe a business that is growing well but is not yet turning that growth into cash.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management at KEI describes its own habit as 'say less and achieve more', and the record supports that on money numbers. In May 2025 management guided FY26 revenue growth of 17% to 18% and an EBITDA margin of 10.5% to 11%; the company delivered 20.66% and 11.81%.[10][23][8] The CFO has resisted pressure to guide higher this year, saying capital, not demand, limits growth.[7] A guide that is beaten is easy to trust, but only for the period it covered.

The record is weaker on construction and on side targets. Sanand phase one arrived about six months after the date given in May 2025, the EHV phase has moved from March 2026 to March 2027, and the FY27 export target slipped from about 20% of sales in May 2026 to 17% to 18% in August, against an H1 FY27 share of about 11%.[10][6][8][7][4] Management also stopped publishing the house-wire and cable split in August 2026 because, in the CFO's words, a competitor was using the numbers, which makes volume harder to check.[7] The table sets each promise beside what happened.

Third, cash. The market is arguing about quarterly margin, but the half-year cash flow statement shows an outflow of ₹489 crore and an inventory build of ₹1,204 crore, and nobody on the October call gave a number for the March 2027 cash flow.[5][6] If management is right that stock will fall back to about 90 days by March 2027, our view improves quickly; if not, it weakens quickly. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could break. This chapter names that assumption for each estimate, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when we will know. It then gives the strongest honest case against owning the stock, which we call the red-team bear case, and names the single claim on which the whole decision hangs.

Estimate 1 (EPS of ₹127): the profit margin holds

The weakest assumption is a profit margin of 7.4% to 7.5% for the next four quarters. The last five quarters ranged from 6.95% to 8.61%, so a swing of one point is ordinary, and a new competitor with ₹1,800 crore of capacity and a record of aggressive launches makes a downward swing more likely than an upward one.[4][1][16] Evidence that the assumption is wrong would be an operating margin below 10% in the quarter to December 2026, or dealer incentives rising in KEI's disclosures. We will know when KEI reports Q3 FY27, expected late January or early February 2027; the date is not yet announced. One weak quarter alone is not proof, because the last five quarters swung by 1.7 points. Two quarters in a row below 6.9%, or a stated cut in the full-year margin guide, would be.

Estimate 2 (35 times earnings): the market keeps paying today's multiple

The weakest assumption is that investors still pay 35 times trailing profit in October 2027. The multiple has already fallen from 52.8 times (the three-year median) to 35.1 times, and it can fall further if Ultravolt gains share or if Polycab's margin keeps contracting; Polycab's wires and cables margin fell 140 basis points on the year in the September quarter.[3][28] The evidence would be a peer de-rating below about 30 times, or KEI's price-to-earnings ratio closing below its three-year low of 33.8 times. We will know through the next four quarterly results of KEI and its peers. A lower multiple would not be a surprise on its own, because the three-year series moves between 33.8 and 74.5 times, so 35 times is already near the bottom. What would be a surprise is a multiple below 33.8 times with earnings still growing, because that would mean the market sees something worse than competition.

Estimate 3 (cash conversion): inventory comes down by March 2027

Management says holdings return to about 90 days by March 2027 and that the Sanand build explains ₹600 crore to ₹700 crore of the ₹1,204 crore rise in inventory.[6] The weak spot is the rest: about ₹500 crore to ₹600 crore is copper-price revaluation on existing plants, and that part reverses only if copper falls, which would also lower revenue. The test is the inventory figure in the Q3 FY27 presentation and the cash flow statement for FY27, which KEI files with results in early May 2027, read against the bridge in Chapter 3.[4][5] A partial result would still matter. If inventory falls to about ₹3,000 crore but cash from operations stays weak, the problem is receivables or supplier credit, not metal. If inventory falls but acceptances rise again, KEI would be swapping one source of finance for another, which we would score as a failure.

Estimate 4 (probability-weighted value) and the entry zone
