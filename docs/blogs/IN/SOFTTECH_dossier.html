Chapter 0

SoftTech Engineers builds the software that Indian city governments use to check and approve building plans. An architect uploads a drawing, a rule engine tests it against the local building bye-laws, and the city issues the permit and collects the fee on one system. The same company now sells digital twins to ports (its first large order is ₹92.95 crore from Jawaharlal Nehru Port Authority) and runs a transferable-development-rights exchange for Mumbai. Its rule engines serve more than 1,500 cities in 18 states, FY2026 revenue was ₹132.9 crore, up 39.5%, and the business is run from Pune by its founder, Vijay Gupta [15][16][21][12].

Our new-capital verdict is REJECTED. At ₹425.90, the last TradingView print on 13 November 2026, and ₹420.95, the last verified close on 12 November, you would pay 119 times earnings for a company whose group profit after tax was ₹5.33 crore in FY2026 and whose free cash flow was negative in every year from FY2019 to FY2026 [27][37][41][42]. The business verdict is CYCLICAL_CONDITIONAL with KPI health PASS_WITH_WATCH: real products and fast growth, but no yet-proven conversion of growth into cash.

The price, not the business, is the reason for the rejection. A reverse discounted-cash-flow model says the price needs revenue to compound at 29-35% a year for five years at a 28-32% EBITDA margin, and even management's own plan of ₹300 crore of revenue in about four years is worth ₹222 to ₹408 a share under different assumptions, all below today's price [16]. My probability-weighted FY2028E value is ₹369, which is 13.5% below the price, built from a bear value of ₹188 (30%), a base value of ₹299 (50%) and a bull value of ₹813 (20%). The pipeline's reference support band of ₹373-₹384 does not rescue the call: the base value is below it, so reward-to-risk from that band is negative. The price at which the base case alone pays 1.5 to 1 is about ₹218-₹242, 43% to 49% below the market.

Here is the debate in brief. The bull case rests on a product that is hard to remove once cities depend on it, pay-per-use revenue up 59% on the audited base (38.6% on management's own base), orders in hand of ₹302 crore, a ₹626 crore pipeline and a first half in which standalone EBITDA margin reached 32.5% [21][1]. The bear case rests on eight straight years of negative free cash flow, a credit-loss charge of ₹12.49 crore that exceeded group pre-tax profit, roughly 40% of gross trade receivables older than a year, a return on invested capital near 4%, a moat that Telangana has already breached, and a stock that trades about ₹20 lakh a day with no institutional owner [27][22][37][42]. I give the bear side more weight because its evidence is audited and the bull side's is guidance.

Long term: NOT PROVEN, because return on capital is about a third of the cost of capital and the evidence of repeat large orders is one port.

Chapter 1

Start with the date on the incorporation certificate. SoftTech Engineers was incorporated on 17 June 1996 at Ahmedabad, with its first registered office in Surat. A shareholder vote in July 2000 and a Company Law Board order in October 2001 moved the registered office to Pune, where it has stayed [2]. Vijay Gupta and Shyam Bhandari signed the original memorandum [2]. Mr Gupta is an M.Tech from IIT Mumbai who worked for multinational companies before founding the firm, and thirty years later he is still chairman, managing director and chief executive [3][1]. That continuity is a strength and a key-person risk. Hold both thoughts as we go.

The first product had nothing to do with municipal governments. STRUDS, released in 1996, helps a structural engineer analyse and detail a concrete building: you give it loads and geometry and it works out the steel. By 1997 more than 100 Indian customers used it, and in 2003 SIDBI's National Venture Fund became the first outside investor [2]. Mr Gupta told investors in June 2026 that STRUDS competed with Bentley's STAAD, held about 29-30% of the Indian market, and was sold to CSC (UK) in 2011 [3]. The sale price is not disclosed in any filing I could find. What matters is the pattern: the founder built a product that won customers, sold it, and rebuilt the company around a different one.

The second act began as e-governance, which simply means a government office doing its work on software instead of paper. PWIMS, a works and procurement system for public works departments, went live at its first customer in 2004. AutoDCR followed; the prospectus dates its launch to 2005 while the FY2026 annual report says 2006 [2][1]. DCR stands for development control regulations, the bye-laws that tell a city how tall, how close to the road and how dense a building may be. AutoDCR reads an architect's CAD drawing, measures it against those rules and lists the violations without a clerk. On 3 November 2007 the company won the Pune Municipal Corporation contract to maintain AutoDCR and its permission workflow. In 2013 Madhya Pradesh's urban administration directorate awarded it automated building permissions for 14 municipal corporations, running June 2013 to December 2018, and Indore Municipal Corporation won a national e-governance gold award for the system in 2014 [2]. Opticon, an ERP (one program that runs a firm's tenders, purchasing and accounts) for construction contractors, followed in 2010 [2].

By the time the company filed its prospectus the mix was lopsided. FY2017 revenue was ₹46.72 crore: AutoDCR ₹27.78 crore (59%), PWIMS ₹9.38 crore (20%), Opticon ₹0.63 crore and everything else ₹8.94 crore. The top five customers were 45.6% of revenue and the top ten 63.3% [2]. Underneath sat a reseller line: as an Autodesk value-added reseller the company booked ₹6.44 crore of Autodesk channel revenue in FY2017, about 14% of the year, because most AutoDCR cities run AutoCAD and each deployment pulls licences through [2]. The prospectus itself names the weakness: the business was 'largely dependent on government initiatives' [2].

The company's rhythm changes in 2018: before it, one engineer building tools and winning tenders; after it, a new platform, overseas entity or capital raise nearly every year.

Read that timeline for its rhythm. From 1996 to 2011 you see one engineer building tools. From 2004 to 2013 you see the pivot to government software, won tender by tender. From 2014 to 2018 you see outside capital and a listing. After 2018 nearly every row is a new Civit platform, an overseas entity or a capital raise, so the company has changed shape more in the last eight years than in its first twenty.

The stock-market chapter needs correcting, because several summaries get it wrong. The company went public on the NSE Emerge platform, the exchange's small-company segment, with trading from 11 May 2018. The offer was 28,51,200 shares, made up of 23,71,200 new shares and 4,80,000 sold by the Rajasthan venture fund, together about ₹22.8 crore, and the share count after it was 94,22,476 [6]. Before the offer the founder held 26.73% and the venture fund 26.77% [2]. That was not a main-board listing. The company moved to the NSE main board and listed directly on BSE with effect from 25 February 2022 [7]. The FY2026 annual report's milestone graphic puts 'listed on NSE and BSE' beside 2018, which squeezes two events four years apart into one [1][7]. The last dividend the company paid was the FY2019 final dividend of ₹0.50 a share; the board has recommended none since [1].

The FY2026 annual report divides the thirty years into three eras. The first decade, 1996 to 2006, built domain depth. The second, 2006 to 2018, was automation: AutoDCR in 2006 and BIMDCR in 2017, which extends the same scrutiny from flat 2D drawings to BIM, a 3D digital model of a building that carries its dimensions and materials. The third, from 2018, is 'products to platforms' [1]. In practice that meant folding separately sold products into the Civit Suite on a shared core, embedding AutoDCR's rule checker inside Autodesk's AutoCAD as RuleBuddy PlanAssist, relaunching Opticon as the SaaS product CivitBUILD (SaaS means customers rent hosted software instead of buying a licence), and starting a BIM and GIS services unit [1][4]. Management also says the commercial model moved from licences toward pay-per-use charges, a shift Chapter 2 tests against the audited numbers [1].

From 2018 the company also began to build a foreign footprint. Its prospectus declared no subsidiary and a plan to enter the Middle East, Africa, South-East Asia, the US and Europe by FY2019-20 [2]. The record since then is below.

Score that against the 2018 plan. Eight years on, revenue from outside India was ₹2.13 crore in FY2026, 1.6% of group revenue, down from ₹2.69 crore (2.8%) in FY2025 [1]. Germany, the stated flagship, has no revenue, and Mr Gupta himself says it needs 'another six months' before commercial traction [3]. Meanwhile the parent-only profit has exceeded group profit every year since FY2020: the gap was ₹1.22 crore, ₹1.21 crore, ₹1.13 crore, ₹2.18 crore, ₹3.65 crore, ₹2.81 crore and ₹4.24 crore across FY2020 to FY2026, ₹16.4 crore in all [4][8][5][1]. Not all of that gap is overseas, since it also reflects other subsidiary costs and inter-company items, but it shows what the group has paid for options that have not yet earned.

Who else runs the company? Priti Gupta has been a whole-time director since 4 March 2008, and Pratik Patel has been one since 18 July 2020; he played a key role in setting up the UK subsidiary [1][5]. The finance chair has turned over: CFO Kamal Agarwal resigned effective 16 September 2025 and Deepak Bang was appointed the next day [1]. The company secretary post has also changed hands, with Shalaka Khandelwal resigning in March 2026 and Aadishri Apte appointed in May 2026, and the November 2026 results presentation is signed by a third name, Nihal Gupta [1][10]. Four independent directors sit on a seven-person board [1]. None of this is alarming alone, but a company with a long-serving founder and fast-changing finance and compliance officers deserves a closer look at its controls, on which the auditor's opinion is unmodified [1].

Now the ownership table, which has been rewritten and deserves slow reading. Before the IPO the founder held 26.73% [2]. In FY2021 the company raised a 'strategic investment' from RIB, the German construction-software group [4]. Mr Gupta later described it as convertible debentures from RIB's chairman, Thomas Wolf, which the company chose to repay rather than convert, adding that it was 'purely financial' [3]. Preferential issues followed. October 2021 brought ₹10 crore of equity; October 2022 brought ₹8.29 crore of compulsorily convertible debentures (loans that must turn into shares) and ₹25 crore of share warrants (rights to subscribe for shares later, paid in instalments), all converted by 2024 [1]. Paid-up equity capital grew from about ₹9.4 crore after the IPO to ₹12.82 crore by March 2024 [6][1].

On 10 June 2024 Mr Gupta, Udyat Indian Ventures LLP and Covisible Solutions India Pvt Ltd agreed to sell 25,00,000 shares, 19.50% of the company, to Einstein Work Pte Ltd [5]. Management told the 2024 AGM the buyer was 'strategic' and not part of the promoter group [9]. Mr Gupta ties the investment to Thomas Wolf's family fund [3]. On 23 December 2024 the company issued 9,87,998 new shares to Einstein and Florintree Technologies LLP, raising ₹40.01 crore [5][1]. Promoter-group holding was 32.05% in December 2023, 20.35% in June 2024 and 18.89% from December 2024, the latest step reflecting dilution by the new issue (rounded data feed). At 31 March 2026 the register showed Einstein 23.41%, Mr Gupta 17.60%, Elimath Advisors 16.23% (which took over Florintree's 16.27% holding) and East India Udyog 7.05% [1]. The annual report discloses no pledge of promoter shares, though ₹6.22 crore of company bank deposits are pledged against facilities [1]. The founder family also lends to the company: ₹3.02 crore was outstanding from Mr and Mrs Gupta at March 2026, and Mr Gupta receives a ₹62.22 lakh annual non-compete fee [1].

Now the revenue and profit cycle. FY2019 revenue was about ₹63 crore (rounded data feed) and FY2020 was ₹57.95 crore. FY2021 then fell 28% to ₹41.57 crore [4]. The company's own explanation is specific: lockdowns delayed order confirmations and milestone approvals; government departments were busy managing the pandemic; the transaction-based revenue stopped because nobody filed building-permit applications; and receivables and unbilled revenue piled up [4]. Note what that tells you. The pay-per-use model that management now prefers is exposed to construction volumes in a way a one-time licence is not. Recovery was quick: ₹57.46 crore in FY2022 (+38%), ₹65.40 crore in FY2023, ₹78.78 crore in FY2024, ₹95.25 crore in FY2025 and ₹132.90 crore in FY2026 (+39.5%) [8][5][1].

Software built and capitalised in earlier years is now being charged against profit, and that charge absorbed most of the operating gain. FY2025 was the trough: group profit of ₹1.33 crore, with management attributing about three points of margin to building the time value of money into its expected-credit-loss provisions [5]. FY2026 swung back with revenue up 39.5% and group profit of ₹5.33 crore, even after a ₹12.49 crore provision for expected credit losses on receivables and unbilled revenue, against ₹2.79 crore a year earlier [1]. Management's own licence-versus-recurring split shows licensing up 69% in FY2026, and the fourth quarter alone delivered ₹46.6 crore, about 35% of the year [3].

The first half of FY2027 shows the same pattern in miniature. Consolidated H1 revenue was ₹64.82 crore (+20.4%), but profit after tax was ₹1.37 crore against ₹1.44 crore a year earlier, which management ties to a deferred Middle East order and German go-to-market costs. The order book stood at ₹302.35 crore after the ₹92.95 crore JNPA win [10][12]. So the company enters its second half-decade as a profitable, growing, thinly earning business that is still reinvesting nearly everything it makes.

What do I take from this history? Three things, and one doubt. The strength is real: a thirty-year founder, a product in daily use by municipal engineers, 1,500-plus cities in 18 states, and a recovery from a 28% revenue fall inside two years [1][4]. The second is that the model has changed twice, from licences to AMC-heavy recurring revenue by FY2021 (recurring was 57% of FY2021 revenue and one-time 31%) and back toward one-time licences in FY2026 [4][1]. The third is that capital has arrived in pulses, an IPO, three preferential rounds and a promoter sale, each paired with a growth story. The doubt is the one a mentor should voice: after eight years of overseas ambition, the foreign subsidiaries have cost more than they have earned, and group profit is still a fraction of parent profit.

Chapter 2

Here is the first thing to know about SoftTech's segments: the company reports none. Under accounting standard Ind AS 108 it declares a single business segment, 'design, development, installation and servicing of information technology related resource', so there is no segment profit, no segment assets and no segment margin to analyse [1]. What the auditors do sign off is a three-line split of revenue by commercial model, a split between India and the rest of the world, and a count of customers above 10% of revenue. Everything finer, the Civit product shares and the licence-versus-maintenance split, comes from management, not from the audited notes. I will keep the two apart so you always know which kind of number you are holding.

The audited split for FY2026, consolidated, runs as follows. One-time licence model: ₹97.92 crore, or 73.7% of revenue. Pay-per-use and SaaS: ₹31.72 crore, 23.9%. BIM and GIS services: ₹3.26 crore, 2.5%. By nature of sale, ₹110.67 crore is services and ₹22.23 crore is products, and because the only goods cost in the accounts is purchases of 'traded software', I read the products line as resold third-party software [1]. India supplied ₹130.78 crore (98.4%) and the rest of the world ₹2.13 crore (1.6%) [1]. Two customers each exceeded 10% of revenue and together paid ₹26.12 crore, 19.7% of the total, while in FY2025 none did; the filing does not name them [1].

Three things in that table ought to bother you. First, the base year does not match. Management says pay-per-use and SaaS revenue grew 38.6% from ₹22.89 crore in FY2025, but the audited note shows FY2025 at ₹19.94 crore, which makes growth 59%; both cannot describe the same ledger, and the filings do not say whether a reclassification explains it. The BIM and GIS line has the same problem: management says 18.6% growth, the audited standalone note implies 26.7% and the consolidated note 57% [1]. Second, annual maintenance appears to have fallen from about ₹19.6 crore to about ₹11.5 crore, while the report says recurring revenue was 'broadly stable' at about ₹42 crore; that holds only if pay-per-use gained exactly what maintenance lost, and the report never says maintenance shrank by about 40% [1]. Third, the company has described its mix three different ways in three annual reports: in FY2021, one-time 31%, recurring 57%, other 12% [4]; in FY2025, licensing 35%, pay-per-use and SaaS 25%, maintenance 18%, implementation 22% [5]; and in FY2026, FY2025 restated as licensing 52%, SaaS 25%, maintenance 21% [1]. My conclusion: judge the business on the audited totals and treat every mix claim as colour.

By product, the CFO told the June 2026 call that CivitPERMIT was about 50% of FY2026 revenue and CivitINFRA 25%, with RuleBuddy, CivitBUILD and BIM and GIS services making up 15-20% [3]. The H1 FY2027 presentation shows the mix moving: Civit PLAN and PERMIT 52.1%, Civit INFRA 11.2%, CivitBUILD 2.8%, e-TDR 3.2% and services and others 30.8%, against 44.6%, 29.0%, 0.4%, nil and 25.9% a year earlier [10]. Infrastructure fell from 29% to 11% of the half's revenue. The presentation does not say why; lumpy licence recognition on large orders is my guess, not a disclosed fact. Note that the slide's product totals, ₹67.06 crore for H1 and ₹34.54 crore for Q2, do not match reported revenue of ₹64.92 crore and ₹32.70 crore, so treat the percentages as indicative [10].

1. CivitPERMIT, the AutoDCR lineage: building permits, about half of revenue. Take Mumbai. The Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai ran building approvals across 26 interlinked departments for a city of about 20 million people. The company's case study says approvals took 90 to 99 days (the same annual report quotes both figures), depended on subjective rule interpretation and leaked revenue [5]. SoftTech built a single-window system: the architect submits a CAD drawing, the engine checks every object against the codified rules, a report lists deviations, other departments give their no-objection certificates in one workflow, and the fee is computed on screen. The case study reports approval time falling to about 5-9 days [5]. Pune Municipal Corporation signed a maintenance contract for AutoDCR on 3 November 2007 [2]. In FY2026 the same product went to the Housing and Urban Development Department of Jammu and Kashmir: AutoCAD drawings checked automatically, GIS master-plan validation, and digital change-of-land-use processing, inaugurated by the Chief Secretary [1]. The CEO also cited a new Gujarat mandate [3].

How does a permit system get built and billed? The scarce skill is not reading a CAD file. It is translating a city's bye-laws (setbacks, floor-space index, parking, fire norms) into rules a machine can evaluate, then getting the authority to accept that interpretation. The annual report says 'a building regulation is not simply a software specification' [1]. Each new jurisdiction needs rule codification, workflow configuration, GIS and payment integration, data migration and training, which is why implementation is the biggest team: 315 of 582 staff [1]. Pricing has two shapes. The old shape is a one-time licence and implementation fee billed in milestones, followed by an operations and maintenance contract of three to five years or more [4]. The new shape, which the CEO says started about three years ago, is: 'we don't give software to the organizations like governments and all. We put it onto a cloud', SoftTech funds the customisation, and 'we charge from the customer based on the permits issued through the application on the square feet basis', collected from the architect or owner directly or through the state treasury [3]. The rate is set in each tender and is not disclosed. In the new shape SoftTech carries the upfront cost, which is one reason capitalised development spending is so large (Chapter 3).

2. CivitINFRA, the PWIMS lineage: works and project monitoring, about a quarter of FY2026 revenue. A works department or airport authority runs hundreds of construction packages at once, and without a system progress reaches head office as periodic paper reports. The Airports Authority of India awarded SoftTech a ₹17.16 crore contract for a BIM-based Project Monitoring System: S-curves (cumulative progress plotted against plan) and Gantt charts for each package, 3D model views, risk alerts on a portfolio dashboard and a mobile app for site engineers [1][11]. The CFO said about 40% of the order was recognised in FY2026 and the rest follows over roughly three years; the CEO called it a one-time licence with enhancements to come [3]. A second example is MIDC, Maharashtra's industrial development corporation, whose 18,500-plus projects ran on manual estimates and slow schedule-of-rates revisions; the company's case study reports 466 locations, 4,000 users and 80% cost savings, a claim not audited [5]. Envee, acquired in November 2024, has worked on MIDC for 25-plus years and earned ₹4.25 crore in FY2026 [5][1]. The newest and largest order is JNPA's ₹92.95 crore digital twin for India's biggest container port, with core functions due by February 2027 and full operation by February 2028 [12]. That one order equals about 70% of FY2026 group revenue, so slippage in delivery would show up quickly in receivables.

3. CivitTDRx, the e-TDR exchange: a transaction fee on property rights. When Mumbai takes a landowner's plot for a road, it pays in a certificate of development rights (TDR) that the owner can sell or use to build more floor space elsewhere. On paper that market was opaque. The Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation, under the Maharashtra Urban Development Department, appointed SoftTech as technology partner for an online exchange serving 18,000-plus registered developers and TDR owners, where buyers and sellers sign digital contracts and the platform provider earns a fee per transaction [14]. The CEO put it at 0.5% of each transaction, mandatory from 10 May 2026, with about ₹8,000 crore of TDR trades a year in the BMC area, which an analyst turned into ₹40 crore of revenue and the CEO accepted as the theoretical figure while cautioning that not all would arrive in FY2027; he put margins at 70-80% [3]. The first evidence is modest. The H1 FY2027 slide gives e-TDR 3.2% of the half, roughly ₹2.1 crore, and 5.3% of Q2, about ₹1.8 crore [10]. At 0.5%, Q2 implies only about ₹370 crore of trades, against a history of about ₹2,000 crore a quarter (my derivation), so volume has to rebuild before the 'theoretical' figure means anything.

4. CivitBUILD, the Opticon lineage: construction ERP for contractors. Picture an estimator at an industrial contractor with a tender due Friday: quantities in one spreadsheet, supplier rates in another, site progress in a third. CivitBUILD puts tender, project, material, finance, logistics, labour, sales and production management in one cloud system. SCON Projects, a pan-India industrial construction and EPC (engineering, procurement, construction) company with 18-plus years of work for clients including Tata Chemicals, KSB Pumps and Indocount, chose it in May 2026; the company claims up to 40% faster delivery and 20% lower cost, which is marketing, not an audited result [13]. The price and billing terms are not disclosed. The revenue evidence is thin: Opticon earned ₹0.30 crore in FY2021 against ₹5.24 crore in FY2020, and CivitBUILD was 2.8% of H1 FY2027 revenue [4][10]. Overseas, the CEO says an Oman tender was lost on price, where SoftTech was second-lowest ('L2') while top on technology, and the H1 FY2027 results cite a Middle East order deferred by the customer [3][10].

5. Services and new platforms. BIM and GIS services (3D compliance modelling, clash detection, spatial validation) earned ₹3.26 crore, and management says they often lead to a platform sale [1]. The US subsidiary's only revenue was ₹6.25 lakh of BIM services [1]. The newer platforms have first orders rather than revenue streams: CivitSUSTAIN for Mitsubishi Electric's central water management centre, which the CEO says serves a Delhi Jal Board project through L&T's water network; CivitMETAVERSE for Metawolf; CivitTWIN, which BMC has issued a letter of intent to deploy; and eBGAI, a localised CivitPERMIT and CivitTWIN for the German market [1][3]. RuleBuddy PlanAssist embeds the rule checker inside AutoCAD for architects and small practices [1]. None of these has disclosed revenue, so do not value them as if they were mature.

6. Resold software. About ₹22.23 crore of revenue, 16.7% of the group, is third-party software sold on, and the only goods cost in the profit statement, ₹20.87 crore of traded-software purchases less a ₹1.66 crore rise in stock, implies a gross margin near 13.6% [1]. This lineage goes back to the Autodesk reseller arrangement that earned ₹6.44 crore in FY2017 [2]. It adds revenue and receivables but little profit, which is why growth in 'sales' flatters the business.

Chapter 3

SoftTech does not sell the way a consumer software firm does. There is no app store, no free trial and no sales army. It sells by tender. A housing department or port authority publishes a request for proposal (RFP), bidders submit technical and price bids, and an evaluation committee decides. The CEO describes the discipline this way: 'we get engaged with the customer before RFP is published', explaining the value, and the company works 'on specific opportunities only rather than going with all different types of RFPs' [3]. He puts the win rate at about 70%, with most losses on cost [3]. The business development team is 25-30 people out of roughly 550 [3]. In Oman the company ranked first on technology but second on price, and the customer asked it to negotiate further [3]. So a tender win is part product, part price, part pre-selling, and it is never guaranteed.

The second route is partners. SoftTech is an Autodesk developer-network member and value-added reseller, and it earned ₹6.44 crore from Autodesk's channel in FY2017 [2]. It signed a sales partnership with CGI, a large government-technology integrator in the US and Canada, and with CDCi in Malaysia [9]. The CEO says its CivitSUSTAIN order from Mitsubishi Electric rides on controllers that Mitsubishi supplies to L&T for city water networks [3]. For Germany, management leans on the network of its investor Thomas Wolf, whose RIB group sold construction software there for thirty years [3]. The annual report describes the plan as relying on 'partner-led go-to-market models rather than on the direct establishment of large overseas sales organisations', a capital-light stance that also means SoftTech does not control the customer relationship [1].

Almost every route ends at a government buyer; only the applicant-pays routes spare SoftTech a government invoice.

From award to cash the cycle has five steps. First, the contract. Second, requirements and rule codification, where SoftTech reads the authority's regulations and turns them into logic. Third, configuration, data migration and integration with GIS, payment gateways and other departments' systems. Fourth, user acceptance and go-live. Fifth, operations and maintenance, or pay-per-use billing. How long does it take? The Madhya Pradesh permit contract for 14 corporations ran from June 2013 to December 2018 [2]. At the 2024 AGM the then CFO, Kamal Agarwal, said the ₹287 crore order book would convert over about three years on average, while the CEO put the window at three to five years [9]. In FY2026 about 40% of the AAI order was recognised in the first year and the rest follows over roughly three years [3]. JNPA expects core functions by February 2027 and full operation by February 2028, about 17 months from award [12].

The order book needs care. It was ₹287 crore in September 2024 [9], ₹231.99 crore after FY2026 [1], and ₹302.35 crore at 6 November 2026 [10]. Strip out the ₹92.95 crore JNPA order and the November figure is about ₹209 crore, below September 2024 even though revenue has grown some 70% since FY2024 (my arithmetic on [9][10][12]). The pipeline of qualified bids was ₹436.23 crore in June and ₹625.57 crore in November; management says it converts at 'more than about 60%' [3][10]. The accounts offer a harder number: unsatisfied performance obligations of ₹114.64 crore at March 2026, up 40% from ₹82.01 crore, which is roughly half the stated order book; the filings do not reconcile the two measures [1].

Now billing. The accounts say 'the billing schedules agreed with customers include periodic performance based payments and / or milestone based progress payments', with invoices payable 'within contractually agreed credit period' [1]. Fixed-price work is recognised over time using an output measure (plans approved, transactions processed, software phase completed); licences are recognised on delivery; maintenance is spread evenly [1]. The gap between revenue recognised and invoices raised is parked on the balance sheet as a contract asset, or unbilled revenue. In FY2026 SoftTech recognised ₹74.11 crore of revenue on such contracts and billed ₹63.05 crore, so about ₹11 crore of earned revenue is still waiting to be invoiced [1]. The auditor made revenue recognition on fixed-price contracts a key audit matter because the choice of milestone and method needs judgement [1].

The other cash drain is capitalised development. Under accounting rules a software company may treat engineering cost for a new product as an asset, not an expense, once the product is technically and commercially feasible, then write it off over its useful life. SoftTech paid ₹30.50 crore for intangible development in FY2026, 22.9% of revenue, and ₹34.47 crore in FY2025, 36.2% of revenue [1]. It amortised ₹20.44 crore in FY2026, with the CEO citing a three- to four-year life [1][3]. Intangible assets and those under development stand at ₹90.22 crore, 53% of total equity [1]. The auditor lists this as a key audit matter and tested payroll, vendor contracts and invoices on a sample basis [1]. Two readings are possible, and both are fair. One: this is investment in products that now earn transaction fees. Two: it is a way of moving operating cost from the profit statement to the balance sheet. Pay-per-use contracts make the second risk sharper, because SoftTech funds the customisation up front.

Chapter 4

Start by naming the market, because a moat only means something inside a defined castle. SoftTech supplies three different products to overlapping customers. The first is statutory permit software: a municipality's rule engine and workflow that reads an architect's CAD or BIM drawing, checks it against the city's building bye-laws (called development control regulations, or DCR) and routes the file to officers for sanction. The second is works and infrastructure monitoring for government departments, airports and ports. The third is private-sector construction software plus new transaction platforms such as Mumbai's digital exchange for transferable development rights (TDR, a tradable certificate given to landowners whose land the city acquires). The permit business is the core. Management said CivitPERMIT was 50% of FY2026 revenue and CivitINFRA 25% [16]. The company's February 2024 deck put AutoDCR, the permit product's earlier name, at ₹40.7 crore of ₹65.3 crore standalone FY2023 revenue, or 62% [18].

Now the number everyone repeats: roughly 80% share of Indian permit automation. It appears in the September 2018 investor deck (“~80% Market Share” and “Largest market share in India (80%) for building plan approval management system”) [17], in the 2021 AGM deck (“80%+ Authorities in India use CIVIT Permit”) [19] and in the February 2024 deck (“over 80% of market share in India in its domain”) [18]. None of them says who measured it, what the denominator is, or as of what date.

The company's own papers pull that number in different directions. The 2018 deck counted 3,700+ urban local bodies and called 80% of them “still untapped”, while 340+ were live [17]. The 2021 deck counted 600+ agencies [19]. The FY2026 annual report says 1,500+ cities against “substantially more than 4,000” statutory urban local bodies, so at most about 37% by head-count, and it does not repeat the 80% claim [15]. When an analyst put “80% market share in permitting” to the CEO on the 2 June 2026 call, he answered about TDR and neither confirmed nor corrected it; the only share he volunteered was “29-30%” for STRUDS, the structural-design product transferred to a UK company in 2011 [16]. My reading: 80% described the installed base of drawing-reading scrutiny engines among authorities that had already chosen automation by 2018. It is not a measured share of today's market, and I would not carry it forward.

The moat is real in how hard it is to rip out a working permit system, but it has been breached once and its second leg, the rule codification, is eroding.

The company's argument for stickiness is fair. Once a municipality's officers, architects and fee collections run through one system, replacing it means data migration, retraining and a period of double handling, and the annual report says so [15]. The localisation work is also real, because each city's bye-laws must be turned into machine-checkable rules. But two forces are shrinking that second advantage. States are consolidating rulebooks: Maharashtra's UDCPR, notified in December 2020, applies to all planning authorities except Greater Mumbai and a few others (The Hitavada), which means fewer rule sets to codify and a smaller head start for whoever codified the old ones. And the annual report itself concedes the AI risk: new entrants do not have the dataset, but “improvements in general-purpose reasoning reduce the value of holding one” [15]. Telangana's replacement was marketed on scrutiny speed and “advanced technologies” [22], which is exactly that threat.

Pricing power is where management was most candid. In a government tender, L1 means the lowest bidder and L2 the second lowest. On Oman, the CEO said: “From the pricing front, we are at L2 in the tender... But from the technology front, we were T1,” and the customer wanted a lower price after a Singapore bidder came in lower [16]. Asked about lost bids, he said: “it could be about 70% winning and 30%, sometimes losing because of cost or something. Mostly, we lose only if it is a cost parameter which comes in” [16]. Read plainly, product quality gets SoftTech onto the shortlist, price decides the award, and the company does not claim a premium. Its pricing power appears after the award, in per-permit fees and maintenance contracts.

The second structural weakness is the payer. The annual report calls government procurement and payment cycles the company's “most material commercial exposure” and quantifies it: days sales outstanding (the average days between billing and cash) of 260 [15]. The CFO explained one slice: “in government businesses, the government uphold some 1%, 2% of our invoice values, which they will process after certain period of time,” and the CEO added that retentions can take “sometimes even seven years” to come back [16]. This is not new. The May 2018 prospectus already reported that 37.22% of trade receivables were more than six months past due and that state and central government supplied 70.86% of revenue. Eight years later the problem is smaller, not solved.

Concentration compounds it. FY2026 was the first of the three years I checked (FY2024 to FY2026) in which a customer crossed 10% of revenue: two customers together contributed ₹26.1 crore, 19.7% of consolidated revenue [15][20]. The report counts 18 states, the same number as the February 2024 deck's “18+” [15][18]. My reading is that growth has come more from deeper wallet share inside known governments (Gujarat, the Airports Authority, the Mumbai TDR platform, the port authority) than from new states. That is less costly to win, but it ties results to a few counterparties and their payment calendars.

Situational Scaling & Moat Twin: Tyler Technologies, Inc.

United States, 1998–2024; software for local, state and federal government

Shared Mechanism: Tyler entered the software business in 1998 and has not paid a dividend since [24]. It supplies a broad set of mission-critical applications (permitting and civic services, ERP, property tax, courts, public safety) to government agencies and keeps them on maintenance or subscription contracts that renew automatically unless the client gives notice; after acquiring NIC Inc. in April 2021 it also supplies payment services into the same client base [24]. The moat is breadth across many departments of the same customer plus recurring revenue.

Historical Numbers & Outcome: Calendar 2024: revenues $2,137.8 million; subscriptions 62.8% and maintenance 21.7% of revenue (recurring about 84%, $1.8 billion); subscription revenue up from $350.6 million in 2020 to $1.3 billion in 2024, including $698.2 million of transaction-based fees; GAAP operating income 14.0% of revenue; accounts receivable $587.6 million, about 100 days of revenue (my arithmetic) [24].

Direct Implication for SOFTTECH: Government software compounds when the vendor owns many workflows of one customer and moves from one-off licences to renewing subscriptions and per-transaction fees. SoftTech's pay-per-use model is the same instinct at small scale: usage-linked revenue is about 24-25% of its sales against Tyler's 84% recurring base, and Tyler is roughly 140 times SoftTech's revenue at ₹88 per dollar (my assumption). Even Tyler's receivables run near 100 days; SoftTech's 260-day DSO says it has not yet earned that quality.

Historical Failure-Mode & Base-Rate Twin: Educomp Solutions Limited (India, FY2011–FY2017; technology and content supplied to government and private schools)

Shared Vulnerability: Educomp's EduReach business set up and ran IT labs in government schools on a build-own-operate-transfer model: it spent first and was paid quarterly by state governments over three to five years [25]. Management described the revenues as “predictable & locked in for three to five years” [25]. The risk is the one SoftTech names in its own annual report: governments pay when they pay, while the vendor carries the upfront investment and, here, the borrowing that funds it.

Historical Numbers & Base Rate: FY2012 consolidated (₹ million): income 14,912.8 versus 13,509.0 (+10.4%); trade receivables 8,650 versus 5,070 (+70.6%), equal to 58% of income or about 212 days (my arithmetic); net profit 1,355.4 versus 3,366.7 (−59.7%); finance charges 1,432.5 versus 1,089.9; cash and bank balances 838.4 versus 2,097.5 [25]. The company has been under insolvency resolution since 30 May 2017 [26].

How SOFTTECH Compares Today: A contract that is “locked in” guarantees work, not cash. For SoftTech the watch-list is receivables-plus-unbilled work growing faster than sales, the expected credit loss provision, and borrowing. The cushion is real: gross borrowings plus lease liabilities were about ₹39 crore against equity of about ₹170 crore at March 2026 [15], far from Educomp's leverage. The shared mechanism is slow public payment funding growth.

Put the two side by side and the lesson is about structure, not size. Tyler's moat is breadth across many departments of the same customer, and even there receivables run near 100 days of revenue [24]. Educomp's trouble started with receivables growing roughly seven times faster than sales, for customers that were governments [25][26]. SoftTech's pay-per-use model is the right instinct, because fees collected from applicants bypass the slow treasury cycle, but it is a quarter of revenue, not four-fifths. The leverage cushion is the difference from Educomp; the breadth and the recurring base are the gap from Tyler.

My judgement: SoftTech has a real but narrower moat than the 80% story implies. It is strongest where a state has embedded the product into its statutory workflow and lets the company charge applicants per permit. It is weakest where a state can rewrite the rules or procure something new on speed or price. The evidence supports a durable niche vendor with good products and ordinary pricing power, not a monopoly. The next test is whether a second Telangana happens.

Other expenses cost ₹36.5, a line that holds ₹9.4 of credit-loss provision (₹12.49 crore) and, in the parent, ₹22.24 crore of outsourced technical consultants, up from ₹13.96 crore [27]. What remains is EBITDA of ₹24.2, or ₹32.19 crore, which is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. Depreciation and amortisation take ₹17.2. Interest takes ₹3.1. Other income adds ₹2.7. Pre-tax profit is ₹6.7. The feed's waterfall tells the same story in rounded form [37].

The margin did not improve in eight years. EBITDA margin was 25% in FY2019 and 24% in FY2026 [37]. What changed is below that line. Depreciation and amortisation rose from ₹4 crore, about 6% of revenue, in FY2019 to ₹22.8 crore, 17%, in FY2026, so pre-tax margin fell from about 17% to 6.7% [37][27]. Interest is the smaller drag. Finance cost was ₹4.07 crore in FY2026: ₹3.18 crore on borrowings and ₹0.71 crore on lease liabilities in the parent [27]. EBITDA covers that 7.9 times. EBIT, which is EBITDA less depreciation and amortisation and before other income, covers it only 2.3 times.

Why does amortisation keep climbing? SoftTech writes its own software, the Civit suite for building permits, infrastructure and construction. Under Ind AS 38 it capitalises eligible development cost, meaning it records the spend as an asset instead of an expense, and amortises it straight-line over four years once the product is available for use. Until then the money sits in 'intangible assets under development' [27]. The auditor makes this a key audit matter 'due to the significant value of the development costs', and the notes concede that 'there is significant management judgement and estimate involved' in deciding which costs qualify [27]. In FY2026 ₹36.48 crore went into the under-development account, ₹33.05 crore moved into finished software and ₹20.43 crore of internally generated software was amortised [27]. Put those next to EBITDA and you see how much of 'operating profit' is really re-invested engineering.

So what is the earnings quality, in plain words? Reported profit is thin and soft: ₹5.33 crore on ₹132.90 crore of revenue in FY2026, after a ₹12.49 crore credit-loss charge, and a loss if development spend were expensed. The cash is real at the operating line, where EBITDA converts at 88% to 97% over five years, but every rupee of it and more goes back into building software, so free cash flow is negative year after year. Revenue turns into cash slowly (242 days) and, for a growing slice, not at all. The business is funded by new shareholders, not by customers or lenders. Returns, 4.1% on my arithmetic, are below the cost of money. What would change my mind is two or three consecutive years of positive free cash flow with days outstanding under 200, falling allowances and amortisation growing slower than revenue.

Chapter 7

Start with what you would actually pay. SoftTech last traded at ₹425.90 on 13 November 2026, up 1.18% on the day, after a verified close of ₹420.95 on 12 November, the last session whose benchmark close was confirmed when I built the ledger [41][42]. On 1,38,42,138 shares outstanding [27] that is an equity value of about ₹590 crore; TradingView shows ₹578 crore because it counts shares slightly differently [41]. Add ₹10.5 crore of net debt (borrowings of ₹32.12 crore plus leases of ₹6.43 crore, less ₹28.06 crore of cash and funds at 30 September 2026 [30]) and the enterprise value, the price of the whole operating business, is about ₹600 crore.

Now set that against what the business earns. Trailing twelve-month consolidated sales are about ₹144 crore and earnings per share ₹3.59, so the stock trades at 119 times earnings, 3.4 times a book value of ₹125 a share, and about 4.1 times sales [37][41]. On operating profit of ₹35 crore the multiple is about 17 times. That last number flatters the stock. Chapter 6 showed that SoftTech capitalised ₹36.5 crore of software development in FY2026, which is 113% of that year's ₹32.2 crore EBITDA [27]. If engineering is a cost of staying in business, as I think it is for a product company, cash earnings before tax were roughly zero. The 17 times is a multiple of a number that has not yet reached shareholders.

The screening tool that feeds this dossier labels the valuation “Reasonable / In-line”. I do not agree, and you should know why. That label compares the multiple with a growth-adjusted band and does not look at return on equity, which is 2.9% [41]. A company that earns 2.9% on shareholders' money and trades at 3.4 times book is asking you to pay for returns it has not yet produced. The multiple is a bet on the years after FY2028, not a description of today.

In the first half of FY2027 consolidated revenue grew 20.4% to ₹64.82 crore, and standalone revenue grew 26.1% to ₹64.92 crore, with standalone EBITDA of ₹21.07 crore, a 32.5% margin, and standalone profit after tax up 69.5% [21]. Orders in hand reached ₹302.35 crore at September 2026 against ₹232 crore at March, helped by a ₹92.95 crore digital-twin order from Jawaharlal Nehru Port Authority, and the sales pipeline stands at ₹625.57 crore [21][12][15]. Recurring revenue rose 46.2% in the half and is now 43.5% of sales, up from 37.5% [21]. Management guides to FY2027 growth of 25-27% and wants days sales outstanding below 200, down from 260 [16].

Four mechanisms could turn that into compounding. First, the product is hard to rip out once a city runs its building permits on it, and SoftTech rule engines serve more than 1,500 cities in 18 states [15]. Second, the pay-per-use model charges every plan as it is scrutinised, so revenue rises with construction activity rather than with licence sales; it was ₹31.72 crore in FY2026, up 59% on the audited note's base and 38.6% on management's own base [1][16]. Third, the Civit products are being re-used for ports, airports and industrial corridors, with JNPA as the first reference [21]. Fourth, the TDR exchange taps a market the company puts at about ₹8,000 crore of annual transfers in Mumbai, where a 0.5% fee per trade would be ₹40 crore a year if the exchange captured all of it; first-half revenue from it was about ₹2 crore [14][16][21]. If those four land together, operating leverage does the rest, and the bull case value of ₹813 is what a 55-times multiple on ₹14.78 of earnings looks like.

Now the other chair. Reported profit is thin. FY2026 consolidated profit after tax was ₹5.33 crore and earnings per share ₹3.57, less than half of the ₹7.87 earned in FY2019 [27][37]. Free cash flow was negative in every year from FY2019 to FY2026; in the five years to FY2026 it consumed ₹26 crore after development spending, and ₹83 crore of new equity filled the gap [27][31][32][33][34][37]. The credit-loss charge of ₹12.49 crore in FY2026 was 1.4 times pre-tax profit, and roughly 40% of gross trade receivables are more than a year old [27]. Return on invested capital was about 4% [27]. In Q2 FY2027 consolidated profit after tax was ₹0.21 crore, down 35.7%, against ₹3.63 crore at the parent, because losses in overseas subsidiaries and a 77% tax rate cut the group number [21][30].

The moat is narrower than the pitch. Telangana replaced SoftTech's permit system with a rival's within about four years [22], a free government platform, UPYOG, offers a building-plan module to any state [23], and the “80% share” claim that appears in earlier decks is not repeated in the FY2026 annual report [15]. One order, JNPA, equals about 70% of FY2026 revenue and 31% of the September order book; without it, orders in hand are about ₹209 crore, below the ₹232 crore at March [21][15]. Two customers made up 19.7% of FY2026 revenue, a first for the last three years [15]. The auditor's report records quarterly returns to banks that differed from the books by as much as ₹15.95 crore [27]. And the stock has no institutional owner and trades about ₹20 lakh a day [37][42].

Here is my own judgement. The bull evidence is about growth and it is good: one-year guidance has been met, orders have risen and the new lines are real products. The bear evidence is about conversion, whether growth becomes cash and profit for shareholders, and it is stronger because it rests on eight consecutive years of audited statements rather than on guidance. The pipeline's closest business label is CYCLICAL_CONDITIONAL. SoftTech's condition is not a cycle. It is proof that the money coming in from customers and shareholders ends up as cash a shareholder can keep. That proof does not yet exist, and a price of 119 times earnings does not allow time for it to arrive.
