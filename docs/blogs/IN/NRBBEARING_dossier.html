1. What the company does

Before the business itself, here is a short guide to the measuring terms. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a close cousin of operating profit. The operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales. ROCE, which stands for return on capital employed, is the yearly profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses. EPS means earnings per share, which is profit divided by the number of shares. Free cash flow, or FCF, is the cash left after running the business and buying machines. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills. Price-to-earnings, often written P/E, is the share price divided by a year's profit per share. Guidance is the company's own forecast for the coming period.

Price terms come next. The RSI, or relative strength index, is a gauge from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has moved. The ATR, or average true range, is the typical daily price swing in rupees. A base is a sideways stretch of trading that a price builds before a move. The pivot is the top of that base, and a breakout is a move above the pivot on strong volume. Stage 2 is a rising trend and Stage 4 is a falling one; Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase between them. An entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned, and a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better. Reward-to-risk is the gain from entry to target divided by the loss from entry to stop, and asymmetry is another word for the same ratio.

A bearing is a small steel part that sits between a spinning shaft and its housing so the two do not grind against each other. NRB Bearings has made them since 1965 and says its parts are in more than 90 percent of the vehicles on Indian roads. [32] [10] Its best-known products are needle roller bearings, which use thin steel rods like long pins, and cylindrical roller bearings, which use short steel cylinders. Crisil, the rating agency, puts its share of the Indian needle bearing market at 65 to 70 percent. [4]

Who pays for them? Mostly vehicle makers. About 65 to 70 percent of sales go to Indian car, truck, tractor and two-wheeler makers and the companies that supply them, 10 to 11 percent to the replacement market, and 20 to 25 percent to exports. [4] No single customer, counting the supplier chain above it, is more than 7 percent of sales. [4] The parts sit inside gearboxes, steering columns, axles and engines. Management names Mercedes-Benz, BMW, Daimler, Magna, ZF and Dana among its customers. [10] [8]

How does it make money? Each product is designed with the customer, and once a bearing is fitted to a vehicle platform it stays for the life of that platform, often five to ten years. Management calls the expected sales from awarded platforms "lifetime nominated business". It put the figure at ₹750 crore in November 2025 (₹600 crore abroad and ₹150 crore in India) and at ₹1,100 crore in August 2026. These are company figures that no one has audited. [10] [7] The company also owns 73.45 percent of SNL Bearings, a small listed bearing maker with revenue of ₹57 crore in FY26, and has overseas subsidiaries in Thailand (a plant), Germany, the United States and Dubai (the holding company). [33] [32]

Two events shaped the last four years. In May 2023 a fire at the Waluj plant burned a production line and stock. Management says it cost more than ₹90 crore of sales over about eighteen months, and insurers have paid most of the loss. [12] [5] In February 2025 the founding family settled a split of the old group: NRB paid ₹55 crore to NRB Industrial Bearings, the other family branch, and the promoters now hold 51.2 percent of the stock as of March 2026. [10] [18] The loan the promoters took to fund that settlement explains the share pledges and the stake sale described in chapters 2 and 3.

The new plan is to widen the business beyond cars. Management wants ₹2,500 to ₹3,000 crore of sales by FY31, up from ₹1,335 crore in FY26, by adding industrial bearings, aerospace parts through the Mahant Tool Room purchase, and a joint venture with the Italian firm Unitec. Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Needle roller bearing: a bearing that uses thin, long steel rods. It carries a heavy load in a very small space.
  • OEM (original equipment manufacturer): the vehicle maker that fits the part when the vehicle is built. Tier-1 is a supplier that sells complete systems, such as a steering unit, straight to an OEM.
  • Aftermarket: sales of spare parts after the vehicle is sold.
  • Pledge: shares a promoter has put up as security for a loan. If the share price falls, the lender can ask for more security or sell the shares.
  • Promoter: the founding family or group that controls the company.
  • Crore: ten million. ₹100 crore is ₹1 billion.
  • Lifetime nominated business: the sales management expects over the life of vehicle platforms it has won. It is not an order book and no one audits it.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Is this a strong business? The evidence says it is a well-placed niche supplier with real strengths and three real weaknesses. We lay out the strengths first, then the weaknesses, then what happened to the owners.

Strengths: share, design-in and low debt

The first strength is share. In India NRB holds an estimated 65 to 70 percent of needle bearings and, by its own count, over 30 percent by value and about 50 percent by volume of cylindrical roller bearings. [4] [10] In the products it leads, management says its share of a customer's purchases is 60 to 70 percent and sometimes 100 percent. [8] A customer who gives a supplier all of one part is not easy to move.

The second strength is that products are designed in. Bearings are "co-designed at the platform level" with the customer, according to management, and the company runs a design and test centre with more than 70 engineers. [8] [12] Crisil lists "strong technical expertise" and a customer base with no one above 7 percent of sales among its rating strengths. [4] The third strength is the balance sheet. Borrowings of ₹154 crore against equity of about ₹962 crore give a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.16 and interest cover of 25.3 times. [4] [3]

The market is also a good one right now. Indian vehicle production grew 11.8 percent in FY26 to about 34.7 million units, and the auto component industry grew 12.7 percent to ₹7.60 lakh crore. [14] [27] Passenger vehicle sales rose 22 percent in September 2026 and two-wheelers 13.5 percent, helped by lower GST rates from September 2025. [25] [14] The industry body ACMA expects 8 to 10 percent growth in FY27. [27] A supplier that tracks the industry gets a lift from this.

Weaknesses: working capital, imported steel and cyclicality

The first weakness is the cash tied up in stock. Crisil says inventory has run at 130 to 170 days for five years because the company holds many product types, delivers just in time, and imports 30 to 35 percent of its key steel from Europe and Japan on a 90-day voyage. [4] Management said in February 2026 that inventory had fallen to 110 days from 120 to 130 and that 90 to 100 days is the best it can do. [9] The auditors named inventory their key audit matter: it was ₹271.9 crore, 24 percent of standalone total assets, and the provision against it rose from ₹64.8 crore to ₹81.4 crore in FY26. [14]

The second weakness is that the business moves with vehicle production. Crisil says bearing makers are "inherently exposed" to the cycle, and that car makers press for lower prices while cost increases are passed on with a lag. [4] The June 2026 quarter shows the lag: other expenses rose about ₹10 crore from the March quarter because of power, freight and petroleum costs. [7] A market ticker on the page we retrieved on 28 October showed December crude oil at $103.37 a barrel, which raises these costs again. [31]

The third weakness is the owners' finances, covered next. The company has also flagged a smaller compliance matter: ₹25.1 crore of export receivables were paid later than the foreign-exchange rules allow, and the penalty, if any, "is not ascertainable". Management expects it not to be material. [5]

The owners: a pledge that was cleared by selling stock

The promoters took a loan in FY25 to settle the family split, and secured it with NRB shares. [4] Trendlyne's quarter-end history shows the share of promoter holding that was pledged at 65.9 percent in June 2025, 77.7 percent in March 2026 and 3.3 percent in September 2026. [21] In June 2025 the company said that promoter and managing director Ms Harshbeena Zaveri "now holds ZERO encumbered shares". [12] Yet on 21 January 2026 she created a top-up pledge of 80,00,000 shares for Tata Capital (disclosed by a letter dated 23 January), taking her encumbered shares to 81.87 percent of her holding, because the promoters wanted "much more than the required buffer" after market volatility. [16] The stock was ₹239 when more shares were pledged on 27 March 2026. [23]

The cure was a sale. On 8 June 2026 the family trust sold 43.7 lakh shares at ₹390 in a block, to funds including Arohi and Iroha, and sold more at ₹330 on 11 May and ₹505 on 11 September. [22] Crisil notes a 6.47 percent stake sale in the June quarter that repaid the trust loan, and says that as of 30 June 14.99 percent of the promoter stake was still pledged. [4] By 30 September Ms Zaveri had released all but 14,00,000 shares (1.44 percent of the company). [17] Promoter holding is now 43.88 percent, down from 51.2 percent in March. [3]

What does this mean for a shareholder? The pledge overhang that drove the stock to ₹213 in the last year is gone, and that is likely a large part of why the price has nearly doubled since March. [3] [29] It also means the easy re-rating is behind us. Two funds, Nalanda India Equity Fund (9.38 percent) and HDFC Small Cap Fund (8.85 percent), now hold almost 18 percent between them, which is a concentration of holders that could sell. [21] Chapter 3 tests the numbers and chapter 2B tests management's record.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management guidance is the second half of Layer 2. We compare what management said in June 2025, November 2025, February 2026 and May 2026 with what the filings later showed. The pattern is mixed. Operating and strategic moves were delivered, often at a larger scale or a higher price than first stated. Growth and margin promises were roughly met on management's own measure but missed on the plain operating margin. The pledge promise was broken and then fixed by a stock sale.

Take the margin first. In June 2025 management said consolidated EBITDA would be "up to 20%+". [12] For FY26 it reported ₹267 crore, or 19.5 percent. [8] That figure includes ₹34.4 crore of other income. (Screener's table shows ₹27 crore of other income because it nets the ₹7.0 crore exceptional loss of FY26 into that line.) On sales alone, operating profit was ₹232.5 crore, or 17.4 percent (our working from the filing). [5] Crisil reports 19.4 percent on its own definition. [4] A reader who compares NRB with a peer that reports operating profit on sales alone should use the 17.4 percent.

Take the capital plan next. The first plan was ₹200 crore, approved in April 2025. [12] [14] The board added ₹70 crore in February 2026 and up to ₹40 crore for land in May 2026, and the company also plans ₹37.5 crore for Mahant and up to ₹67 crore for the Unitec joint venture. [14] Crisil now sizes the total at about ₹400 crore over three years, with ₹150 crore of new debt. [4] The plan has roughly doubled in fifteen months. That is not a failure, but it means early cash-flow comfort will fade.
