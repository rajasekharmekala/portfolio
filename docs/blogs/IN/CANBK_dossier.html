Before you read

Canara Bank is India's fourth-largest government-owned bank, with ₹30.72 lakh crore of loans and deposits and 62.93 percent owned by the Government of India [1][13]. The bank is healthy: record profit of ₹5,175 crore last quarter, bad loans down to 1.33 percent of loans, and shares at about 5.2 times earnings [1][24]. We still say REJECTED because of the chart, not the bank: the stock is in a confirmed downtrend, called Stage 4, and has not passed the recovery test [42]. If the chart repairs, we see a central value near ₹142 against ₹118.01 now, with an exit price of ₹111.34. We would be wrong if the one-time loan-loss bill tops ₹15,000 crore, if funding costs outrun loan yields, or if the stock falls through ₹114.63 (chapter 6 lists each risk) [2][42].

1. What the company does

A bank is a business that borrows money from many people and lends it to others at a higher price. Canara Bank does this at very large scale. At 30 September 2026 it held ₹16.97 lakh crore of deposits and had lent ₹13.75 lakh crore, so its total business was ₹30.72 lakh crore [1]. It runs 10,146 domestic branches and 13,087 outlets staffed by agents, called business correspondents, which gives 23,237 banking outlets in all [1]. The bank was incorporated in 1906, was nationalised in 1969, has its head office in Bengaluru, and absorbed Syndicate Bank on 1 April 2020 [24].

The President of India, which means the Government of India, owns 62.93 percent of the shares, and the stock exchange filing for 30 September 2026 answers No to both pledged and encumbered shares [13]. That stake has stood at 62.93 percent for twelve straight quarters, and the government has put no new equity into the bank for four years [24][14]. Mutual funds hold 7.00 percent, foreign investors 12.27 percent and insurers 4.32 percent [1]. Government ownership matters twice. It gives depositors and bond buyers a strong safety net, which is why CRISIL and CARE rate the bank's infrastructure and Tier-II bonds AAA [15][16]. It also means the government can decide the bank's future, a point we return to in chapter 6 because Delhi is drawing up a new round of bank consolidation [21].

The bank earns most of its money from the gap between what it charges on loans and what it pays on deposits. That gap, in rupees, is called net interest income. In the September 2026 quarter the bank earned ₹35,159 crore of interest and paid ₹24,154 crore, which left ₹11,005 crore, up 20.39 percent in a year [1]. The rest of its income, ₹5,772 crore or about a third of operating income, is fees (₹2,755 crore), trading gains on bonds (₹499 crore), cash recovered on loans written off earlier (₹1,260 crore) and other receipts such as priority-sector certificates [1]. The last two groups are less steady than fees, and chapter 2 shows why that matters now.

The loan book has three retail-style arms and one corporate arm. The three arms are retail loans (home, vehicle, education) at ₹3.31 lakh crore, up 31.61 percent in a year; farm loans at ₹3.04 lakh crore; and small-business loans at ₹1.78 lakh crore. Together the bank calls them RAM, and RAM was ₹8.12 lakh crore, or 59 percent of all loans [1]. Corporate and other loans were ₹5.63 lakh crore. Gold loans are the largest single product at ₹2.82 lakh crore, about a fifth of the whole book, of which ₹1.74 lakh crore is classified as farm credit and ₹1.08 lakh crore as other [2].

On the funding side, a bank wants cheap money. The cheapest is the balance customers leave in current and savings accounts, which the bank calls CASA. Canara's domestic CASA was ₹4.49 lakh crore, which is 29.00 percent of domestic deposits, down from 30.69 percent a year earlier [1]. The rest is term deposits, which cost more: ₹10.99 lakh crore, of which ₹6.60 lakh crore is retail [1]. In 2026 the bank also raised about $5.8 billion, close to ₹55,000 crore, from non-resident Indians through foreign-currency deposits known as FCNR(B), and lent $3.2 billion of it back at a thin spread of 40 to 50 basis points [2]. That is a new feature of the funding mix, and we study its risk in chapters 2B and 6.

Three ratios describe how well a bank earns. The net interest margin (NIM) is the spread between what the bank earns on its assets and what it pays for its funds; it was 2.55 percent in the quarter and 2.53 percent for the half year [1]. The GNPA ratio, which stands for gross non-performing assets, is the share of all loans on which borrowers have stopped paying for 90 days or more; it was 1.33 percent. The NNPA ratio, for net non-performing assets, is the same share after the bank has set money aside, and it was 0.30 percent [1]. Falling ratios mean fewer bad loans and a safer bank.

Two valuation terms appear throughout. Earnings per share (EPS) is the bank's profit divided by its number of shares; for the half year to September 2026 it was ₹22.06 on an annualised basis, on the bank's own accounts [1]. The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) is the share price divided by a year of earnings per share. It tells you how many years of today's profit the market is paying for. At ₹118 and trailing consolidated profit per share of ₹22.47, the P/E is 5.24 [24].

A note on price basis. The bank split every ₹10 share into five ₹2 shares, with a board decision on 26 February 2024 and a record date in May 2024 [19][20]. Every price and per-share figure in this report is on the ₹2 face-value basis, the same basis as the ₹118.01 close of 27 October 2026 [42]. Older filings use the pre-split basis: for example, earnings per share for FY24 were ₹80.23 on the old basis and ₹16.05 after dividing by five [9][39]. Figures from before May 2024 are comparable only after that division, which we do whenever a per-share number from an old document appears.

How a customer would use the bank

A salaried person in Bengaluru or Chennai opens a savings account, takes a home loan and pays a car loan through the same branch. A farmer takes a crop loan or borrows against gold jewellery. A small manufacturer takes a working-capital loan and keeps a current account. Each of these relationships earns the bank a little spread or fee, and most of them last for years. The bank says it is 'born' in the southern states, where it faces little brand problem, and that it is the first-choice bank in Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana [2].

The government is also a customer and a channel. The bank is the lender for public schemes such as the emergency credit line scheme, under which it disbursed more than ₹13,000 crore [2]. It exceeded every priority-sector target at September 2026, with total priority lending at 44.99 percent of adjusted net bank credit against a 40 percent norm [1]. Such loans support volumes but do not always carry high margins. The short glossary below explains the terms used later.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The honest summary is that Canara has become a much better bank than it was four years ago, but it is not yet a premium one. The improvement shows in every year of the record: bad loans fell from 7.51 percent of loans in March 2022 to 1.33 percent in September 2026, net bad loans from 2.65 percent to 0.30 percent, and the share of bad loans already covered by provisions rose from 84.17 percent to 95.34 percent [11][1]. Return on equity rose from 12.82 percent to 19.61 percent over the same stretch [11][4]. The weak side is funding and the quality of recent profit, which the rest of this chapter takes in turn.

The first strength is scale and franchise. ICRA, a rating agency, puts the bank at 5.8 percent of all net advances and 6.2 percent of all deposits in India at March 2026, the fourth-largest state bank and the sixth-largest bank of any kind by total business [14]. A network of 10,146 branches with a strong footprint in South India gives it a steady stream of savings money and a local brand [1][14]. ICRA rates its Basel III Tier-II bonds [ICRA]AAA and Tier-I bonds [ICRA]AA+ with a stable outlook, and says it would reassess only if government ownership changed, return on assets fell below 0.3 percent or the capital cushion above the regulatory minimum shrank below 1 percentage point [14]. Return on assets is 1.04 percent, so the cushion is wide.

The second strength is capital. The total capital ratio is 17.19 percent and the highest-quality ratio, CET1, is 12.80 percent against a requirement the chief executive puts at 8.5 percent [1][2]. Risk-weighted assets, the denominator of these ratios, grew only 12.9 percent in a year while advances grew 19.46 percent, because the loan mix is shifting to lower-risk retail loans: risk-weighted assets are 64.20 percent of gross advances, against 67.95 percent a year ago [1]. Management says the full ₹12,000 to ₹15,000 crore estimated cost of the new loan-loss rules would take only 1 to 1.2 percentage points off CET1 [2]. That is why we treat the new rules as a profit and valuation risk, not a solvency risk.

The third strength is asset quality that is backed by numbers. Gross bad loans fell from ₹27,040 crore to ₹18,299 crore in a year, new slippages were ₹1,844 crore in the quarter, and the bank wrote off ₹2,800 crore in the same quarter [1]. By segment, bad-loan ratios are 0.32 percent in retail, 0.94 percent in corporate, 1.65 percent in farm credit and 3.90 percent in small-business credit [1]. Overdue-but-not-bad loans above ₹5 crore were ₹7,043 crore, or 0.51 percent of loans, with the most serious bucket (61 to 90 days overdue) at ₹2,838 crore against ₹3,321 crore a year earlier [1]. Counting every ticket size, management puts the total stressed pool at 2.42 percent of loans, or ₹33,340 crore [2]. One caution: that most serious bucket was ₹3,482 crore in June, against ₹1,394 crore a year before, and an analyst asked about it on the July call [3].

Where the bank is weaker than it looks

The weakest link is cheap funding. Domestic CASA was 29.00 percent of domestic deposits in September 2026, against 30.69 percent a year earlier and 31.17 percent at March 2025 [1][8]. Current-account balances fell 16.71 percent in a year [1]. ICRA notes that Canara's cost of funds, 5.45 percent in FY26, sits above the state-bank average of 4.94 percent for that year because the CASA share is low and term-deposit rates are high [14]. The bank has partly fixed this with FCNR(B) money, which cost about 1 percentage point less than the bulk deposits it replaced, and the cost of deposits fell from 5.66 percent to 5.27 percent in a year [2][1]. But that fix is borrowed from the foreign-currency market, not earned from customers.

Earnings quality is the next concern. Net interest income rose 20.39 percent in a year, yet operating profit rose only 1.98 percent, to ₹8,758 crore, because trading gains fell from ₹1,583 crore to ₹499 crore and recoveries from written-off accounts fell from ₹1,680 crore to ₹1,260 crore [1]. Net profit still rose 8.40 percent because provisions fell 6.06 percent, to ₹3,583 crore [1]. In FY26 trading gains of ₹6,904 crore and written-off recoveries of ₹6,537 crore together were 40.7 percent of operating profit [4]. In the first half of FY27 the same two items were 24 percent of operating profit, and the chief executive said the pool of written-off accounts is dwindling, so recoveries will fall [1][2]. Profit is therefore moving from one-time and irregular sources toward interest income, which is healthier, but it is not yet clear the interest income can carry the load.

Margin and growth are the third concern. The bank's margin on earning assets fell from 3.05 percent in FY24 to 2.80 percent in FY25 and 2.51 percent in FY26 [9][8][4]. It turned up to 2.55 percent in the September 2026 quarter [1]. ICRA measures the margin on all assets instead and gets 2.15 percent for Q1 FY27 against 2.09 percent for FY26, so the direction is the same on both methods [14]. Loans grew 19.46 percent against deposits at 13.13 percent, and the loan-to-deposit ratio rose from 76.72 percent to 81.01 percent [1]. That leaves less room for another year of fast lending without paying up for funds.

Concentration is the fourth. Gold loans are about 20.5 percent of the book (₹2.82 lakh crore on ₹13.75 lakh crore of advances), and the annual report says the gold-loan portfolio grew 35.3 percent in FY26 [2][12]. On new lending the bank says it earns 9.10 percent on gold loans against 7.72 percent on corporate loans, so the product is profitable, and management says it is a market leader and prices below finance companies [2]. A sharp fall in the gold price or a rise in competition would test both the yield and the collateral cover. Three states are declared drought-affected, with ₹13,900 crore of the bank's exposure in drought-prone districts; management expects restructuring through state bankers' committees rather than defaults [2].

How we weigh the evidence

Strengths and weaknesses do not carry the same weight. Capital strength and government support protect against a disaster, but they are already priced in a share that trades below its accounting book value. The weaknesses (cheap funding, earnings quality, bond-price risk and the new accounting rules) decide whether profit rises or falls from here, so they matter more for the price. That is why the Layer 2 scorecard in chapter 2B gives CASA a zero and the margin and funding only half marks, and why the verdict in chapter 7 leans on the chart rule, not on the strengths.

What this means for an owner: you would hold a lender with a near-certain safety net, a strong capital cushion and profit that is currently helped by low provisions and fast loan growth. You would not hold a franchise with a funding advantage. The business earns a fair return, and the price already reflects some doubt about how long it can last. Chapter 2B asks whether management has delivered what it promised, which is the best available test of how much of that doubt is fair.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Bank accounts do not look like a factory's accounts, and four of the usual tests do not fit. A bank's 'revenue' is mostly interest income. Its 'debt' is the deposits it takes, which are its raw material and are about 13 times its own equity. So the usual debt-to-equity ratio, borrowings divided by shareholders' money, says little. Interest paid is its cost of goods, not a financing cost. Its cash flow from operations, the cash the business itself generates, moves with deposits and loans, not with earnings. The usual ROCE, which is return on capital employed, counts deposits as capital and is not meaningful either. So we replace these tests with the ones that matter for a lender: return on equity, bad loans, provision coverage, capital ratios, margin and cost of funds. The number

We place the exit price, or stop loss, at ₹111.34. A stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong and the position is cut. It is 5.65 percent below the ₹118.01 close and 5.64 percent below ₹118.00, the top of the entry zone, the band of prices where buying is planned.

How it was calculated

The rule for a Core stock is the 63-day floor (₹114.63) minus one average daily price swing (the average true range, or ATR, ₹3.29). That gives ₹111.34. The reward-to-risk ratio then follows: (₹142 minus ₹118.01) divided by (₹118.01 minus ₹111.34) is 3.60.

The inputs

Close ₹118.01, 63-day low and 20-day swing low ₹114.63, ATR ₹3.29, from the session ledger of 27 October 2026 [42]. The target of ₹142 comes from chapter 5.

The range

A tactical stop (swing low minus half an ATR) would be ₹112.99 and would raise the ratio to 4.78; the 200-day average at ₹133.97 is not used because the rule rejects a far-away 200-day stop in a trend that is already falling. At a bear value of ₹96, the loss from ₹118.00 would be 18.6 percent, which is more than three times the planned stop loss, because a stop on a close does not protect against a gap down.

If the number is off

If the stock gaps below ₹111.34 on a bad result, the actual loss is larger than 5.6 percent. That is why the sizing in chapter 7 caps the position at 3.5 percent of the portfolio if the stock ever qualifies: even an 18 percent loss would cost 0.63 percent of the portfolio.

The number

At ₹118.01 the market is paying for a return on equity of about 11 to 12 percent, against 18.49 percent earned in the half year and 16.75 percent that analysts expect for FY27.

How it was calculated

A bank's fair price-to-book ratio equals (return on equity minus growth) divided by (cost of equity minus growth). Turning that around gives the return on equity that justifies the price: P/B times (cost of equity minus growth), plus growth. We use a 12 percent cost of equity, the discount rate in our scoring file, and 6 to 8 percent long-run growth.

The inputs

Price-to-book of 0.83 on Screener's consolidated book value of ₹141 and 0.95 on the bank's own book value of ₹124.56 [24][1]; analysts' FY27 return on equity of 16.75 percent [39]; cost of equity and growth are our assumptions.

The range

With growth at 6 percent the implied return on equity is 11.0 to 11.7 percent; at 8 percent it is 11.3 to 11.8 percent. Even the lowest delivered number, FY22 at 12.82 percent, is above that range [11].

If the number is off

If investors expected a 17 percent return on equity to last, the stock would be worth well above the book value. The gap means the market doubts the return will last, because it comes partly from low provisions and one-off gains. If the return falls toward 12 percent, the price is fair and there is no upside; our central case assumes it settles around 16 percent.

Momentum and turnaround inflection gate

The momentum gate has three tests. Test A asks whether the stock is in an uptrend now. It needs a price above a rising 50-day average that is above a rising 200-day average, and it needs the stock to beat the market. Test B asks whether the stock is entering an uptrend from a quiet base. It looks for a 50-day average that holds up, relative strength that turns, and volume that dries up and then returns. The pivot is the top of that quiet stretch, the level the price must clear to start a move. Test C asks whether a fallen stock has stopped falling. It looks for a higher floor, a short-term turn up, closeness to support, and no free fall. Each test needs all its conditions. Only Test C matters for a Stage 4 stock. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could fail. This chapter names that assumption for each estimate, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when we will know. We then give the bear case in its strongest honest form and name the single claim that decides the outcome.

The profit estimate assumes the loan-loss bill stays inside ₹12,000 to ₹15,000 crore and does not hit run-rate profit

The weakest link in the ₹23.6 estimate for FY28 is the cost of the new accounting rules. The bank's own number has already moved once, from ₹10,000 crore in May to ₹12,000 to ₹15,000 crore in October, and the cause was higher bond yields, which the bank cannot predict [5][2]. A bank official said the cost could be ₹14,000 to ₹15,000 crore at the maximum and as low as ₹10,000 to ₹12,000 crore if oil prices fall and tensions ease [2]. If the yield rise continues, the estimate rises again. The chief executive also said that if the bank decides not to pass the cost on to customers, 'the new normal' for return on assets could be 80 basis points, against 1.04 percent today [2]. That would cut profit by nearly a quarter on the same assets, so it deserves a place in the bear case.

The evidence that would show us wrong is simple: a Q3 FY27 results note that gives a new ECL estimate above ₹15,000 crore, or a statement that the bank will take the cost through profit in one year. The CEO said the bank would run a trial calculation in the October to December quarter, so a clearer number should appear with the December-quarter results, which we expect in late January 2027 on last year's timing (29 January 2026); the date is not announced [2][39]. How the bank books the transition should be visible in the FY27 results, around April or May 2027, because the rules start on 1 April 2027 [18].

Bond-yield risk: the treasury line could turn from a gain into a loss

The bank's trading gains were ₹6,904 crore in FY26 and ₹1,556 crore in the first half of FY27, and the second quarter was ₹499 crore against ₹1,583 crore a year earlier [4][1]. The chief executive said yields hardened by 44 basis points in the September quarter and that the swing in trading result was about ₹600 crore, from a ₹555 crore gain in June to a ₹55 crore loss in September on the bonds marked through profit [2]. The Reserve Bank has since raised the repo rate by 25 basis points and said further moves could be hikes [17]. The bank holds ₹63,633 crore of available-for-sale bonds and ₹17,409 crore marked through profit, and an additional loss of about ₹1,300 crore on the available-for-sale book in the quarter went to reserves, not to profit [1][2]. If yields keep rising, the profit line for FVTPL bonds can turn negative and book value falls with the AFS reserve.

We will know quarter by quarter. The Q3 FY27 trading-gain line and the AFS reserve movement will show whether the pain from the September quarter has stopped. The next Reserve Bank policy meeting is scheduled for 2 to 4 December 2026 [17].

Funding: CASA is falling and FCNR(B) money has to be refinanced

The estimate assumes the margin holds near 2.5 to 2.6 percent. The chief executive said margin would hold at 2.55 percent despite the rate hike, because the roughly $3.2 billion of FCNR(B) money lent back earns only a 40 to 50 basis point spread, which offsets the gain from repricing loans [2]. The bank raised about $5.8 billion in total, about ₹55,000 crore or 3.2 percent of deposits [2][1]. If that money has to be replaced at higher cost, or if it cannot be rolled over, the margin falls. The bank also said it chose not to raise more bulk deposits this quarter, so the pool of ready cheap funding is already used [2].

The first sign would be the cost of deposits rising again from 5.27 percent, or the CASA ratio staying below 29 percent in the December quarter. Both appear in the Q3 FY27 deck, which we expect in late January 2027.

Government and structure: a consolidation round, a new chief executive and a gold-loan book

Three risks sit outside the numbers. The first is government action. A report on 23 October said the Centre is drawing up a plan for the next round of public-sector bank consolidation, with the long-term aim of keeping three or four large state-owned banks, and that it is considering cutting its stake in four other banks (Central Bank, UCO, Punjab & Sind and Indian Overseas) to 75 percent [21]. Canara is not on the list, because its 62.93 percent is already below 75 percent, so this report has no basis for expecting a government share sale in Canara [21][13]. But a merger that makes Canara an acquirer or a target would change the case in either direction. At the October call the managing director said the early timing of the results had nothing to do with any merger [2].

The second is leadership: a new chief executive since 1 June 2026, after an interim period, and calls in July and October that kept the old guidance [22][23][3][2]. The third is the gold-loan book at about a fifth of loans, which depends on gold prices and on competition from finance companies [2]. None of these shows up in a ratio, so we treat them as reasons to size small if the stock ever qualifies.

The red-team bear case, in its strongest honest form

The strongest case against this stock is that a bank's profit peaks when credit costs are low and the cycle then turns, so a low multiple is the right price for peak profit. Canara has traded between 4.5 and 7.7 times earnings over three years, with a median of 6.0 [25]. Credit cost is 0.47 percent annualised, the lowest of the last four quarters (0.68, 0.59, 0.49 and 0.47 percent) [1], and trading gains and recoveries of written-off loans, which are not repeatable, were 40.7 percent of FY26 operating profit [1][4]. The pool behind the recoveries is shrinking, and ICRA says asset quality stays monitorable as the loan book seasons after several years of fast growth (loans grew 19.46 percent in the last year) [2][14]. A bank that is growing loans at 19 percent while funding at higher cost, entering a stricter accounting regime and a tightening rate cycle, is not a bank whose current profit can be projected forward.

On that view the 5.2 times earnings is fair, not cheap, and the chart is telling us the market agrees. We think the case is half right: the one-time costs are real, but the capital cushion and asset quality make a disaster unlikely, which is why the bear value is ₹96 and not lower.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED for new money today. The rule is the technical routing rule. Only a Stage 4 breakdown can disqualify a stock whose business passes. That means a close below a falling 50-day average that is below a falling 200-day average, with the recovery test failing. Canara closed at ₹118.01 under ₹123.48 under ₹133.97, and Test C fails on one condition out of four [42]. The business passes every bank test in chapter 3. Layer 2 scores 3.0 out of 5.0, at the floor. The reward-to-risk ratio is 3.60 to 1, above the 2.0 floor for a Core stock. The rule is binary, so none of that changes the verdict.

The verdict can change fast, because the miss is narrow. Suppose the stock closes above its 20-day average (₹119.19 on 27 October) with a positive two-week change. Then Test C passes and we re-run the ledger. The rerun must show a close above the then-current 20-day average, a positive two-week change and the other Test C conditions still met, not just a cross of the old ₹119.19 level. A pass would move the verdict to PASSED_CONDITIONAL with a limit order, and only after we re-check earnings, the loan-loss treatment and the multiple. The distance from the ₹114.63 support would then be about 1.5 ATR. That is the reduced-size band of our support ladder, not a market-buy band [42]. The limit orders would sit in the zone of ₹114.60 to ₹118.00. Half the position would go at ₹118.00 and half at ₹115.00. We do not chase a rebound above ₹118.00. At ₹123.50 the reward-to-risk ratio falls to 1.5 to 1 against the ₹111.34 exit.

Sizing, if the stock qualifies, follows the risk-unit rule. We would use conviction tier 3, with a 50 basis point risk budget. The stop distance from ₹118.00 is 5.64 percent. The budget alone would allow 8.9 percent of the portfolio. The tier cap is 3.5 percent, so the initial size is 3.5 percent, bought half at a time (1.75 percent each). If the half position is stopped out at ₹111.34, the loss is about 0.10 percent of the portfolio. Today the tier is zero and the weight is zero. After entry the let-winners-run rule applies. We would not trim just because the RSI passes 70. The stop would trail the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average while the trend and the fundamentals hold. The weight could grow up to 22 percent.

A close below ₹111.34 would stop us out after a fill. So would a Q3 FY27 margin under 2.45 percent, bad loans above 1.70 percent, or a loan-loss estimate above ₹15,000 crore. We would add to the position on a second-half result with profit at or above ₹5,000 crore, margin at or above 2.55 percent and CASA no lower than 29 percent, with the price in the zone. A close above the 50-day average (₹123.48 on 27 October) would end the Stage 4 label by itself, because Stage 4 needs the close to be below it. These stop-out triggers are tighter than the scorecard lines (bad loans PASS below 2.0 percent, margin FAIL below 2.50 percent). They govern orders, while the scorecard governs the quality rating, so a Layer 2 re-run can still pass a bank that has hit one.
