1. What the company does

EIH Ltd is the flagship company of the Oberoi group. It owns and runs luxury hotels under the Oberoi brand, five-star business hotels under the Trident brand, and Maidens Hotel in New Delhi. It also runs luxury Nile cruisers, a flight-catering arm called Oberoi Flight Services (OFS), airport restaurants and some project-management work.[2] On 30 June 2026 the group listed 3,801 keys in India and 408 abroad, counting hotels it manages for others.[4] A key is one room that can be sold to a guest, and it is the unit hotel people use to count size.

The company earns money from three lines: rooms, food and drink, and other services. In the year to March 2026 consolidated revenue was ₹2,939.6 crore. Rooms brought in ₹1,410.5 crore, about 48% of the total. Food and beverage brought in ₹1,095.6 crore, about 37%, and other services brought in ₹433.5 crore, about 15%.[16] Rooms are the high-margin line, because a room costs little to sell once the building is paid for. Food and drink need staff and ingredients, so they keep less of each rupee.

Flight catering sits inside those lines and is large. In the June 2026 quarter OFS revenue was ₹154 crore, against consolidated revenue from operations of ₹657 crore, so about 23% of the quarter's sales came from meals served to airlines.[5][4] The chief financial officer said OFS was profitable and did not hurt margins much in that quarter.[5] In earlier quarters management said a faster-growing OFS lowered the profit margin, because it changes the revenue mix toward a lower-margin line.[10] So the business is a luxury-hotel company with a sizeable airline-catering business attached, and the two do not earn the same margin.

EIH also earns fees. For hotels that it manages but does not own, it charges a management fee and a marketing fee.[16] The company does not disclose this fee income on its own. When an analyst asked for it in August 2026, the chief financial officer said the company normally does not disclose that separately.[5] That matters because rivals such as Indian Hotels Company (IHCL) report fee income growing at 26%, and the market gives asset-light fee income a higher price.[25] We cannot test how much of EIH's profit is fee income, so we do not give it any credit in our numbers.

Demand for Indian hotels is seasonal. The summer and monsoon months (April to September) are slow, and the winter months (October to March) are busy. EIH's operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, shows it: in the five quarters from June 2025 to June 2026 it was 28%, 26%, 43%, 37% and 25%.[2] Any quarter should therefore be read against the same quarter a year earlier, and the winter quarters carry most of the year's profit. The next report, for the September 2026 quarter, falls in a slow season.

A hotel's sales power is measured by RevPAR, short for revenue per available room, which is occupancy multiplied by the average room rate. In the June 2026 quarter, across all hotels EIH runs including managed ones, occupancy rose from 70% to 72%, the average room rate rose from ₹16,268 to ₹17,868, and RevPAR rose from ₹11,352 to ₹12,801, a gain of 12.8%.[4][5] At the hotels EIH owns, RevPAR rose from ₹13,082 to ₹14,972, a gain of 14.4%, on occupancy of 73% and then 75% and a rate of ₹17,941 and then ₹19,950.[4] The whole industry gained 11% to 13% in RevPAR in the same quarter, so EIH grew with the market, not ahead of it.[4]

Where the hotels are and what is shut

The portfolio is concentrated in a few cities. Three Mumbai hotels hold 1,262 keys: Trident Nariman Point with 586, Trident Bandra Kurla with 439, and The Oberoi, Mumbai with 237.[4] That is about one-third of the 3,801 India keys, by our count. Delhi and Gurgaon add The Oberoi New Delhi (220 keys), The Oberoi Gurgaon (202), Trident Gurgaon (136) and Maidens (55), about 16% of India keys by our count.[4] A weak Mumbai or Delhi winter therefore moves the whole company.

Some keys cannot be sold today. The Oberoi Grand in Kolkata (209 keys) and Trident Jaipur (132 keys) are marked as under renovation, which together are 341 keys, or about 9% of the India count.[4] The same list still shows Wildflower Hall near Shimla with 85 keys, although the state of Himachal Pradesh took possession of that hotel on 31 March 2025. The Tribune reported in October 2025 that the Oberoi Group was running it on an interim basis until a new operator is chosen.[4][32] Those keys are therefore temporary, and EIH's long-run count of rooms it can sell is lower than 3,801.

Terms used later for the share price

Later chapters read the price chart, so a few terms are set out here. The relative strength index (RSI) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been moving, and the average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing in rupees. A base is a sideways stretch of price that follows a fall and forms the floor for a new rise. The pivot is the top of that base, the level a rise must clear. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the holding is sold. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, so it never chases a rising share. The entry zone is the planned range of prices for buying.

The Wildflower Hall dispute

Wildflower Hall near Shimla was held by a subsidiary, Mashobra Resort Limited. A long dispute with the state ended with a Supreme Court order of 20 February 2024 to hand the hotel back, and EIH did so on 31 March 2025.[12] On 2 June 2025 the Himachal Pradesh High Court settled the money claims. EIH cut its investment in the subsidiary to ₹13.00 crore, wrote down an amount owed to it by half, and reversed a user-fee obligation, for a net charge of ₹110.32 crore in the June 2025 quarter.[12] The state then decided to invite global bids from hotel chains to operate the hotel, and the CEO said in August 2026 that the date for the qualifying bid had moved to 10 September 2026.[32][5] We could not find the outcome of that process, so it is an open item rather than a thesis point.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Key - one room that can be sold; hotel size is counted in keys.
  • RevPAR - revenue per available room: occupancy multiplied by the average room rate. It is the single best summary of how well a hotel sells.
  • Occupancy - the share of available room-nights that were sold.
  • Average room rate - room revenue divided by rooms sold, in rupees per night.
  • RGI - revenue generation index: a hotel's RevPAR divided by the RevPAR of a chosen set of rival hotels, times 100. Above 100 means it earns more per room than that set.
  • MICE - meetings, incentives, conferences and exhibitions: group business that fills many rooms at once.
  • Owned versus managed - an owned hotel puts the building and its profit on EIH's books; a managed hotel earns EIH only fees.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

EIH's competitive strength is its brand and its locations. Oberoi hotels sell at a large premium to other luxury hotels. In the June 2026 quarter the Oberoi brand earned a RevPAR of ₹16,090 against ₹9,965 for the Indian luxury segment, and Trident earned ₹10,490 against ₹6,484 for upper-upscale hotels.[4] Those premiums are about 61% and 62%, by our count. The company's own index of revenue against its chosen rival set was 125 in June 2026, up from 121 a year earlier, and 14 of the 15 hotels that have outside benchmarks ranked first or second in their cities.[5]

There is a caveat on those rankings. The CEO himself warned on the same call that the index depends on which hotels are chosen as the rival set, and that a set picked to flatter would show good numbers.[5] We take the rankings as evidence of a strong position, but we weight the plain revenue figures more. Those also look good: for the full year to March 2026 the Oberoi brand's RevPAR grew 10.4% against 6.4% for the luxury segment, and Trident's grew 10.2% against 5% for its segment.[7]

The second strength is the balance sheet. Borrowings at March 2026 were ₹252 crore against equity and reserves of ₹5,262 crore, and most of the borrowing is lease liabilities.[2][18] The company's surplus funds were ₹1,368 crore at 30 June 2026.[4] CARE Ratings gave EIH its AA+ Stable issuer rating in December 2025 and noted that management does not plan to use debt for its current building plans.[18] For a business with big buildings and long gestation periods, having no debt is a protective moat: it can keep renovating through a bad year without selling anything.

The third strength is cash conversion. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business earns before any financing. It was ₹993 crore in the year to March 2026, against profit after tax of ₹657 crore.[2] The company spent ₹680 crore on capital expenditure, which is spending on buildings and equipment. It paid ₹97 crore in dividends in that year.[7] Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying assets, and it was ₹302 crore on Screener's measure. That is a yield of 1.7% on the ₹18,276 crore market value, by our count.[2][1] The yield is low because the company is in a building phase.

Why a high return on capital needs a caution

EIH's return on capital employed (ROCE) was 21% in the year to March 2026 and 20.7% on Screener's latest reading, which is the best in its peer group, as Chapter 3 shows.[2] ROCE is operating profit after depreciation, divided by the money tied up in the business. The caution is that EIH's old hotels sit in the accounts at historic cost. Hotels owned for a long time are carried at what they cost then, so the capital employed is small compared with what the land and buildings would cost today. That flatters the ratio against peers who bought or built recently, and it is our reading of the accounting, not a figure the company gives.

The market value tells the same story from the other side. Fixed assets were ₹3,880 crore at March 2026, while the ledger puts the enterprise value, which is market value plus debt less cash, at ₹17,624 crore.[2][1] Against the trailing twelve-month operating profit of ₹1,030 crore, that is an enterprise value of about 17 times, by our count.[2][1] The high ROCE is real, but it does not mean a new investor earns 21% on what the shares cost.

Ownership and the family dispute

The Oberoi family holds 32.85% of the shares, unchanged in every quarter from December 2023 to September 2026 on Screener's table, and none of the promoter shares are pledged as security for loans.[2][17] Mutual funds held 12.71% and insurers 1.06% in June 2026.[17] CARE lists Reliance Strategic Business Ventures, an investment arm of Reliance Industries, as holding 18.83% in September 2025.[18] Foreign funds raised their stake from 6.65% to 7.68% in the September 2026 quarter, while domestic funds trimmed theirs from 13.78% to 12.50%.[2]

There is a legal dispute inside the family. In September 2024 the Delhi High Court, acting on a suit by Anastasia Oberoi, a daughter of the late PRS Oberoi, passed an interim order restraining the transfer of shares in EIH and its holding companies that had been held by him.[29][30][31] CARE noted in December 2025 that the matter is under legal consideration within the family, does not affect operations, and stays a monitorable item.[18] For a shareholder the risk is indirect: a dispute over who controls the promoter block could slow big decisions, or change who sits on the board, without touching the hotels.

Where the business is weak

The first weakness is that owned supply is not growing for two years. The owned pipeline is seven properties with 825 keys, opening between 2027 and 2030, and the first of them, Trident Visakhapatnam, is held through an associate company.[4] In May 2026 an analyst pointed out that apart from that associate hotel there was nothing meaningful on the owned side for FY27 and FY28. The CEO agreed that with existing rooms there are two levers only, higher rates and lower waste.[7] Growth in the next two years must come from rate, not from more rooms.

The second weakness is cost. Employee costs are rising because of new labour codes and because the company has chosen to cut working hours for staff, a decision the CEO said raises costs.[5][7] Power and fuel costs rose in the June 2026 quarter because of the Strait of Hormuz crisis, and the company spent more on marketing and information technology.[5] A hotel with high fixed costs gains a lot when rooms fill, and loses a lot when they do not.

The third weakness is exposure to foreign guests and to air travel. Oberoi hotels draw a higher share of foreign business than Trident hotels, and foreign tourist arrivals fell 14.4% in April 2026.[5][24] ICRA also reports that foreign arrivals fell 7.9% in calendar 2025, and its site shows that domestic air passenger traffic fell 6.5% year on year in September 2026.[22][23] The flight-catering business depends on the same airlines. The demand picture is covered again in Chapter 5.

The fourth weakness is that the contingent liabilities have grown. The annual report shows disputed sales tax and VAT claims of ₹99.96 crore at March 2026, up from ₹5.24 crore a year earlier, by our conversion of the million-rupee figures.[16] That is small against ₹5,262 crore of equity, but the jump is large and the report does not say what drove it, so we flag it as an open question.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 of our method asks which three to five measures run this particular business, and scores each as PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). A company qualifies at 3.0 out of 5.0 or more, with at most one FAIL. For a luxury hotel owner we picked five measures: how fast RevPAR grows and whether it beats rivals, whether the EBITDA margin holds, whether net cash funds the building plan, whether new rooms open on schedule, and how much of the profit is high-margin fee income. The term EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, and for a hotel it is the closest figure to the cash profit of the hotels before the cost of the buildings.

The reason for these five and not others is that they split the business into its levers. A hotel has only a few: how full the rooms are, what each room is sold for, what it costs to run, how many rooms there are, and what the owner can do with the cash. The first measure covers the first two. The second covers cost. The fourth covers the room count. The third covers the cash, and the fifth covers the part of the business that does not need rooms at all.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means we compare what management said one to two years ago against what happened. A company's guidance is its own forecast of the coming period, and a management team that hits its stated dates and ranges earns more trust than one that does not. The audit below covers eight statements. Three were delivered, one is mixed, two were missed and two cannot yet be judged.

The clearest miss is the Oberoi Grand in Kolkata. In February 2025 the CEO said it should open in about 18 months, which pointed to August 2026.[15] In May 2025 he said it would open in about 12 months and that the opening would be partial, in two phases.[14] In August 2026 he said it is scheduled for September 2028 with 197 keys.[5] The slip is about two years against the first statement. He gave two reasons: the old building needed more work to meet today's fire and structural rules, and construction in Kolkata was halted after an incident in which 15 people were killed.[5] The first reason is within the company's control to foresee, since the building was opened up when the renovation began, so we count this as a planning miss, and the second as bad luck.

The pipeline of new hotels also shrank and slid. In May 2025 management spoke of 21 properties and about 1,500 keys over two to three years.[14] In August 2025 it showed 25 new hotels with 2,033 keys, of which 8 were to be owned and 17 managed.[13] In May 2026 the managed pipeline was 24 hotels with 1,893 keys, and by August 2026 it was 23 hotels with 1,833 keys, after one hotel of 60 keys moved beyond 2032.[7][5] The owned list is now 7 properties with 825 keys.[4] The Goa resort shows a different kind of slip: the August 2026 slide says 2028, while the annual report says late 2029, and the CEO told analysts that the annual report is the fairer guide.[4][16][5] Pipeline numbers on managed hotels depend on partners, and the CEO said so in August 2025.[13]

On pricing and funding, management did what it said. In May 2025 the CEO said the aim was to push rates up, even at some cost to occupancy, as long as RevPAR grew strongly.[14] In the year to March 2026 the owned hotels reported an average room rate of ₹22,651 at 76.8% occupancy, and the Oberoi brand beat its segment on RevPAR growth.[16][7] On funding, CARE recorded in December 2025 that management expected no debt for its building plan and net borrowing below 1.5 times operating profit even in the event of acquisitions.[18] Surplus funds rose from ₹1,335 crore in March 2026 to ₹1,368 crore in June 2026 while the company spent ₹148 crore on capex and investments in that quarter.[4]

Margin is the statement still open. After the May 2026 results an analyst said FY27 should show better margins and growth because the base was cleaner, and the CEO answered that the analyst had summed it up and that he agreed.[7] Three months later the June 2026 quarter showed an EBITDA margin of 29.7% against 32.0%, on the company's own definition, which counts other income.[4] The CFO gave reasons: the new Rajgarh hotel is still ramping, power costs rose, about ₹4 crore went on extra marketing to fill rooms with domestic guests, and a ₹7.5 crore write-off came from room renovations in Mumbai.[5] One quarter in the slow season does not settle the question, but it is a point against the statement so far.

5. What could happen next

Fatal flaw 2: foreign demand and air travel do not recover

EIH's Oberoi brand draws a larger share of foreign guests than Trident, and the CEO said in August 2026 that the decline in overseas guests would probably continue through the September quarter.[5] Foreign arrivals to India fell 7.9% in calendar 2025 and 14.4% in April 2026.[22][24] ICRA's own site shows domestic air passenger traffic down 6.5% in September 2026. It also lists a 25 basis point rise in interest rates on 7 October 2026, with a further rise expected in December.[23] If the winter is weak, three-quarters of the year's profit is hit, and a high-fixed-cost hotel company loses more profit than revenue. The flight-catering business, about 23% of June revenue, depends on the same airlines.[5]

Fatal flaw 3: owned growth slips again, or control becomes uncertain

The Oberoi Grand has already slipped from a promised August 2026 to September 2028, and the Goa hotel shows 2028 in one document and late 2029 in another.[15][5][4][16] Hebbal is the largest owned project. If it slips in the same way, the 2030 earnings that the market may be counting on move out by a year or more. The family dispute is a separate risk: a Delhi High Court order of September 2024 restrains transfers of the late PRS Oberoi's shares, and CARE calls the matter monitorable.[29][18] A final ruling that changes who controls the promoter stake could lead to a change of leadership or a forced sale, which would hit the share price whatever the hotels earn.

The red-team case against our REJECTED verdict

The strongest honest case against our own verdict is that it is too mechanical. EIH is a net-cash company. It has a 20.7% return on capital, a three-year cash conversion of 1.20 and an AA+ rating. Industry room rates rose 9% to 10% in FY26 and 6% to 8% in the June 2026 quarter.[2][18][7][4] The shares trade 23% below their own median multiple and 28% below their high, and the sellers have gone quiet, with volume at 0.21 times average.[1] The close is only 1.35% below the line that would pass Test C. The reward-to-risk ratio from the top of the buying zone is 4.35 to 1, above the 2.0 floor, and the probability-weighted value is 16.7% above the price. A buyer who sizes small, uses a stop at ₹271 and waits for the foreign-guest recovery could do well.

We still reject, for three reasons. The rule is binary by design, so that attractive-looking numbers cannot talk us into buying a falling trend. The company itself gave us a margin warning in the same quarter in which the chart turned bearish, so the fundamental evidence is not neutral. And the cost of waiting is small. The trigger sits 1.35% above the close. If the red-team case is right, the verdict flips within days at a price only slightly higher.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, which means we do not buy at the ₹291.30 close and we do not place a limit order today. The rule is the Layer 1 trend red line: the close of ₹291.30 sits below a falling 50-day average of ₹295.94, which sits below a falling 200-day average of ₹315.19, and Test C fails on its short-term turn.[1] The business passes every solvency test, qualifies as a COMPOUNDER, and clears the reward-to-risk floor of 2.0, so this is a rejection on timing and trend, not on quality. It changes to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT on a close at or above the 20-day average, ₹295.24 on 27 October, because that passes Test C and lifts the red line.

Layer 3 is the part of the method that decides where and how to buy once the gate allows it. It measures the distance from the nearest verified support, in units of the average true range. Support here is the 63-day base low of ₹280.30, and the share is 1.18 ranges above it, which sits in the STANDARD band (below 1.2). If the red line lifted at a close of ₹295.24, the distance would be about 1.61 ranges, which falls in the NO_BUY band, so we would then place a limit order, an order that fills only at your price or better, in the buying zone and not chase. The entry zone is ₹280.00 to ₹288.00. The top of it is 0.8 ranges above the ₹280.30 low.

The stop is the price at which the idea is wrong, and we set it at ₹271.00. It is the base low of ₹280.30 less one average true range of ₹9.29, as the rule for a core holding on a basing share requires. The stop is ₹0.15 below the 52-week low of ₹271.15, so a break of that low would also be a new 52-week low. From the ₹288 top of the zone, the stop is 5.9% away. The reward-to-risk ratio is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop: with a base target of ₹362 it is 3.48 to 1 at the ₹291.30 close and 4.35 to 1 at ₹288. Both clear the 2.0 floor for a core compounder. The bull and bear values are ₹465 and ₹240.

On size, the risk-unit rule takes a loss budget, in basis points of the portfolio, divided by the stop distance, and caps the answer at the tier limit. Today the tier is TIER_0 and the size is zero, because the verdict is a rejection. If the red line lifts and the fundamentals hold, we would use TIER_3, which has a 50 basis point risk budget and a cap of 3.5%. At a 5.9% stop distance the formula gives 8.5%, so the cap binds at 3.5%. In the present capitulation-watch regime, where only 17.7% of stocks trade above their 50-day average,[1] new names may start at up to half the tier weight, which is 1.75% of the portfolio.

The let-winners-run rule applies once the position works. We would not trim only because the relative strength index passes 70 or because the weight passes 15%. We would trail the stop to the rising 21-day exponential average or 50-day average and let the position grow to 22%, unless the trend or the fundamentals break. For this company the fundamentals test is simple. The winter-quarter operating margins should stay near last year's 43% and 37%, and the owned openings should not slip again. If the December quarter margin falls below 38%, the rule stops us from adding even if the chart looks good.
