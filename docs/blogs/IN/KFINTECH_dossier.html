1. What the company does

KFin Technologies Limited is a registrar and transfer agent: it keeps the official record of who owns what for the funds and companies that hire it and handles the paperwork around purchases, redemptions, dividends and share issues. It was incorporated on 8 June 2017 and began trading on 29 December 2022.[11][33] It says it is the largest investor solutions provider to Indian mutual funds by number of asset managers served, and the only Indian provider serving funds, wealth managers, pension schemes and corporate issuers together.[1]

The mutual fund line is the core. A fund house pays KFin per unit of assets served and per transaction, so revenue rises with markets and inflows. In the June 2026 quarter KFin served 26 operating fund houses with average assets of ₹27.3 trillion, a 32.8 percent industry share, and the line earned ₹215.2 crore, 60.4 percent of revenue.[3][2] The issuer line serves listed companies as registrar for dividends, splits, mergers and listings: 11,275 clients, 174.8 million investor folios, a 50.0 percent share of NSE 500 companies by value. It is seasonal, with corporate actions about 30 percent of its revenue and the September and December quarters busiest.[3][4][5] The younger lines are alternative funds (731 funds, 37.3 percent share), the pension system (2.33 million subscribers, 12.2 percent share), wealth platforms and the international business (511 clients).[3][2]

Most international growth came from one purchase: on 13 October 2025 KFin bought 51 percent of Ascent Fund Services (Singapore) for ₹307.7 crore (US$34.68 million), with the other 49 percent to be bought in three tranches after July 2028, 2029 and 2030 at prices tied to Ascent's profit.[11] International investor solutions earned ₹73.0 crore in June 2026 against ₹11.8 crore a year earlier, so the 30.1 percent revenue growth to ₹356.5 crore includes the purchase and not only organic growth.[3]

How one rupee of fund assets becomes revenue

A fund house pays KFin a fee measured in basis points of assets served. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. Domestic fund revenue divided by average assets gives a yield of 3.15 basis points in June 2026, about ₹31.50 for each ₹1 lakh served a year.[3][39] The CFO says KFin earns about 3 basis points on Indian assets against 6 to 7 on Ascent's assets abroad, and wants international revenue to rise from about 20 to 30 percent of the total in three to five years.[25] Pricing is telescopic, so only assets above a threshold earn the lower rate, and fast-growing clients get discounts: concessions and credits were ₹51.99 crore in FY26 against ₹29.65 crore in FY25, 3.8 percent of contract price against 2.6 percent.[8][11] Asset mix also matters, because debt funds pay two to two and a half times what liquid funds pay.[4] What this means for an owner: fee per rupee of assets is falling faster than guided, and chapter 2 measures it.

Plain-English glossary for this report

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The mature core is strong in the ways that are hard to copy and weaker in the one way that shows in the price. Only two platforms serve the Indian fund industry at scale (KFin's 32.8 percent share and CAMS's 67.2 percent add to about 100 percent), and management says won mandates tend to stay.[3][31][4] But the fee per rupee of assets is falling faster than guided and the group margin has dropped 7.3 points in a year.[1][39] The numbers sit in the tables of chapter 3.

Competition: a two-company market

The only listed rival is CAMS. Its June 2026 quarter shows revenue up 11.5 percent, an EBITDA margin of 46.3 percent (up 270 basis points) and profit up 17.6 percent, while KFin's margin fell 730 basis points to 34.2 percent and its profit fell 2.6 percent.[31][1] CAMS also grew equity assets faster, 17.6 percent against 14.1 percent, and KFin's share of industry equity assets has slipped from 34.9 percent in FY23 to 32.4 percent. Management blames client choices it cannot control: heavier gold, silver and index-fund business, which pays less, and fewer hybrid funds.[31][2][5] CAMS trades at 32.6 times trailing earnings with a 47 percent return on capital, KFin at 40.6 times. Both fell on 23 October, KFin 5.15 percent and CAMS 3.1 percent, for a reason we could not source.[30][33] What this means for an owner: share is stable, and the rival earns more on each rupee of revenue.

The fee yield: the number that is falling

The yield was 3.60 basis points in June 2024, 3.43 in June 2025 and 3.15 in June 2026, which is 8.2 percent lower than a year earlier and 12.5 percent lower than two years earlier.[16][3][39] In July 2025 management guided a fall of 3.5 to 4 percent a year and said it expected no further compression that year. The yield then fell from 3.43 to 3.23 by March 2026, and the FY26 average of 3.37 was 7.4 percent below FY25's 3.64.[8][16][39] Management ties the latest 2 percent step partly (about 30 percent) to a move from debt funds into liquid funds and the rest to a provision for a large renewal not yet settled.[4] It still guides domestic fund revenue growth of 11 to 12 percent for FY27, against 6.9 percent in June.[25][2] Our central case assumes the industry's 4.9 percent September-quarter asset growth and a further 2 percent yield fall, which is about +2.8 percent on June and +2.2 percent on the year-earlier quarter. With assets up 2.5 percent and yield down 1 percent a quarter after that, domestic fund revenue for FY27 is about 4.2 percent above FY26's ₹852.8 crore.[29][39] What this means for an owner: until the yield stops falling, extra assets do not turn into extra profit at the rate the price assumes.

The rules that set the price

From 1 April 2026 the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) replaced the single total expense ratio with a capped base expense ratio plus separate statutory costs. It cut the cap for small equity schemes from 2.25 to 2.10 percent and listed registrar fees among the capped costs.[27] Every fund house therefore has a reason to push its registrar on price. Management says most contracts were renegotiated last year with one renewal this year and one next, but the June yield step shows the pressure continued.[5][8][4] SEBI also settled a case with KFin in August 2025 for ₹87.75 lakh. The SEBI copy we read (integrity not verified) says it was paid. An inspection covering February to November 2023 had alleged due-diligence failures on demat requests and unmarked folios lacking PAN or bank details.[26] A consultation of August 2025 on one fee structure for all registrars has no outcome that we found.[28] What this means for an owner: the rule risk is a steady fee squeeze and a small compliance cost, which we price through the yield.

International and issuer lines: growth with thinner margins

The annual report's segment results show why the margin fell. In FY26 the domestic fund segment earned 56.7 percent on its revenue (60.1 in FY25), the issuer segment 49.2 (51.8) and the international segment 9.6 (22.2), before central costs.[11] Ascent is the drag: management says its EBITDA margin is about 8.4 percent, and our arithmetic from the presentation gives 6.3 percent in June (₹3.5 crore on ₹56.1 crore of revenue).[4][2] Management targets double digits within twelve months. The April 2025 aim was to lift Ascent to KFin's own margin over time.[4][10] The price paid is high: ₹307.7 crore for 51 percent plus ₹579.1 crore of deferred and contingent payment for the rest, about 4.2 times annual revenue and about 50 times EBITDA at an 8.4 percent margin (our arithmetic on US$23.6 million of annual revenue).[11][4] The issuer line is seasonal, with ₹30.5 crore in June 2025, ₹41.7 crore in September and ₹32.6 crore in March. Management expects tepid corporate actions in September and names Jio Platforms as a mandate, for which KFin is registrar. We found no disclosure of the fee.[16][3][4][34] What this means for an owner: the growth lines add revenue today and margin later.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on assumptions, and some are weak. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each, the evidence that would show it wrong and when we would know. The red-team case against the investment: earnings have been flat for five quarters while the price asks for about 15.6 percent growth a year, the fee yield is falling faster than guided, and a sponsor with no lock-in is still selling. The red-team case against the verdict: the business passes every other test and Layer 2 qualifies, so a chart rule may keep us out of a rebound the numbers could justify.

Earnings: the margin and the yield

Our central FY28 earnings per share of ₹24.19 assumes the group EBITDA margin climbs from 34.2 percent in June to 36.0 percent for FY27 and 38.5 percent for FY28. It also assumes the yield falls only 2 percent more and then 1 percent a quarter.
