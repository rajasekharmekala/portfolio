1. What the company does

Happy Forgings Limited is a Ludhiana-based maker of safety-critical steel parts. It was incorporated in 1979 and listed on the exchanges in December 2023, and its founder, Paritosh Kumar Garg, still chairs the company while his son Ashish Garg runs it as managing director.[2][17][29] The parts go into heavy trucks, tractors, passenger-car engines, mining and construction machines, wind turbines, railways, and equipment for power plants and data centres. In the March 2026 year the company sold Rs 1,546 crore of goods and earned Rs 302 crore of profit after tax.[4]

The core process is forging, which means heating a steel billet until it glows and pressing it into shape between metal dies under enormous force. Forged steel is stronger than cast steel because the grain of the metal is squeezed into the shape of the part. The second step is machining: cutting, grinding and finishing the rough forging until it fits to a few thousandths of a millimetre. Happy Forgings does both under one roof, and 89 to 90 percent of its sales are parts that are forged and machined in-house.[2][3] Machined parts sell at much higher prices per kilogram than raw forgings, and that mix is the main reason the margins are high.

The best-known product is the crankshaft, the part that turns the up-and-down push of the pistons into the spin of an engine. Management says about half of revenue comes from crankshafts made to tolerances of 5 to 10 microns, and the company calls itself the second-largest producer of commercial-vehicle and high-horsepower industrial crankshafts in India.[2][14] The other products include front-axle carriers, steering knuckles, differential cases, planetary carriers, pinion shafts and valve bodies. Customers include Ashok Leyland, Mahindra and Mahindra, Tata Motors, JCB, Yanmar, Escorts Kubota, American Axle, Dana, Liebherr and Bonfiglioli.[14][17]

The company is paid per kilogram of finished part. In the June 2026 quarter it shipped 17,793 tonnes at an average of Rs 253 per kilogram, and earned about Rs 79 of operating profit on each kilogram, up from Rs 70 a year earlier.[2] Analysts call that operating profit EBITDA, which stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. Steel is the largest cost, about Rs 100 per kilogram on our division of Rs 177 crore of raw-material cost by that volume, and it is passed on to the customers with a one-month lag in India and a one-quarter lag on exports.[2][7] Because steel is passed through, the margin depends on how much machining and engineering the company adds, and on how fully its presses and machines are used.

The business is capital-heavy. Forging capacity grew from 67,000 tonnes in the March 2021 year to 148,000 tonnes in March 2026 and 152,000 tonnes by June 2026, and machining capacity grew from 29,500 to 75,200 tonnes over the same period.[2][3] The company is now building a Rs 650 crore line to forge parts of 250 kilograms to 3,000 kilograms for energy, data-centre, mining and wind customers, and a captive solar plant for its own power.[6][12] The Rs 650 crore programme is planned in two phases, with the machining part timed to the use of the new presses.[9] Both are meant to lift revenue from the 2028 financial year, which is why the next two years carry more execution risk than the last five.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Forging: shaping hot steel under pressure in a die; the result is stronger than cast metal.
  • Machining: cutting and grinding a rough forging to its final size; here, to tolerances of 5 to 10 microns (a micron is a thousandth of a millimetre).
  • Crankshaft: the engine part that converts the pistons' push into rotation; about half of the company's revenue.
  • Press line and tonnage: a forging press is rated by the force it applies; a 14,000-tonne press can shape much larger parts than a 4,000-tonne press.
  • Capacity utilisation: the share of installed capacity actually used; low use means fixed costs are spread over fewer parts.
  • Deemed export: a sale to a foreign-owned buyer's plant inside India, counted by the company as an export.
  • Pass-through: a contract term that moves steel-price changes on to the customer, usually with a lag of a month or a quarter.
  • Promoter pledge: promoter shares offered as security for a loan; a pledge can force sales in a falling market.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence of an edge is the operating margin, which is operating profit (EBITDA) as a share of sales. It was 30.4 percent in FY26 and 31.3 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[2][4] That was the fourth quarter in a row above 30 percent.[2] The peers that Screener lists show 15 to 18 percent: Sansera 18, Bharat Forge 17, Ramkrishna Forgings 15 and Craftsman 15.[22][23][24][25] Return on capital employed (ROCE) is the profit earned on each rupee the business uses. It was 18.2 percent in FY26, against 5.6 to 14.5 percent for the same four peers.[3][22][23][24][25]

Four things explain the gap. First, the machined share of sales rose from 73 percent in FY21 to 89 percent in FY26, and machined parts earn a higher price per kilogram.[7] Second, the 8,000-tonne and 14,000-tonne presses let the company make heavier and more complex parts than smaller forgers can.[2][17] Third, crankshafts need tolerances of 5 to 10 microns and long approval cycles, so customers are slow to switch suppliers.[2][14] Fourth, the company has run for four decades under the same family, and it has won quality awards from Ashok Leyland, Mahindra, Dana, American Axle and Escorts Kubota.[2]

Barriers to entry, and where they are thin

Forging at this scale needs hundreds of crores of presses, dies, furnaces and test labs, and years of customer approvals.[2][3] New rivals are therefore slow to appear. ICRA, a credit-rating agency, lists the long relationships with Ashok Leyland, JCB, VE Commercial Vehicles and Escorts as a credit strength.[17] The barrier is thinner in one place. The buyers are large truck and tractor makers who can ask for price cuts, and the company says its last domestic price increase before 2026 was agreed almost three years earlier.[6]

The edge also depends on cost control, because steel prices move. Management says it won price increases of 4.5 to 5 percent from domestic customers in 2026, and about 30 percent of the benefit was already in the June quarter.[6] That shows pricing power with customers it has served for years. It does not show pricing power in a downturn, when the same customers have fewer orders to give.

Balance sheet and funding as a strength

Borrowings were Rs 330 crore at 31 March 2026 against net worth of Rs 2,128 crore.[4] The debt-to-equity ratio is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, and here it is 0.15. Liquid assets of Rs 421 crore left the company with a small net cash position.[3][4] Both CRISIL and ICRA rate the company AA with a stable outlook, and CRISIL reaffirmed that on 7 August 2026.[17][18][19] Internal cash paid for most of the build. Operating cash flow from FY21 to FY26 was Rs 1,277 crore against capex of Rs 1,393 crore, which the company counts as about 92 percent funded internally.[3][12] Two items sit outside the balance sheet. The company had signed capital commitments of Rs 423 crore at 31 March 2026. It also owes export sales of Rs 183 crore under a duty-concession scheme, to be met over six to eight years.[13] Tax and indirect-tax claims that the company disputes total about Rs 6 crore.[13]

Where the business is weak

Trucks and tractors were 69 percent of FY26 sales by ICRA's count and 65 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[2][17] Both are cyclical. Truck demand follows freight and road building, and tractor demand follows the monsoon and farm incomes. Forging capacity was only 59 percent used in the June 2026 quarter and in FY26, so part of the fixed cost is idle until new volume arrives.[2][14]

The business also ties up cash. Working capital is the money locked in stock and unpaid customer bills, and ICRA puts it at 44 percent of operating income.[17] Debtor days were 93 and inventory days 134 in FY26, a cash conversion cycle of 193 days.[1] Free cash flow (FCF) is operating cash flow minus capex. It was Rs -16 crore in FY26, because capex of Rs 461 crore took everything the business earned.[3]

In short, the company has a real cost and quality advantage inside a cyclical, capital-hungry business. The next chapter tests whether the operating numbers back that up.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Guidance is the company's own forecast for a coming period. It is a fair test of this team, because management set specific targets in 2025 for passenger-vehicle share, capex and the solar plant. In May 2025 it said the passenger-vehicle share, then 4 percent of sales, would reach 8 to 10 percent in two years.[11] The share was 6 percent in FY26 and 8 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[2][7] That promise is being met ahead of time. The same call set FY26 capex at about Rs 400 crore, and the company spent Rs 461 crore, which ICRA calls in line with guidance.[3][11][17]

The record is weaker on timing. In August 2025 management expected the solar plant to start in the first quarter of FY27.[10] In February 2026 it said power savings would begin in the third quarter.[8] In August 2026 it said January 2027, with a contribution from FY28.[6] The plant has slipped by about three quarters. The 4,000-tonne press was expected during FY26 and was commissioned in the June 2026 quarter.[2][10]

One claim was loose. In May 2026 management said debt had been reduced over FY21 to FY26.[7] Borrowings actually rose from Rs 153 crore to Rs 330 crore.[3] Only the ratios improved, because equity and cash grew faster.

Two items are still open. The first is the FY27 outlook of late-teen volume growth with a margin in line with FY26.[7] The first quarter delivered 23.1 percent volume growth and a 31.3 percent margin, which is one quarter of four.[2] The second is the ultra-heavy forging line. Equipment installation is due by the end of FY27, with first revenue from FY28 and meaningful revenue from FY29.[2][6] The next four results will show whether the first-quarter pace holds.

5. What could happen next

A reverse discounted cash flow (reverse DCF) starts from today's price and asks what growth would justify it. We take the market value at the 27 October close. We assume profit grows at one steady rate for ten years. We discount those profits at 12 percent a year and add a terminal value of 20 times the year-ten profit. Then we solve for the growth rate that gives today's price. Free cash flow cannot be the base here, because it was negative in FY26 after Rs 461 crore of capex. Trailing net profit stands in as the measure of owner earnings.[3][31]

The result is 19.28 percent a year. A profit of Rs 327 crore would have to grow to about Rs 1,906 crore in year ten, which is 5.8 times today's level. The market is therefore paying for the last five years of growth to carry on for a decade, at about two-thirds of the past pace of 28 percent a year, with no downturn.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate above rests on one assumption that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest one, says what evidence would show it, and gives the date when we will know. Weakest assumption in the earnings estimates: the cycle and the depreciation step-up

Our FY27 and FY28 estimates assume that trucks and tractors do not fall sharply and that new capacity fills on time. Three things would show the assumption is wrong. One is Q2 FY27 volume growth below 15 percent. Another is an operating margin below 29 percent. The third is forging utilisation still near 59 percent by the March 2027 quarter.[2][6] The Q2 FY27 results are due on 5 November 2026, with the call on 6 November.[1] The Q3 results are likely in early February 2027 on last year's pattern, although no date is announced.[1][8]

Depreciation is the second weak point. It was Rs 26 crore in the June 2026 quarter, and we assume Rs 140 crore for FY28. Suppose the ultra-heavy line and the 4,000-tonne press come on stream but orders lag. Then depreciation would rise while sales did not, and the margin would fall faster than we assume.[2][18]

Weakest assumption in the valuation: a 39.8 times median from only 2.8 years of history

The median multiple is our calculation from 611 sessions. The company listed in December 2023, so the history is shorter than the three years that the scoring rule names, and it includes an IPO-era rerating.[29] If the market settles at 43.2 times, the central value rises to Rs 1,976. A second problem is the pairing: the median is a trailing multiple, and FY28 earnings are forward, so a blend of FY27 and FY28 earnings (Rs 42.5) would give about Rs 1,691. The strongest case against this report is simple. The company is reporting peak-cycle profit while committing Rs 800 to 900 crore of capital, at 51.8 times earnings. In that case the truck and tractor cycle turns in FY28 after a very strong year.
