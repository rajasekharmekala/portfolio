1. What the company does

Suprajit Engineering started in Bengaluru in 1985 and today says it is the largest maker of control cables in India and the second largest in the world.[11][12] A control cable is a steel wire inside a flexible sleeve. When you pull a lever at one end, the wire pulls a part at the other end. A car uses more than twenty of them, for doors, seats, the fuel flap, the bonnet and the handbrake. A two-wheeler uses about five, for the brake, throttle, clutch, seat and speedometer.[4] The company says it makes more than 300 million cables a year.[4]

The second product is the halogen lamp, the old type of vehicle bulb with a glowing filament. Suprajit says it is the third largest maker in the world by volume, at more than 80 million lamps a year, mostly sold as replacement parts in India and abroad.[4] The third group is newer and sits under a research arm called the Suprajit Technology Center. It is moving the company 'beyond cables' into digital instrument clusters (the dashboard screen), electronic throttle sensors, motor-driven latches and complete braking systems.[2][11]

Who pays? Vehicle makers and their first-tier suppliers pay for the cables built into new vehicles. Car and bike owners pay for replacements through the aftermarket. In the July 2026 investor presentation, the largest customer group, Volkswagen and Audi, is 9.5 percent of revenue, then TVS Motor at 8 percent and Honda at 7 percent, and the company states that no customer or group is above 10 percent.[11] The business is also spread across geography. In the June 2026 quarter, about 60 percent of revenue came from global operations and 40 percent from India, against 56 percent and 44 percent in FY26.[2]

How does it make money? It sells a very wide range of parts at very different prices: a cable can cost ₹6 or ₹600, a combined braking system ₹400 to ₹2,000 and a digital cluster ₹700 to ₹7,000, according to management.[3] Steel, zinc and other metal costs are passed on to customers with a delay, so profit dips when prices spike and recovers when contracts reset. In the June 2026 quarter, management said it faced a different problem, higher wages in India, and was asking customers to share that cost for the first time in many years.[3] Chapter 2B tests how well that pass-through is working.

How the group is built and what it bought

Suprajit grew in two ways: its own plants in India, and a string of purchases. The July 2026 presentation counts seven acquisitions, five in cables and two in halogen lamps, and the investor FAQ counts eight.[11][4] The large ones are Wescon Controls in the United States, the Phoenix Lamps business, the light-duty cable unit of Kongsberg Automotive, bought in FY23, and, in 2024 and 2025, Stahlschmidt Cable Systems out of German insolvency proceedings.[11][1] The FY23 purchase lifted sales from ₹1,840 crore to ₹2,752 crore in one year but pulled the operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, from 14 percent to 11 percent, which is the main reason the group's returns look lower than a decade ago.[27]

Goodwill, the premium paid over the book value of what was bought, stands at ₹158.0 crore on the March 2026 balance sheet, of which ₹139.5 crore relates to Wescon and ₹18.5 crore to Luxlite Lamps.[9] The company tests it each year using a discount rate of 13.2 to 15.2 percent and says no write-down is needed.[9]

Who owns the company and what the owners have done with their shares

The founder's family holds 45.26 percent through a family trust and direct holdings, and none of it is pledged to lenders, a figure that has stayed at 0.0 percent for twelve straight quarters.[28][27] Foreign funds hold 7.20 percent and Indian institutions 16.44 percent at September 2026.[27] Pledging matters because a pledged stake can be sold by a lender in a falling market, which can drag the share price down. Here that risk is absent, which removes one way a good business can still hurt a shareholder. The 41st annual meeting on 12 September 2026 was attended by the chairman, the group chief executive and four independent directors, and the auditor gave an unmodified opinion on FY26.[25][18] What follows for an owner is that governance is a small worry here, and the larger questions are about margins and cash, which Chapters 2B and 3 take up.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Control cable: a steel wire in a sleeve that moves a part when you pull a lever. The company's original product.
  • OEM: original equipment manufacturer, the vehicle maker that buys parts for new vehicles. The aftermarket is the replacement trade.
  • Beyond cables: the company's name for newer products such as digital clusters, electronic throttles and braking systems.
  • Content per vehicle: the rupee value of one company's parts in one vehicle. Higher content means more revenue per vehicle sold.
  • Pass-through: raising selling prices to match higher input costs. It usually lags the cost rise by a quarter or two.
  • Goodwill: the extra price paid for a business over the book value of its assets.
  • Other income: profit that is not from selling the company's own products, such as investment gains and currency gains.
  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is operating profit before financing costs and accounting write-offs on assets.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share. It is the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • ROCE stands for return on capital employed. It is the profit earned each year on the money that owners and lenders have put into the business.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow, which is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment.
  • RSI stands for relative strength index. It is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been rising; above 70 is hot and below 30 is cold.
  • ATR stands for average true range. It is the typical daily price swing in rupees, and we measure distances from support in ATR units.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing prices over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. They are slow lines that the price tends to bounce off.
  • A base is a sideways stretch where the price rests before its next move, and the pivot is the top of that base, the price a rise must clear.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein chart scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. Stage 1 is a base and Stage 3 is a top or a pullback.
  • A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better. The entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy, and a stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop. Our Core floor is 2.0 to 1.
  • Debt-to-equity is total debt divided by shareholders' money, and net debt is debt less cash and liquid investments.
  • Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by profit per share: the price paid for each rupee of earnings.
  • Guidance is the company's own published forecast for the coming period, and working capital is the cash tied up in stock and customer bills, less supplier credit.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong where it is oldest. Crisil, the rating agency, describes the group as the largest automotive cable maker in India and the world's largest supplier of two-wheeler control cables, and rates its bank debt AA with a stable outlook, which is a high grade for an auto-parts maker.[12] The same rating was in place in June 2025, so it is not new.[13] The company's own slide puts it number one in India by a wide margin and number two worldwide in cables and in halogen lamps, while its FAQ calls it third in lamps, so we treat it as top three in lamps.[11][4] Emkay Global's July 2025 note ranks Suprajit number two in the world in control cables, after Hilex, a Japanese firm that works mainly with Japanese vehicle makers, which shows where the room for share gain is.[15]

Why is that position hard to copy? A vehicle model stays in production for five to seven years, and the cable supplier is chosen before the launch.[5] Management said that a project awarded five years ago drops off about every fifth or sixth year and has to be replaced by new wins, so growth depends on winning, not just keeping.[8] Suprajit also builds plants close to customers in India, the US, Mexico, Hungary, Morocco and China, so a car maker can ask it to ship from the cheapest or the safest place when tariffs change.[11] The company says it adds capacity in small, low-cost modules, which keeps capital needs light.[11]

The market it sells into

India is the growth market. Total auto-component turnover rose 12.7 percent to ₹7.6 lakh crore in FY26, with supplies to vehicle makers up 16.3 percent and the aftermarket up 9 percent, according to the industry body ACMA.[22] The same body expects 8 to 10 percent growth in FY27.[23] Vehicle sales have been stronger than that: in the first half of FY27 passenger-vehicle sales rose 28.3 percent and two-wheeler sales 17.6 percent, and passenger vehicles rose 30.7 percent in the July to September quarter alone.[21][20] Suprajit told investors that the overseas car and non-automotive markets stayed flat to weak, so its overseas growth comes from winning share, not from the market.[2][3] The company put the direct effect of an extra 25 percent US tariff on its exports at about US$0.5 million.[19]

The Indian tailwind has two soft spots for this company. First, Suprajit's Indian division grew 20.8 percent in the June quarter while the two-wheeler industry grew 22.8 percent, so it did not clearly outgrow its home market.[2] Second, management said that for the full year FY26 the Indian division, once the lamp business is taken out, grew in line with the industry or slightly ahead after counting price cuts it gave customers.[5]

What makes the business strong

Three things stand out. The first is the spread of customers: no customer above 10 percent, with 40 percent of revenue from a long tail of smaller names, so no single vehicle maker can break the earnings.[11] The second is the restructuring now behind it. The Global Cables division earned an operating margin of 12.6 percent in the June 2026 quarter against 5.8 percent a year earlier, after the company shut a Polish plant, moved a German warehouse to Hungary, merged two Mexican plants into one and cut German staff.[2][4] The third is the electronics line, which grew 48 percent in the quarter, and management says July was its highest sales month ever.[2][3]

A fourth strength is financial. Crisil reports net worth of ₹1,171 crore (lower than the ₹1,436.9 crore reported, because the agency adjusts reported net worth by its own method, which the text we read does not itemise), gearing of 0.67 times, interest cover of 6.60 times and bank limits used to only 69.63 percent on average over the year.[12] It would cut the rating if operating margin fell below 9 percent or cash fell below ₹100 crore, and the company is well away from both lines.[12] It also signed a captive solar power deal for its Uttar Pradesh plants in October 2026, for a stake costing ₹38.4 lakh, to lower its electricity bill.[26]

What makes the business weak

Margins are the weak point. The operating margin was 14 to 17 percent from FY15 to FY22, then 10 to 11 percent from FY23 to FY26 after the larger overseas purchases.[27] In the June 2026 quarter the Indian division margin fell from 14.9 to 12.8 percent and the lamp division margin from 12.8 to 6.7 percent, because raw-material and wage increases had not yet been passed to customers.[2] Management says the pass-through will complete in the next two quarters, but wage pass-through is still being negotiated: some customers have agreed to pay and some have not.[3]

A second weakness is technology risk. Speedometer and throttle cables are being replaced by electronics in many two-wheelers, and halogen lamps are giving way to LED lamps. Management answers with its own electronics and braking lines and says the lamp business will see 'a slow sunset', not a sudden death.[2][5] It cannot yet say what the company's content per vehicle is, and said so on the August call, so the growth in each vehicle's spend is a claim and not a number.[3] Analysts at Nirmal Bang guess 25 to 30 percent more content per vehicle over three to five years, but that is their estimate, not a company figure.[14]

A third weakness is the cost of the overseas turnaround. The five loss-making subsidiaries lost a combined ₹147 crore (₹1,469.5 million) after tax in FY26, led by Suprajit Germany and Suprajit Brownsville, which lost about ₹59 crore each; Chapter 3 shows the table.[9] Trade tariffs add a further drag: the company says it absorbs US$1 million to US$2 million a year of Mexican steel duties it cannot pass on.[5] The recovery of earlier US duties, a claim of about US$6 million, is still before the court.[5]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management guides in public and the record is long enough to check. The strongest promise was about the German purchase. In August 2025 it said the acquired SCS business would reach break-even operating profit by the fourth quarter of FY26.[8] In the March 2026 quarter SCS earned ₹27 million, a 2.1 percent margin, after losing ₹176 million in the June 2025 quarter.[4] That promise was kept, although for the full year SCS still lost ₹237 million at the operating line.[4] The company also said the Controls division would reach double-digit margins by the end of FY26. It reached 11 percent excluding SCS.[8][4]

The record is less clean on growth, cash and timing. In November 2025 management admitted that growth was below its own double-digit goal, and the full year came in at 8.7 percent excluding SCS.[7][4] Capex was guided at ₹150 to 160 crore spread over two years, and ₹118.0 crore was spent on property, plant and equipment in FY26.[8][9] The new technology building was due for inauguration in 2026 and is now due in the December 2026 quarter.[8][4] Tariff recoveries were expected 'within a reasonable period' in May and are still open.[4][2]

On capital allocation the record is mixed. The dividend per share rose from ₹3.00 to ₹3.50 for FY26.[4] The payout ratio, which is the share of profit paid out, fell from 41 percent to 26 percent because profit recovered, so the larger dividend is affordable.[27] In FY25 it spent ₹112.5 crore on a buy-back plus ₹26.2 crore of tax on it, in the same period it paid ₹82.8 crore for the first tranche of SCS.[10][9] Net debt then rose from ₹255.5 crore to ₹443.7 crore by March 2026.[14] The buy-back looks poorly timed against the German losses that followed, even though it shrank the share count.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could break. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong and gives the date when we will know. After that comes the strongest case against our own view, and the one claim on which the decision hinges.

Weakest assumption in the profit estimate: Indian pass-through and other income

We assume operating margin climbs from 12.0 percent in June to 12.8 percent in March, because Indian wage and material costs are passed to customers. Evidence against it would be a September group margin below 12.0 percent, or an Indian division margin that is not above its June level of 12.8 percent. A group margin of exactly 12.0 percent is not evidence against it. The December 2026 quarter is the decisive test, at 14 percent. Management said some customers had agreed to pay for wage rises and some had not. It hoped to win back about 100 basis points, one percentage point, by the second or third quarter.[3] We will know on 7 November 2026, when the September quarter is filed, and again on 12 November at the call.[16][17] A second weak point is the ₹10 crore a quarter of other income we assume. It depends on currency moves the company cannot control.[3]

Weakest assumption in the target: the 22 times multiple

The 22 times is a judgement. Against it, peers trade at a median of 38 times trailing profit, and Nirmal Bang uses 23 times.[14][29][30][31][32][33] For it, Suprajit's return on capital of 15.5 percent is below four of five peers, and its margins are lower. Evidence that would show us wrong is a September quarter in which return on capital moves toward 18 percent and the market pays 25 times or more. The multiple resolves slowly, over the next two to four quarters. If we are wrong on the high side, the cost is limited by the order level: at ₹456 the base case pays about 14 percent, but a lower multiple of 20 times gives ₹472, and the reward to risk would then be only 0.52 at ₹456 and 1.48 at ₹444, below the 2.0 floor across the whole zone. The order level therefore does not remove the multiple risk. It lowers it, and it is why we re-test the arithmetic before arming the order. If we are wrong on the low side, we miss part of a rerating but keep the capital for better setups.

Weakest assumption in the probabilities: that overseas losses keep shrinking

We give the bear case 25 percent because the overseas turnaround is recent. The German unit lost about ₹59.8 crore in FY26, and SCS earned operating profit in only one quarter.[9][4] If September shows the four reviewed subsidiaries losing more than ₹19.3 crore, or the Global Cables margin below 10 percent, the bear probability should rise. We will know on 7 November 2026 and again when the Q3 FY27 results arrive in February 2027.
