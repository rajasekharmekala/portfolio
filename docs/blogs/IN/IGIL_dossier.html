1. What the company does

IGI is a testing laboratory for gems. A manufacturer or jeweller sends a diamond, a coloured stone or a piece of jewellery to one of its laboratories. Trained gemologists and machines measure the stone and write a report. The report records the 4Cs (carat weight, colour, clarity and cut) and, for diamonds, whether the stone was dug from the earth or grown in a factory. Buyers and sellers use that report as the common language for setting a price. IGI does not make, trade or own stones. It earns a fee for each report, and the annual report counts about three dozen laboratories and 21 gemology schools in 10 countries.[7]

Volume and fee per report drive the revenue. In the quarter to 30 September 2026 IGI issued 4.35 million reports, 26% more than a year earlier, at an average realised price (ARP) of ₹891 a report, up 4%.[2][23] The average moves with the mix of work, because a lab-grown loose stone report costs less than a jewellery report. Quarter on quarter, ARP fell 12% from ₹1,010 to ₹891 because reports rose 22% while certification revenue rose only 8%; the company puts the gap down to mix, and the call explanation was brief, so we treat that swing as a number to watch rather than a settled fact.[2][3] The number of reports is the figure to watch, and the fee per report is the figure to protect.

Lab-grown diamonds (LGD) are the growth engine. LGD loose stones were 54% of certification revenue in the September quarter and LGD jewellery another 9%, so about 63 paise of every rupee of certification revenue now comes from lab-grown stones.[2] The CEO says IGI was the first international laboratory to certify lab-grown diamonds, in 2005, and the company describes itself as category-neutral: it certifies natural stones, lab-grown stones, jewellery and coloured gemstones to the same standards.[3] Natural diamond loose stones were 14% of revenue, natural jewellery 16% and coloured gemstones with other certification 6%.[2]

Customers are mostly dealers and manufacturers. In the 15 months to March 2026, 98.79% of sales went to dealers and distributors, and the 10 largest of them took 46.46% of that, up from 40.74% in 2024.[7] The company counts 15,803 such customers, so no single name is dominant, but a handful carry nearly half of the sales.[7] A large share of revenue comes from the Indian states of Gujarat and Maharashtra.[7] The annual report also states that nine of the ten largest jewellery chains are IGI customers.[7]

The model needs little capital. In the 15 months to March 2026 the company spent ₹81.8 crore on equipment and building work and earned ₹711 crore of profit, and it holds no bank borrowings; its only debt is lease liabilities on its premises.[7][28] It is controlled by Blackstone funds through BCP Asia II TopCo, which holds 76.55% of the shares. Blackstone bought IGI in 2023 for US$569.65 million and sold shares to the public in an IPO in December 2024 at a price band of ₹397 to ₹417.[11][13][14][10] The company renamed itself International Gemological Institute Limited and moved its year-end from December to March, so FY26 is a 15-month period.[7]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

IGI's strength is trust that took decades to build, and that is hard to copy in a short time. A diamond certificate has value only if buyers and sellers across the world accept it. The CFO told analysts that the moat has two parts: IGI was first to certify lab-grown diamonds, and its brand has 50 years of acceptance.[3] The company states a 50% share in India, and a 65% share of global lab-grown diamond certification.[7] The investor presentation adds that 70% to 80% of US loose diamonds are certified in India, which is where IGI's laboratories sit.[2] These are company statements built on a commissioned industry study, not audited figures, so treat them as direction rather than precision.

The fee is small compared with the price of the goods, which protects the fee. The company says a certificate costs 2% to 3% of the retail price of a lab-grown diamond.[2][3] The reasoning is that a shopper who is buying a first diamond wants a neutral grade before paying, and the retailer wants one to close the sale. The company also expects total certifications worldwide to grow from 33 to 38 million in 2025 to 75 to 80 million by 2030, a 17% to 19% yearly rate.[2] That is a commissioned forecast, so it is an aspiration that we do not use as a fact; we use it only to size the opportunity.

Scale feeds quality. IGI says it issued 16.45 million reports in the 15 months to March 2026 and that its database of stone images lets it use machine learning to speed up grading.[7][3] The management also says the laboratory is ISO accredited and certified by the Responsible Jewellery Council.[3] A small new laboratory cannot match that history of images or that trust quickly. A cheaper rival would still have to persuade the dealers who set the market, which is why management says entry is not as simple as undercutting on price.[3]

The weak points are specific. First, the biggest competitor, GIA, changed its lab-grown reports in October 2025 from a full 4Cs grade to a simple Premium or Standard label, and it charges a minimum of $5 for a stone that does not qualify.[18] If buyers decide that a cheap lab-grown diamond needs only a simple label, IGI's detailed grade is less valuable. Management says it sees no demand for the simpler label.[3] Second, one analyst on the Q2 call said dealers report grading inconsistency at IGI, which management rejected, citing multiple layers of review.[3] We have no way to test that claim from filings, so it stays an open question.

Third, customers are concentrated and getting more so. The 10 largest dealers took 23.63% of revenue of the pre-acquisition group in 2021, 33.94% in 2022, 40.69% in 2023 and 44.17% in the nine months to September 2024, per the prospectus.[9] In the 15 months to March 2026 the figure for the 10 largest dealers or distributors was 46.46%.[7] The prospectus warns that the loss of one or more of them would hurt results, although none left in the period covered.[9] The company has 15,803 customers, which softens the point but does not remove it.[7]

What lab-grown price deflation does to IGI's fees

This is the key debate on the stock, so here is the arithmetic. Wholesale prices of loose lab-grown diamonds fell 26% in 2025, 14% year on year in the first quarter of 2026 and 13% in the second quarter, and the index is down 96% since 2018.[15][16] Management says prices have stabilised: the investor presentation shows a one-carat stone at about $100 in both 2025 and 2026.[2] The two views can both be true, since the outside data say the pace of decline is easing but no floor has been found.[15] Natural diamonds have also lost value: a one-carat natural stone now averages $3,898, 51% below its 2021 price.[17] Natural jewellery certification grew only 3% in the quarter, and management links that to high and volatile gold prices.[1][3]

IGI's fee does not move with the stone's price. It is a per-report fee. An ARP of ₹891 at the ₹95.23 rate per US dollar is about US$9.4 per report.[2][24] Against a retail price of $300 to $700 that is 1.3% to 3.1%, which matches the 2% to 3% the company cites.[2] Against a wholesale price of about $100 it is 9%, and that is where pushback can start, because the manufacturer pays the fee and sells at the wholesale price.[2] Cheaper stones also bring in more buyers and more stones, which is why LGD loose revenue still rose 37% in the quarter.[1]

The risk is therefore not a fee cut that nobody has announced; it is a slowdown in volume or a push to smaller, cheaper classifications. The company's own prospectus warned that falling lab-grown prices could pressure certification fees, even though revenue from lab-grown certification had not fallen from 2021 to 2023.[9] The warning sign to watch is quarterly LGD loose revenue: it was ₹211.1 crore in the September quarter against ₹209.5 crore in June, which is flat.[2][3] Management calls that a pause, not a stall, and expects no slowdown in coming quarters.[3] We score this as WATCH in section 2B.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record since the IPO is good on delivery and weaker on forecasting. The IPO prospectus said most of the fresh-issue money would buy the Belgian and Dutch groups, the company bought them, and the ICRA monitoring agency reported no deviation in the use of IPO proceeds for the September 2026 quarter.[9][7][28] The company then bought AGL, a US coloured-stone laboratory, effective 10 February 2026.[7]

On guidance, the company is conservative to the point of being unhelpful. In May 2026 it guided to 15% revenue growth and 20% EBITDA growth for FY27, and it reaffirmed that on 23 July, when the first quarter had already grown revenue 23% and EBITDA 29%.[3][4][5][26] H1 FY27 delivered 27% and 34%.[1] On the October call management said it would 'keep outperforming' for the full year 'similar to the H1 delivery' but would not give a number.[3] A guide you can beat by double is easy to meet; it tells an investor little.

One claim does not match outside data. In January 2026 the company said wholesale lab-grown prices had been stable for four quarters.[6] Independent trackers show wholesale prices still down 14% year on year in the first quarter and 13% in the second.[15][16] Both can hold if prices stopped falling month to month, but the year-on-year drop shows the slide is not over. We treat management's word 'stable' as optimistic.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on a weak point. This chapter names the weakest assumption in each, builds the strongest case against us (the red-team bear case), picks the single claim the whole thesis hangs on, and says when we will know.

Weakest assumption in the EPS estimate: lab-grown volume keeps growing

Our base case assumes H2 profit growth of 25% and FY28 growth of 20%. That depends on lab-grown loose-stone certification, the largest line at 54% of certification revenue, continuing to grow. In Q2 the line was ₹211.1 crore, flat on Q1's ₹209.5 crore, which management explains as scale-up and a festive delay, while October is described as 'rock solid'.[2][3] The evidence that would show us wrong is two quarters in a row of flat or falling LGD loose revenue. We would see it in the Q3 FY27 results, which TradingView estimates for 3 February 2027, though the company has not confirmed a date.[32]

Weakest assumption in the target: a 22 times multiple on FY28 EPS

The multiple has no history behind it. The stock has traded for under two years, so there is no three-year median, and peers are rating agencies at 21.9 to 38.1 times.[29][30][31] If growth is seen as peaking, the stock can trade at 15 to 18 times, which is the bear case. The evidence we would watch is the market's reaction to the Q3 results: a beat that does not lift the price would suggest the multiple is already full.

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF: the cash base

The 15.5% figure depends on a free cash flow of ₹433.6 crore, scaled from a 15-month period that included a ₹118.7 crore receivables build-up and an acquisition year.[7] If we had used 0.90 times TTM profit the figure is 11.2%. The truth is somewhere in that range, and the next audited full-year cash flow (the annual report for FY27, expected mid-2027) is the cure.

The red-team bear case

The strongest honest case against us runs as follows. A diamond certificate costs about US$9.4 at the current rate; for a stone with a wholesale price near $100 that is 9%, and the wholesale index has fallen 96% since 2018 and 13% to 14% in the last two quarters on a year-on-year basis.[2][24][15][16] The manufacturers who pay the fee have the most to lose and the most power: the 10 largest are 46.46% of sales.[7] GIA has already created a cheaper, simpler label for lab-grown stones,[18] and a large grower could push the trade toward it. Blackstone has owned the business since 2023 and has 56.55% of the equity free to sell since the 1-year lock-in ended in December 2025, and on our reading of the 2026 rules must lift public holding from 23.45% to 25% within three years of listing, so it has both a reason and a right to sell.[11][9][20][34] The holding company's pledge on the TopCo shares means a lender stands behind the stake.[11] Finally, the stock moved 22% in a week on 3.5 times normal volume at an RSI of 78.6, a pattern that often marks short-term exhaustion.[36] On this case, a price of ₹257 (15 times lower EPS) is not extreme.
