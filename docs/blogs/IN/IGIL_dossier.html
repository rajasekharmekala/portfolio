1. What the company does & day-to-day vertical operations

IGI sells an independent assessment of a stone's identity, quality and origin. A grower, manufacturer, jeweler or retailer usually pays for the report. It helps the next buyer trust the description. It also gives the retailer a reference after the sale. Management says certification costs little beside the jewelry's retail value. Revenue depends more on report count and mix than on each diamond's resale price. [2]

At March 31, 2026, IGI reported 36 labs in 10 countries and 21 schools. These are issuer figures for a 15-month period. Customers send stones or jewelry, and labs route each job to graders and instruments. The lab checks the work, then issues a certificate that can be checked later. IGI says in-factory labs can reduce handling and turnaround. It does not publish a single network utilization rate or service-time breakdown. [3]

From parcel intake to report issuance

Grading uses color, clarity, cut, carat weight and origin. The annual report lists several tools. These include photoluminescence and UV imaging. It also lists UV-VIS-NIR, Raman, FTIR and EDXRF. Each tool checks different properties or materials. A finished report includes proportion diagrams and may include a laser inscription. The inscription helps link the report to the stone. A wrong natural-versus-lab-grown call can damage trust. One bad result can affect future reports too. These methods come from the issuer. IGI does not publish error rates or outside test results. [3]

In the latest call, Managing Director and Group CEO Tehmasp Printer said, “We issued 4.35 million reports in the quarter.” That was +26% YoY. He also said the same team was grading more stones faster with AI scheduling, while standards stayed constant. That is a useful productivity signal, but not a unit-cost result. Investors should watch whether report growth and margins stay firm as IGI adds staff and new markets. The company does not report a measurable error rate. [2]

Customer, billing and working-capital cycle

The FY2025-26 business-responsibility filing says 98.79% of sales were to dealers or distributors, across 15,803 counterparties, while the top ten accounted for 46.46% of sales to that channel. This is meaningful channel concentration, but it is not the same as 46.46% of all company revenue: the denominator in the disclosed top-ten ratio is sales to dealers and distributors. It gives a better picture than the broad assertion that the business is diversified, yet it does not identify the names or individual exposure of those accounts. [3]

The CFO reported ₹4,157 million of operating cash flow for H1 FY27, or 89% of EBITDA, helped by a ₹662 million working-capital release as receivables came down from a peak of about ₹2,885 million to ₹2,402 million. The conversion is healthy, but the release is a source of current-period cash and may not repeat. Watch receivables alongside revenue: if sales expand while collection days rise, the high accounting margin will convert less well into distributable cash. [2]

2. Competitive moat, industry structure & external learning hub

A certificate matters only when buyers trust its grade. IGI's edge may come from network reach and trade recognition. The company trains graders and opens labs near manufacturers. Its schools also train workers for the trade. At March 31, 2026, the annual report listed 36 labs, 21 schools and 10 countries. These counts come from the company. They do not show use or quality at each site. [3]

IGI is not a monopoly. GIA, HRD Antwerp, GCAL and SGL also certify stones. Some large retailers may grade stones in-house. The CEO wants an independent standard accepted around the world. This is a goal, not proof of pricing power. The moat rests on repeat use, consistent grading and retail trust. The filings give no customer-retention or churn data. [24]

The advantage is distribution plus reputation

Trust and distribution may help IGI win work. Local labs can serve manufacturers near their factories. Schools can train gemologists and keep them connected to the trade. In-factory service may reduce handling. The company has not shared an error rate or a measured accuracy lead. This limits how firmly we can rate its process advantage. [3]

Competitors limit pricing power. GIA offers natural and lab-grown grading. HRD Antwerp and others also offer reports. A trader may compare fees, turnaround and buyer acceptance. This keeps customers from being locked into one lab. IGI has a recognized name, but it must protect report quality. Volume growth matters more than an unverified share claim. [25]

Demand is shifting across natural and laboratory-grown stones

Q2 FY27 showed a clear split. Loose LGD certification grew 37%; LGD jewelry grew 47%. Natural jewelry certification grew 3%. The CEO said high and volatile gold prices delayed some natural-jewelry buyers. He also said festive stocking shifted from last year. IGI earns fees for certification, not for the diamond's resale price. Still, it needs customers to submit more items. The current tailwind is report count. The risk is that reactor supply grows faster than retail demand. [1][2]

2B. Operational business KPIs, structural potential & CEO walk-the-talk

The best operating dashboard is a small set of ratios that links reports processed to fees, cost and cash. Management has started reporting report volumes and average realized price, while quarterly accounts give margins and cash conversion. It does not publish a company-specific capacity utilization rate, report turnaround distribution, error rate or customer-retention series, so we cannot claim that higher throughput has already produced a measured unit-cost decline.

The business KPIs that actually matter

The recent quarter is strong on throughput and margin, but the composition matters. The four key tests are whether volume growth persists, whether average fee growth comes from sustainable mix rather than one-off acquisition consolidation, whether margins absorb expansion costs, and whether earnings convert to cash without relying on working-capital release. The fifth is service quality: current public sources describe methods and controls but do not provide a report-error trend.

6. Key risks, thesis breakers & bear case

The central bear case is a valuation reset if IGI's role as a trusted outside standard weakens. New LGD reports still depend on growers and retailers sending work to outside labs. A fast rise in report count can hide lower fees, higher marketing costs or slower collections. The company itself lists retailer self-certification, grading consistency and LGD price pressure as risks. [4]

LGD supply and retail demand can diverge

IGI's CEO said large Indian growers were adding reactors and could double capacity in three years. Q2 LGD submissions also rose quickly. More stones create certification work only when manufacturers and retailers submit them. If retail demand slows, higher production may not become durable report growth. The supply tailwind could then turn into a trade inventory problem. [2]

A third-party certificate can be displaced

Large jewelry chains can develop their own grading and disclosure systems. They can also promote a different laboratory. If buyers trust those systems, the chain may use outside reports less often. IGI responds with digital links, co-branding and consumer awareness. Repeat customer data are not public. That leaves the effect of these steps unproven. [4]

Premium margins invite imitation and execution drag

Q2 consolidated EBITDA margin was 61.4%. India standalone margin fell 340 bps as IGI added leaders, marketing and commissions. If those costs do not bring more repeat work, margins may fall. A slowdown in reports would add to that risk. Volume alone will not protect the valuation. [2]

Dealer concentration and working capital

The top ten dealers made up 46.46% of dealer and distributor sales in the annual report. That is a real, but not extreme, concentration risk. The filing does not name those accounts. A break with large customers could reduce submissions. Receivables fell in H1 FY27, but the period also had a working-capital release. A reversal would leave less cash for dividends and expansion.
