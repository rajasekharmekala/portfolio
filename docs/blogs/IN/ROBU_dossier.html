Terms used in this dossier

The terms below appear throughout the dossier. Each one is defined the first time it matters, with a comparison to everyday experience, so that a reader new to equity research can follow the argument without a separate glossary.

ROCE (return on capital employed) measures how much operating profit a business earns on the money tied up in it, both the owners' money and the borrowed money. It is the closest thing in accounting to asking how much a shop earns on the stock and fittings it needs to operate. A ROCE of 15 percent means that every 100 rupees of capital produces 15 rupees of operating profit before tax. The watchlist floor for this measure is 15.0 percent, and the company's most recent header figure is 33.8 percent[1].

ROE (return on equity) is the profit after tax divided by the shareholders' own money. It is a companion to ROCE that ignores borrowed money. A company with high ROE and low ROCE is usually leaning on debt, and a company with both high is earning well on everything it uses.

Debt to equity (D/E) is total borrowings divided by shareholders' equity. A ratio of 0.70 means that for every 100 rupees of owners' money, the company owes 70 rupees to lenders. The floor in Layer 1 is that the ratio must be below 0.70.

CFO (cash flow from operations) is the cash the business actually collects from customers after paying suppliers, staff and taxes in the ordinary course of work. It is the cash that a shopkeeper could count at the end of the day. PAT (profit after tax) is the accounting profit that remains after every cost, depreciation and tax. The two can differ widely. A business can book profit on a sale and not collect the cash for months. The Layer 1 cash test compares the two over three years: cumulative CFO divided by cumulative PAT must be at least 0.70.

OPM (operating profit margin) is operating profit as a share of sales. It shows how much of each rupee of sales is left after the direct costs of buying and running the business. EBITDA is a close relative, earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. The company reports under a new accounting standard, Ind AS, from 1 April 2026, which changes how some items are measured and makes older and newer figures less directly comparable.

Free cash flow is the cash left after the company has paid for the stock, staff and equipment it needs to keep running. It is the cash truly available to owners or to repay lenders, which is why the numbers section tracks it alongside profit.

Working capital days and the cash conversion cycle measure how long money is tied up. Inventory days say how long stock sits on the shelf before it is sold. Debtor days say how long customers take to pay. Payable days say how long the company takes to pay its suppliers. The cash conversion cycle combines the three, so a rising cycle means more money is locked in the business for longer.

SMA (simple moving average) is the average closing price over a fixed number of trading days. The 20-day, 50-day and 200-day averages are the short, medium and long trend lines used by traders and by this run. A price above a rising average is in an uptrend. A price below a falling average is in a downtrend. A moving average is a smoothed line, not a forecast, and it reacts late to turns.

Weinstein stage is a way of naming where a stock sits in its longer cycle. Stage 1 is a flat base after a fall. Stage 2 is a rising markup, the phase the watchlist wants to buy. Stage 3 is a topping or pullback phase, where the trend has stalled. Stage 4 is a decline, where the stock is falling below a falling trend. The stage is a label that summarises the moving averages and the price, and it is calculated in the ledger.

RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 gauge of recent price momentum over 14 days. Readings below 30 are called oversold, meaning the stock has fallen fast, and readings above 70 are overbought. An RSI does not say that a bounce must come. It says how hot or cold the recent move has been.

ATR (average true range) is the average daily price swing over 14 days, in rupees. It is a yardstick for distance. A stop 1 ATR below a price sits about one typical day's swing below it, which makes the stop a volatility-aware distance rather than an arbitrary percentage.

Swing low is the lowest price of recent weeks, which marks a level where buyers stepped in before. A stop is a price level at which the position is considered broken and should be exited. For a tactical holding the rule in this run is a stop below the lower of the swing low and the 20- or 50-day average, less half an ATR.

Asymmetry compares what can be gained with what can be lost. It is calculated as (target minus current price) divided by (current price minus stop). A tactical candidate needs at least 3.5 times as much upside as downside. The target must be anchored to a verifiable method, and an unverified target counts as a failure.

Momentum gate is a computed test that decides whether a stock is currently in a usable trend. Test A asks whether the stock is in a confirmed uptrend that is also leading the benchmark. Test B asks whether a stock is starting to enter an uptrend from a base. A stock that fails both tests receives NO_MOMENTUM and no allocation, however good the business. RS (relative strength) is the stock's return compared with the benchmark index over the same days.

Promoter and pledge. The promoter is the founder group that controls the company. A pledge is shares the promoters have used as security for a loan. A high pledge can force selling if the price falls, so it is a risk marker.

KPI (key performance indicator) is an operating measure that tests whether the business is doing what its strategy requires. Layer 2 uses three to five of them, scored as pass, watch or fail.

The verdict follows a fixed sequence, and the sequence is the reason the result can be checked by someone else. The first step is the ledger. The second step is research. The company's financial figures were taken from the consolidated page on Screener, which reproduces the filed profit and loss, balance sheet and cash flow tables[1]. Where only one source gave a figure, the dossier says so.

The third step is the Layer 1 gate. Each of the three financial floors and the three technical floors is tested on its own, and a single failure is enough to reject a candidate. The asymmetry floor is tested last. A failure at Layer 1 ends the scoring. The rules say that a rejected candidate is not scored on Layers 2 or 3, so those layers are reported as not evaluated, with the rule given.

The fourth step is the holding audit. Because the company is a current holding, the position is tested against the four-word taxonomy. KEEP_COMPOUNDING needs every gate to pass and a healthy Stage 2 uptrend. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP needs a high-conviction compounder pulling back to verified support. TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING needs an extended price, a hit target, or a position above the single-name cap. REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER needs broken structural support, two or more failed operating KPIs, or a thesis impairment. The stop is computed separately from the ledger.

Two rules were applied without exception. The first is that no number was estimated. Where a figure could not be verified, it is shown as not available, with the reason. The second is that the analyst-target field was not filled from a forecasting website. Forecasting sites publish algorithmic price predictions, which are not analyst targets, and they are not used for the asymmetry test. That is why the asymmetry floor fails, and why that failure is stated plainly rather than hidden behind an estimate.

The two verdicts are kept apart on purpose. The watchlist decision asks whether the company deserves a place among ideas that could be bought. The holding decision asks what to do with shares already owned, where the cost, the stop and the size of the position all matter. A company can fail the first test and still need a decision on the second, so this dossier gives each its own rule and its own result, and one does not quietly stand in for the other.

The result is intentionally conservative. The stock is not being judged as a bad company in general. It is being judged against a test that asks whether the cash has followed the profit, whether the trend supports a new purchase, and whether there is a verified price target that justifies the risk. The company fails all three of the big questions, and that is enough.

What the company does

Macfos Limited operates Robu.in, which the company describes as a specialised e-commerce platform for electronic and mechanical parts[1]. In plain terms it is an online catalogue for people who build things with circuits, motors, sensors and small computers. Its customers include hobbyists, students, product developers, engineering teams and institutions, and the range covers robotic parts, drone parts, IoT and wireless modules, 3D printer parts, development boards, sensors, motors, drivers, pumps, batteries and chargers, displays and DIY kits. The company's own description lists the website launch in 2014, the Android app in 2017 and the iOS app in January 2023[1].

The business runs in two parts, and the split is the single most useful way to read it. The first part is called Robu 1.0. It is an electronics distribution arm that covers product sourcing, inventory, online sales and fulfilment, and the company describes it as working with suppliers to broaden the range and to shorten delivery times[1]. An analogy helps: Robu 1.0 is the wholesale and retail counter that buys from many makers and sells to many buyers, earning a margin on each sale. Its economics depend on volume, on buying well, and on getting goods to the customer quickly and accurately.

The second part is Robu 2.0. Here the company designs and sells its own brands rather than only reselling other people's products. The brands named are SmartElex for electronics, EasyMech for mechanical parts, Simply Fly for drones and ProRange for original-equipment-manufacturer products made to the company's specification[1]. The company says its product-development team for this part grew from around four people to around twenty or twenty-five over two years, and that it added 650 ProRange products in FY26 across sensors, modules, filaments, drone motors, propellers, tools and measuring instruments[1]. An analogy: Robu 2.0 is the company moving from being a shop that sells other people's tools to being a workshop that makes its own, which earns a higher margin if it sells well and carries more risk if it does not.

The scale of the platform is large by the standards of a small listed company. In the June 2026 quarter the company reported 67,480 customers served, up from 59,771 a year earlier, and 1,19,661 orders, up from 1,14,066[8]. Management described the catalogue as more than one lakh SKUs, meaning distinct products, and more than 150 brands[7]. The company's own disclosures put its average monthly website and app visitors at 9,78,555 in the June 2026 quarter, up from 6,02,470[8]. These figures describe reach and activity. They do not describe profit per customer on their own.

The company is majority-owned by a founder group. The promoter holding is 69.12 percent as of June 2026, which is down from 73.66 percent in March 2024[1]. The promoters' shares are not pledged: the pledged share of the promoter holding is 0.0 percent in the latest shareholding data[3]. Institutions hold very little. Foreign portfolio investors held 0.20 percent and domestic institutions held 6.09 percent at June 2026, while the number of shareholders rose to 2,626[1].

What the company does not disclose clearly, in the sources available for this run, is customer concentration. A distributor that depends on a few large buyers is more exposed than one with many small buyers. The Robu.in model suggests a broad base of many customers, but no source in this run gave the share of sales from the largest customers, so that figure is shown as not available. What follows from this is that the company's risk profile depends on the mix between the high-volume distribution counter and the higher-margin own brands, and the next sections show how the numbers have moved between those two.

Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when it earns a good return on the money it uses, converts that profit into cash, keeps its balance sheet light, and grows without needing constant new capital. It is weak when one of those four things fails. Macfos shows the first two in an unusual pattern. Its accounting return is high, but its cash has not kept up with its profit. That gap is the most important thing to understand about the company, and it is the reason the watchlist verdict is a rejection rather than a hold.

On the strength side, the return on capital is high. The consolidated header shows ROCE of 33.8 percent and ROE of 30.9 percent[1]. The consolidated table shows ROCE of 42 percent in FY24 and 34 percent in FY25[1]. A secondary research note repeats the same header figures, but it does not add any independent check[26]. Read together, these figures say that the business has earned well on the capital it used through the period. That is a real strength, because it is hard to earn 30 percent or more on capital without a product or a service people want.

The growth record is also strong on paper. Three-year sales growth on the standalone basis is 57 percent a year, and three-year profit growth is 51 percent a year[2]. Consolidated sales rose from 125 crore in FY24 to 255 crore in FY25 and 309 crore in FY26, and profit after tax rose from 11 crore to 18 crore to 26 crore over the same years[1]. The first step in that growth was very fast, and the second step was slower. The pace is not a forecast, but it does show that the business has been scaling.

Now the weakness. Cash flow from operations was 4 crore in FY24, minus 23 crore in FY25 and minus 8 crore in FY26[1]. Across the three years that is minus 27 crore of cash against 55 crore of profit. The result is a cash-to-profit ratio of minus 0.49, which means the business has consumed cash rather than generated it, even while reporting profit. The Layer 1 floor for that ratio is 0.70. The company is far below it, and it fails the test by a wide margin, not a narrow one.

Why would a profitable distributor consume cash? The working-capital figures give the most likely answer. Inventory days rose from 95 in FY24 to 98 in FY25 and 122 in FY26. Working capital days rose from 51 to 68 to 81. The cash conversion cycle rose from 75 days to 88 to 113[1]. In plain words, the company is holding more stock for longer and collecting more cash later. A growing catalogue needs stock, and stock sitting on the shelf is money that has been spent but has not yet come back. The company does say that its very slow-moving inventory, meaning stock older than nine months, fell to 5.52 percent of inventory in June 2026 from 6.21 percent in March 2026[7]. That is an improvement, but it does not show that the total stock held has stopped rising.

The Q1 FY27 margin is the second weakness. Revenue rose 37 percent to 81.34 crore, which is a strong top-line result. But operating margin, which is the share of each sales rupee left after direct costs, fell to 10.61 percent from 12.52 percent a year earlier[1]. The company reports EBITDA margin of 11.83 percent against 13.40 percent, and PAT margin of 5.94 percent against 6.88 percent, on its Ind AS basis[7]. The company attributes the margin softening to scaling and to investments in capability. That may be right, but the investment is real spending, and it lowers the profit each rupee of sales produces. A business that grows faster than it can margin is a business that needs more discipline, not less.

Returns and replacements are a third small weakness. The company gives a cost-to-company figure for returns and replacements. It rose to 109.89 lakh in FY26 from 58.19 lakh in FY25[7]. The amount is small against 309 crore of sales, but it nearly doubled, and in a catalogue with thousands of products quality problems can spread quickly. The company's commentary notes this as a watch item, and so does this dossier.

The balance sheet is the company's best defence. Borrowings were 36 crore against equity of about 96 crore at March 2026[1]. Cross-checked against an accounting extract, the short-term borrowings of 3,538.38 lakh and long-term borrowings of 59.01 lakh add to about 35.97 crore, and total equity is 9,596.65 lakh, or about 95.97 crore[18]. The resulting debt to equity ratio of about 0.37 is below the 0.70 floor. Borrowing rose from 8 crore to 20 crore to 36 crore over the three years, so the company is using more debt each year, even though the level is still low. The balance sheet does not explain the cash shortfall. It makes it more manageable.

So the verdict on the business is mixed in a specific way. It earns well on its capital, it grows quickly, and its debt is modest. It does not turn profit into cash, its margins compressed in the latest quarter, and its working capital has been absorbing money for two years. The next section sets out the numbers one by one.

The number

The stop for the holding is 954.55 rupees. It is the level below which the tactical structure is considered broken, and it is the only number in this audit that turns a chart reading into an exit rule.

How it was calculated

The lower of the swing low and the 20- and 50-day averages is taken, which is the swing low at 980. Half of the 14-day ATR of 50.9 is subtracted from that level, which gives 980 less 25.45, or 954.55.

The inputs

The swing low of 980, the 20-day average of 1,068.9, the 50-day average of 1,110.86 and the ATR of 50.9 all come from the ledger for the 12 October 2026 session. No outside source enters the calculation.

The range

The stop is a single level, not a range. Because a thinly traded stock can gap through a level, the realised exit price could be lower than 954.55 in a bad session, which the liquidity note in the fatal flaws section describes.

If the number is off

If the swing low were measured on a different window, the stop would move by the same amount. A change of 10 rupees in the swing low would move the stop by 10 rupees, and the holder would then need to recheck the distance from the close, which is about 9 percent at the stop shown here.

The verdict must be read against the taxonomy, and each of the four options has a test. KEEP_COMPOUNDING requires every gate to pass and a healthy Stage 2 uptrend. This holding fails the cash gate and is in Stage 3, so KEEP_COMPOUNDING is closed. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP requires a high-conviction compounder pulling back to verified support. The company fails the cash gate, so it cannot be treated as a high-conviction compounder, and ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP is closed. TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING requires an extended price, a hit target or a position above the cap. None of those applies, so TRIM is closed. That leaves REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER, and the rule behind it is the one that matters.

The rule is fundamental thesis impairment. The reason to own a distributor like this is that it earns high returns and turns them into cash, so that the cash can fund more growth or be returned to owners. The three-year cash test is the direct check on that thesis, and the company fails it at -0.49 against a floor of 0.70. The shortfall is not a single bad year: cash was negative in FY25 and FY26, while profit grew each year. A thesis that depends on profit becoming cash is impaired while the cash stays negative. That is a judgement the taxonomy asks for, and the evidence supports it.

The verdict is also limited, and the limit is stated so that it is not over-read. The stop has not been breached, and the stage is 3, not 4. The taxonomy's broken-structure trigger, which is a close below the stop or a Stage 4 breakdown, is not met. A Stage 3 stock above its stop is not automatically a replacement. The replacement decision here rests on the financial gate, which is a stronger and more lasting reason than a chart signal. The chart would matter if the price closed below 954.55, because that would add a second, independent reason to exit.

The operating KPIs give a further reason to be cautious, although they are not the basis of the verdict. Working capital days rose in each of the last two years. Cash conversion cycle rose from 75 to 113 days. The Q1 operating margin fell about two points year on year. Very slow-moving stock improved, and customer numbers rose by about 13 percent. Scored against the usual distribution tests, the working-capital measure and the cash measure fail, and the margin measure is a watch item. Two failed operating measures would also support REPLACE on the second trigger, but the dossier relies on the thesis rule, because the cash test is the firmer and more directly measured evidence.

The stop and the verdict work together in practice. The stop is the level at which the technical case for holding is broken. The verdict says that, even before that level is reached, the financial case for holding does not pass the company's own test. A holder who wants to stay for the chance of a turnaround would need a different reason than the one this audit has found, and that reason would have to be written down with a level and a date. Without such a reason, the consistent action under this framework is to treat the position as a swap candidate and to compare it with the shortlist challengers before the next review.

It helps to see why the gates are kept strict rather than flexible. A hard gate is a simple pass or fail, and it protects the reader from the most common error in small-company research, which is to explain away a weak number with a strong story. The cash test is the clearest example. A story about future growth could justify a low cash figure in any single year, but the test is set over three years so that one unusual year cannot carry the verdict. The company's three-year figure is negative, so the story would need to explain two years of cash outflow, and the filings do not yet explain them.

The disposition is therefore REJECTED. The cash test, the technical stage and the momentum gate each fail independently, and any one of them would reject the candidate. Because Layer 1 fails, Layers 2 and 3 are not evaluated. The rule is that a Layer 1 or momentum failure sends the candidate to REJECTED without scoring. That keeps the process honest: a company is not allowed to score its way past a hard gate.

The disposition describes the watchlist question, not the holding question. Whether the company is a candidate for purchase is answered by REJECTED. Whether the shares you own should be kept is answered separately, in the holding audit, with REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER. The two answers agree on the fact that the financial case is not currently sound, and they differ on what follows from it: a rejected idea is simply not bought, while an underperforming holding becomes a candidate for a swap.

Layer 2 scorecard

Layer 2 is the scorecard of three to five operating measures chosen for the business, scored as pass, watch or fail. Its purpose is to test whether the business is doing what its strategy requires, which is a question that a clean balance sheet cannot answer on its own. It sits after Layer 1 for a reason. There is no value in judging the operating quality of a company whose cash does not match its profit, because the operating story would be incomplete. The rule is that a Layer 1 failure means the scorecard is not run, and this company failed Layer 1.

The status for this dossier is therefore NOT EVALUATED, and the rule is the Layer 1 disposition described above. The scorecard is not filled in with estimates to make a pattern look complete, and no pass or fail is recorded for the purpose of a score. A reader who wants a scored view should note that the scorecard would normally test measures such as sales per square foot of warehouse, client concentration, value-add margin change, and working-capital days. Several of those measures cannot be verified from the sources available in this run, and that gap is itself a reason the scorecard is not completed.

The operating measures that the holding audit reads are a different thing. They are descriptive readings that inform the holding verdict, and they are not a Layer 2 score. Working capital days rose from 51 to 81 over three years. The cash conversion cycle rose from 75 to 113 days. The Q1 operating margin fell from 12.52 to 10.61 percent, and the very slow-moving stock share improved from 6.21 to 5.52 percent[7]. Customers served grew about 13 percent and orders about 5 percent[8]. These readings are mixed, and they are presented as evidence for the holding discussion, not as a score for the watchlist.

Two conclusions follow for a reader. The first is that the company has a real operating engine: its customer and order counts are growing, and its catalogue is large. The second is that the engine has not yet translated into cash, and the working-capital readings point to that as the main issue. If a future version of this dossier is produced after the company clears Layer 1, the scorecard would need to test whether the working-capital trend has reversed, whether the Robu 2.0 products are adding margin rather than diluting it, and whether customer concentration is acceptable. Until then the scorecard is not evaluated.

It is worth being specific about why the scorecard is skipped rather than softened. A scorecard that is partly filled, with some measures marked pass on the strength of the revenue story, would look more complete than the evidence allows. The effect on a reader would be to suggest that the operating picture had been tested when it had not. Leaving the layer unevaluated keeps the record clean, and it tells the reader exactly which parts of the picture are still missing.

What would bring the scorecard into play is a change in the Layer 1 result. If the three-year cash ratio rises to 0.70 or above on the next audited year, and the technical posture returns to a Stage 2 uptrend, the company would move back to Layer 2 and be scored. Until then the scorecard remains a plan, not a result, and the dossier says so plainly.

Layer 3 entry posture

Layer 3 maps the distance from the nearest verified support onto a set of bands, and it turns that map into an execution posture: buy at support, buy on a dip, wait for support, or treat the level as broken. It is the timing gate, and it exists so that a good company is not bought in a place where the risk to the stop is too wide. Like Layer 2, it is applied only after Layer 1 passes, so it is not evaluated for this company.

The status for this dossier is therefore NOT EVALUATED, with the rule given. The company is rejected at Layer 1, so no entry posture is assigned. The ledger does provide the distance from support, which is 1.37 ATR above the 980 swing low. On the support-distance ladder used by the system, that distance falls in the NO_BUY band, which is reserved for distances of 1.2 ATR or more. The reading is shown here as context for the holding discussion, not as a Layer 3 verdict.

The NO_BUY band has a plain meaning. It says that the price is too far above its support to buy at the current level without taking a stop that is wide relative to the typical daily swing. A buyer in this band is asked to wait for a pullback to support, a retest that holds, or a new base. The stock is currently 70 rupees above the swing low, and that gap is what the band is measuring.

For the holding, the same distance reading has a practical consequence. The position is not in an entry zone and is not being added to, so the NO_BUY reading does not block a decision. It does tell the holder that any increase in the position would need to wait for a support test or a new base, and that the stop of 954.55 is the level that defines the risk of holding.

The distance measure is also easy to misread. A reader who sees a price above the 200-day average may think the stock is in a healthy place to buy. That is the wrong inference here. The 200-day average is the long trend, and the price sits above it, but the short and medium trends have turned down. Entry timing needs the shorter trends to support the purchase, and at present they do not. The band reading and the trend reading point the same way, which is that this is not a place to add shares.

The full posture would be re-run only if the company clears Layer 1. Until then the stop is the operative risk control, and the distance reading is a context note. A reader should not infer that a buy signal exists because the price is above the 200-day average. The 200-day average is rising, but the 50-day average is falling and the momentum gate has failed, so there is no entry setup. Layer 3 would require a clean setup before any timing decision, and none exists.

Fatal flaws and the red-team case

A fatal flaw is a problem that, on its own, would make the investment case fail. A red-team case is a deliberate attempt to argue against the verdict as strongly as the evidence allows, so that the verdict can be tested rather than assumed. The paragraphs below do both. It lists the flaws the evidence supports and then builds the strongest honest bear case, followed by the one claim that the whole verdict hangs on.

The first flaw is that profit is not turning into cash. The three-year cash-to-profit ratio is minus 0.49. Cash from operations was negative in two of the last three years, and free cash flow was negative in the same two years[1]. A business that reports profit and consumes cash for two years needs an explanation, and the working-capital data supply a plausible one, but an explanation is not a cure. Until cash from operations turns positive and stays there, the flaw stands.

The second flaw is that working capital is absorbing money faster than sales are growing. Inventory days rose from 95 to 122 over two years, and the cash conversion cycle rose from 75 to 113 days[1]. Sales grew about 21 percent in FY26 while inventory days rose sharply. For a distributor, stock is the business, and stock that waits longer is a cost the profit figure does not show. A bear would say that this is the signature of a company buying ahead of demand, and that the demand may not arrive at the price assumed.

The third flaw is that margins compressed in the latest quarter. The operating margin fell to 10.61 percent from 12.52 percent, and the PAT margin fell to 5.94 percent from 6.88 percent on the Ind AS basis[7]. The company attributes this to scaling and to capability investment, which may be true. But the bear's point is that the investments are being made from a cash base that has not yet been proven, so the cost is real and the return on it is unproven.

The fourth flaw is the accounting transition. The company moved to Ind AS from 1 April 2026, and the comparative quarter has been restated[7]. The FY26 figures differ slightly across sources: the consolidated table gives 309 crore of sales and 26 crore of profit, while the Ind AS summary gives total income of 311.80 crore and profit of 25.61 crore[1][7]. The differences are small. The bigger point is that the cash-flow history was prepared under the old standard, so a three-year comparison spans two accounting bases.

The fifth flaw is the small-company structure. The stock trades on the SME platform, and the volume on the ledger date was 2,805 shares, about 0.65 times the 20-day average[4]. A thinly traded stock can move sharply on small orders, and an exit in a falling market can be costly. The stop is therefore not a guaranteed exit price. The gap between the stop and an actual fill could be wide in a bad session.

The sixth flaw is that the promoter holding has fallen. It was 73.66 percent in March 2024 and is 69.12 percent now[1]. The pledge is zero, which is reassuring[3]. But a falling promoter stake, combined with a rising number of shareholders, is a pattern that a careful reader should watch, because it can reflect share sales or dilution, and the sources here do not explain which.

The bear case, stated as strongly as the evidence allows, runs like this. The company earns a 30 percent return on capital and grows fast, which is the profile of a business that deserves a high multiple. But its profit has not reached the bank for two years. Its stock and receivables have grown faster than sales, its margins fell in the quarter when it spent on new products, and its shares trade at about 41 times earnings. If the cash does not arrive, the multiple will compress, and the stock will fall toward the levels that the 200-day average and the swing low mark. The verdict is not a forecast of that fall. It is a judgement that the evidence does not yet support the high multiple.

The hinge claim is this. The whole verdict depends on whether cash from operations turns positive and stays positive, so that the three-year cash-to-profit ratio can climb back above 0.70. If that happens, the Layer 1 cash test passes, the holding argument changes, and the company re-enters the scoring process. If it does not happen, the thesis is impaired and the replacement verdict stands. That single measure is more useful than any single chart reading, and it is the one the next audited year will test.

What would change the verdict

A verdict should name the evidence that would change it, with a level or a date. Otherwise it is a belief, not a rule. The paragraphs below give the triggers in both directions for the watchlist decision and for the holding decision, so that the next review can check them without re-reading the whole dossier.

For the watchlist decision, the rejection would change only if the Layer 1 gates were passed. The cash test would need to reach 0.70 or above over the three most recent audited years. On the current figures, with FY24 at 4 crore of cash and FY25 and FY26 negative, that would need a large turn in the next audited year. The technical test would need the price to close above a rising 50-day average, which means above the current 1,110.86 and with that average turning up from its falling direction. The asymmetry test would need a verifiable target, such as a documented forward earnings estimate multiplied by a justified multiple, that gave at least 3.5 times the downside to the stop. The momentum gate would need to pass Test A or Test B. A change in any one of the Layer 1 rows would not by itself reverse the rejection, but a change in the cash test would be decisive, because it is the fundamental failure.

For the holding decision, the verdict would change in three ways. If the cash test recovers and the stage returns to Stage 2 with a price above the 50-day average, the position could be re-scored under KEEP_COMPOUNDING, provided every gate passes and the operating KPIs are healthy. If the price closes below the stop of 954.55, the structural trigger is met independently, and the verdict stays REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER with a second reason for the swap. If the company showed a clear turnaround in working capital, with the cash conversion cycle falling for two consecutive quarters and cash from operations turning positive, the thesis-impairment rule would be weakened, and the verdict could be revisited at the next review.

The dates that matter are concrete. The next quarterly results are expected after the trading window that the company closed from 1 October 2026 until 48 hours after the second-quarter results[1]. No date for that board meeting was published in the sources checked, so the review should be set for the day the results are filed. The next annual audited figures for FY27 will matter more than any quarter, because the three-year cash test is built on audited years. The review should therefore be scheduled both after the second-quarter filing and after the FY27 annual filing.

Some things would not change the verdict on their own. A rising share price would not, because the technical rules already show a stalled trend and the fundamental failure is independent of the price. A good quarter of sales growth would not, because growth has been present throughout and has not solved the cash problem. A change in the peer multiples would not, because the peer comparison is context, not a gate. The verdict is anchored to the cash test and to the stop, and those are the two numbers to watch.

In short, the holding should be reviewed against three checkpoints: the second-quarter results, the FY27 audited cash flow, and the closing price against 954.55. A holder who wants to keep the position should write down, before the next review, the specific cash figure and the specific price level that would justify doing so. That discipline is the difference between a verdict and a hope.

Holding trigger: a daily close below 954.55 rupees would confirm a broken stop, which is a second and independent reason to exit the position under the taxonomy.

Watchlist trigger: a three-year ratio of operating cash to profit of 0.70 or more on audited figures, together with a close above the falling 50-day average of 1,110.86, would be needed before the rejection could be reopened.
