1. What the company does

Robu.in is a website and phone app where an engineer, a student or a hobbyist can buy the small parts that go inside robots, drones, 3D printers and gadgets. Think of it as a very large hardware shop that never closes. The company behind it, Macfos Limited, started the site in 2014, added an Android app in 2017 and an iPhone app in January 2023. It lists more than 1.06 lakh products (a lakh is 100,000), from over 150 brands and 210 vendors. It is the exclusive Indian distributor for names such as Seeed Studio, Arduino, Raspberry Pi, Bamboo Lab, Creality and M5Stack.[18][15]

The business has two parts. Robu 1.0 resells other firms' products: development boards, sensors, motors, batteries, displays and 3D-printer parts. Robu 2.0 sells products the company designs itself under four brand names. They are SmartElex (circuit modules), EasyMech (mechanical parts), SimplyFly (drone parts) and ProRange (parts made to order by outside factories). Management says it wants about 10 points more gross margin (sales minus the direct cost of the goods sold, as a share of sales) on its own brands than on resold goods. Revenue from Robu 2.0 is not published, so we cannot tell how big it is.[5][22][24]

The company makes money on the gap between what it pays and what it charges. An analyst on the April 2026 call put the gross margin at 24 percent, and management did not dispute it.[23] After paying salaries, delivery, marketing and rent, about 12 percent of sales is left as operating profit. In the year to March 2026 that was ₹36 crore on ₹309 crore of sales (a crore is 10 million).[7]

Who buys? Management groups customers into hobby, education, research and engineering. Schools, colleges and labs under government schemes are a large part. So are start-ups and factories that need a few hundred to a few thousand parts a month. Very large buyers, such as a carmaker, deal directly with chip makers and are not customers.[22] In the last quarter of each financial year, company and government buyers spend what is left of their yearly budgets, which lifts sales and the average order size in that quarter.[24]

How a sale turns into cash, and where it can stall

A distributor pays suppliers first, holds the goods, and only then gets paid. The money tied up between those steps is called working capital: stock on the shelf, advances paid to suppliers and bills customers have not paid, less what the company owes its own suppliers. If sales grow fast, working capital grows too, and the company needs more cash just to stand still. That is why a growing distributor can show rising profit and still report cash going out.[23]

Macfos is at that stage. Stock on the balance sheet rose from ₹55.6 crore to ₹79.6 crore in one year, and advances to suppliers rose from ₹12.5 crore to ₹29.5 crore.[11] Management says it is not worried about debt, because almost all of the borrowing is turned into stock that is later sold.[23] Chapter 3 tests whether that is true by looking at cash that actually came in.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The honest summary is that Macfos has a good business position but a thin shield around it. Management says it is the widest one-stop store for maker electronics in India, and that it sees no other player covering all the segments it does. That is a claim by management, not a measured market share, because management says it is very difficult to put a number on a market that is largely unorganised.[23] The company's own estimate, from an internal study, is that the Indian market for these products could reach about ₹10,000 crore by 2030.[22]

The case for strength rests on four facts. Sales grew from ₹80.80 crore in FY23 to ₹311.74 crore in FY26.[18] The three founders, who are engineers from MIT Pune and IISc Bangalore, each own 23.03 percent and none of their shares is pledged.[27][18] The rating agency Crisil reaffirmed a BBB/Stable rating on 1 October 2026 and calls the financial risk profile comfortable.[15][16] The auditor reports no key audit matters, which means it found no single item that needed special attention.[14]

The case for weakness is longer. Operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, has slid from 16.4 percent in FY22 to 12.4 percent in FY26, which Crisil ties to a growing share of corporate business that gets bulk prices.[15] Management itself says exclusive supply deals "don't mean a lot" in a country as large as India, because a brand can hand its products to someone else if sales are slow.[23] The moat is therefore the catalogue, speed and service, which a well-funded rival can copy over time.

Evidence of competition and pricing pressure

Management expects more rivals. In October 2025 the chairman said that where there is honey there will be bees: more players will enter, and large bulk orders will carry slightly lower margins.[26] The company's answer is to build its own brands, which it hopes will earn 10 points more gross margin. That plan is early. Management said in April 2026 that within two years it will "either be in or out" of the drone business, and that drone sales to defence buyers so far are low in value and volume.[22]

A fair reading is that the resale business is a good, growing trade with falling margins, and the own-brand business is an option, not yet a profit engine. The option is worth something. It is not yet worth paying for.

Governance and disclosure: small flags, not a red line

Four small items argue for caution. The April 2026 deck had a clerical error and was reissued, and on the call the chairman corrected a printed order count of 3,00,000 to 2,67,126.[18][24] When an analyst asked how much of short-term advances went to suppliers, the chairman replied that "the number, whatever it is, doesn't matter".[23] The annual report discloses a customs notice dated 23 April 2026 that alleges a short payment of duty on imported goods; the amount is not stated, and no provision was made.[12] Finally, the revenue split between company buyers and consumers, and between Robu 1.0 and 2.0, is no longer published.[24]

None of these is a sign of fraud. Together they make the figures harder to verify, which is a cost for a stock with no published analyst coverage. The ledger shows no analyst consensus and no street price target for ROBU.[1]

The shares trade on the BSE SME segment, a market for smaller firms with lower trading volumes and a short list of buyers.[5][15] On 23 October the stock traded 6,105 shares, worth about ₹58 lakh, and the day's range was ₹900 to ₹1,064.90.[1][34] Our holding of 165 shares is about 2.7 percent of that day's volume, so exit size is not the problem. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 shows revenue, profit, cash and debt in plain numbers, and then runs the Layer 1 test. Layer 1 has two halves. The first half is the level: where each measure stands today against a fixed pass mark. The second half is the trajectory: which way each measure is moving. A company can fail a level test but be improving fast, or pass a level test while sliding. The framework looks at both.[4]

Sales rose from ₹55.51 crore in FY22 to ₹80.80 crore in FY23, ₹126.36 crore in FY24, ₹257.68 crore in FY25 and ₹311.74 crore in FY26. The FY25 figure includes a one-time bulk sale of ₹71.38 crore in the first half.[18] Take that sale out and FY25 sales were about ₹186 crore, so growth in FY26 was 67 percent, which is the figure management quotes. As reported, it was 21 percent.[18] Both numbers are true; which one you use depends on whether you think the bulk order was a fluke or a repeatable business. Management calls it one-time.

Profit follows sales. Net profit was about ₹11 crore in FY24, ₹17.9 crore in FY25 and ₹25.6 crore in FY26, and earnings per share was ₹24.75 in FY26.[7][17] The company says that over three years sales, EBITDA and profit grew at annual rates of 58, 53 and 53 percent.[18] Our own calculation of sales growth, from ₹80.80 crore to ₹311.74 crore over three years, gives 56.8 percent a year. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by one year of profit per share, so it counts how many years of profit you pay for; Macfos trades at 37.25 times.[1]

The weak spot is cash. Profit is an accounting figure. Cash from operations is what actually came in from customers less what went out to suppliers, staff and the tax office. In FY25 and FY26 that figure was negative every time, by ₹22.8 crore and ₹8.0 crore. The tables below show why, and Layer 1 then scores it.[9]
