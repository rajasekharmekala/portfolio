Before you read

Jyothy Labs makes Ujala fabric whitener, Henko detergent, Exo dishwash, Maxo mosquito repellents and Margo soap, and sold ₹2,944 crore of goods in the year to March 2026.[5][32] It has almost no debt, holds ₹997 crore of cash and investments, and its promoters have pledged no shares.[5][15] We rate it REJECTED because the share is in a confirmed downtrend and the recovery test, Test C, fails.[35][36] Profit is falling because crude-linked raw materials rose 30 to 35 percent and Henkel took back the Pril and Fa brands on 1 June 2026.[6][9] The 4 November 2026 results are the first test, and we would reopen the case only if the refreshed chart then passes Test C and our own FY28 earnings estimate rises to about ₹9.6 a share, as Chapter 7 sets out[17][35].

1. What the company does

Jyothy Labs sells low-priced products that Indian households use every week and replace often. A tin of Ujala whitener, a pack of Henko washing powder, a bar of Exo dishwash, a Maxo mosquito coil or liquid vaporizer, and a bar of Margo neem soap each cost a few rupees to a few hundred rupees. The company makes them in 23 factories and sells them through about 10,000 channel partners, who supply roughly 4 million shops, of which 1.4 million are served directly by the company's own sales force.[4][11] The shopkeeper pays the distributor, the distributor pays the company, and the shopper pays the printed retail price. The company's income is therefore a large number of small sales, repeated every month, with no single customer that matters on its own.

In the year to March 2026 the company sold ₹2,944 crore of goods. A crore is 10 million, so that is about ₹29.4 billion. Volume grew 6 percent and value grew 3.5 percent, which means the company sold more units and the average price per unit fell.[5] From the June 2026 quarter it reports three segments. Fabric care (Ujala, Henko, Mr. White, More Light) was 48 percent of FY26 sales and earned a segment profit of ₹264 crore. Home care (Exo and Pril dishwash, Maxo household insecticides and other home products) was 41 percent and earned ₹150 crore. Personal care (Margo, Neem, Jovia) was 11 percent and earned ₹25 crore.[4][32] Under the older reporting, dishwash alone was ₹959 crore (about a third of sales) and household insecticides were ₹196 crore, which lost ₹5 crore.[4]

The cost structure explains why this business is cyclical. Out of every ₹100 of FY26 sales, about ₹53 went on materials and packing, so the gross margin, which is what is left of each rupee after those costs, was 47.0 percent. A further ₹7.7 went on advertising and promotion, and 24 on staff and other running costs. What was left, ₹15.3, was operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax.[5][6] Detergent chemicals, soap noodles, plastic bottles and pouches are all made from crude oil or its by-products. Management says about 50 to 60 percent of its inputs are linked to crude, directly or indirectly, and that fabric care and home care, which together are about 90 percent of the business, feel this most.[7][6] When crude jumps and the company cannot raise prices as fast, the gross margin falls and almost all of the loss flows to profit, because the other costs do not shrink.

Three kinds of brand ownership sit inside the portfolio, and the difference matters. Margo, Neem toothpaste, Tuhina and Chek are owned outright. Henko and Mr. White are used under perpetual licences from Henkel with no royalty. Pril (dishwash liquid) and Fa (personal care) were used under fixed-term licences with royalties.[8][10] Management says Jyothy Labs acquired Henkel's India consumer business in 2011. The Pril and Fa licence agreements were signed with Henkel on 31 May 2011 and had automatic renewal provisions until 31 May 2026, which management describes as an automatic renewal every five years. On 9 May 2026 Henkel gave notice that it would not renew beyond 31 May 2026.[8][9] From 1 June 2026 the company stopped making and selling both brands, which management puts at 7 to 8 percent of group revenue, or roughly ₹225 to ₹240 crore a year.[8][23] The company has started arbitration in Singapore over the payment due for the goodwill it built, but it will not say what it claims or when a result may come.[8][10]

Who pays, and how the money moves

The company does not sell to the household. It sells to about 10,000 channel partners (distributors and wholesalers), who sell to shops; modern trade chains, online platforms and quick-commerce apps are served separately. Modern trade, e-commerce and quick commerce grew 26 percent in FY26, and the company says online and quick-commerce sales are growing 25 to 30 percent, but it also says general trade, the small shops, was muted in the June 2026 quarter because urban demand was weak.[11][6] Rural and small-town shops are reached partly by mopeds and vans, with small packs priced for low budgets.[11] Because most sales are in cash or short credit, the business runs on little working capital, which is the money tied up in stock and customer credit. Customers owed the company about 32 days of sales at March 2026, inventory was 80 days and suppliers were owed 74 days, and management quotes operating working capital of 15 days.[1][5] Distributor stock is kept at 15 to 20 days, which limits the risk of stuffing the channel to flatter sales.[6]

What the company earns on each rupee, and why it moves

The gross margin was 50.2 percent in FY25, 47.0 percent in FY26, 45.2 percent in the March 2026 quarter and 38.5 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[5][4] The advertising and promotion budget was 8.4 percent of sales in FY25, 7.7 percent in FY26 and 6.5 percent in June 2026, so management has cut brand spending to protect profit, which helps this year and costs share later.[5][4] The company's operating EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), its operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax, was ₹499.8 crore in FY25, ₹449.9 crore in FY26 and ₹64.7 crore in the June 2026 quarter, against ₹124.2 crore a year earlier.[5][4] The company has no subsidiaries, so the standalone and group numbers are the same. Its Bangladesh subsidiary was sold on 25 March 2025.[11] The next chapter asks what protects these margins and what exposes them.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when customers keep buying it, when it can pass higher costs on to them, and when it earns a good return on what it has invested. Jyothy Labs scores well on the first and third and badly on the second at present. That combination explains both why the shares were once worth ₹595 and why they are now about 70 percent lower.[20][35]

What protects the business

Brand position is real in several niches. Screener's company summary says Ujala Supreme holds about 84 percent of the fabric whitener category, that Exo dishwash bar and Pril liquid are each the second-largest product in their categories with a 13.7 percent share, and that the Maxo coil holds 23.8 percent of its category. These shares are undated, so treat them as a guide to position, not a current reading.[1] Fabric care grew 14.1 percent in value in the June 2026 quarter, with powders and bars growing in double digits and liquid detergents, which nearly doubled in FY26, growing faster still.[4][6][7] Volume growth excluding Pril and Fa was 5.3 percent in the same quarter, so people are still buying more packs even at higher prices.[4]

What protects the balance sheet

The company has almost no debt. Borrowings were ₹52 crore against shareholders' funds of ₹1,589 crore at March 2026, which is a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.03 (borrowings divided by shareholders' funds), and CARE Ratings counts interest cover at 90 times for FY26 and 54 times for the June 2026 quarter.[1][11] Cash and investments were ₹997 crore at March 2026, about 15 percent of today's market value of ₹6,429 crore, and the chief financial officer put the free cash balance at about ₹850 crore in August 2026.[5][6][35] CARE reaffirmed its AA (stable) rating on 23 September 2026, and says it would turn negative only if the operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, stayed below 11 percent on a sustained basis or if borrowing rose above half of equity.[11] That gives the company time, but the June 2026 margin of 8.4 percent is already below that 11 percent line, so the rating agency's patience is the thing to watch.[4][11]

What exposes the business: pricing power in a crude spike

Management says the inputs that hit it rose 30 to 35 percent, a move it had never seen. Management put the raw-material hit at about 15 to 16 percent. Price rises of about 3 percent and savings and mix gains of 3 to 4 percent cut that to a net hit of about 9 to 9.5 percent, in line with the 9.5-point fall in gross margin.[6] Price rises of 4 to 4.5 percent in total were far smaller than the cost rise because competitors cut prices on some packs. The chairperson and managing director said price rises were set pack by pack to match rivals, and that premium brands in home care were being sold at a discount to their printed prices.[6] The gross margin fell from 48.0 percent to 38.5 percent in a year.[4] Fabric care's segment result fell from ₹67 crore to ₹38 crore on 14 percent more revenue, and home care's from ₹46 crore to ₹8 crore.[4] An analyst on the August call said Hindustan Unilever and Godrej Consumer had each passed through about 5 percent in the quarter. Their margins fell much less: Hindustan Unilever's EBITDA margin was down 140 basis points to 22.6 percent in the September 2026 quarter and Godrej Consumer's operating margin slipped from 20 percent to 19 percent.[6][26][28]

How the last cost cycle ended

The company has seen this before. In FY22 the operating margin fell to 11 percent from 17 percent in FY21, and profit fell from ₹190 crore to ₹139 crore.[1] By FY24 the margin was back at 17 percent and profit was ₹370 crore.[1] The earlier cycle suggests that margins can recover within two years if crude eases. The difference now is that the June 2026 quarter is far lower than FY22 (8.4 percent against 11 percent for the year), Pril's contribution is gone for good, and crude was still rising in October 2026, with Brent closing at $109.66 on 29 October against $97.60 on 30 September.[1][4][33] The comparison is a guide, not a forecast: the FY22 squeeze and the current one have different causes, and only the crude-linked part is likely to reverse.

What the Pril loss says about brand rights

Pril had been run as if it were an owned brand. Management told investors it had renewed twice in ten years, that Henkel had never hinted at recall, and that it therefore had no basis to treat Pril differently from Henko and Mr. White.[8] The loss of about 7 to 8 percent of revenue shows the risk in using a brand the company does not own. The company says the remaining licences (Henko and Mr. White) are perpetual with no royalty, and that Margo, Neem and Chek are owned, so a repeat is unlikely, but the claim rests on the text of agreements that investors cannot read.[8][10] The Singapore arbitration could return money for the goodwill the company built over 15 years, but management has declined to give an amount, so we assign it no value in our numbers.[8][10]

Where profit is weak: household insecticides and the Pril gap

Household insecticides lost money in every year through FY26 because coils, the format Jyothy has led, are shrinking as buyers move to liquid vaporizers; the loss was about ₹25 crore in FY25 and ₹5 crore in FY26, and liquid vaporizers are now about 55 percent of the portfolio.[20][12][7] In dishwash, the segment's operating margin fell 7.7 percentage points year on year to 10.4 percent in Q4 FY26, before Pril left, because competition on price intensified.[19] Replacing Pril liquid with Exo liquid means selling at the same price as the market leader instead of at a premium, so the new revenue will earn less per rupee than the lost revenue did.[7][8] The common thread is that two parts of the old profit base, coils and Pril, are shrinking at the same time.

What Marico and the large peers tell us

Marico, the company that led us to this name, is a clean contrast. Its main input is copra, a farm product, which fell about 35 percent from its peak, so its margin had recovered to 20.7 percent in the June 2026 quarter, and it expected double-digit volume growth in the September quarter. Marico reports on 2 November 2026.[31] Jyothy Labs' inputs are linked to crude, which rose about 12 percent in October. Marico's recovery therefore does not carry over to Jyothy Labs; the better read-across is from the large detergent and home-care companies, whose growth was healthy (Hindustan Unilever's home care underlying sales growth was 22 percent and Godrej Consumer's revenue rose 19 percent in the September 2026 quarter) and whose margins fell only a little.[26][25][33] That tells us demand is not the problem. The problem is the company's cost position and price discipline, which Q2 will test on 4 November.[17] Pressure is also sector-wide: the FMCG share index was down nearly 16 percent from 1 January to early October 2026.[24] The next chapter turns these facts into five scored operating measures.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a weakest assumption. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when that evidence arrives. It also gives the strongest case against our view and the one claim that, if false, breaks the analysis.

Weakest assumption in the earnings estimate

The central case assumes the operating margin climbs from 8.4 percent in Q1 to 13.5 percent by Q4 FY27 and 14 percent in FY28. That depends on input costs and prices, and both are outside management's full control. Management said the benefit of lower crude-linked inputs would be visible 'more meaningfully from October, provided commodity prices remain stable'.[6] Brent was $97.60 on 30 September and $109.66 on 29 October, so the condition has not been met so far.[33] The first checkpoint is the Q2 FY27 result on 4 November 2026: operating margin of 8.4 percent or higher and gross margin of 38.5 percent or higher would be a floor; margin under 8 percent would mean the trough has not been found.[17] The second is Q3 FY27, expected around February 2027 (last year's call was in February and the date is not announced), where we need a margin of at least 11 percent.

Weakest assumption in the multiple

We hold the multiple at 22.6 times. That assumes the market does not mark the company down further for slower growth after Pril and a lower margin. The median of 33.9 over three years reflects a period of 17 percent margins and 3-year profit growth that no longer exists, and the chart's earnings series stops in August 2025, so the median is an upper bound.[3] At 20 times the target is ₹185, only 5.8 percent above the close; at 18 times, ₹167.[35] Fund selling is a warning: mutual funds cut their holding from 12.03 percent to 8.00 percent in the September 2026 quarter, though foreign investors raised theirs from 12.13 percent to 14.48 percent.[16] The multiple is therefore the second-weakest link after the margin.

Weakest assumption in the probability weights

The 15, 45 and 40 percent weights are judgement. We tilted them against the bull case because consensus FY27E has been cut by 23 percent in 90 days and the chart shows a fresh low.[35] The evidence that would prove us wrong is a Q2 result with gross margin above 41 percent and ex-Pril value growth near 10 percent, which would move 10 points of weight from the bear to the bull case and raise the expected value to about ₹211, and a Q2 result with margin under 8 percent, which would move 10 points the other way and lower it to about ₹177.[17] The weights matter because the expected value sits close to the 12 percent hurdle, so a swing of ten points of probability decides whether the return clears it.

Weakest assumption in the stop and the reward-to-risk ratio

The stop is ₹166.46, only 4.9 percent below the close, so the reward-to-risk ratio looks attractive (4.01 at the close). The ratio is flattered by a tight stop in a stock that can gap on results: the 4 November result falls before any order could be armed, and a stop on a close below ₹166.46 can be executed well below that level if the share opens lower.[36] The bear target of ₹134.88 is 19 percent below the stop, so a stop that fails to execute at its level is the main way a position here loses more than planned. Because of this we do not rely on the 4.01 ratio alone. The stressed ratio of 1.19 and the zone-top ratio of 2.73 are the numbers a careful reader should keep in mind, and the probability-weighted return is a better guide to the real odds than any single ratio.

The red-team bear case

The strongest case against owning Jyothy Labs is that the market is right to sell it. Input costs rose 30 to 35 percent, prices rose only 4 to 4.5 percent, and competitors cut prices on some packs, so pricing power is weaker than the 50 percent gross margin of FY25 implied.[6] Pril's premium brand, 7 to 8 percent of revenue, is gone, and its replacement, Exo liquid, sells at the market leader's price, not a premium.[8] Dishwash margin had already fallen to 10.4 percent before Pril left.[19] Advertising was cut to 6.5 percent of sales, which supports profit now but risks share later, and management says it will step it up, which would take more margin.[4][6] Urban demand is weak and general trade is muted.[6] Management missed its second-half FY26 margin guidance in Q3 and Q4, and withdrew margin guidance in February 2026.[14] If the margin stays at 11 percent in FY28 and the multiple is 20 times, the share is worth about ₹135, 23 percent below the close.
