0. Executive Thesis, Dual Verdict & Opportunity Matrix

Aluwind Infra-Tech designs, fabricates and installs aluminium windows, curtain walls and facades. Its customers are real-estate developers and large contractors such as Larsen and Toubro, Godrej, Runwal, Vascon and Prestige. The work is done to order from one plant at Koregaon Bhima near Pune, and a single project can run for one to three years. The company was founded in 2003, listed on NSE Emerge in April 2024 at ₹45, and reports results half-yearly. FY26 revenue from operations was ₹138.6 Cr and profit after tax ₹10.5 Cr; the H1 FY27 half-year to September 2026 added ₹74.0 Cr of revenue and ₹6.6 Cr of profit [1][2][5].

What it does well is visible in the income statement: revenue grew about 6.5 times from FY21 to FY26, operating margins have held at 11% to 14%, the auditor's opinion is unmodified and the IPO money was used as promised [1][3]. What it has not yet done is turn profit into cash. Operating cash flow was negative in FY24, FY25 and FY26 (₹-37.5 Cr in total against ₹26.1 Cr of profit), because inventory, receivables, retention money and unbilled revenue absorbed the cash as sales grew. Banks filled the gap and borrowings rose from ₹13 Cr to ₹46 Cr in 18 months [1][4][5]. This is the central fact of the dossier.

Our verdict on the business is CYCLICAL_CONDITIONAL with KPI health PASS_WITH_WATCH. We do not call it a failure, because profit, margins and audit quality hold and H1 FY27 operating cash flow turned slightly positive at ₹+0.8 Cr. We do not call it a compounder, because the incremental return on the capital added since FY24 is about 5% to 6.5%, below the 8% to 13.5% the company pays its lenders. We read the cash gap as about 60% to 65% structural, and we give the ₹200 Cr FY27 revenue target a probability of 10% or less, with a more likely range of ₹150 Cr to ₹170 Cr [1][5].

1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Stock Cycles

Aluwind was incorporated on 22 April 2003 as Aluwind Architectural Private Limited. Its stated purpose was to take over Alu-Wind Industries, a partnership between Murli Manohar Kabra and Rajesh Kabra [3]. A third brother, Jagmohan Kabra, joined the board in October 2018 and runs manufacturing in Pune; the next generation, Akshat and Govinda Kabra, now serve as Chief Technology Officer and Chief Marketing Officer [1]. Fenestration, the trade word for windows and doors, was the original business. Façades, the glass and aluminium outer skin of a larger building, came later and are now the growth story. The registered office in Malad (West), Mumbai, and the factory land at Koregaon Bhima near Pune are owned; the Pune experience centre in Kalyani Nagar and a Goregaon branch complete the footprint [3].

The company has three eras: a slow family-firm era to 2020, an Eternia-led window ramp to 2024, and a façade-led scale-up since 2025 that is straining cash.

The early cycle was slow. Revenue from operations was ₹21.28 Cr in FY21, ₹28.99 Cr in FY22 and ₹48.87 Cr in FY23 on the prospectus’s restated basis, while profit after tax stayed under ₹1 Cr in the first two years [3]. The breakout came with Eternia. Projects using Eternia products went from ₹0.57 Cr (2.67% of revenue) in FY21 to ₹25.28 Cr (51.74%) in FY23, and Hindalco supplied 44% of FY23 purchases [3]. That dependence matters because the anchor-partner agreement, signed in December 2023, runs for three years and falls due for renewal in December 2026 [3][4]. I found no public disclosure of renewal terms as of the research cutoff.

The FY25 margin dip is the company’s most important past cycle. EBITDA grew just 1.5% (₹11.81 Cr to ₹11.98 Cr) on 31% revenue growth [7]. The margin fell from 14.2% to 11.0%. The filings give no segment margin, so the cause is my inference: larger façade and window contracts, more labour and factory cost (₹15.0 Cr in FY25 and ₹23.3 Cr in FY26) and more rent (₹1.14 Cr to ₹1.87 Cr) as premises were added all arrived before scale benefits did [1]. Profit still rose 7.6% to ₹8.12 Cr, helped by ₹1.30 Cr of other income [7][1]. The back-loaded pattern repeats: H2 FY25 revenue of ₹63.8 Cr was 40% above H1, and receivables kept rising into September 2026 (₹54.7 Cr against ₹39.1 Cr at March), even as inventory fell [7][5].

The IPO was a fixed-price issue of 66,00,000 fresh shares at ₹45, worth ₹29.70 Cr, with no offer for sale. Corpwis Advisors was lead manager and NNM Securities was market maker, holding a reserved 3,30,000 shares [3]. It was subscribed 8.19 times, with 1,796 allottees [23]. Promoters and promoter group fell from 98.22% to 72.13% after the issue and a 62:10 bonus in January 2024 [3][6]. The shareholder register as of September 2026 shows promoters at 72.61% and institutions at 1.17% [6].

The prospectus stated two objects: ₹20.45 Cr for working capital and ₹6.50 Cr for general corporate purposes, with ₹2.75 Cr of estimated issue expenses [3]. The record matches the plan. The statement of deviation for March 2025 shows ₹20.45 Cr used for working capital, ₹6.49 Cr of ₹6.55 Cr for general purposes and ₹2.70 Cr for expenses, with ₹5.67 lakh unused; no monitoring agency was needed [10][11]. An auditor’s certificate dated 10 November 2025 confirms full use, and the FY26 report records no deviation [1]. My judgement: the spending was clean, but a working-capital object cannot be audited for effect. The IPO money was absorbed within a year, because FY25 alone added ₹19.2 Cr of inventory and ₹21.9 Cr of receivables and unbilled work [1].

Compare the promise with the outcome. The prospectus assumed inventory would fall to 72 days of material cost, receivables would run at 114 days and trade payables would be paid in 27 to 29 days, a cycle of about 157 days [3]. The FY26 filed ratios imply inventory of about 167 days, receivables of 90 days and payables of 118 days on the company’s own turnover ratios, a cycle near 139 days [1]. The measures differ in method, so only direction is reliable. Receivables came in better than planned. Inventory is far higher, and suppliers fund the business for about four months, not one. On the Screener basis the cycle is 181 days, worse than the plan (Chapter 3).

Capacity followed the order flow. At the prospectus date the factory covered 45,000 sq ft, with installed capacity of 160,368 m² a year and utilisation of 17%, 24% and 41% in FY21 to FY23 [3]. A second 44,000 sq ft premises was leased in October 2024 [22]. By June 2025 the company stated 18,600 m² a month of capacity at 50% utilisation, and a new CNC (computer-controlled cutting) line added 3,162 m² a month for ₹3.90 Cr including GST, funded by internal accruals and debt [9]. An imported Italian five-axis CNC machine followed, and FY26 plant and machinery additions were ₹4.11 Cr [8][1]. The company also runs an in-house powder-coating line, which applies a durable paint finish to extrusions, and in FY26 it signed a toll agreement so that Eternia’s West and South India window orders can use its idle capacity [2][1].

The latest chapter is about funding. Borrowings rose from ₹13.1 Cr at March 2025 to ₹41.3 Cr at March 2026 and ₹46.1 Cr at September 2026, and the board sought shareholder approval at the 3 September 2026 AGM to raise the borrowing ceiling from ₹125 Cr to ₹200 Cr [1][5]. The order book was ₹302 Cr in May 2025, ₹315 Cr in November 2025 and ₹300 Cr in May 2026, which suggests order inflow roughly matched billing without growing the backlog [7][8][2]. Management guides FY27 revenue to ₹200 Cr. With H1 FY27 at ₹74.0 Cr, the second half must deliver ₹126 Cr, 72% above H2 FY26’s ₹73.1 Cr [2][5]. H2 has exceeded H1 in each year, but never by that margin; I would put the chance of reaching ₹200 Cr at 10% or less.

2. What the Company Does: Segments, Revenue Mix & Concrete Examples

Aluwind designs, fabricates, supplies and installs aluminium-and-glass building envelopes. In practice that is four product families: system windows and doors; unitised and semi-unitised curtain walls (factory-built glass-and-aluminium wall panels that are lifted into place on a tower); other architectural works such as aluminium composite panel (ACP) cladding, skylights, canopies, railings and spider glazing; and, since FY26, tolling, meaning contract manufacturing for another company’s orders [1]. The accounts do not report these separately. The FY26 note splits revenue only into “aluminium windows and façade work” and “tolling”, and the half-year filing states that the company has one reportable segment because tolling is below 10% of revenue [1][5]. Anyone claiming a window-versus-façade revenue percentage for Aluwind is estimating; I will say “not disclosed” where that is the case.

Windows and doors, including the Eternia channel

A system window is an engineered product: aluminium sections from a branded range, a gasket and hardware kit, tested for wind, water and air leakage. Aluwind makes these for developers of apartment towers. The customer’s problem is volume with repeatability: hundreds or thousands of identical openings per tower, delivered tower by tower as construction advances. The Godrej Reserve order shows the model end to end. On 16 January 2026 Godrej Properties awarded the design, manufacture, fabrication and supply of aluminium windows, deck railing, glazing and louvers for Towers 3, 4 and 5 at Kandivali, Mumbai, for ₹43.15 Cr excluding GST, to be finished within 38 months of the notice [14]. Aluwind’s described process runs: shop drawings approved by consultants, material purchase through its ERP system, pretreatment and powder coating of the extrusions, CNC cutting and machining, assembly, dispatch in its own vehicles and installation by contract labour [3]. Pricing is contractual and fixed, with revenue booked on percentage of completion [1]. At ₹43.15 Cr over 38 months, the order bills about ₹1.14 Cr a month.

The Eternia channel is the same product under a premium brand. In Tata Realty’s Tata Serein project in Thane, Aluwind installs the Eternia window system under a ₹7.5 Cr order due by the end of 2026 [19]. Capacit’e Infraprojects ordered Eternia system windows for the MHADA BDD Chawl redevelopment in Worli, Mumbai, for ₹8.74 Cr [18]. The largest window-led award is Runwal Realty’s October 2024 acceptance for aluminium windows across 19 towers in Mumbai, Thane and Pune, about 14.5 lakh sq ft, plus façade work, worth ₹89.9 Cr over three years [17]. That works out to at most about ₹620 per sq ft including the façade element, against about ₹1,100 per sq ft for the older Birla Vanya job (₹22 Cr for about 2,00,000 sq ft of system windows) [1]. Prices per sq ft differ sharply with window grade, so that comparison is a range, not a benchmark. Lodha’s Bhandup award of 28 July 2026 (₹12.59 Cr, finishing 1 July 2028) is written as a Full Material plus Labour, item-rate contract: Aluwind supplies everything and is paid a rate per measured item, not a lump sum [15].

Unitised and semi-unitised curtain walls

A curtain wall hangs from a building’s floor slabs and carries no load itself. In a unitised system the glass, aluminium frame and gaskets are assembled in the factory into storey-high panels, shipped to site and hung by crane. In a semi-unitised system the frames are fixed first and glass follows. The L&T Innovation Campus in Powai, Mumbai, is the company’s showcase: the FY26 report describes unitised façade panels of 1.5 m by 4.2 m and calls it the single largest façade order from L&T Construction [1]; Eternia’s own project page lists the campus at 36,000 m² of unitised façade [24]. The contract value is not disclosed in anything I found. The Capgemini SIPCOT campus in Siruseri, Chennai, is a second case with a known price: Vascon Engineers awarded design, supply, fabrication and erection of façade work for ₹43.03 Cr excluding GST, within 18 months of the letter of intent [16], the highest façade order in the company’s history. Eternia’s page lists a 12,200 m² unitised system at a Capgemini campus in Chennai [24]. If Aluwind’s scope is the whole area, the price is near ₹35,000 per m² (about ₹3,280 per sq ft), but the filing does not confirm that the two are the same scope.

Other architectural works, tolling and Clean-Tech

The third family covers cladding, skylights, canopies, railings and spider glazing (glass held by stainless-steel point fixings) [3]. It shows up as shorter, project-specific orders. The Mumbai International Cruise Terminal needed second-skin façade works from Ballard Pier Private Limited for ₹15.4 Cr within six months [19]. L&T Construction awarded façade and aluminium joinery for the Sabarmati depot of the Mumbai-Ahmedabad bullet-train project at ₹15.61 Cr [13], façade design, supply, installation and handing over at AIIMS Madurai at ₹19.21 Cr [20], and an ancillary façade package for the Navi Mumbai airport at ₹6.23 Cr in 3.5 months [21]. Prestige Estates ordered the clubhouse and entrance lobby façade at The Prestige City, Mulund, for ₹8.30 Cr over 18 months [19]. Tolling began in FY26 under a Toll Agreement with Hindalco Industries; the company amended its charter to add job work and booked ₹1.57 Cr [1]. Management says the aim is to manufacture Eternia’s West and South India retail window orders in Aluwind’s plant [2]. Pricing is undisclosed. In a toll arrangement the customer normally supplies the metal and pays a conversion fee, so tolling revenue should carry less material cost and less cash tied up; that is my understanding of the term, not a disclosed contract term.

Aluwind Clean-Tech, formed 21 May 2025 and 60% owned, plans façade cleaning and maintenance under annual contracts, including drone cleaning of façades, solar panels and windows [1]. It had no revenue and a ₹7.46 lakh loss in FY26 and a ₹8.90 lakh loss in H1 FY27 [1][5]. No customer or contract is named in the filings, so there is no worked example to give. Building-integrated photovoltaics (BIPV), which are solar panels built into a façade, are described as research; no BIPV order has been disclosed [2]. Both lines are options, not revenue.

That table is the heart of the façade-versus-window question. A façade job is larger and shorter. Material is bought early, panels are built in a factory, and revenue is booked as cost is incurred, so a façade can add ₹2 Cr or more a month to revenue and to inventory and receivables. A window job is smaller per month but stretches for two to three years, which means money is tied up for a long time and a slice of every bill (the rate is not disclosed) is held as retention until the defect-liability period ends. Retention is a customer’s withheld payment, released after the work proves sound. It stood at ₹13.68 Cr of the ₹39.05 Cr billed receivables at March 2026, against ₹1.69 Cr retention that Aluwind itself withheld from vendors [1]. Performance bank guarantees (PBGs), the bank-backed promise that the contractor will finish the job, are standard in façade tenders and weigh on credit limits [3].

So what does “the contribution of facades will increase” mean? Management links it to execution and margins [2]. I read it as three claims. First, higher engineering content should earn a higher gross margin. Second, bigger tickets spread design and project-management cost. Third, façade revenue arrives faster per rupee of order book. The evidence so far is partial: the H1 FY27 EBITDA margin was 13.4% against 12.3% in H1 FY26 (my calculation from the filings), supporting the margin claim [5]. But the same half saw billed receivables rise from ₹39.05 Cr to ₹54.70 Cr, and in FY26 inventory rose 88% while revenue rose 27% [1][5]. My judgement is that the mix shift probably lifts margin by one to two points over two years (about 55% likely), and almost certainly raises working capital needs before it lowers them. The order book of ₹300 Cr, about 2.2 times FY26 revenue, gives cover, but it only becomes cash through the process described in the next chapter [2][1].

Supply chain is the next constraint. The core materials are glass, aluminium profiles, ACP, mild steel, galvanised sheet, sealants, gaskets and hardware, sourced locally and abroad [3]. Hindalco supplied 34% of FY22 purchases, 44% of FY23 and 30% of H1 FY24, with glass from Sejal, Jajoo and FG Glass and extrusions from Ramco and Jindal Aluminium in the top ten [3]. The prospectus says the company has no long-term supply agreements and buys mostly on the spot market [3]. It also says contracts usually carry escalation and delay-charge clauses and that the company recovers cost increases from customers [3], but the FY26 accounting policy says it signs fixed-price contracts [1], and the FY26 results release cites commodity volatility as a headwind [2]. The aluminium price-variation clause, if any, is not disclosed, and I treat price risk as largely the company’s own. Larger raw-material and stores balances (₹44.4 Cr against ₹22.4 Cr a year earlier) may reflect early buying, which would be a hedge as well as a cash cost; the filing does not say [1]. The Italian five-axis CNC machine, a computer-controlled cutter that can machine all faces of a profile in one setup, drove the FY26 foreign-exchange outgo of ₹2.78 Cr; no foreign-currency exposure remained at year end [8][1].

The Pune plant is where the economics are decided. In June 2025 the company stated capacity of 18,600 m² a month at 50% utilisation, plus 3,162 m² from a new CNC line costing ₹3.90 Cr, which it expected to be fully used from H2 FY26 [9]. That makes a rated 21,762 m² a month, about 261,000 m² a year, against 160,368 m² a year and 65,378 m² produced in FY23 [3]. FY26 utilisation is not disclosed. Employees rose from 178 permanent staff in September 2023 to over 500 in March 2026 [3][1]. Site installers are hired through labour contractors on contract rather than subcontracting whole projects [3]. Labour and factory cost was ₹23.27 Cr in FY26, 16.8% of revenue, against ₹15.01 Cr or 13.7% in FY25 [1]. Plant is light: net block is ₹9.59 Cr against ₹138.56 Cr of sales, so capital intensity sits in working capital, not in machines.

Contract terms turn that into cash timing. Revenue follows the percentage-of-completion method, in which sales are recognised as cost is incurred against total estimated cost [1]. Customers hold retention money, a slice of each bill held back until the defect-liability period ends, and contractors usually furnish performance bank guarantees and bear liquidated damages for delay [3]. At March 2026 retention receivable was ₹13.68 Cr, up from ₹9.82 Cr, while retention payable to vendors was only ₹1.69 Cr [1]. Advances received on live contracts fell from ₹9.50 Cr to ₹5.78 Cr, so customers financed less of the work [1]. Unbilled revenue, work done but not yet invoiced, was ₹32.75 Cr. Together billed receivables, retention and unbilled work came to ₹71.80 Cr, 52% of revenue, against ₹55.67 Cr or 51% a year earlier [1]. Contract cost incurred on live projects was ₹206.92 Cr with ₹23.82 Cr of recognised profit, an 11.5% margin on cost [1].

Use the audited consolidated FY2026 accounts to follow ₹100 of revenue. Materials, stock changes and construction inputs used ₹69.98. Employee cost used ₹12.29 and other expenses ₹5.90. That leaves an EBITDA of ₹11.83, or ₹16.39 Cr. Depreciation took ₹1.26 and interest ₹1.56; other income added ₹1.11; tax took ₹2.58. Profit after tax was ₹7.54 per ₹100, or ₹10.45 Cr [1]. Operating profit before working-capital changes was ₹16.93 Cr, ₹12.22 per ₹100. Then working capital absorbed ₹30.04 Cr, ₹21.68 per ₹100. Inventory took ₹27.02 Cr, receivables and unbilled work ₹16.13 Cr, and the fall in customer advances and other liabilities ₹3.69 Cr; suppliers financed ₹14.36 Cr, and vendor advances and other assets released a net ₹2.44 Cr [1].

Why was operating cash flow negative? The statement’s “net cash flow used in operating activities” is ₹16.33 Cr negative for FY26 and ₹18.85 Cr negative for FY25, consolidated, after ₹3.23 Cr of tax paid, and before interest (₹2.16 Cr), which sits in financing [1]. Cash generated from operations was −₹13.11 Cr. The standalone figure is −₹16.31 Cr, and the H1 FY27 filing restates the FY26 comparative as −₹16.32 Cr; I use the audited consolidated figure, and Screener’s “−16” rounds the same number [1][5][6]. The cause is straightforward. Profit is booked as work progresses, but cash arrives when customers pay. Inventory grew 88% (₹30.54 Cr to ₹57.56 Cr, with stores and spares up from ₹5.05 Cr to ₹13.81 Cr) against 27% revenue growth, and customers owed more while advancing less. A growing contractor therefore consumes cash even while profitable, unless billing is front-loaded or suppliers lend.

4. Industry Structure, Competitive Moat & External Learning Hub

India has no single agreed size for its facade and window market, and the sources disagree widely. Ken Research, in an August 2026 report written for a peer's IPO, puts facades at ₹9,060 Cr in FY26 (provisional) and windows and doors at ₹26,070 Cr, so ₹35,130 Cr together, growing about 11.9% a year to FY30 [35]. IMARC, quoted in Aluwind's annual report, sizes facades at ₹31,477 Cr for 2025 and windows and doors at ₹65,666 Cr, growing 6.49% and 3.77% a year [1][36]. The facade gap is more than threefold. The likely cause is scope, since IMARC appears to count cladding material as well as installed systems, but neither report reconciles the two. I use Ken for share maths because its segments match what Aluwind sells. On that basis ₹138.6 Cr of FY26 revenue is about 0.4% of the combined market [35][1].

The market is a few organised firms on top of a very large unorganised base. Ken defines organised players as firms above ₹30 Cr of revenue and counts about 18,000 participants in all. Organised firms hold 29.9% of facade value (₹2,710 Cr) and are forecast to grow 18.8% a year to FY30, against 9.1% for the other 70.1% [35]. Residential windows are more fragmented still: about 88% of that ₹20,280 Cr segment is unorganised or semi-organised [35]. For Aluwind this cuts two ways. Rising codes and bonding needs push work toward organised firms, which helps. But thousands of low-cost fabricators cap what any mid-sized firm can charge for plain windows.

Revenue rose ₹29.3 Cr between the two dates, so orders won roughly equalled revenue billed, about ₹137 Cr (my arithmetic: revenue plus the change in book). A book-to-bill ratio near 1.0 and cover of 2.2 times FY26 revenue are healthy. They do not match a plan to grow 44% in FY27, because a flat book cannot support a step-up in billing. The H1 FY27 filing gave no order book at all [5].

Margin is the second. Operating margin excluding other income was 11.0% in FY25, 11.8% in FY26 and 13.5% in H1 FY27 on standalone figures (13.4% consolidated), against 12.4% in H1 FY26 (12.3% consolidated), an improvement of about 110 bps [1][5]. But H1 FY27 profit growth is not all operating. Other income was ₹2.0 Cr against ₹0.3 Cr a year earlier and explains 83% of the ₹2.1 Cr rise in pre-tax profit, while interest more than doubled to ₹2.25 Cr [5][26]. The third is working capital: net trade working capital (inventory plus receivables plus unbilled work less payables) went from about 41% of revenue at March 2024 to 55%, 64% and 66%. Since March 2024 each extra rupee of revenue has come with roughly one extra rupee of working capital (approximate, because the FY24 balance sheet does not split unbilled work) [4][1][5]. Customer advances fell from ₹9.5 Cr to ₹5.8 Cr in FY26 [1]. Concentration is a blind spot: the last list showed the top ten customers at 59% of revenue, with L&T entities at about 12% [3].

Two further KPIs are not reported at all: the facade-versus-window mix and the contribution of new lines. Segment reporting is single, and FY26 revenue was 98.9% windows and facade work and 1.1% tolling [1]. Management says 'the contribution of facades will increase gradually leading to better execution and margin profiles' [2], but without a split the claim cannot be tested. The subsidiary, Aluwind Clean-Tech, has booked no revenue and lost ₹0.09 Cr in H1 FY27, and the July 2026 solar-facade line carries an investment of 'depend on market scenario' [5][29][30]. 7. Why the Price Is What It Is: Valuation, Reverse DCF & Peer Comps

At the 13 November 2026 close, Aluwind Infra-Tech traded at ₹44.90 on NSE Emerge, for a market value of ₹110.6 Cr. That is a trailing P/E of 9.25 and about 1.45 times book value (book value is ₹30.9 a share) [43][6]. The IPO was priced at ₹45 in March 2024 [3]. After 2.6 years the stock sits on its issue price, while trailing earnings per share (EPS) are ₹4.85 against ₹4.13 for FY24 [6][4]. The price is not far from where outside investors first put money in, although the company has since grown revenue from ₹83.4 Cr to ₹138.6 Cr and then to a ₹147 Cr trailing run-rate [4][1][6].

The path matters. Month-end prices on Yahoo Finance rose from ₹51 in April 2024 to ₹90.9 in October 2025, after a run of Larsen and Toubro order wins and the Eternia partnership. The stock has since fallen about 51% from that close, and the 52-week range is ₹40.0 to ₹85.0 [44][43]. During the fall, FY26 results were audited and showed revenue up 27% and profit up 29% [2]. What changed was the cash-flow statement, the new borrowing and the cost of carrying inventory and receivables. The market de-rated the stock as the profit stopped arriving as cash, and it did so on very thin trading: the 30-day average volume is 9,650 shares a day, worth about ₹4.3 lakh [43].

Cheap on earnings does not mean cheap on cash. Enterprise value (EV) adds net debt to the market value, so it prices the whole business rather than just the equity. Consolidated borrowings at 30 September 2026 were ₹46.06 Cr, against ₹4.80 Cr of cash and ₹14.25 Cr of non-current investments, which are mostly bank deposits pledged to lenders, gold and property [5]. Treating all of those as offsets leaves net debt of ₹27.0 Cr and an EV of ₹137.6 Cr. Against trailing operating EBITDA of ₹18.32 Cr (the last two half-years before other income), that is 7.5 times. If you only net the cash and keep the pledged deposits as debt-like, it is 8.3 times [5]. These are our own sums from filed figures, not company metrics.

A reverse DCF (discounted cash flow) asks what the price already assumes. A normal DCF starts from forecasts and ends with a price. Here we start with the price and solve for the growth it needs. The usual input, free cash flow, is negative for Aluwind in each of the last three years, so we cannot extrapolate it. Instead we use trailing profit of ₹12.14 Cr and ask how fast profit must grow for five years, and how much of it must turn into free cash for the owner, to justify ₹110.6 Cr. Conversion is the swing factor.

The number:

At ₹110.6 Cr the price needs profit to grow about 12% a year for five years if 80% of profit turns into cash, about 20% a year if only 60% does, and about 6% a year if conversion is a full 100%.

How it was calculated:

We discount five years of profit growth plus a terminal value at 5% growth, with each year's free cash to equity set at a fixed share of profit, and solve the growth rate that makes the present value equal today's market value. The solver is a short deterministic script run on the same-session snapshot.

The inputs:

Market value ₹110.57 Cr [43]; trailing profit ₹12.14 Cr (H2 FY26 ₹5.45 Cr plus H1 FY27 ₹6.69 Cr, standalone) [5]; discount rate 17% as a mid case for an illiquid SME-listed company (15% and 20% shown in the grid); terminal growth 5%; cash conversion of 40%, 60%, 80% and 100% of profit.

The range:

At a 15% discount rate the 80%-conversion case needs 7.0% growth; at 20% it needs 18.5%. The realised record is far below any of these: operating cash flow was negative in each of FY24, FY25 and FY26, a total of ₹-37.5 Cr against profit of ₹26.1 Cr [4][1].

If the number is off:

If working capital normalises and conversion reaches 80% or more, the stock is priced for modest growth and today's price looks low. If conversion stays near 40% to 60%, the price needs 20% to 31% growth for five years and is not cheap at all. The same P/E can mean either, which is why the cash-flow statement outranks the multiple.
