Terms used in this report

This dossier answers one question: should BSE, the Indian stock exchange, be bought for a long-term core holding today, and if not, what would have to change? It is not a recommendation to buy or sell any share. It is a worked record of a decision, written so that a reader who has never met a moving average can follow each step and check it. The report reads in about ten minutes for the verdict and the one-line reasons, and in about an hour for the full arithmetic and sources.

The underwriting method has three layers, applied in order. Layer 1 is a set of hard floors. A company that fails any floor is rejected on the spot, and the later layers are not run. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five operating measures that drive the specific business, graded PASS, WATCH or FAIL. Layer 3 is an entry-timing check: how far the price sits above the nearest believable level of support, measured in units of ATR (average true range), the average daily price swing. Only a company that clears all three layers can be bought, and even then the timing rule decides whether to buy now, buy on a dip, or wait.

The terms that the rest of the report uses are these. ROCE (return on capital employed), or return on capital employed, measures how much profit the business earns on the money tied up in it; a figure of 15 percent or more is the Indian floor, and 18 percent or more is preferred for Core names. Debt-to-equity compares borrowed money with owners' money; below 0.70 is the floor. CFO to PAT, the cash conversion ratio, compares cash from operations with reported profit over three years; a ratio of 0.70 or more means the profit is backed by real cash. These three come from the company's financial statements, and a reader can compare them with any annual report.

The technical terms are the ones traders use to describe price behaviour. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, a bit like a trend line drawn through the noise. The 50-day and 200-day averages are the two most watched. Weinstein staging divides a stock's life into four seasons: Stage 1 is a flat base where buyers and sellers are balanced; Stage 2 is the markup, the sustained uptrend; Stage 3 is the topping phase, where the trend loses momentum; and Stage 4 is the decline. Core names need to be in Stage 1 base breakout or Stage 2. RSI (relative strength index), the relative strength index, is a speedometer for momentum on a 0 to 100 scale; above 70 the stock is called extended, meaning it has run ahead of its own trend. ATR, the average true range over 14 sessions, is the usual daily wobble of the price, measured in rupees. Distance from support in ATR units tells you whether a buy is close to a level where sellers have previously stepped in or far from it.

The momentum gate is a code-computed test that sits before the three layers in practice. A candidate must pass either Test A, which asks whether the stock is in an established uptrend that is also outperforming the benchmark, or Test B, which asks whether it is entering an uptrend from a tight base with volume drying up before a breakout. A candidate that fails both gets no allocation however good the business is. The stop-loss and asymmetry rules then size the bet. The Core stop sits below the lower of the recent swing low and the 50-day or 200-day average, minus one ATR. Asymmetry is the potential gain to a defensible target divided by the potential loss to the stop. Core names need at least 3.0 to 1, and the target must be anchored to forward earnings multiplied by a historical median price-to-earnings ratio, or to a measured move from the base. An arbitrary target is not allowed.

The verdict labels follow from these tests. IMMEDIATE_BUY means the price is at support with the asymmetry floor met. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP means it is near support and a small pullback is still needed. WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT means it is extended and should not be chased. REJECTED means a hard floor failed, and the report names the floor. Each verdict in this dossier names its rule and the number that triggered it, and each one states the level that would change it. Colours carry the same meaning as words: the labels are always printed next to the colour so that the verdict still reads in black and white.

How the framework works for this company

The framework is a sequence of filters, and the order is deliberate. A business can be excellent and still be a poor purchase today, because the price has already moved past the point where the buyer is paid for taking risk. The framework separates those two questions. Layer 1 asks whether the company is financially sound and whether the chart is in a state where a new position fits the strategy. Layer 2 asks whether the operating scorecard is healthy. Layer 3 asks whether the price is close enough to a believable support level to make a buy sensible. Stopping at the first failed layer saves time and protects the reader from a common mistake, which is to fall in love with the business and ignore the entry price.

For BSE the sequence stops at Layer 1, and the reason is worth stating in plain words. The company passes the financial floors with clear room. What it fails is the technical posture and the asymmetry test, and those are about the market's current view of the stock. A Layer 1 failure means the report does not ask whether the operating scorecard would be healthy, because a company that is not yet at a buyable price does not need a scorecard to be rejected.

The momentum gate runs before the three layers in practice, and it is the part of the framework most likely to surprise a reader. It is not a judgment about quality. It asks whether the stock is behaving like a stock that can be bought now, based on the price history alone. Test A asks whether the trend is already up. Test B asks whether a quiet base is forming before a move. A stock that passes neither has no allocation, because the house rules say that buying a good business in a bad chart is how capital gets trapped. The gate's thresholds are marked as needing calibration in the configuration file, and this report says so in the methodology note, because a reader should know which rules are settled and which are still being tested.

What the company does and how it earns

BSE is a market operator, not a bank and not a lender. It runs the venues where investors buy and sell shares, index options, currency and debt instruments, and mutual funds, and it earns a fee on each trade and each listing. A simple way to picture it is a toll road operator that collects a small charge on every vehicle: the road is not owned by the drivers and it does not need to lend them money, so it carries very little debt. That is why the balance sheet is almost empty of borrowings, and why the question is about traffic and tolls rather than about interest rates.

The revenue mix has shifted sharply toward derivatives. In the June 2026 quarter the company said its equity cash segment recorded its highest-ever average daily turnover of ₹9,955 crore, while the derivatives segment achieved an all-time high average daily premium turnover of ₹29,615 crore, up 96 percent. [2] Derivatives fees are where most of the growth has come from, which means the earnings depend on how much speculative and hedging activity clears through the exchange, and on the rules the regulator sets for that activity. The company's own call summary says the business has had to contend with regulatory headwinds, including an RBI circular effective 1 July 2026 and higher securities transaction tax. [3]

The cost side is more mechanical than it looks. Operating expenses in the June 2026 quarter rose 56 percent to ₹520 crore, and 54 percent of that was regulatory fees and clearing and settlement costs that move with trading volume. [4] In other words, a large share of the cost base rises and falls with the same volume that drives revenue, so the operating margin is sensitive to volume but not very sensitive to the company's own decisions. The operating margin was 68 percent for the year to March 2026 on the Screener basis, and 67 percent in the June quarter. [1]

Three points about the franchise are worth separating. The first is the index brand: the company co-developed the S&P BSE SENSEX index. The second is that the venue runs under rules set by the regulator, so its franchise can change as quickly as those rules do. The third is the liquidity network: traders go where the other traders are, so once a venue wins the derivatives volume, it tends to keep it, unless a rival offers better prices or a regulator changes the rules. The company's own language reflects that tension. It describes cash-market share gains as constrained by what it calls "artificial barriers" and says a target of meaningfully double-digit cash-market share by the beginning of calendar 2027 is aspirational. [3] That tells us the cash-market share is still small, and that the main lever for growth is the derivatives franchise.

Layer 1: the hard financial and technical gates

Layer 1 is the set of floors a Core candidate has to clear before anything else is considered. The financial floors ask whether the company earns a good return on the money it uses, whether it is free of heavy debt, and whether its profits arrive as cash. The technical floor asks whether the chart is in a state where a new buyer is joining the trend rather than arriving after it has finished. A failure on any one of these ends the analysis for a Core name, and the report names that failure as the gate status.

The return on capital is the first test. ROCE divides operating profit by the capital the business uses, and a figure of 60 percent means the company earns about 60 rupees of operating profit for every 100 rupees of capital tied up in it. The Indian floor is 15 percent, and 18 percent is preferred for Core names. BSE clears both. Screener's ratio table gives 60 percent for March 2026, and the 46 percent return on equity for the last year and 36 percent over three years show the same story from the shareholders' side. [1] A high return of this kind is usually a sign that the business needs little physical capital to grow, which is true of an exchange.

Debt is the second test. The floor is a debt-to-equity ratio below 0.70. Screener's balance sheet shows borrowings of zero in March 2026, against equity capital of 82 crore and reserves of 6,591 crore. [1] The debt-to-equity ratio is therefore 0.00. The rule excludes financial institutions and NBFCs because for a lender, debt is the raw material and a high ratio is normal. BSE is neither. It is a market operator that takes no deposits and makes no loans, so the rule applies to it directly. It would pass whether or not the exclusion applied, because it has no borrowings to measure.

Cash conversion is the third test, and it is where the reader should look most carefully. The floor is cumulative operating cash flow divided by cumulative net profit over three years, at least 0.70. On the Screener figures, operating cash flow for the three years to March 2026 was 2,643 crore, 414 crore and 3,104 crore, a total of 6,161 crore. Net profit over the same years was 772 crore, 1,322 crore and 2,487 crore, a total of 4,581 crore. The ratio is 6,161 divided by 4,581, which is 1.34. [1] A second aggregator reports a higher figure for 2024, 2,842 crore against Screener's 2,643 crore, and gives a ratio of 1.39. [14] [15] The report uses the lower figure, which is conservative, and both pass.

The technical floor is the fourth test and the one that fails. A Core candidate must be in a Stage 1 base breakout or a Stage 2 markup. The ledger classifies BSE as Stage 3, which is the topping phase. The 50-day average, at 3,335.22, is falling and sits below the price of 3,375.40, and the stock is 24.09 percent below its 52-week high of 4,446.8. The price is above both moving averages, which is encouraging, but only the 200-day average is rising; the 50-day average is falling, and a Stage 2 uptrend needs both rising. A stock in a topping phase may still rise, which is why the report does not call it a short. It means the house rule for a new Core buyer is not met.

Technical posture and the momentum gate

The momentum gate is the code-computed test that decides whether a candidate can receive any allocation at all. It is separate from the three layers because it answers a narrower question: is this stock behaving like a stock that can be bought now? It has two routes. Test A, for names already in an uptrend, requires that the price sit above the 50-day average, the 50-day average be rising over 21 sessions, the 200-day average not be falling over 63 sessions, the stock outperform its benchmark over 63 sessions, and the support distance be within 1.2 ATR. Test B, for names entering an uptrend, requires that the 50-day average not fall by more than half a percent over 21 sessions, relative strength be rising over 28 sessions, the price sit within one ATR of the base pivot, volume dry up during the base, and volume expand later. A candidate that passes either route qualifies. A candidate that fails both does not.

For BSE both tests fail. Test A fails on three conditions. The first is the structure condition: the price (3,375.40) is above the 50-day average (3,335.22) and that average is above the 200-day average (3,323.30), so the order is right, but the 50-day average has not been rising over the last 21 sessions. It stood at 3,518.62 then, and the drop is 5.21 percent. The second is relative strength over 63 sessions, which is minus 5.12 percent against the Nifty, where the rule needs zero or more. The third is the support distance, which is 3.41 ATR above the 63-day base low of 3,000.00, where the rule allows 1.2. A reader can see that the stock is above its averages and still fails the test, because the 50-day average is falling while the 200-day average is still rising, and the stock is not leading the market.

Test B fails on three conditions. The 50-day average fell 5.21 percent in 21 sessions, against a limit of half a percent. The price is 3.41 ATR from the base pivot, against a limit of one ATR, so no breakout is near. Volume expansion over 20 sessions is 0.71 times the average, against a required 1.0. Two conditions do pass. Relative strength over 28 sessions is rising, at plus 8.49 percent, and volume has dried up during the base to 0.31 times the 63-day level, against a limit of 0.9. The gate therefore sees a stock that has quieted down and started to outperform over the last month, but that has not yet confirmed the move with volume or a clear breakout. Under the rule, that is a watch, not an allocation.

Asymmetry and target anchoring

Asymmetry is the ratio that tells a buyer whether the risk is worth taking. It divides the potential gain to a defensible target by the potential loss to the stop. A ratio of 3.0 to 1 means the reader can lose one unit if the stop is hit and expects to gain three units if the target is reached. The house rule for Core names is at least 3.0 to 1, and the formula is the target minus the current price, divided by the current price minus the stop. The rule matters only if the target is honest. The house rules require the target to be anchored either to forward earnings times a historical median price-to-earnings ratio, or to a measured move from the base. An arbitrary target, however optimistic, is not allowed, and if a target cannot be verified the gate fails on asymmetry.

The stop comes first. For Core names the stop sits below the lower of the swing low and the 50-day or 200-day average, minus one ATR. BSE's swing low over 20 sessions is 3,000.00, and the 50-day and 200-day averages are 3,335.22 and 3,323.30, so the lower of the three is 3,000.00. Subtract one ATR of 109.95 and the stop is 2,890.05. The downside is therefore 3,375.40 minus 2,890.05, which is 485.35 rupees a share, or 14.4 percent of the price. That is a wide stop for a stock of this size, and it reflects the company's large daily swings.

The target is harder. A forward-earnings target would need a forecast of next year's earnings per share and a historical median multiple over three years. The sources reached for this report contain a June 2026 quarter per-share figure of 21.43 rupees. [1] They do not contain a verified three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, and the research file records that figure as not found. The searches for that figure returned five-year averages and medians, but not the three-year figure the rule asks for, and the sources that might have supplied it were unavailable. The report therefore records the target as not verified. Analysts publish their own targets, and the sources show a consensus average of 3,704 rupees from 19 analysts, a median of 3,815 and a high of 4,711. [16] [17] The Sharpely page, dated to the same 13 October snapshot, gives a median of 3,807.50 and the same high of 4,711, so the 2.75 figure does not rest on the August data alone. Those are useful as a sense check, but they are not the house method, and the report does not promote them to the target base.

Glossary

The terms below are the ones a new reader is most likely to meet. ROCE, return on capital employed, is operating profit divided by capital used. ROE, return on equity, is net profit divided by shareholders' funds. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by equity. CFO, cash from operations, is the cash the business generates from its activities before investing and financing. PAT is profit after tax. OPM is operating profit margin, the share of revenue left after direct costs. CAGR is compound annual growth rate, the steady yearly growth that would produce the same change over the period. P/E is price divided by earnings per share. Stage 1 to Stage 4 are the four phases of a stock's price cycle. SMA is the simple moving average. RSI is the relative strength index on a 0 to 100 scale. ATR is the average true range over 14 sessions. Stop-loss is the price at which a position is exited. Asymmetry is potential gain divided by potential loss. The momentum gate is the code test for whether a stock can take new money. ADPT and ADT are average daily premium turnover and average daily turnover, the two volume measures the exchange reports. STT is securities transaction tax. Co-location is the service of placing trading servers next to the exchange's matching engine. FII and DII are foreign and domestic institutional investors. An expiry is the date on which an options contract settles.
