1. What the company does

Dynamic Cables Limited, listed in India as DYCL, makes the cables that carry electricity from power stations to towns, factories and solar farms. It began in 1986 as a partnership firm called Dynamic Engineers run by the Mangal family, became a private company in 2007 and a public one in 2017[13]. Its plants are in Jaipur (two sites) and Reengus in Rajasthan[15]. The range runs from low-voltage and medium-voltage cables to high-voltage and extra-high-voltage power cables, control cables, solar cables, railway signalling cables, building wires, aerial bunched cables and aluminium conductors[19]. A conductor is the bare metal line on a pole or tower, and a cable is metal wrapped in insulation so it can run underground or inside a building.

The people who pay are mostly private. In the year to March 2026 private customers gave 80 percent of revenue, government buyers 13 percent and exports 7 percent[7]. The company does not publish its largest customers. Private here includes turnkey contractors and private power distribution companies[5]. Government work was 22.8 percent of sales in FY22 and 13.4 percent in FY26[5]. Crisil says direct work for government bodies is 10 to 20 percent of turnover, down from 50 to 55 percent about five years ago[13]. In the June 2026 quarter the split moved to 71 percent private, 16 percent government and 13 percent export, with high-voltage cables at 68 percent of sales, low-voltage cables at 30 percent and conductors at 2 percent[6]. Solar cables were about 20 percent of revenue in that quarter[6].

The company earns money by turning metal into cable and charging for the conversion. About 80 percent of each rupee of sales is material cost, about 10 percent is staff and other costs, and about 10 percent is operating profit[8]. Most orders let the company pass metal-price changes to the customer, and about 20 percent are fixed-price orders hedged when booked[7][6]. So the margin stays steady while rupee sales move with metal prices.

Capacity is the limit on growth. The existing plants can produce about ₹135 crore of sales a month[9] and ran at about 85 percent in July 2026[6]. A new plant is being built for about ₹45 crore with electron-beam cross-linking, a way of hardening cable insulation that is needed for solar cables[5]. Management says that at its usual six to seven times asset turnover the plant could add roughly ₹270 to ₹315 crore of annual sales once full (our arithmetic on the company's figures)[6]. It said on 20 July 2026 that it expected to start the plant from September 2026 and to see meaningful revenue only from the March quarter[6]. No start has been announced as of 23 October.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • RSI is the relative strength index, a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has moved; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought. ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions.
  • EPS is earnings per share, profit divided by share count. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. ROCE is return on capital employed, the yearly operating profit earned on the money tied up in the business.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of Dynamic Cables is a fast-growing, low-debt manufacturer that earns a high return on a small amount of capital. Sales rose from ₹669 crore in FY23 to ₹1,198 crore in FY26, a compound rate of 21.4 percent a year (our arithmetic on the company's figures), and profit after tax rose from ₹31 crore to ₹84 crore[5][3]. The return on capital employed was 26.8 percent in FY26 and borrowings were only 0.09 times shareholders' money[5]. Crisil raised its rating to A with a stable outlook in April 2025 from A-, and reaffirmed it in March 2026 on bank limits raised to ₹350 crore[13]. India Ratings affirmed IND A/Stable in June 2026 and withdrew the rating at the company's request, so only Crisil now rates it[14].

The weakness is scale, thin margins and working capital. Operating margin was 10.8 percent in FY26 and has stayed between 9.4 and 10.8 percent since FY22[5], so a 1-point slip would take about a tenth off operating profit. The company is small next to the leaders: its market value is ₹1,694 crore, against ₹1,12,126 crore for Polycab[19][28]. Crisil lists moderate scale and competition from larger players among the weaknesses, and names an operating margin under 7 percent or a stretched working capital cycle as downgrade triggers[13]. Management itself says every major player is adding high-voltage capacity[6].

Evidence for the moat: product approvals, mix and pricing discipline

A moat is something that protects profit from competitors, as a moat protects a castle. Management argues that its protection is the approval process: a cable maker must pass long product approvals with utilities before it can supply, and few players hold the approvals Dynamic Cables carries[6]. In FY26 it won Power Grid Corporation approval for two conductor types[5]. It has moved toward high-voltage and renewable products and exited railway signalling cables, 4 percent of FY25 sales and nothing in FY26[7].

The honest limit of this claim is that approvals protect against new entrants, not against the large incumbents, and several large groups are adding cable capacity. Management said Adani and Birla group companies buy cables from it today, and that the Birla group seems focused on house wire, but it does not know the Adani business plan[6]. The moat is real but narrow, and the test of it is whether the margin holds when more capacity arrives.

Pass-through of copper, aluminium and PVC: what the evidence shows

Pass-through means the company charges its customers for changes in metal cost. Three pieces of evidence support it. Operating margin was 10.3 percent in FY25, 10.8 percent in FY26 and 10.9 percent in the June 2026 quarter while aluminium rose sharply[5][6]. About 80 percent of orders carry a price-variation clause and the other 20 percent are hedged by buying metal at booking[6]. And Crisil says copper and aluminium are 75 to 80 percent of raw material cost and that most changes are passed on[13]. The metal move was large: one trade source puts the London Metal Exchange aluminium price near $3,600 a tonne in May 2026, up 35 percent on the year, after the Strait of Hormuz closure[36].

Pass-through is not free, and the evidence has two cracks. The first is volume. In the June 2026 quarter revenue grew 33 percent but tonnes grew only 5 to 6 percent, so most of the growth was price[6]. Customers are deferring orders at high prices: the order book was ₹811 crore at June 2026, up 10 percent in rupees on a year earlier, which in tonnes is probably a fall[6][5]. The second crack is cash. The company's inventory value rose 20 to 25 percent from March to June 2026 because of the metal price, and its borrowings rose too[6]. Higher metal prices make each rupee of growth need more working capital, so the margin is passed through but the funding need is not. Chapter 3 shows how this appears in cash flow.

The weak spots: capacity delays, thin customer data and people changes

The new plant has slipped a full year so far. On 28 January 2025 management said it was on track to be operational by the second quarter of FY26, that is July to September 2025[12]. The date then moved to the second half of FY26, to the end of FY26, and on 12 May 2026 to September 2026, with three reasons: delayed approvals, imported machines held up by the Iran war and freight, and compliance requirements from the AERB, the atomic energy regulator[11][9][8][7]. The cost estimate also moved from ₹35 crore to ₹45 crore[12][5].

Customer concentration is not published. Screener shows the top-five share only to paying users, and neither the annual report nor the calls give it, so we cannot say how much of the private 80 percent comes from a few contractors[19][1]. Two people changes came in September 2026. A change-in-management announcement was filed on 10 September; the record shows only its headline, so the role and the reason are not known[23]. On 23 September the head of human resources, a member of the promoter family, left for personal reasons[18]. On 28 September the company reported that a designated person had made a trade that broke its insider-trading code and was warned in writing[19]. None is large, but with the delayed plant they explain why this is not a clean story. Chapter 2B scores the operating measures.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on a weakest assumption. This chapter names each one, says what evidence would show it wrong, and when that evidence arrives. The most important date is 27 October 2026, when the September-quarter results are due[23][19]. That is after the 23 October ledger session, so nothing from those results is used anywhere in this report.

Weakest assumption of the central earnings: that volume growth returns

The central case needs sales to grow about 18 to 22 percent a year. In the June 2026 quarter most of the 33 percent came from the aluminium price and tonnes rose only 5 to 6 percent; the order book was flat in rupees and management said customers were reluctant to book at high prices[6]. If the metal price keeps falling, rupee growth fades even if tonnes recover. Evidence of failure: September-quarter sales under ₹324 crore (15 percent above ₹281.6 crore a year earlier) or an order book below ₹811 crore[4][6]. A 10 percent shortfall in FY28 sales gives earnings of ₹21.42, a value of ₹386 and a ratio of 1.50 at the zone top. Tonnes are not published, so volume is read from management's statements.

Weakest assumption of the margin: that pass-through works in both directions

The margin held while metal rose. When metal falls, price-variation clauses cut the selling price, but stock bought at the higher price stays on the books, and the fixed-price fifth of the order book keeps its old price only until it is delivered[6]. On 12 May an analyst put the Q4 FY26 gross margin at 17.9 percent and management replied that operating profit is the right measure[7]; gross margin would move first. Evidence of failure: an operating margin under 10.0 percent in the September quarter or under 10.5 percent for FY27. One point of margin in FY28 gives ₹21.61, a value of ₹389 and a ratio of 1.66; a 9.5 percent margin gives ₹379 and 1.19. Margin is the first thing to read in the September-quarter release.

Weakest assumption of the multiple: that 18 times holds in a falling market

The central value assumes no change in the multiple while the market is in a downtrend and the stock has no analyst coverage. The stock traded as low as 13.9 times earnings on 30 March 2026 and as high as 52.5 times on 17 December 2024, so the range is wide[25][19].
