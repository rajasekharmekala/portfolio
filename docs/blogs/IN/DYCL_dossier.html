1. What the company does

Dynamic Cables Limited makes the cables and wires that carry electricity. A power cable is a metal core, usually aluminium or copper, wrapped in layers of plastic insulation so that current can run safely under streets, across fields and inside plants. The company began in 1986 as a partnership called Dynamic Engineers, run by the Mangal family of Jaipur, and its shares have been listed on the Indian stock exchanges since 2017.[17][7] Ashish Mangal, the second-generation managing director, runs the business day to day.[7] It has four plants at Jaipur and Reengus in Rajasthan, and it sells in India and in more than 40 other countries.[7][19] Its range runs from low-voltage cables for buildings and factories, through medium- and high-voltage cables for the grid, to bare aluminium conductors and solar cables.[1][7]

Three groups of buyers matter. Power distribution companies, called discoms, buy cables to extend and upgrade the local grid. Private engineering contractors build solar parks, factories and smart-meter networks, and they buy cables as part of the job. Exporters and overseas distributors take the rest. In FY26, private customers gave 80% of sales, government bodies 13% and exports 7%.[6] In Q1 FY27, the first quarter of the year that ends in March 2027, the split moved to 71% private, 16% government and 13% exports.[5] Five years ago direct government work was 50% to 55% of turnover, and Crisil puts it at 10% to 20% now. That matters because state utilities often pay late.[17][2] No single customer gave more than 8% of FY26 revenue, and the top five gave 31%.[19]

The business sells metal that has been shaped, tested and delivered on time. Copper and aluminium make up 80% to 85% of a cable's value.[19] Most contracts carry a price variation clause, which passes any change in metal price on to the buyer. About 20% of the order book is at a fixed price, and for those orders the company buys the metal on the day it books the order.[5][6] Management calls the business 80-10-10: 80% of revenue is material cost, 10% is staff and other costs, and 10% is profit before interest, tax and depreciation.[9] The consequence is simple. When aluminium rises, sales rise even if the company ships no more cable. In Q1 FY27 sales grew 33%, and management said only 5% to 6% of that was volume, with most of the rest coming from the aluminium price.[5]

High-voltage cables were 64% of FY26 sales and 68% in Q1 FY27. Low-voltage cables were 30% in both periods, and aluminium conductors fell from 6% to 2%.[6][5] Solar cables are the fastest-growing line: about 10% of sales in FY25, about 18% in FY26 and about 20% in Q1 FY27.[6][5] Management expects solar cable sales to grow 25% to 30% a year for the next three to four years.[5] Railway signalling cables were 4% of FY25 sales and zero in FY26, because competition was fierce and the profit margin was poor.[6] The company says it has only held that line back and could return to it when the market is healthier.[5]

How an order turns into cash

A cable order is won by tender or by negotiation. The company then buys metal, makes the cable to the customer's specification, tests it, ships it and waits to be paid. India Ratings says order execution usually takes three to nine months.[19] The order book was ₹811 crore on 30 June 2026, about 0.63 times the last twelve months of sales of ₹1,285 crore, on our division of the cited figures.[5][1] Supply is heaviest in the last quarter of the fiscal year, so March is the busiest month.[13] The company measures how long customers take to pay on six months of sales, and it reported about 68 days for Q4 FY26.[8] The gap between paying for metal and being paid is the reason cash trails profit in this industry, a point chapter 3 takes up in numbers.

What revenue capacity means for a cable maker

Management cannot state capacity in kilometres of cable, because each order is customised and uses machine time differently.[13] It quotes a revenue capacity instead: ₹1,150 to ₹1,200 crore a year in May 2025, and about ₹135 crore a month at peak by May 2026.[13][6] Because revenue includes the metal price, that rupee capacity grows when aluminium rises, even if the plants make no more cable. The existing plants ran at about 85% in Q1 FY27, inside the 80% to 90% band that management calls optimum.[5][6]

The new plant at Reengus

The growth plan rests on a new plant at Reengus. It will make solar DC cables and other products using electron-beam cross-linking, a process that hardens cable insulation with a beam of electrons so the cable can take more heat. The Atomic Energy Regulatory Board approved the beam facility in January 2026.[10] The project costs about ₹45 crore, and management expects each rupee of plant to earn six to seven rupees of yearly sales, which gives ₹270 to ₹315 crore of sales at full use on our multiplication.[5] It said meaningful revenue should come only from Q4 FY27 and that the plant should reach 80% to 85% use by the end of FY28.[5] Chapter 2B shows that this plant has already slipped by about a year.

The 2026 metal shock

On 28 February 2026 the war with Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz, and aluminium futures on the London Metal Exchange rose to a four-year high in March.[27] Management said prices shot up from March into May, and customers delayed orders, which weakened order intake in April and May.[5][6] Crisil expects copper and aluminium to be 22% to 27% dearer this year and cable selling prices to rise 18% to 20%.[23] The company's own annual report records aluminium moving from about $2,400 to $2,600 a tonne at the start of FY26 to highs near $3,300 to $3,600.[2] That single event explains most of the growth in sales in 2026 and most of the pressure on cash, and the next chapters keep coming back to it.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Discom, conductor and HTLS. A discom is a power distribution company. A conductor is a bare metal wire, usually aluminium, strung between poles and towers. HTLS means high-temperature low-sag conductor, a newer wire that carries more current on existing towers.[5]
  • Order book and price variation clause. The order book is the value of signed orders not yet delivered. A price variation clause is a contract term that moves the selling price when metal prices move.[8]
  • RDSS. The Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme is a central government programme, launched in July 2021, that pays for smart meters and distribution network upgrades.[24]
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is the profit from running the plants before financing costs, tax and the wear charged on equipment.
  • Operating margin is operating profit divided by sales. Management quotes it as the EBITDA margin.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is the year's profit divided by the number of shares, so it is the profit that belongs to one share.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is operating profit after depreciation divided by the money tied up in the business, so it is the yearly return on every rupee the company uses.
  • Debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' money. A figure of 0.09 means the company owes nine paise for every rupee its owners have put in.
  • Net debt is borrowings minus the cash and bank balances the company holds.
  • Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company owes its suppliers.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the core business actually collected after paying costs and tax. This report shortens it to operating cash flow.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow, which is operating cash flow less the money spent on plant and equipment.
  • Capital expenditure is money spent on plant, machines and buildings that last more than a year. People often shorten it to capex.
  • Guidance is what management tells investors to expect. Dynamic Cables gives a long-term growth range but no yearly figure.
  • Consensus estimate is the average of professional analysts' forecasts for a company's profit or price.
  • Price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share. A P/E of 21.7 means the market pays ₹21.70 for each rupee of yearly profit.
  • Dilution is the fall in each owner's percentage when a company issues new shares.
  • The 20-day moving average, the 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the average closing price over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. They smooth out daily noise and show the direction of the trend.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has moved. A reading under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions. It is the yardstick for how far a price is from a support level.
  • Stage 2 is the rising phase of a stock's cycle, with the price above rising 50-day and 200-day averages. Stage 4 is the falling phase, with the price below falling averages. Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase in between.
  • A base is a flat resting zone where a stock trades sideways after a move. The pivot is the price level that, if crossed, ends the rest. A breakout is a close above that level, and a retest is a pullback to it that holds.
  • A stop loss is a pre-set price at which you sell to cap the loss. A limit order is an order to buy only at or below a chosen price. The entry zone is the price range in which a purchase is acceptable.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, so 3.5 to 1 means you stand to make ₹3.50 for each ₹1 at risk. A measured move is a target found by adding the height of a base to its pivot.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Dynamic Cables is a well-run, low-debt specialist in a fragmented industry. Its protection comes from product approvals and customer trust, not from scale or brand. Management says only a few players carry the approvals it holds, and that the approval process is long for any new entrant.[5] Recent proof points include approval from Power Grid for its ACSR and AL-59 conductors, two UL standards that allow some supply into the United States, and accreditation of the Reengus test laboratory.[8][10] The Reengus plant also holds a GreenCo Bronze rating from the Confederation of Indian Industry.[7] These are real barriers, but they are barriers of paperwork and testing time, and larger rivals have crossed them too.

The financial position is the second strength. Crisil reaffirmed Crisil A/Stable and Crisil A1 on 20 March 2026 and enlarged the rated bank limits to ₹350 crore from ₹295.55 crore.[17] India Ratings affirmed IND A/Stable before the company asked it to withdraw the rating on 12 June 2026.[19] Interest cover, which is operating profit divided by interest, reached 11.28 times in FY26 against 6.9 times in FY25 and 3.7 times in FY24, and the agency expects steady credit metrics because no large debt-funded plant is planned.[19] The upgrade from Crisil A- to Crisil A in April 2025 came after revenue grew 31% a year over the three years to FY24.[18]

Scale is the weakness. On the last twelve months of sales, Dynamic Cables earned ₹1,285 crore, against ₹3,368 crore at Universal Cables, ₹6,939 crore at Finolex, ₹10,832 crore at R R Kabel, ₹12,343 crore at KEI, ₹23,593 crore at Apar and ₹33,978 crore at Polycab.[1][35][34][33][32][36][31] Crisil lists moderate scale and intense competition from larger players with wider product ranges as the main limits on the rating.[17] Industry capacity is expected to rise 20% to 22% by the end of FY27, and nearly half of that will come from new players.[23] An analyst also asked about cable entry by the Adani and Birla groups, and management replied that long approval timelines will slow new entrants.[5] More supply means price competition, and price competition is the first thing that would show up in the profit margin.

Pricing power is real but narrow. Operating margin has stayed in a 10% to 10.5% range for about ten years, and was 10.8% in FY26 and 10.9% in Q1 FY27.[13][6][5] Management explains why: on products with only two or three approved suppliers the margin is higher, and as more suppliers qualify the margin falls, so the company must keep launching new products to hold the average.[13] That is a treadmill, not a moat. The cushion is wide, though. Crisil's downgrade trigger is a margin below 7%, which is about a third below today's level.[17]

Why demand for cables should keep growing

Cable demand follows electricity spending, and that spending is rising for several reasons. Under RDSS, smart meters have been sanctioned for 19.79 crore consumers across 45 distribution utilities, and by 30 June 2026 a total of 5.73 crore smart meters had been installed, of which 5.53 crore were consumer meters, about 28% of the 19.79 crore sanctioned on our division.[24][25] The company's annual report points to per-capita electricity use of about 1,460 kWh in FY25 against 957 a decade earlier, to a target of 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030, and to central infrastructure spending of ₹11.21 lakh crore.[2] Crisil counts ₹10 to ₹12 lakh crore of planned investment in renewables, power, real estate, data centres and smart meters, and notes that cables are under 5% of a project's cost, so buyers rarely halt a project over cable prices.[23] Industry estimates in the annual report put growth at 12% to 14% a year.[2]

There is a caution inside this good news. Crisil expects sector volume growth of about 10% in FY27, while Dynamic Cables reported volume growth of 5% to 6% in Q1 FY27, and its order book grew about 10% year on year.[23][5] Management says it aims to grow at 1.5 times the industry rate.[11] Over one quarter the data do not show that yet. The demand story is intact. The proof that this company is gaining share is not.

What the ownership register and the filings say about governance

The promoter family holds 68.18% of the shares, led by Ashish Mangal at 31.81%, Rahul Mangal at 21.85% and Saroj Mangal at 13.62%.[20] The holding fell from 74.47% in March 2024 to 68.17% in June 2024, but it has not moved since.[1] The cause was new shares, not selling: the company raised ₹96.59 crore in a preferential issue in June 2024, a form of dilution that lowers each existing holder's percentage.[16][18] Pledged shares were 0.0% of the promoter holding in every quarter from March 2024 to June 2026, and the company's own filing for March 2026 answers no to the pledge question.[20][22] Trendlyne lists one small pledge of 8,000 shares in May 2023, which is far too small to matter.[21] Institutions own just 1.65%, and 51,019 shareholders hold the rest, so trading is thin and moves can be sharp.[20][1]

Three smaller points deserve a line each. First, the auditor, A Bafna and Co, gave an unmodified opinion on the accounts and the company's financial controls, and named revenue recognition as the one key audit matter.[2] Second, the company reported no material related-party transactions for FY26, but an earlier call disclosed that a group property company, Indo Krates, had mortgaged property and given a guarantee for the bank limits, and in May 2026 management said the guarantee had been withdrawn.[2][11][6] Third, in September 2026 the company reported a minor insider-trading code breach by a design manager, who sold shares within six months of buying them, and it issued a warning letter. The head of human resources, Aditi Mangal, resigned on 23 September 2026, and a holder of the same name appears in the promoter list.[29][28][20] None of these changes the picture, but they show a company whose controls work and whose senior bench is small.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of reported numbers show a company that doubled its sales and almost tripled its profit. Sales rose from ₹564 crore in FY22 to ₹1,198 crore in FY26, which is about 21% a year on our working from rounded figures, and the twelve months to June 2026 reached ₹1,285 crore.[1] Net profit rose from ₹31 crore to ₹84 crore over the same four years, about 28% a year, and Screener puts the three-year profit growth rate at 39%.[1] Earnings per share rose from ₹7.02 to ₹17.43, after adjusting for a one-for-one bonus issue in July 2025 and a ₹96.59 crore share sale in June 2024.[1][16]

Profit grew faster than sales for two reasons. The operating margin held near 10% while the company got bigger. Interest cost also fell, from ₹21 crore in FY24 to ₹11 crore in FY26, as borrowings fell and the credit rating improved.[1][6] The tax rate stayed near 25%, so almost all of the gain reached shareholders.[1]

Cash tells a harder story. Operating cash flow was ₹64 crore, ₹27 crore, ₹1 crore, ₹56 crore and ₹62 crore across FY22 to FY26. In FY24 the company earned ₹37.77 crore but collected only ₹1.26 crore in operating cash, because it was financing a 15% jump in sales with stock and unpaid bills.[1][4][3] Over FY24 to FY26 free cash flow added up to only ₹16 crore (minus ₹20 crore, plus ₹32 crore and plus ₹4 crore), against ₹187 crore of reported profit.[1] That gap is the single weakest number in the dossier.

The debt story is better than it first looks and also weaker than it first looks. Borrowings fell from ₹119 crore in FY24 to ₹43 crore in FY26, and debt-to-equity fell from 0.56 to 0.09.[1][7] But most of that deleveraging was paid for by new shares, not by operating cash: the ₹96.59 crore preferential issue arrived in June 2024, and shareholders' money more than doubled from ₹214 crore to ₹457 crore.[16][1] Since September 2025, net debt has climbed again from ₹17.3 crore to ₹58.2 crore in March 2026.[8]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate rests on one assumption that it cannot survive losing. This chapter names that assumption for each estimate, builds the strongest honest case against our conclusion, and says when we will know. The red-team case has three fatal-flaw scenarios, and each one is a way the stock could fall below the bear value of ₹329 or stay on the sidelines for years.

Flaw 1: cash never catches up with profit

The first flaw is the one already in the numbers. Over FY24 to FY26 the company converted 64 paise of each rupee of profit into operating cash, and free cash flow over those three years was ₹16 crore.[4][3][2][1] In Q1 FY27 the value of inventory rose 20% to 25% because metal cost more, and management said that is why borrowings rose.[5] Crisil also notes that higher metal prices raise the cash tied up in the business and that these needs are met through channel financing and bank limits.[23] Annual-report risk notes add that state utilities sometimes delay payment even after orders are secured.[2]

If FY27 operating cash is only ₹30 crore, the three-year ratio would be 0.58 and the compounder gate would stay failed through the FY28 accounts. A repeat of FY24, with about ₹5 crore of cash, would give 0.48, still above the 0.40 red line. The ratio would breach the red line only if FY27 operating cash flow fell below minus ₹16 crore, which we think is very unlikely. So this flaw does not bankrupt the case. It keeps the stock cheap for longer than we expect.

Flaw 2: the profit is metal, not demand

The second flaw is that Q1 FY27's strength may be a metal-price effect. Sales rose 33% but volume rose only 5% to 6%, and Crisil expects sector volume growth of about 10% for the full year.[5][23] Management also says a reversal in raw material prices has already started.[5] If aluminium falls, sales growth will drop toward volume growth. Any profit from buying metal ahead of price rises would also reverse, and fixed costs would be spread over smaller sales. Our bear-case earnings per share of about ₹17.4 for FY27 describes that outcome: it is flat on FY26.

The warning sign to watch is the gap between operating profit growth and volume growth. In Q1 FY27 operating profit grew 41% on 5% to 6% volume growth.[5] If a pass-through business shows that gap for two more quarters, either its mix is improving faster than we think, which is good, or its profit is being flattered by the price cycle, which is not.

Flaw 3: the new plant ramps slowly and competition arrives first

The third flaw is about timing. The plant is already about 12 months late, and management says it needs 18 months after start to reach 80% to 85% use.[6][5] Crisil expects industry capacity to rise 20% to 22% by March 2027, with nearly half from new players, and management itself named new entrants from large groups.[23][5] Solar DC cables and electron-beam products are the plant's selling points, but the high-temperature conductor line needs about a year of testing and approvals before any order, and the US route is distributor-led with a first shipment only now.[6][5] If the plant starts late in FY27 and ramps slowly, FY28 earnings per share would be nearer ₹21 than the ₹26.0 of our base build in chapter 3, and a multiple of 18.3 times would give about ₹384, below today's price.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, and the rule that decided it is the technical posture floor. A tactical position needs a Stage 2 chart, and the stock is in a Stage 3 pullback: the close of ₹408.75 is under the 50-day average of ₹450.47, and the momentum gate reads NO_MOMENTUM.[1] Layer 2 also fails, at 2/5 KPIs against a 4/5 bar, and cash conversion of 0.64 misses the 0.70 compounder floor even though it clears the 0.40 red line.[2] The reward-to-risk floor is met on paper at 7.17 to 1, so asymmetry is not the problem. Our verdict names work like this. REJECTED means at least one gate fails. WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT means every gate passes but the price is too far above its support, or too overbought, to buy now. This stock moves up to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT or better only when the chart and the KPI score both pass. We do not hold the stock, so there is no position to trim or exit.

The entry posture is a separate question. We measure how far the price sits above its nearest support in ATR units and read the answer off a ladder with four bands. Within 0.6 ATR is the FULL_SIZE band, where a full risk-based size is allowed. Up to 1.2 ATR is the STANDARD band, up to 1.6 ATR allows only a small starter order, and beyond that is the NO_BUY band, where we do not chase and wait for a pullback. This stock is 0.27 ATR above support, so it sits in the FULL_SIZE band, and its RSI of 37.4 is far from the 70 level that would also stop us chasing. On the ladder alone, the stock is at a price where we would be willing to buy. The chart gate overrides it, because we want proof that the stock has turned before we commit capital to a stock that has just fallen 16%.

If the stock re-qualifies, the levels are as follows. The entry zone is ₹404.00 to ₹412.00, by limit order only, which spans the swing-low support of ₹403.95 to just above the close.[1] The stop is ₹394.90, which is the 20-day swing low of ₹403.95 less half an ATR of ₹9.05, following our rule for tactical positions. The targets are ₹508 in the base case, ₹678 in the bull case and ₹329 in the bear case. At the entry-zone top of ₹412, the stop is 4.15% away.

Position size comes from risk, not from hope. Every stock gets a conviction tier, from TIER_1 for our highest conviction to TIER_4 for our lowest, and TIER_0 means no position at all. Each tier has a risk budget, which is the share of the whole portfolio we are willing to lose if the stop is hit. We state it in basis points, and one basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so 50 basis points is 0.5% of the portfolio. The position size is the risk budget divided by the stop distance, and the tier cap is the largest weight that tier may ever carry, which is 3.5% for TIER_3. Today the size is 0%, because the stock is rejected and so carries TIER_0. The next sentence is a what-if and not a recommendation. If the stock were TIER_3 with a 50-basis-point budget and a 4.15% stop, the formula would give about 12.0%, and the 3.5% cap would bind, so the size would be 3.5%.
