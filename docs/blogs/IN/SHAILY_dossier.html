1. What the company does

Shaily Engineering Plastics Limited is a Gujarat-based maker of injection-moulded plastic parts. Melted plastic is forced into a steel mould, cooled and ejected as a finished part. CARE records that the company was incorporated in 1980, and the company's deck says it was established in 1987[9][1]. It has seven plants in Gujarat, more than 200 moulding machines and more than 2,000 employees. Revenue was ₹990.7 crore in the year to March 2026 (FY26).

The consumer segment makes home furnishings, packaging, LED parts, toys and steel furniture. The industrial segment makes automotive and appliance parts, consumer-electronics parts and semiconductor trays. The healthcare segment makes drug-delivery devices, platform devices and pharma packaging[11]. In the June 2026 quarter consumer sales fell 24 percent on weak home-furnishings demand in Europe and the United States, while healthcare sales rose 85 percent[2].

Who pays matters more than the product list. In healthcare the customers are pharmaceutical companies, mostly Indian generic makers who put a drug into Shaily's pen and sell it in India, Canada, Brazil and other markets[1][2]. Contracts are volume and market-driven, reviewed once a year, and management says they are not cost-plus. The main raw material comes from crude oil. Most customers have a pass-through clause, but price changes arrive late[9].

The profit pool reflects that mix. FY26 EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, the profit from running the plants) was ₹287.7 crore, a 29.0 percent margin against 15.9 percent in FY23[1]. Raw materials cost ₹426.2 crore, about 43 percent of sales, so the gross margin is the remaining 57 percent, the share of sales left after raw material. A pen injector is a higher-skill product than a furniture part, which is why profit grew far faster than sales.

How a pen injector business earns money

A pen injector is the plastic body, dose dial and needle mechanism a patient uses to inject a drug. Shaily designs the platforms itself, which is why management calls the business intellectual-property led. Six of its eight device platforms are fully commercial, built over seven to eight years[2]. The drug maker fills the pen and files the combined product with a health regulator. That filing creates the lock-in: once a regulator has approved a drug with a given pen, changing the pen means a new development programme.

Management puts numbers on the lock-in for Canada. Adding another device to a filing takes no less than 24 to 36 months. Generic companies spend ₹100 crore to ₹150 crore on such a programme, and would need 70 to 80 percent of that again for a second device. Of the first six filers in Canada, 70 percent used a Shaily device, and both pharma companies approved there use its pen[3]. Health Canada approved the first generic semaglutide on 28 April 2026[30][29].

Volume is the second driver. Shaily shipped close to 9 million pens and similar devices in the June 2026 quarter, and says 50 to 60 percent were for GLP-1 drugs, the class that includes semaglutide[2]. Healthcare revenue of ₹142.4 crore on those 9 million devices is about ₹158 a device, our own division of two reported figures[1]. Management says its pens sell above $2.

Capacity, new customers and the pipeline

Capacity is the constraint, and capital expenditure is cash spent on long-lived plant and machines. Management said in August 2026 that 25 million more pens of capacity would run by the end of September, taking installed capacity to about 75 million a year[2]. In February it put capacity at 80 million and announced an Abu Dhabi plant for about 75 million more pens, costing AED 130 million to AED 150 million (about ₹300 crore to ₹350 crore), due by the fourth quarter of FY28[4]. CARE puts FY27 and FY28 capital spending at about ₹500 crore, including ₹80 crore to ₹100 crore for a consumer-electronics plant in south India[9].

Outside pens, the company has started supplying a consumer-electronics customer, signed a supply agreement with a Korean company for semiconductor trays, and says it expects to be the only manufacturing partner in India for that product[3]. Tray revenue is due from the fourth quarter of FY27, with about ₹5 crore of investment[2]. A ₹423 crore, four-year pen supply contract with a large domestic pharma company was announced in February 2026, and the customer is not named[27].

What is missing matters too. Management has no commercial agreement yet with an innovator, one of the large drug companies that own the original brands. It has appointed heads of business development for Europe and North America and expects to announce something within four to six quarters[3][2]. That is a hope, not a contract, and is not in our numbers.

Who owns and runs the company

The promoter family holds 41.09 percent, down from 43.39 percent in March 2026. One promoter-group member, Vanita L Nagda, sold her whole 2.27 percent stake in the open market on 18 June 2026 at about ₹2,635 a share[14][11][17][16]. Mahendra Sanghvi is Executive Chairman and his son Amit Sanghvi is Managing Director[9]. Foreign institutions hold 19.43 percent and domestic institutions 16.14 percent.

The auditor is B S R and Co., and the board's report says the auditors' report has no qualification or adverse remark[8]. Contested tax claims total only ₹2.97 crore, and no promoter pledge is disclosed[14]. The live governance question is capital. 2. Why the business is strong or weak

A company is strong when customers find it hard to leave and growth does not need ever more borrowed money. On both tests Shaily beats its plastics peers. The weak points are as concrete. One customer is close to half of revenue, the fast-growing line depends on a handful of generic partners, and factory ramps have run behind schedule since August 2025. The subsections below cover the moat, the thin spots and the competition. Chapter 2B then scores the operating measures.

The moat: regulatory filings, platforms and precision moulding

The first strength is the regulatory lock-in from chapter 1. A drug filed in Canada with a Shaily pen cannot switch pens without a 24 to 36 month programme that costs ₹70 crore to ₹120 crore by management's arithmetic[3]. The annual report says early filings helped customers win four of the first semaglutide approvals in Canada with a Shaily device[8]. That evidence is management's own and rests on a few partners, so the moat is real but narrow.

The second strength is skill in precision moulding. Management says its consumer-electronics parts are more complex than its pen parts, and semiconductor trays use conductive plastics that fewer than a dozen makers worldwide can produce[2]. The third is financial. The return on capital employed (ROCE, return on capital employed, which is operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business) is 29.3 percent, debt is 0.25 times equity, and CARE rates the bank debt A+ with a stable outlook[11][9].

Where the business is thin

Concentration is the first weakness. The annual report names one customer with revenue of ₹452.38 crore in FY26 and ₹426.56 crore in FY25, against product and service revenue of ₹980.83 crore and ₹782.33 crore. That customer was 46.1 percent of FY26 revenue and 54.5 percent of FY25 revenue on our division of the filed figures[8]. The filing does not name the customer. CARE says its revenue counts in the consumer business and in the company total, and the ₹452.38 crore is above healthcare revenue in both years (₹393 crore and ₹165 crore), so it cannot be healthcare alone. CARE flags high customer concentration and moderate bargaining power[9].

Dependence on a few generic partners is the second weakness. Health Canada recalled Dr. Reddy's generic semaglutide on 26 August 2026 after an impurity was found, according to a consumer-information site, and supply was reported disrupted until at least late October[29]. An analyst on the August call named that company as the likely buyer of Shaily's ₹423 crore order. Management does not name customers. It says it still has most approvals and keeps its guidance, which is its own forecast of 36 million pens for FY27[2]. We cannot verify which customer was involved.

Execution is the third. Across five calls from August 2025 to August 2026 the first new 25 million pen line moved from the end of Q2 FY26 to the end of Q3, then to a March 2026 start. The second line moved from July or August to the end of September[6][5][4][3][2]. In February management said qualification, due by October 2025, was still running because the line was very complicated. That is why the capacity measure in chapter 2B scores WATCH.

Competition and pricing

Management names West, Phillips-Medisize, Stevanato and Haselmeier as competitors[2]. Winning programmes with innovator drug companies is where Shaily wants to grow, and it has no innovator agreement yet[3].

Chinese suppliers are the price threat. Management says they offer copies of 20-year-old technology at $1.50 to $1.70 a device, some infringing its patents, against Shaily's price above $2. It is studying a response but says it is not too concerned about the gap[2]. If the gap closes or a partner switches, the device price falls. That is why chapter 2B tests the cost-pass-through record and not only the latest quarter. In electronics and trays the rivals are plastic makers in Korea, China and the Philippines, some of which might set up in India, according to management[3].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in chapter 5 has one assumption that would hurt most if wrong. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong and when that evidence arrives, and then gives the strongest bear case against our own conclusions. The bear case matters because the verdict is a deferral. If the company is weaker than we think, the deferral should become a rejection. If it is stronger, waiting costs money. The dated tests in chapter 7 decide between them.

The strongest honest bear case runs like this. The shares price about 25 percent a year of profit growth for a decade at 76 times trailing profit. The largest customer is 46 percent of revenue, and the fast-growing line depends on a few generic drug makers, one recalled in Canada in August[8][29]. Chinese pens are offered at $1.50 to $1.70 against Shaily's price above $2[2]. Every factory milestone since August 2025 has moved later, crude is above $114 while polymer pass-through lags[28][9], and shareholders have authorised a ₹500 crore issue[18]. The case for buying now is that healthcare beat its guidance widely, the margin has held near 29 percent, the balance sheet is nearly debt-free, and consensus FY27 EPS is 18 percent above our central estimate[3][1][25]. We weigh the first case more, because price is what we can observe.

Weakest assumption 1: pen volume follows the new lines

Our central case takes management's pen plan, 36 million in FY27 and 50 million in FY28, at ₹168 a device, giving healthcare revenue of ₹605 crore and ₹840 crore. It assumes two new 25 million lines run at their specified speed. The record says otherwise so far. The first line ran at about 45 percent efficiency in May, and in August management reported a 9 percent speed gain and a plan for a further 30 percent once equipment arrives[3][2]. The second line moved from July or August to the end of September. Output at the June rate of 9 million a quarter gives 36 million only if every quarter repeats it.

The rule is the same in Trigger 4: a September-quarter count below 8.0 million cuts the central case (FY27 pens to about 32 million), and a count below 9.0 million blocks or cancels any order. A count of 9.0 million or more with line 2 commercial confirms the assumption; 8.0 to 8.99 million is a watch band. Both are known on the 30 October call and in the December-quarter numbers, due about mid-February 2027 on last year's timing (date not confirmed)[19]. The bear case, about 34.5 million pens in FY27 and 35.7 million in FY28, is where lateness shows. A waiting customer can also open a second source, though management says that takes 24 to 36 months in Canada[3].

Weakest assumption 2: the margin survives crude oil and freight

Our central case holds the EBITDA margin at 29.5 percent in FY27 and 30 percent in FY28. The risk is cost, not demand. The main raw material comes from crude oil and the pass-through to customers arrives late. Management says gross margin fell in the June quarter as freight and polymer prices rose after March, with pass-through delayed until May to July[9][2]. It expects gross margin to be normal by the third quarter. Brent was above $114 a barrel on the ledger date[28].

The stress in chapter 5 shows the stakes. A margin 2 points lower cuts FY28 EPS by about ₹4.8 and the value by about ₹240 a share, and a drift back to 25 percent cuts the value to ₹2,300. Evidence of a wrong assumption would be an EBITDA margin below 28 percent in the September quarter, known on 29 October. Margin held at 28 to 31 percent in four of the last five quarters, but a margin that rose 13 points in three years can also fall, and CARE sets its rating threshold at 15 percent[11][9].

Weakest assumption 3: the largest customer and the generic partners

There are two separate risks. The first is the largest customer. Its revenue was ₹452.38 crore in FY26, 46.1 percent on our division of two filed figures, down from 54.5 percent a year earlier[8]. CARE says it contributes to consumer-business revenue and to the company total, and the arithmetic agrees, since ₹452 crore exceeds healthcare revenue of ₹393 crore. We do not name it. Its revenue rose about 6 percent in FY26 while consumer revenue fell 9 percent, and consumer fell 24 percent in June[9][2]. The filing does not say what share of consumer it is, so a consumer slowdown could hit it directly.

The second risk is the generic drug partners behind healthcare growth. They file and sell the drug, so a quality problem at a partner becomes a Shaily volume problem. The 26 August 2026 Canadian recall of an Indian generic maker's semaglutide is a live example, though management says it still has most approvals[29][2]. Evidence of a wrong assumption would be a partner losing an approval or a second recall, or a consumer quarter that cuts the largest customer's revenue. A change in that customer's size shows only in the next annual report, in mid-2027. A signed innovator deal would lower partner concentration, but it is not in our numbers.

Weakest assumption 4: the multiple stays near 50 times

The central value of ₹2,900 rests on 50 times FY28 earnings, which is our judgment and not a fact. The stock's own history is wide. Its trailing ratio ranged from 37.5 to 179.1 over three years with a median of 83.2, and five turns of multiple are worth about ₹290 a share[13]. Small-cap multiples compress when breadth narrows, and the ledger shows only 10.2 percent of stocks above their 50-day average[28].

Evidence of a wrong assumption would be a re-rating up that holds on volume, such as a close above the 50-day average of ₹3,200.69, or a re-rating down through the 200-day average. The most direct test is the reaction to the September results. On the last three results-call days the shares moved -7.4 percent on 13 February, +3.7 percent on 20 May and +6.5 percent on 10 August, so a 5 percent move either way is ordinary around a release (our own computation from the exchange's daily files, with call dates from the transcripts)[4][3][2]. On the same computation, eight of the last 248 sessions moved the stock 7 percent or more.

Weakest assumption 5: capital is funded without dilution

CARE expects the roughly ₹500 crore of FY27 and FY28 capital spending to come mainly from internal accruals of over ₹250 crore a year, with debt repayments of ₹25 crore to ₹60 crore and low gearing as headroom[9]. That is reasonable, since FY26 operating cash flow was ₹225.7 crore, but it leaves little room for a second large project. Management has said it may need one-off, substantial spending beyond India and Abu Dhabi, which is why shareholders approved the ₹500 crore enabling resolution with 32,507,194 votes for and 4 against[3][18].

At today's value, ₹500 crore is about 3.7 percent of the company, so an issue at market would dilute owners by about that much and an issue at a discount by more. Evidence of a wrong assumption would be a board notice of a qualified institutions placement or a preferential issue, which the company must file with the exchange. The Abu Dhabi budget of AED 130 million to AED 150 million could also move, and management says it is in talks with the Abu Dhabi government on support, not yet agreed[4][9].
