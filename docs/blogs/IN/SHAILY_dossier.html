1. What the company does

Shaily Engineering Plastics is a Gujarat company that turns plastic pellets into finished parts. It melts the pellets, presses them into steel moulds and assembles the parts into products for other companies. This is called injection moulding. The company was founded in 1987 and now runs seven plants in Gujarat, six for plastic and one for carbon-steel furniture, with more than 200 moulding machines and over 2,000 employees.[5] Consolidated sales were ₹991 crore in FY26 (the year to 31 March 2026), and about 68% of them were exports.[8][1]

It sells to three groups of customers. Consumer customers are large retailers and brands in Europe and the United States that buy home furnishings, packaging for everyday goods, lamps and toys. Industrial customers buy parts for cars, appliances, power tools and electronics. Healthcare customers are drug companies. Shaily designs and builds pen injectors for them. A pen injector is the plastic device a patient holds to inject a measured dose of a drug such as insulin or semaglutide. [5] Healthcare is the part that changed the company. Its revenue grew from ₹165 crore in FY25 to ₹393 crore in FY26, which is a rise of 139%, and it overtook consumer sales in the June 2026 quarter.[8][5]

The money mechanics differ by segment. In consumer and industrial work, Shaily earns a processing margin: it buys polymer, shapes it, and sells the part at a price that follows resin cost with a delay. Management says it passes resin cost increases to most customers, but the pass-through arrives weeks or months later.[6][13] In healthcare the product is Shaily's own design. The company owns the pen platforms, which are named Neo, Harmony, Toby, Axiom Max and Protean, and drug companies file them with regulators inside their own drug applications.[15][8] Management says these contracts are not cost-plus. They are priced on volume and market conditions, with an annual price review.[6]

The numbers give a rough price per device. Healthcare revenue of ₹142.4 crore in the June 2026 quarter was earned on about 9 million devices, so each device brought in about ₹158 on our division of two cited figures.[5][6] That figure includes design services and non-pen work, so it overstates the pen price. Management puts its own pen price at a little above $2, against $1.50 to $1.70 for the Chinese copies now entering the market.[6] Price per pen is the number that matters most in this report, because every other forecast sits on top of it.

Profit measures need defining before the tables. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is the profit from running the plants before financing and wear-and-tear charges. Shaily's EBITDA was ₹287.7 crore in FY26, a margin of 29.0%, up from 22.7% a year earlier.[5] The gross margin is sales minus the cost of raw material, shown as a share of sales. It was 56.6% in the June 2026 quarter, so for every ₹100 of sales about ₹43 went on polymer and bought-in parts.[5] Both margins rose because the mix moved to healthcare, where Shaily owns the design.

Who the customers are, and why they are few

The credit rating agency CARE calls the company's customer and segment concentration relatively high. A single customer is a sizeable share of the consumer business and of total sales.[13] The company does not publish the share of its top ten customers, so we cannot put a percentage on it.[15] In healthcare the buyers are generic drug makers. Management names no customers, but it says the first two companies to win Canadian approval for generic semaglutide both use a Shaily pen, and that Shaily's device sits in about 70% of the first six Canadian filings.[8]

Concentration cuts both ways. Few customers mean large orders: in February 2026 the company announced a ₹423 crore, four-year pen order from a large Indian drug company, which it did not name. The company said the contract is not a related-party transaction.[22] It also means that trouble at one customer lands on Shaily. In the August 2026 call an analyst said that one partner has impurity problems in Canada, and management replied that it still has approvals in many markets and stands by its pen volume plan.[6] A trade publication reported on 21 September 2026 that Canada approved a further generic, from Sandoz, while Dr. Reddy's faced supply shortages. The company has not said whether Dr. Reddy's is its customer.[24]

How the plants and capacity work

A pen is made on a line of moulding machines feeding an assembly and printing line. Each line is a large piece of equipment from one of a handful of global suppliers. Management says the time from ordering a line to running it has stretched from about 12 months to 18 to 24 months.[8][15] Shaily has 30 million pens a year of older capacity that is shared between insulin, teriparatide and other pens. It added a 25 million pen line in March 2026 and had a second 25 million line due by the end of September 2026, which would take the total to about 75 million pens a year. That date has passed, and no filing or call after 10 August 2026 confirms the line is running.[6][15]

Machines are far from full. Machine use across all plants was 50.2% in the June 2026 quarter, up from 48.7% a year earlier.[5] The company says contracts with its customers usually guarantee about 80% of capacity, so it never plans to run at 100%.[12] For a plastics business this is both a risk and an opportunity: fixed costs are high, so each extra pen on an installed line adds more profit than the average pen. The risk is the reverse. If volume stalls, the depreciation bill on the new lines weighs on profit. Depreciation has already risen from ₹42 crore in FY25 to ₹52 crore over the last twelve months.[1]

What the company is building next

Three new areas are being built beside healthcare. The first is consumer electronics: a south Indian plant costing ₹80 to ₹100 crore, with commercial supply to a first customer already started and five new parts won from a second. Management says it can reach about $10 million of revenue in 24 to 30 months, and warns that this is not guidance, which means a forecast the company commits to.[6][8] The second is semiconductor trays, made from conductive plastic for a Korean customer, with supply expected in the March 2027 quarter and about ₹5 crore of investment.[6][8] The third is a ₹300 to ₹350 crore pen plant in Abu Dhabi, aimed at 75 million pens a year, due to run by the end of FY28.[12][13]

The rating agency counts about ₹500 crore of spending over FY27 and FY28, of which ₹350 crore is Abu Dhabi.[13] Separately, the board in May 2026 approved a resolution to raise up to ₹500 crore from shareholders through a share sale. Management says it is renewed every year and does not signal a planned raise.[8][7] We return to the funding question in Chapter 6.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position has one strong feature and several soft spots. The strong feature is regulatory switching cost. When a drug company files a new generic with Health Canada, the pen is part of the filing. Management says that adding a second device to an existing Canadian filing takes 24 to 36 months and costs 70% to 80% of the original ₹100 to ₹150 crore programme, and the new device might still fail.[8] A customer that has been approved with a Shaily pen therefore has a reason to stay for years. That claim comes from management, so we weigh it as a claim, not a measurement. Two facts support it: the first two Canadian approvals use Shaily pens, and the CARE rating agency describes the company's own pen technologies as a strength.[8][13]

The soft spots are price, delivery and proof. On price, the Chinese devices selling at $1.50 to $1.70 are about 15% to 25% below Shaily's price above $2, on our division of two management figures. Management calls them copies of old technology and says some infringe patents, but it admits it has not settled how to respond.[6] On delivery, the company shipped about 23.5 million devices in FY26 against a plan of 30 million, because its lines ran below rated speed.[8][10] On proof, the company has not signed any agreement with an innovator drug maker for the branded GLP-1 market. Management hopes to announce one within about six quarters.[6][8]

Consumer is the older, thinner business. Shaily supplies large home-furnishing buyers and its sales depend on demand in Europe and the United States. In the June 2026 quarter that segment fell 24%, and management said in August 2026 that Indian demand does not matter because India is only about 2% of the segment's volume.[5][6] US tariffs on Indian goods were cut to 18% from 50% under a February 2026 trade deal. That should help Indian exporters, but the effect had not shown in Shaily's consumer sales by June.[26][5]

What the industry data says about demand for pens

Demand comes from the end of Novo Nordisk's semaglutide patent in India, China, Brazil and Canada during 2026. In India the patent expired on 20 March 2026 and at least five domestic drug makers launched generics within days, at prices up to 80% below the original.[23] More than 50 brands are expected in the coming months, and Dr. Reddy's has said it is targeting about 12 million semaglutide pens in its first year.[23] In Brazil the regulator approved a first semaglutide copy in May 2026.[24] IQVIA lists India, Canada, China, Brazil and Turkey, which hold about 40% of the world's population, among the countries where the patent ends in 2026.[27] Each pen sold needs a device, and Shaily is one of few suppliers with a filed, approved one.

The same data shows three limits. First, the United States, the largest market, is protected from generic semaglutide until 2032, so the biggest pool is closed to Shaily's current customers.[23] Second, pills are arriving: Goldman Sachs forecasts that pills could take about 24% of the global weight-loss drug market by 2030, which reduces the share left for injection pens.[25] Third, generics launch at low prices. Lower drug prices mean the pen is a larger share of what the drug maker earns, and that gives the drug maker a reason to push the pen price down at the next annual review.[23][6]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of reported numbers show a company that nearly doubled sales and multiplied profit by about five. Consolidated sales rose from ₹568 crore in FY22 to ₹991 crore in FY26, and net profit rose from ₹35 crore to ₹170 crore.[1] Three-year growth is the fairer yardstick, because FY22 was a recovery year. From FY23 to FY26 sales grew about 18% a year and profit grew about 69% a year, on our workings of (991 / 607) and (169.9 / 35.1), each raised to the power of one-third.[1][5]

The profit growth came from a shift in what the company sells. EBITDA margin rose from 15.9% in FY23 to 29.0% in FY26 as the share of healthcare rose from 17% of sales in FY24 to 40% in FY26.[5][8][10] The company's return on capital employed, which management quotes at 35.8% for March 2026 and Screener shows at 29.3%, jumped for the same reason.[8][1] The two figures differ because they use different capital bases. We use the lower, Screener number, so we are not flattering the company.

Cash and debt moved the right way. Cash from operations was ₹428 crore over FY24 to FY26 against profit of ₹320 crore, a ratio of 1.34. Borrowings were ₹177 crore at March 2026, against net worth of ₹716 crore, and CARE puts the overall gearing at 0.26 times.[1][5][13] Free cash flow, which is operating cash minus capital spending, was ₹41 crore in FY26 because the company spent ₹185 crore on new lines and plants. Spending of about ₹500 crore is planned for FY27 and FY28, so free cash flow will stay thin and debt may rise.[13][20] A common yardstick for price is the price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E: the share price divided by the last twelve months' earnings per share. At ₹3,109.70 and ₹38.46 of earnings, Shaily's P/E is 80.9, which means investors pay ₹80.9 for each ₹1 of yearly profit.[1]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks two questions. Do the static levels clear the floors, and is the direction improving? The red lines come first because failing one ends the case. They are debt-to-equity of 1.20 or more, interest cover below 2, a promoter pledge of 20% or more of the promoter holding, three-year cash conversion below 0.40, and a Stage 4 breakdown. Stage 2 is the rising markup phase of a price trend, and Stage 4 is the falling markdown phase. The company must then clear the compounder floors (ROCE of 15% or more, debt-to-equity under 0.70, cash conversion of 0.70 or more), a chart posture floor, and the asymmetry floor of 3.0. The chart floor for the Core bucket is a Stage 1 breakout, which is a price move up through the top of a flat zone where the stock had been resting, or a Stage 2 markup. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a weakest assumption. For the FY27 EPS of ₹46.7, it is that gross margin returns to normal and the second pen line, due by the end of September, is running. It was unconfirmed at 16 October. For the ₹4,626 target, it is that the market keeps paying 83.2 times earnings. For the reverse DCF hurdle of 25.3%, it is that growth lasts ten years. This chapter takes each in turn, then lays out the red-team case against our own view: three ways the stock could go badly wrong even though the business is good.[6][2][20]

Fatal flaw 1: the pen price and the partners turn out thinner than planned

Shaily's profit now depends on one product family. About 50% to 60% of the roughly 9 million devices made in the June quarter were for GLP-1 drugs, and healthcare is 51% of sales.[6][5] The pen is priced on volume and market conditions with an annual review, not on cost plus a margin.[6] Chinese copies are entering at $1.50 to $1.70 against Shaily's price above $2.[6] Indian drug makers launched generic semaglutide at prices up to 80% below the original, so the drug maker has every reason to press for a cheaper pen at the next review.[23]

The customers are also fragile. An analyst said on the August 2026 call that one partner has impurity problems in Canada, and a trade publication reported supply shortages at Dr. Reddy's in September.[6][24] The company has not named its customers, so we cannot tell which of them is affected.[6] Pills could take about 24% of the weight-loss market by 2030, and the United States stays closed to generic semaglutide until 2032.[25][23] On our working, a 10% cut in the pen price on 50 million pens in FY28 would remove about ₹79 crore of revenue at the ₹158 per device from Chapter 1. Almost all of it would fall to profit, since costs do not change. After 25.5% tax that is ₹59 crore, or ₹12.8 a share, which is 20% of our FY28 EPS of ₹64.6.

Fatal flaw 2: the lines stay slow and the capital plan needs shareholders' money

The first new line was commissioned in March 2026 and ran at about 45% efficiency in May. Its speed rose by 9% by August, and management still needs equipment that has not arrived.[8][6] The second line was due at the end of September. That date has passed with no confirmation in any filing we hold (the latest management statement is from 10 August 2026), so it is unconfirmed. Management says lines now take 18 to 24 months from order to running, up from 12.[8][6] Each month of delay costs sales that cannot be recovered, since the contracts are for supply, not for capacity.

The plan also needs money. CARE counts about ₹500 crore of spending over FY27 and FY28, funded mostly from accruals, and says the gearing should stay comfortable.[13] The Street expects capex of ₹172 crore in FY27 and ₹240 crore in FY28 and free cash flow of ₹56 crore and ₹101 crore.[20] Those forecasts leave little margin for error. The board has asked shareholders to approve raising up to ₹500 crore by issuing shares, and management says it is renewed each year.[8][7] Dilution means that new shares reduce the slice of the company each existing share owns. A ₹500 crore sale at today's price is about 3.5% of the company's ₹14,303 crore value, so existing holders would own about 3.4% less of every rupee of future profit. If the share price fell first, the cost would be higher.

Fatal flaw 3: the multiple falls even if profit grows

The stock trades at 80.9 times trailing earnings, and the peers we fetched trade at 18.1 to 50.5 times.[1][28][29][30][31] The three-year median of 83.2 hides a wide range: from 37.5 to 179.1.[2] If profit grows 38% in FY28 but the multiple falls to 47 times, the price would be about ₹2,610 to ₹3,040 on our EPS, which is a fall of 2% to 16%.

Three signals could make the market less generous. Consumer sales are falling 24% a year, so growth depends on one segment.[5] Promoters have sold: 1.5 lakh shares in a block deal in November 2025, and 10.43 lakh shares in June 2026. The June sale accounts for the fall in the family's holding from 43.39% to 41.09%, since the November sale came before the March 2026 figure.[17][18][1] An advance of ₹7.6 crore to the managing director sits on the balance sheet.[15] None of these breaks a red line. Each is a reason for a buyer to ask for a lower price.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, because three gates fail. The reward-to-risk ratio is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, and it is 2.63 against a floor of 3.0 for the Core bucket. The chart is in Stage 3, not at a Stage 1 breakout or in Stage 2 markup. And the Layer 2 scorecard is 3/5 against a 4/5 bar. The quality tests all pass: debt-to-equity is 0.25, cash conversion is 1.34, ROCE is 29.3% and nothing is pledged. This is a good business at a price that leaves too little margin for the plant delays we have seen. SHAILY is not a current holding, so no sale is needed.[1][16]

We did not use the turnaround route, because it does not fit. That route is for companies that miss a static floor but have an improving trend and a cheap price. Shaily meets every static floor, and its P/E of 80.9 is only 2.8% under its three-year median, not the 25% discount that route requires. Test C passing means a starter entry near support is allowed for a turnaround, not for a compounder.
