1. What the company does

Lodha Developers Limited builds homes and sells them to buyers before the buildings are finished. Its shares trade on the National Stock Exchange under the symbol LODHA. The registrar of companies approved the change of name from Macrotech Developers Limited on 16 June 2025, and the trading symbol did not change.[19] The company says it has delivered about 110 million square feet of property and is developing more than 130 million square feet.[1] In the year to March 2026 it booked ₹20,530 crore of home sales, reported revenue of ₹16,676 crore and reported profit of ₹3,431 crore.[5][27]

Three numbers describe this business, and mixing them up is the most common mistake. Pre-sales are the value of homes that buyers agreed to buy in a period. Collections are the cash that actually arrived from buyers. Revenue and profit are what the accounts record as construction advances. In FY26 pre-sales were ₹20,530 crore, up 16%, but collections were ₹15,160 crore, up only 5%.[5] A buyer pays in instalments over the three to four years it takes to build, so a sale booked today turns into cash and profit over several later years.

Lodha records revenue as it spends on construction and receives minimum payments from buyers, not when it hands over keys. The managing director, Abhishek Lodha, named three drivers of quarterly revenue: the minimum payments received from buyers, the construction cost spent in the quarter, and whether land or rental deals closed in that quarter.[3] That is why quarterly profit jumps around, and why he asked investors not to multiply any single quarter by four.[3] The company is also its own general contractor, which it presents as an advantage in cost and quality control.[2]

The company describes itself as three businesses. The first, called DevCo, builds and sells homes and some for-sale offices across Mumbai, Pune and Bengaluru, with a first launch in the Delhi region due in FY27.[2][3] The second, RentCo, owns or is building offices, shops, warehouses and data-centre buildings that earn rent. Its rent was ₹300 crore a year at June 2026, and the company targets more than ₹3,000 crore a year by FY32, of which more than ₹2,000 crore would come from data centres.[2][3] The third, LandCo, sells surplus land. About 660 acres near Navi Mumbai are set aside as a data-centre park, and 132 acres had been sold by 30 June 2026.[2]

Most buyers are in one region. Of the ₹4,630 crore of June-quarter bookings, ₹4,090 crore, or 88%, came from the Mumbai Metropolitan Region, ₹220 crore from Pune, ₹200 crore from Bengaluru and ₹130 crore from land and other sales.[2] Almost 60% of those sales were premium or luxury homes, and buyers in the Middle East made up 4% to 5% of sales.[3] The rating agency ICRA expects the Mumbai region to stay at 60% to 70% of sales over the medium term, so a slowdown there would reach Lodha's results quickly.[20]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Lodha's first advantage is that buyers pay for its name. Management says like-for-like prices rose 3% in the June quarter and expects 5% to 7% across micro-markets over the year, still below the 9% to 10% growth in white-collar pay.[2][3] In the ₹100 crore-plus homes segment it says it holds a 40% share.[4] These figures come from the company's own statements, and no independent price study was found, so treat them as management's claim rather than proof.

The second advantage is a market that is moving towards trusted names. Management estimates that branded developers held less than 20% of the Mumbai-region market five years ago, almost 30% by the end of 2025, and could reach 40% to 45% by 2030.[3] Anarock data supports a steady local market: Mumbai-region sales rose 5% to 31,750 homes in July to September 2026.[23] The same data show that supply is growing faster than sales. Unsold homes in the seven largest cities rose 12% to 6.31 lakh and launches rose 18%, so a weaker market would put pressure on prices.[23][24]

The third advantage is land. ICRA counts about 4,250 acres of land holdings and Palava, the township near Navi Mumbai, sells homes at about ₹11,000 a square foot of carpet area against more than ₹25,000 in Mulund and Thane, according to management.[20][4] The company expects the Mulund to Airoli to Palava freeway to open after the 2026 monsoon, which would put Airoli within a 20-minute drive and Mulund within 25 minutes.[3] Land prices in the data-centre park have already moved: the latest sale, to Digital Edge, was at about ₹425 million an acre in June 2026 against about ₹210 million an acre in 2025.[2]

The balance sheet is the fourth strength. Net debt was ₹4,931 crore at June 2026, which is 0.2 times equity against a self-imposed ceiling of 0.5 times, and the exit cost of debt was 7.8%.[1] ICRA reaffirmed its AA (Stable) rating on 22 September 2026, and CRISIL assigned AA/Stable in December 2025.[20][21] There is a catch inside that strength. Gross debt rose 40% to ₹9,896 crore in FY26 as the company paid for land and growth, and ICRA notes that gross debt to cash flow rose to 1.39 times from 1.09 times.[20]

The weaknesses are specific. Audited cash from operations was ₹959 crore against profit of ₹3,431 crore in FY26, so only 28% of profit arrived as operating cash.[8][27] In the June quarter about ₹600 crore of the ₹1,373 crore profit came from land sales, which management calls a planned, recurring pillar rather than a one-off, while also warning that quarterly profit will not follow a straight line.[3] Two promoter entities sold about 2% of the company at ₹937.85 a share on 25 June 2026, which cut the promoter stake from 72.27% to about 70.3%.[25] The promoter group's 70.29% includes 18.01% held by the Lodha Philanthropy Foundation, which a 2024 report says may sell no more than 0.5% of the company's equity a year after 31 March 2026. The June sellers were two other promoter entities.[15][26][25] A separate scheme to absorb two group companies, Roselabs Finance Limited and National Standard (India) Limited, won 99.95% of the votes polled at the 9 October 2026 shareholder meeting and awaits tribunal sanction. The outcome filing states no share exchange ratio, so our per-share figures use today's share count.[16] None of these is a solvency problem, but each is a reason a careful buyer would want more evidence before paying a full price.

On 7 October 2026 the Reserve Bank of India raised its policy repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% and changed its stance to calibrated tightening, citing the West Asia conflict, crude oil volatility and consumer price inflation of 4.8% in August.[22] It still projects growth of 7.1% for 2026-27.[22] LODHA has been weak around that period: it closed 24.87% below its 52-week high on 27 October, and its one-month move, which began before the rate decision, was a fall of 12.75%.[34] The rate decision is one plausible cause, and the data do not show how much of the fall it explains. 2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A home developer cannot be judged by revenue growth and a price-to-earnings ratio alone, because profit and cash arrive at different times. We chose five measures that govern this business. Bookings against the company's own target show whether buyers still want the product. Cash conversion shows whether profit turns into money. Borrowing and interest cover show whether growth is being paid for safely. Embedded margin and return on equity show whether growth is profitable. Land and unsold inventory show how long the sales engine can run without buying more land.[2][5][8]

Each measure scores 1.0 for a pass, 0.5 for a watch and 0.0 for a fail. The rule in the scoring file is that a company qualifies at 3.0 out of 5.0 or more with at most one fail. Lodha scores 4.0 out of 5.0 with no fail, so it qualifies as PASS_WITH_WATCH. The two watches, bookings against target and cash conversion, are timing questions that the 30 October result will start to answer.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

We compared what management said it would do in February 2025 and February 2026 with what it reported. On bookings, new projects, borrowing and operating cash flow for FY25, the company delivered or beat its targets. For FY26 it missed bookings by 2.2% and missed its original operating cash flow target by 8%, although it met the lower figure it published in February 2026.[13][12][5][4]

Two items need plain mention. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate rests on one assumption that is weaker than the rest. This chapter names each one, says what evidence would show it is wrong, and says when we will know. It then gives the strongest honest case against the idea, written as a sceptic would write it.

Weakest assumption in the profit estimate: land sales and a 20% growth path

Our FY28 profit per share of ₹49.2 assumes 20% growth in both FY27 and FY28. The June quarter looked far ahead of that pace. But management said the quarter held about half of the year's expected land sales. Land profit was about ₹600 crore of the ₹1,373 crore.[3] That leaves about ₹2,727 crore for the next three quarters to meet the ₹4,100 crore goal. That is about ₹909 crore a quarter. The September quarter of 2025 earned ₹790 crore and the March quarter earned ₹1,008 crore.[27] The goal is reachable, but it is not a cushion. A September or December quarter below ₹850 crore with no land sale to explain it would show the assumption wrong. So would a cut to the ₹4,100 crore goal. We will know the first on 30 October 2026 and the second in late January 2027.

Weakest assumption in the value: that the price-to-earnings ratio holds at 24.4

The base value keeps today's multiple. The lowest reading in the three-year series was 20.8, on 2 April 2026, only seven months ago. On ₹49.2 of profit, a multiple of 20 gives ₹984. That is only 2.6% below the last close.[28] The Reserve Bank has just started to tighten. It changed its stance to calibrated tightening.[22] So a lower multiple is possible even if profit is on target. A further rate rise at the next policy meeting would show the assumption wrong. So would a close below ₹899.74. The next policy date is not confirmed on the page we read, so we flag it as unknown. Because the low came so recently, a fall back towards it is a live possibility if the market decides that rising rates deserve a lower price for every rupee of profit. What this means for you: the base value is a view on profit, not on mood, and the buying range is set low enough to absorb a lower multiple.

Weakest assumption in the safety case: that cash from operations recovers in FY27

The Layer 1 pass on cash conversion rests on the three-year figure of 0.65. The single-year figures were 1.62, 0.57 and 0.28. Management expects its own operating cash flow to grow about 20% a year from ₹7,100 crore. It says spending on new land will be muted for two years. That should help the audited figure.[4] Half-year audited cash from operations that is negative or well under ₹800 crore would show this wrong. The full year needs at least ₹1,594 crore to keep the three-year ratio above 0.40. The half-year statement comes with the 30 October result. The full year follows in April 2027. The weakness in this assumption is that management gave no numerical target for audited cash, only a growth rate for its own measure, so there is nothing to hold it to. What this means for you: if the half-year cash figure disappoints, the company would still be safe, but the Core Alpha label would be in doubt and the second tranche would not be bought.

The red-team bear case

A sceptic would put it this way. The headline of a 103% profit rise hides the fact that 44% of the quarter's profit was land. Bookings grew only 4% and sit at 19.3% of the annual target. So the second half must be about 25% above last year's second half. If the second quarter delivers ₹5,000 crore, the second half needs ₹14,370 crore. The second half of FY26 delivered ₹11,510 crore.[1][3][5] Audited cash was 28% of profit, and the three-year ratio could cross the red line. Two promoter entities sold about 2% of the company at ₹937.85 in June. The shares touched ₹937.10 again at the 26 October low.[25][34] Unsold homes in the seven largest cities are up 12%, and the Reserve Bank has started to raise rates.[24][22] The data-centre story rests on land prices doubling in under a year, from about ₹210 million to about ₹425 million an acre. Management believes fair value is higher still. That is an expectation, not a contract.[2][3]

What the sceptic concedes: the balance sheet is not the problem. Net debt is 0.2 times equity. ICRA and CRISIL rate the company AA. Contingent liabilities were only ₹145 crore at March 2026.[1][20][21][9] So the bear case is a price fall to about ₹860, which is 15% below today. It is not a collapse. We have priced that case at 30%. It is the reason the position is sized at 3.5% and not 6.5%.
