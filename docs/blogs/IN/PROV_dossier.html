1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Cycles

Core Investor Question: How was the business built, and what did each stage do to revenue, margins and cash?

Proventus Agrocom has rebuilt its business model more than once in eleven years. Each rebuild reset revenue, margin and cash in a different way, and the latest one has not yet run through a full year. We trace four operating regimes: commodity bulk trading to FY2020, the brand pivot of FY2021–FY2022, the brand scale-up of FY2023–FY2026, and the manufacturing build-out that began in FY2027. Dates and figures come from the prospectus, annual reports and exchange filings [10][3][1].

Founders from the commodity desk

The company was incorporated in Mumbai on 20 October 2015 as Manthan Agrocom Private Limited. It was renamed Proventus Agrocom within two months and became a public company in March 2023 [10]. Its first stated object was commodity broking, trading and hedging, including derivatives, across agricultural products, metals and energy. Agriculture came second [10].

The two operating founders are commodity-desk people. Durga Prasad Jhawar, the managing director and CEO, is a chartered accountant with more than 26 years in the field and was managing director of Edelweiss Commodities. Deepak Kumar Agrawal, the whole-time director, ran Aster Commodities DMCC in Dubai. CFO Ankush Jain came from Edelweiss Treasury [3]. A third promoter, Shalin Khanna, left in 2025. A Dubai commodity entity followed in May 2016 [10]. These are traders who learned to run a factory and a brand, not brand managers who learned to trade.

Regime one: bulk trading and the FY2020 loss

In the prospectus the company says it began by “undertaking bulk trades” in agro produce from the Navi Mumbai wholesale market, importing almonds from California and Australia [10]. By FY2020 consolidated revenue was ₹896.2 crore. Bulk sales of other commodities were ₹474.9 crore (53.0%), bulk and white-label dry fruits were ₹385.4 crore (43.0%), and the ProV brand was only ₹35.05 crore (3.9%) [10]. This was a trading house with a small brand attached.

FY2020 ended with a restated EBITDA of –₹21.67 crore and a loss after tax of ₹21.90 crore [10]. The prospectus gives the cause. Derivative positions in commodities lost ₹19.26 crore when the Covid lockdown halted trade and international prices fell. The company also held a large stock of unsold goods at that point, since inventory of stock in trade rose by about ₹173 crore in the year, and finance cost was ₹11.24 crore [10]. The loss equalled 44% of the ₹49.8 crore net worth that remained at year-end [10]. A thin-margin book of ₹900 crore can lose a large part of its capital on one bad price move, and the pivot to branded goods set out to remove that risk.

Regime two: the deliberate shrink into brand

FY2021 revenue fell 66% to ₹300.9 crore. The fall was chosen, not forced. Bulk and white-label sales dropped 71% from ₹861.2 crore to ₹250.5 crore, the company discontinued its agri-commodity business, and derivative activity was cut back [10]. ProV brand sales rose 42% to ₹49.8 crore. Finance cost fell 93% to ₹0.81 crore because the credit lines were barely used [10]. The business made a ₹1.88 crore profit, and EBITDA margin moved from –2.4% to about 1.0%.

The legal structure was built for this pivot. Prov Foods Private Limited, the brand and manufacturing subsidiary, was incorporated on 25 November 2019. Proventus Retail dates to December 2015, the Dubai commodity entity to 2016, and the Pro-Nova Bio Technologies shell to October 2020 [10]. The Mumbai plant lifted packing from 40,000 to 60,000 pouches a day around 2021–2022, then moved from Mahape to Khairane after a vacate notice in January 2023 [10]. Sources disagree on the ProV launch year: the prospectus says 2017, the website 2016, and the FY2026 annual report timeline implies 2019. We use 2017 [10][3].

Between 2015 and 2020 the entity was a trading company with a side brand. From 2022 the dates cluster around capital, people and plants: an IPO, a promoter exit, an auditor change and two factories. Each of those events carries a governance or execution question that later chapters weigh.

The endorsement deal and the subsidiary web

The November 2022 endorsement agreement with Chef Sanjeev Kapoor and KS Narayanan, amended in March 2023, was paid largely in equity and warrants of Prov Foods rather than cash [10]. That is why the listed parent now owns 98.75% of its main operating subsidiary and not 100% [3]. The structure saved cash in a year of thin margins, and it hands the endorsers a small slice of Prov Foods’ future profit. Proventus Retail has no turnover, Pro-Nova Bio is a 51% shell, and the Dubai entity lost ₹0.18 crore in FY2026 [3]. In economic terms the group is two companies, the parent and Prov Foods, which chapter 2 separates.

The IPO: what was promised and what was done

The March 2023 draft was book-built; the April draft and May final prospectus were fixed-price [12][11][10]. The issue was 9,02,000 shares at ₹771, worth ₹69.54 crore. Fresh shares were 6,71,853, raising ₹51.80 crore. The offer for sale was 2,30,147 shares worth ₹17.74 crore, which went to the sellers, chiefly the promoter LLP (1,02,147 shares) and Guruprasad Rao (95,001 shares) [10]. About a quarter of the issue was therefore not company money. After ₹1.25 crore of issue expenses, net proceeds to the company were ₹50.55 crore.

After the IPO: exits, slippage and open claims

Shalin Khanna, a named promoter and whole-time director, resigned on 22 May 2025 “to pursue other opportunities and interests” [29]. The CEO called it personal reasons [9]. He was reclassified from promoter to public by September 2025 and holds 3.07% [21]. The promoter holding fell from 70.45% to 67.34% as a result, so the decline is reclassification, not open-market selling [3][21].

The auditor change is the second flag. NBT & Co was appointed in February 2023, re-approved in August 2023 for five years, and resigned on 26 December 2025, about 2.3 years into that term. Its letter cites a “strategic focus on specialised certification and advisory engagements” and bandwidth for a larger audit [26][16]. Chechani Soni & Co audited FY2026 with an unmodified opinion and no key audit matters, and was re-appointed in September 2026 [3][27]. We see no evidence of a dispute, but a mid-term exit by the auditor of the IPO-era accounts is worth weighing beside the cash-flow record.

Surat is the clearest case of execution slippage. In September 2024 the CEO said the new plant was “set to be up and running by the end of the year” [19]. In May 2025 the target became the end of FY2026 [9]. Phase 1 was declared ready in July 2026 and “completed and operational” in November 2026, at a Phase 1 capex of ₹14 crore [7][2]. That is roughly 22 months after the first promise. Capacity promises also moved, from 8 lakh units a day in the filing, to 4 lakh at the AGM, to 5 lakh pouches on completion [25][19][2]. Phases 2 and 3, with cold storage, are now due by June 2027 [2].

Two claims remain open. The Bihar makhana unit was announced in May 2026 at 2,500 tonnes a year, but a July 2026 press release says it is being “progressively expanded” towards that figure over three years [8][7]. A secondary summary of the May 2026 call says the CFO aims to migrate to the NSE main board by the end of FY2027, and we found no filing that shows an application [42]. Both stay unproven.

Definitions to hold in mind

Management reports a 22.1% ‘gross margin’ for FY2026 and ₹75 crore of ‘marketing investment’. The audited statements show a 9.9% gross margin on materials and purchases, and a marketing, advertising and promotion line of ₹20.2 crore [5][3]. This chapter uses only audited lines for margin and cash.

The evidence supports a clear story on origin: a commodity desk that lost money in bulk, shrank on purpose, built a brand to 71% of sales, and raised small IPO money that mostly funded the subsidiary. It does not yet prove that brand scale lifts the margin, that plant build-out can be funded from operating cash, or that management can keep to its dates.

2. What the Company Does: Segments, Revenue Mix & Concrete Examples

Core Investor Question: What does PROV actually sell, to whom, and how much of the money comes from each part?

Proventus reports a single business segment [1][3]. Every split in this chapter is therefore management’s own cut or our arithmetic on it, and we label each number as disclosed, derived or illustrative. Management cuts sales two ways: ProV brand against white-label and bulk, and core dry fruits against wholesome nutrition. Profit by segment is not disclosed anywhere. The only profit split that exists is by legal entity, and that is where this chapter ends.

White-label and bulk is the remainder, about ₹266 crore by our subtraction, and ₹177 crore (28%) in H1 FY27 by the company’s own figure [3][2]. Group sales are mostly one chain of goods, and the split depends on who the final buyer is.

White-label and bulk

This is the original business. The company sells bulk produce, processed and unprocessed, through the Navi Mumbai wholesale market and broker networks, and packs processed nuts for retail chains under their own labels. The website claims capacity to pack 1,00,000 packages a day for institutional buyers [10][38]. Revenue is about ₹266 crore in FY2026 by derivation and ₹177 crore in H1 FY27. Management gives three reasons for keeping it: market visibility, procurement scale and plant use [2]. No margin is disclosed, and no named customer.

The best evidence on its economics is the parent’s own result. The standalone company earned an EBITDA before other income of about 0.66% of its ₹535.3 crore revenue, roughly 66 paise on every ₹100 [3]. In our illustration, a mandi trader who buys a tonne of almonds from the parent pays import cost plus a spread of that order, and the price follows the commodity. The business offers volume and sourcing power and almost no margin. Management is candid that brand growth is the priority and bulk supports it [2].

Makhana and backward integration in Bihar

Makhana (fox nut) is the brand’s newest growth category. Bihar grows 80–90% of India’s supply, according to the government’s factsheet [51]. Prov Foods runs a unit at Billori in Purnia that the company says has 2,500 tonnes a year of capacity and began on 12 May 2026 [8]. The annual report calls Phase 1 operational [3]. A July 2026 press release says Bihar is being ‘progressively expanded’ towards 2,500 tonnes over three years, so we cannot tell whether the full figure is running today [7]. The company also claims the plant will add more than 300 bps of margin once running [5]. No revenue or margin for makhana is disclosed.

The annual report describes the route from pond to pack. Local farmers hand-harvest the seeds from ponds. They are sun-dried, roasted twice at up to 300°C, hand-popped, graded, seasoned and packed, then sold through the four channels [3]. The pack price is not disclosed. Annualising the 1,700-tonne half gives about 3,400 tonnes a year for the newer categories, so a full 2,500-tonne plant would equal roughly three-quarters of that. That group also includes figs, dates and seeds, so Bihar covers part of the supply, not all of it. This is our arithmetic, and the plant’s tonnes may be input weight.

The smaller subsidiaries

Three smaller entities complete the group, and none changes the revenue picture. Proventus Commodities DMCC in Dubai sells bulk dry fruit and showed a ₹0.18 crore loss in FY2026 on turnover of about ₹3 crore as printed [10][3]. Proventus Retail sells ProV products to selected customers but had no turnover in FY2026 [10][3]. Pro-Nova Bio, 51% owned, turns shells and skins into bio-fuel briquettes for boilers. It made ₹0.73 crore of revenue in FY2022 and paused when the plant moved, and shows a ₹1.75 lakh loss in FY2026 [10][3]. All three together are below 1% of group revenue.

3. How PROV Sources, Sells and Collects: Distribution & Unit Economics

Core Investor Question: How does a pouch of dry fruit get from an orchard to a shelf, and how much of each ₹100 of sales turns into cash?

Sourcing: from California packers to a Mumbai warehouse

PROV starts as an importer. In FY2026 the standalone parent, Proventus Agrocom Ltd (PAL), paid ₹281 crore for imports in foreign currency and earned no foreign exchange [3]. That equals about 34% of consolidated cost of goods (₹833 crore) and, by our arithmetic, 53% of PAL's own ₹530 crore of stock purchases. The rest comes from Indian mandis and traders; the prospectus put local procurement at 40–50% of needs [10].

Named FY2026 origins are the USA, West Africa, Australia, Afghanistan, Chile, the Middle East and India [5]. The USA supplied 33.5% of imports in FY2022 and 61.2% in the first nine months of FY2023 [10]. The prospectus claims direct ties with 30+ California packers and showed the top ten suppliers at 36.2% of purchases [10]. Later supplier concentration is not disclosed.

Two policy levers sit on top. In mid-2025 India charged 100% duty on US walnuts and ₹100 a kilogram on almond kernels, and importers delayed clearance hoping for cuts of roughly 50% [52][53]. Whether a cut took effect by November 2026 is not verified, and PROV does not disclose landed cost. From September 2025 GST on nuts and makhana snacks fell from 12% to 5%, and PROV says 98% of its brand portfolio sits in the 5% slab [35][51]. Our reading is that this mainly lifts demand and eases shelf prices, not margin.

Price swings are the bigger test. After Diwali 2024 dry-fruit raw-material prices rose about 25%, and the CEO said passing costs on takes about one month [9]. During that month the P&L absorbs the hit. FY2025 EBITDA of ₹12.92 crore missed the ₹22 crore an analyst said had been guided [9]. No hedging policy is disclosed, and there were no open commodity futures at 31 March 2026 [3]. So protection comes from repricing speed, and ₹59.45 crore of stock sat unhedged that day [3].

Packing: one plant at about three-quarters load, a second just opened

Mumbai is where almonds, cashews and mixes become pouches. The Khairane plant packs 1.5 lakh pouches a day on 47,000 sq ft with 300+ workers [3]. H1 FY27 sales averaged about 1.13 lakh pouches a day (2.07 crore pouches over 183 days), roughly 75% of that figure [2]. The utilisation is our inference, and co-packer volume is undisclosed.

Surat shows how plans slip. In September 2024 the subsidiary bought a 180,000+ sq ft site for about ₹12 crore and promised operations by year-end [25]. In May 2025 the CEO said Surat should run by the end of FY2026 [9]. The 13 November 2026 letter reports Phase 1 complete with ₹14 crore of capex, and Phases 2 and 3 plus cold storage due by June 2027 [2]. That is roughly a year and a half late. Target capacity moved from 8 lakh units a day to 4 lakh, then 4 lakh+ and now 5 lakh+ [25][19][2]. An ICICI term loan helps fund it [3].

In Bihar the subsidiary runs a makhana unit at Purnia, called 2,500 tonnes a year and commenced on 12 May 2026 [8]. A July 2026 release says Bihar is being expanded towards that level over three years [7]. The statements conflict, so we treat current output as unproven. The deck claims 300+ bps of margin gain after commissioning [5], with no volume or yield figure behind it.

The group's internal goods flow

PAL's own accounts show a trader that sells most output to its subsidiary. Of ₹535 crore standalone revenue in FY2026, ₹324.67 crore (61%) went to Prov Foods Pvt Ltd (PFPL), against 70% in FY2025 [3]. PFPL, 98.75% owned, packs the goods and sells them as ProV. It reported turnover of ₹721.3 crore and PAT of ₹10.19 crore, about 71% of group PAT [3]. Adding the two companies and removing ₹324.7 crore gives ₹931.9 crore, ₹7 crore above group revenue, so other internal flows exist that we cannot itemise [3].

PAL's external sales, our estimate ₹210.6 crore, are mostly bulk and white-label. PAL also lent PFPL ₹29.86 crore of IPO money in 2023, and ₹14.86 crore was outstanding in March 2026 [3][24]. Shareholders approved related-party dealings up to ₹400 crore a year in September 2025, and the 2026 AGM notice sought up to ₹677.5 crore [3][33]. PAL's standalone EBITDA margin of 0.66% before other income therefore says little: the brand, the pouches and most credit risk sit in PFPL.

Four routes to the shelf

PROV sells through general trade, modern trade, e-commerce and quick commerce, plus bulk and white-label sales and a small online store [2][40]. The FY2024 deck's pie charts, whose labels extracted jumbled, imply general trade fell from about 73% of brand sales in FY2022 to 32% in FY2024 as modern trade and e-commerce each reached about a third [17]. Management still calls general trade the backbone [9]. The FY2026 split is not disclosed, except quick commerce at 5–7% with a goal above 10% [42][3].

If Surat cold storage cuts inventory days, or H2 collections release more than in FY2026, intensity falls and growth funds itself faster. If large buyers keep stretching payment, intensity moves toward 15% and bank debt must keep rising with sales.

By September 2026 consolidated short-term borrowing was ₹46.73 crore and long-term ₹9.68 crore [1].

Profit first, cash later

PROV earns its rupee twice: once when goods are invoiced and again when retailers pay and stock turns. The first has been shown for four years, the second for one half-year cycle.

The evidence says PROV is a real distribution business with a thin margin and a heavy working-capital appetite. It does not yet show that growth can finance itself, that the 22% management margin survives trade spend, or that Bihar and Surat lower unit costs.

Three things survive the noise. Most sales are loose or unbranded. The branded slice is small. And growth forecasts range from 6.45% to 25–30% a year. Management gave per-capita consumption as 150 g in May 2025 and 600 g in its FY2026 deck, a fourfold shift in a year [9][5]. We would not underwrite either. The branded opportunity is real but unmeasured, and no current organised share exists in our sources.

Imports, commodity cycles and who sets the price

Dry fruit is a commodity before it is a brand. Materials and stock cost took 90.1% of PROV's FY2026 revenue [3]. At Farmley it was 71% of FY2025 revenue (₹281 crore of ₹394 crore) and at Happilo 73% of total spending [46][47]. Most almonds come from the USA, and India imported about 180,000 tonnes in marketing year 2024/25 [36][50]. Crop prices and duty are set outside the industry, so every branded player buys the same almond.

A price spike like the 25% after Diwali 2024 hits all of them. Our reasoning is that a loose seller reprices at the counter, while a brand must change printed pack prices and promotions, and PROV says that took about a month [9]. Brand therefore costs flexibility in a rising market. In a falling market the same lag may widen margin for a while, but no filing shows PROV earning that.

Our judgment is that PROV's moat is narrow at best. It is a scaled, low-cost distributor that is trying to become a brand. On a five-year view we put about 25% on a sustained EBITDA margin above 8%, about 50% on 3–6%, and about 25% on staying below 3%. The first requires the premium mix and Bihar to work together; the last is what peers have shown so far.

The number:

Our estimate is a through-cycle EBITDA margin of about 5% for a branded dry-fruit player like PROV, with a range of 2.5% to 7%.

How it was calculated:

EBITDA before other income equals gross margin less employee and other expenses. PROV's audited gross margin was 9.9% in FY2026 and 12.0% in H1 FY27, while those two cost lines took 7.9% and 9.4% of revenue. We assume gross margin can reach 12–15% through mix and scale, and overheads settle at 8–9.5%. A midpoint of 13.5% less 8.7% gives 4.8%, or about 5%. The ends are 12.0 − 9.4 = 2.6% and 15 − 8 = 7%.

The inputs:

PROV FY2026 and H1 FY27 audited and limited-review lines [3][2]. Peer margins: Happilo 0.89% and Farmley −3.68% in FY2025 [47][46]. Bikaji at 12.5–14.2% rests on a 34.7% gross margin that nut pouches do not have [59]. Management's claim of 300+ bps from Bihar [5], added to H1's 2.5%, gives 5.5%, inside our range.

The range:

2.5% to 7%. Peers sit at −3.7% to 0.9% and PROV at 1.8% to 2.5%; only an unverified small-company claim of about 13% [61] sits above our top end.

If the number is off:

If the brand earns a lasting premium over private label, margin could pass 10%. If platforms and private labels set prices, it stays near the peers' 0–2%, and growth adds revenue without adding much profit.

What other companies teach: three parallels

Parallels test mechanisms, not valuation. One shows how a commodity business becomes a brand, and two show how nut businesses fail. Each uses only figures from public documents.

Situational Scaling & Moat Twin: LT Foods Ltd (Daawat)

India, commodity basmati trading into a branded rice leader, FY2025 outcome

Shared Mechanism: LT Foods moved from commodity basmati trading and export into consumer brands led by Daawat. It used control of a staple crop's sourcing and processing, then added brand to climb away from trader margins. It is the closest structural twin: an agri-commodity trader turning into a brand business.

Historical Numbers & Outcome: In FY2025 LT Foods had sales of ₹86.8 billion and EBITDA of ₹9.8 billion, a margin of 11.3% in a broker estimate table. Fourth-quarter FY2025 gross margin was about 36% and branded volume grew 13% year on year.

Direct Implication for PROV: A branded staple can earn a double-digit EBITDA margin, but only after roughly two decades of brand building and market leadership. PROV's ProV brand is about nine years old, bulk and white label are 28% of H1 FY27 revenue, and its margin is 2.5%.

Historical Failure-Mode & Base-Rate Twin: Diamond Foods (Emerald nuts, Kettle chips) (USA, fiscal 2010–2011 walnut cost accounting, restated November 2012)

Shared Vulnerability: The SEC said the former CFO directed delay of recording payments to walnut growers into later periods and called commodity costs a lever to manage earnings. Special grower payments were treated as advances on crops not yet delivered. In a branded-nuts business, raw-material accounting is where earnings quality can fail.

Historical Numbers & Base Rate: Diamond restated its results in November 2012 and paid $5 million to settle with the SEC. Its former CEO paid a $125,000 penalty and returned or forfeited more than $4 million in bonuses and other benefits.

How PROV Compares Today: PROV carries ₹19.05 crore of supplier advances and ₹59.45 crore of inventory against ₹14.26 crore of annual profit. We found no sign of misstatement, and the FY2026 audit was unmodified, but these balances are where the audit trail matters most.

Historical Failure-Mode & Base-Rate Twin: Happilo and Farmley (India, branded dry fruit and snacks, FY2024–FY2025)

Shared Vulnerability: Two well-funded brand-led rivals raised venture money, spent heavily on advertising and grew quickly, but sold the same imported nuts as everyone else. Raw materials took roughly 71–76% of their revenue, which left little room to earn a brand premium.

Historical Numbers & Base Rate: Happilo's revenue fell from ₹329 crore to ₹280 crore (−15%) and its loss shrank from ₹136.6 crore to ₹9.5 crore only after ad spend fell 59% to ₹28.2 crore. Farmley grew 71% to ₹394 crore with a −3.68% EBITDA margin and ₹52 crore of ad spend.

How PROV Compares Today: Brand spend bought growth or awareness but not margin for the two peers we can see in detail. PROV differs by being profitable and by carrying a large trading book, yet it shares the same input costs and the same shelves.

The pattern is consistent. The successful twin took about two decades and a staple product with global reach, and the two failures show what an unproven premium looks like in this category. PROV sits earlier on that curve than the success and shares the input exposure of the failures. We read it as unproven rather than doomed, because its profits are positive and its growth is volume-led.

A narrow moat, not a wide one

The evidence supports a scaled distributor with sourcing reach and a brand still being built. It does not yet show repeat buying, a price premium, or a margin above what Happilo and Farmley earn.

The two things that could widen the moat are Bihar integration and a mix shift toward makhana and seeds. Both are claimed and neither is measured.

What is disclosed, what is not, and why the gaps matter

For a food brand, the KPIs that predict durability are repeat rate, distributor productivity and margin by channel. ProV discloses none of them. Repeat purchase is described only as what a good first experience “begins” [2]. Distributor numbers stopped at 45+ in the FY2024 deck. The company reports one segment, so brand and bulk profit cannot be separated. Channel mix after FY2024, customer concentration after nine months of FY2023, and plant utilisation are also missing [17][3][10].

These gaps sit exactly where bulls and bears disagree. If repeat rates are high, the 10–11% marketing spend is a fading entry fee. If they are low, it is permanent shelf rent, as the Farmley and Happilo accounts suggest for peers [46][47]. Without segment margin, we cannot tell whether the 71% brand share earns the claimed 22–23% or whether bulk trading earns almost nothing and carries the working capital.

Structural potential: capacity runway and the management guide

The runway is physical and financial. Mumbai packs about 1.5 lakh pouches a day; H1 FY27 sales averaged about 1.1 lakh, or roughly 75% of capacity [2][3]. Surat Phase 1 was declared complete on 13 Nov 2026 at ₹14 Cr of capex; Phases 2 and 3, with cold storage, are due by June 2027 [2]. The July release targeted over 4 lakh pouches a day by end-FY2027; the November letter says 5 lakh-plus, about 4.4 times today’s daily volume [7][2]. The Bihar makhana unit is called operational at 2,500 tpa in one release and “progressively expanded” toward that level over three years in another [8][7].

Management guides to FY2027 brand revenue of ₹850 Cr, FY2028 ₹1,100 Cr, gross margin of 24.5% and 27.0%, and marketing near 10–10.5% of brand revenue, or about ₹85–95 Cr, then 20–25% annual growth for three to five years [3][5][2][42]. The arithmetic is revealing. H1 FY27 brand revenue of ₹456 Cr already equals 54% of the FY2027 target. Hitting ₹850 Cr needs only about ₹394 Cr in H2, barely 5% above the roughly ₹377 Cr booked in H2 FY2026. Either the guide is deliberately low, or H1 included channel stocking ahead of the festive season, which the doubled receivables would support.

The number:

We put about 75% on brand revenue reaching ₹1,100 Cr in FY2028, 25% on a 24.5% brand gross margin in FY2027, 15% on 27.0% in FY2028, and 30% on Surat Phases 2 and 3 finishing by June 2027.

How it was calculated:

H1 FY26 brand revenue was about ₹282 Cr (₹47 Cr a month for six months), so H2 FY26 was about ₹377 Cr. H2 FY27 growth of 20%, 35% and 50% on that base, plus H1’s ₹456 Cr, gives FY2027 brand revenue of about ₹907 Cr, ₹963 Cr and ₹1,020 Cr. FY2028 growth of 15%, 25% and 30% then gives ₹1,043 Cr, ₹1,204 Cr and ₹1,326 Cr. At weights of 25%, 50% and 25%, only the bear case misses ₹1,100 Cr. For margin, the middle case needs H2 near 25.8% for a 24.5% year, after H1 gained only 90 bps against about 240 bps in each prior year. For Surat, the end-2024 promise slipped about 19 months and the end-FY2026 promise by three to seven.

The inputs:

In the bear case Surat’s capacity runs ahead of demand. If margin falls short there is little cushion, because employee and other expenses take about 9.5% of revenue. Brand figures are management definitions; audited consolidated revenue is the cross-check.

The guide is credible on volume and unproven on margin, so the record on earlier promises carries weight.

The scorecard we would give

Credit first. Brand revenue grew 37% in FY2025 and 58% in FY2026, against a 35–40% promise. Reported gross margin improved from 7.2% in FY2023 to 9.9% in FY2026 and 12.0% in H1 FY27, so the direction is real in audited numbers. IPO money went where the prospectus said. The management margin gain over FY2024–FY2026 was 490 bps against 650 bps planned, about 75% of the promised increment.

Penalties follow. Surat missed two dated commitments, the first by well over a year. FY2025 EBITDA missed the cited guide by 41%. Operating cash flow broke the no-burn promise in most periods. Four definitions do not reconcile: brand gross margin, the ₹75 Cr marketing figure against a ₹20.21 Cr P&L line, the 54% CAGR, and per-capita consumption quoted as 150 g in 2025 and 600 g in 2026 [3][9][5].

How we haircut the guide

Our working rule is to accept volume and revenue guidance at face value, add two to three quarters to any facility date, cut announced margin increments by about a quarter, and treat cash conversion as unproven until a full year of audited operating cash flow is positive. This is a judgment from three years of filings and one transcript. The FY2026 call has no published transcript and no H1 FY27 call was found, so the newest claims cannot be tested against spoken words.
