1. What the company does

Syngene was founded in 1993 and is based in Bengaluru. It sells scientists' time and factory capacity to drug companies. A customer that wants to find a new medicine, test it, or make it in quantity can hire Syngene instead of building its own laboratories and plants. The company says it serves about 400 active clients, including 16 of the top 20 pharmaceutical companies, with 8,373 staff of whom 5,778 are scientists as of March 2026.[3] Revenue from operations was Rs 3,739 Cr in the year to March 2026.[3] One crore (Cr) is ten million rupees. Management also gives guidance, which is its public forecast of revenue and margin for the year.

Customers pay in four main ways. In a dedicated research centre, a client rents ring-fenced space and a team for five years or more. In a full-time-equivalent contract, the client pays for a set number of scientists, renewed each year. In a fee-for-service contract, the client pays a fixed price for a defined piece of work. In a risk-and-reward contract, part of the payment depends on milestones.[3] All intellectual property belongs to the client, so Syngene earns service fees and no royalties on the medicines it helps to create.[3]

The business has two halves. Research services (discovery, safety testing, clinical and translational work) made up 65% of sales in the September 2026 quarter, and manufacturing for clients (the contract development and manufacturing side, called CDMO) made up 35%.[4] In the June quarter the split was 78% and 22%.[5] The manufacturing half has three sites for large-molecule biologic drugs: the original Bengaluru plant that made animal-health product for Zoetis,[6] Unit 3 in Bengaluru bought from Stelis, and the Bayview plant in Baltimore, United States, bought in 2025.[7] Together they give 50,000 litres of single-use bioreactor capacity.[3] A small-molecule plant in Mangalore makes active ingredients.[3]

Most costs do not move with revenue. Staff cost was Rs 280 Cr of the Rs 833 Cr revenue in the September quarter. Depreciation was Rs 112 Cr and finance cost about Rs 10 Cr.[3][7] That is why a 9% fall in revenue became a 26% fall in operating profit in the same quarter.[7] The operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of revenue, fell from 22% to 18%.[8] Syngene also bills in dollars and hedges the exchange rate with forward contracts. Those contracts cost Rs 61 Cr in the quarter.[4] Cash follows the calendar: clients pay large advances early in the calendar year, and the company spends them through the year, so cash on the balance sheet usually falls from March to September.[5]

Biocon, the biotechnology group founded by Kiran Mazumdar-Shaw, is the promoter and holds 52.32% directly. The promoter group holds 52.59% in total, and none of those shares is pledged.[1] Ms Mazumdar-Shaw returned as Executive Chairperson in April 2026, and Siddharth Mittal, who ran Biocon, became Managing Director and CEO on 1 July 2026.[9][10]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • CRDMO — contract research, development and manufacturing organisation: a company that does research (CRO) and development and manufacturing (CDMO) work for other drug makers.
  • Operating EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) — the profit from running the business before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, and before other income. Syngene's operating figure here already includes foreign-exchange gains and losses.
  • Hedge — a contract that fixes a future exchange rate. When the rupee weakens past the fixed rate, the hedge shows a loss even though the company's dollar bills are worth more rupees.
  • Full-time-equivalent (FTE) and fee-for-service (FFS) — two ways of charging for science. FTE bills for people for a period; FFS bills a price for a defined result.
  • Return on capital employed — operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. Higher means each rupee of capital earns more.
  • Reward-to-risk ratio — rupees gained if our central-case price is reached, divided by rupees lost if the stop is hit.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Syngene's strength is that few Indian companies can do what it does in one place. It offers discovery chemistry and biology, early clinical work, small-molecule manufacturing and large-molecule biologics manufacturing, with plants approved by the US, European, UK, Canadian and Japanese regulators.[3] Management counts 85 client and regulator audits in the last financial year and six successful US Food and Drug Administration audits in five years.[3] The Bristol Myers Squibb relationship, which began in 1998, was extended in January 2026 to run until 2035, and its research centre in Bengaluru holds about 700 Syngene scientists.[12] ICRA rated the company AA+ with a stable outlook in October 2025 and noted the parent's support.[11]

Its weakness is that revenue is lumpy and depends on a few customers. ICRA found that the top two customers gave about 41% of FY25 revenue and that about 61% came from the United States.[11] The loss of offtake of Librela, an animal-health product made for Zoetis, is the clearest example. Management says the impact of that one client was nearly US$50 million.[5] In the September 2026 quarter Zoetis revenue was zero, and the company still owes Zoetis batches that it expects to deliver in the fourth quarter.[6] The chairperson also admitted that the company had "drifted towards the larger share of commoditized research services" and had become "disproportionately dependent on a single large customer".[5]

Research services face price pressure. On the September call (cited from the preliminary AlphaStreet transcript) the CEO said that some services are becoming commodities and that large Chinese research companies and emerging Indian ones are competing on price.[6] He also said that one-third of drugs now in trials are in-licensed from China and that large Western drug makers are cutting their own research spending.[6] Syngene's response is to move from charging for people (FTE) to charging for results (FFS) and to add artificial intelligence and automation. The company says this will take time, and Q2 gave no number to test it.[6]

The company built capacity ahead of demand, and that capacity is now idle. Unfinished construction on the balance sheet rose from Rs 177 Cr in FY23 to Rs 1,141 Cr in September 2026.[2] Management said Mangalore had very low use in past years and that the Unit 3 biologics plant mostly holds clinical and development work, not large commercial volumes.[5] Idle plants earn nothing but still carry depreciation, which is why the return on capital employed fell from 15% in FY24 to 10% in FY26.[2]

Policy winds: the BIOSECURE Act and US tariffs

The BIOSECURE Act became law on 18 December 2025 as part of the US defence bill. It stops US federal agencies from buying from or contracting with entities that use services from "biotechnology companies of concern", and the law names no company. A list is due from the US budget office by December 2026.[13][14] Syngene's management argues that it should gain as customers move work out of China, and said so on both the July and October calls.[5][6] We treat this as a possible tailwind but give it no value in our numbers, because the list is not yet published and the company gave no order figure.

US tariffs look small for Syngene. A presidential order dated 2 April 2026 set a 100% tariff on patented medicines and their ingredients, with lower rates for companies that build plants in the United States, and generics were left out.[15] The United States later gave India and 19 other nations a zero rate on certain speciality drugs.[16] Management's view in April was that the effect on Syngene would be negligible.[9] A Baltimore plant also helps customers who want US-made supply, which the CEO named as part of the pitch.[6]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue grew from Rs 2,604 Cr in FY22 to Rs 3,739 Cr in FY26. That is 5% a year over three years and 11% over five years.[2] The growth came early: about 23% in FY23, 9% in FY24, 4% in FY25 and 3% in FY26. The last twelve months to September 2026 total Rs 3,522 Cr, which is below FY26.[2] Slowing sales and rising fixed costs explain most of what follows.

Profit fell faster than sales. The operating margin sat at 29-30% from FY22 to FY25, dropped to 25% in FY26 and was 21% over the last twelve months.[2] Net profit fell from Rs 510 Cr in FY24 to Rs 317 Cr in FY26 and Rs 186 Cr over the last twelve months, and earnings per share (EPS) fell from Rs 12.69 to Rs 7.86 and Rs 4.62.[2] FY26 profit includes charges for new labour-code rules and for staff exits; before those charges it was Rs 380 Cr.[3] The latest half-year is weaker still: H1 FY27 profit after charges was Rs 23 Cr against Rs 154 Cr a year earlier.[8]

Cash flow is the strongest part of the record. Over FY24 to FY26 the company took in Rs 3,125 Cr of cash from operations against Rs 1,323 Cr of profit, a ratio of 2.36.[2] Depreciation of about Rs 449 Cr a year, which takes no cash, and customer advances explain the gap.[2] Free cash flow (FCF), which is cash from operations minus spending on plants and equipment, was Rs 547 Cr in FY26 and Rs 398 Cr in FY25.[2] The first half of FY27 reverses this: operating cash flow was negative Rs 143 Cr against negative Rs 45 Cr a year earlier, because other current liabilities, which include customer advances, fell from Rs 781 Cr in March to Rs 467 Cr in September.[17][7] Management says advances are used up through the year, a yearly pattern.[5] It is a pattern that we will test at the March 2027 year-end.

The balance sheet is conservative. Borrowings including lease liabilities were Rs 507 Cr against equity of Rs 4,859 Cr in September 2026, a debt-to-equity ratio (borrowings divided by shareholders' equity) of 0.10, and net cash was Rs 1,392 Cr.[2][3] Net cash is lower than the Rs 1,541 Cr of June and the Rs 1,800 Cr of March.[5][21] The pressure point is the return on capital. Capital employed is about Rs 5,366 Cr (equity plus borrowings), and Rs 1,141 Cr of it is unfinished construction that earns nothing yet.[2] The return on capital employed was 10% in FY26 and, by our calculation, about 5.5% over the last twelve months (profit before tax Rs 252 Cr plus interest Rs 44 Cr, divided by Rs 5,366 Cr).[2]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks two questions. The first is static: does the company pass the red lines, and does it meet the fixed standards of an archetype? The second is dynamic: is the trend improving? The red lines are debt-to-equity of 1.20 or more, interest cover below 2, a promoter pledge of 25% or more, three-year cash conversion below 0.25, and a Stage 4 breakdown. A Stage 4 breakdown is a falling price below a falling 50-day average that itself sits below a falling 200-day average. There are three archetypes. A compounder needs a return on capital of 15% or more, low debt and strong cash conversion. A turnaround misses one of those bars but shows at least two improving signals and a cheap price. A tactical archetype is a Stage 2 leader, which is a stock in a rising trend, or a fresh move out of a Stage 1 floor. Each archetype also needs asymmetry, which is the ratio of the gain we expect to the loss we risk. The floor is 2.0 for a compounder and 2.5 for the other two. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, the evidence that would show it wrong and the date on which that evidence arrives. The next dated events are the Q3 FY27 results, which the company has not yet dated but which came on 22 January in the last fiscal year,[25] the US list of biotechnology companies of concern due by December 2026,[13] and the Q4 FY27 results with FY28 guidance, expected in late April 2027.

Estimate 1: FY27 earnings of Rs 5.86

The weakest assumption is the second-half margin of 29% before currency loss, together with a currency loss of Rs 120 Cr. If the Zoetis batches that management says it will deliver in the fourth quarter are larger than we assume, margin could be higher.[6] If cost cuts keep deepening, the staff cost line, which fell to Rs 280 Cr in Q2 from Rs 307 Cr a year ago, could fall further.[3] The evidence that would show this estimate too low is a Q3 FY27 reported operating margin above 24% on revenue of at least Rs 917 Cr. (This tests the estimate. The reopen and hinge tests in Chapter 7 use different thresholds, because they test the investment case.) The evidence arrives with the Q3 results and is confirmed with the Q4 results in April 2027.

Estimate 2: FY28 earnings of Rs 10.14

The weakest assumption is that research revenue stops shrinking while the currency loss falls to Rs 90 Cr. Management said, in the preliminary transcript, that the hedge contracts run to FY28 and that it will move to options, but it did not say how large FY28 losses will be.[6] Bayview revenue also depends on a customer product that is not yet approved, with first regulatory approval expected in the second half of calendar 2028 at best, so the plant will not lift FY28 reported profit.[6] The evidence that would show this estimate too high is research revenue below Rs 541 Cr in both the December and March quarters (the hinge-fails test in Chapter 7), or a currency loss above Rs 60 Cr in the March quarter. We will know in April 2027, when FY28 guidance is given.

Estimate 3: the base price of Rs 426 and the 42-times multiple

The weakest assumption is the multiple. The market paid a median of 55.1 times earnings over three years and as little as 38.5 times at its low.[24] A multiple is a judgment about growth and returns, and it can swing 10 turns in either direction without any change in earnings, which is Rs 100 a share on our FY28 estimate. We would be wrong on the upside if the return on capital recovers faster than we expect, and wrong on the downside if the market treats Syngene as an ordinary services company. Position size follows the risk-unit rule. Weight equals the smaller of the tier cap and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. The name is rejected, so it is TIER_0 and the weight is zero. Suppose it were reopened at the third conviction tier, which has a 50 basis-point risk budget and a 3.5% cap. The stop distance from Rs 355 would be 7.57%. The raw weight would be 6.6%, and the cap would hold it at 3.5%. A stop-out would then cost about 0.27% of the portfolio. The let-winners-run rule would apply after that. We would not trim only because the relative strength index passes 70. We would trail the stop at the rising 21-day average or 50-day average, with a ceiling of 22% of the portfolio.

Syngene is not held. The Zerodha transaction record contains no Syngene trades, so there is no holding to audit and no displacement question.

Layer 3 is the third part of our test. It sets the rules for where to place an order, how large the order may be and when to trim. It matters even for a rejected name, because it fixes the price at which we would reopen the case and the size we would allow. A basis point (bps) is one hundredth of a percentage point, so a 50 bps risk budget means we accept losing 0.5% of the portfolio if the stop is hit. A conviction tier is our grade of confidence. It sets the risk budget and the cap on position size. TIER_0 means no allocation, and TIER_3 means a small position with a 50 bps budget and a 3.5% cap.
