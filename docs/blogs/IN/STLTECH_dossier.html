Terms used in this report

EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is the profit from running the plants before financing and accounting charges. ROCE (return on capital employed) is yearly profit before interest and tax divided by the long-term money tied up in the business. EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares. Operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales.

Debt-to-equity compares borrowings with shareholders' money, and our rule asks for less than 0.70. Net debt is borrowings minus cash, so a negative figure means net cash. Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid bills. Cash flow from operations is cash the business makes before investing, and free cash flow is that cash less the money spent on plants. Dilution is the fall in each owner's share when new shares are issued. Guidance is the outlook management gives for the coming year.

ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing, which is Rs 48.45 for this stock. RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of recent buying, and a reading above 70 is stretched. Stage 2 is the markup phase of an uptrend, when price sits above a rising 50-day average and a 200-day average. A base is a flat price zone where a stock rests before a move. An entry zone is the price band where a first purchase would be allowed. A measured move is a chart target found by adding the height of the base to the high.

Asymmetry is the size of the possible gain divided by the size of the possible loss, which some call reward-to-risk. Other short forms: QIP is a share sale to large institutions; OFC is optical fibre cable; LTSA is a long-term supply agreement that lets the customer release orders over time; CFO is cash flow from operations; PAT is profit after tax; DMA is daily moving average.

Before you read

Sterlite Technologies (STL) makes the glass fibre, cable and plug-in parts that carry internet traffic, and it has just won large orders to wire AI data centres.[4] The business has clearly turned: sales were Rs 1,910 crore in the June 2026 quarter, up 87% in a year, and the company says it holds no net debt.[2][7] Our conclusion is still to reject the stock for now, because the price (Rs 1,015, about 12 times its 52-week low) already assumes the best case, the return and debt numbers fail our entry rules, and the reward-to-risk is negative on our base case.[1][18] Price data is current for 2026-10-09 and STLTECH is a watchlist name, not a holding. The conclusion would be wrong if the company delivers its Rs 20,000 crore sales target for 2029 and the stock keeps rising faster than profit[8].

What the company does

STL builds the physical layer of the internet. It makes its own glass preform (the glass rod that is drawn into fibre), draws it into optical fibre, wraps fibre into cables, and assembles connectors and pre-terminated cable kits that data centres and telecom operators plug together.[8] Management says it is one of few firms worldwide that does every step in-house, and that it holds about 9% of the global optical fibre market outside China.[8][6] It holds more than 785 patents and runs more than 10 plants.[4]

History in brief

The company was incorporated in 2000 in Pune and grew up inside the Anil Agarwal group, building India's fibre backbone over about 30 years.[8] It bought and built plants abroad, and it announced the separation of its global services business in 2024 through a demerger called Project Success, which took effect on 31 March 2025.[27][1] Sales fell from Rs 6,925 crore in FY23 to Rs 3,996 crore in FY25, partly because that business left and partly because telecom spending slowed, and the company lost money in FY24 and FY25.[1] A jury verdict in the United States in August 2024 added a legal overhang that is still open.[2]

Who pays, for what

Two paying groups matter. Telecom and citizen-network customers (operators, government broadband programmes) buy fibre and cable and made up 81% of FY26 revenue.[3] Data centre and enterprise customers (large cloud operators, called hyperscalers) buy high-density cable and pre-terminated kits and made up 19% of FY26 revenue. The data centre part alone jumped to 21% of June 2026 quarter revenue from 1% for FY26.[3][6] A small digital services arm (STL Digital) earned Rs 284 crore of revenue and Rs 3 crore of EBITDA in FY26, which is too small to move the story.[2]

Industry and competition

Optical fibre demand used to follow telecom spending: broadband, 4G, 5G and government programmes such as BharatNet in India and BEAD in the United States.[8] Management now argues that AI data centres add a second engine. This is because a rack of AI chips needs far more fibre (about 1,000 fibres per old rack against roughly 64,000 for the newest design).[8] That claim comes from the company's own event and is not independently checked, so we treat it as a direction, not a number to build on.

Competition is intense. Global cable makers such as Prysmian, Corning, Fujikura and others sell in the same data centre and telecom markets, and in India the listed peers are HFCL, Tejas Networks, Finolex Cables and Polycab, each with a different mix.[14][15][16][17] Crisil lists large working-capital needs, competition and the cyclical nature of telecom spending as the main weaknesses, and a lowest-cost position in fibre as a strength.[11] About 54% of June-quarter sales came from North America. This means US trade policy matters: the tariff on STL's exports fell to 10% in February 2026 and helped margins, and a rise would hurt.[6][11]

The orders

Order intake doubled to Rs 7,687 crore in FY26. The open order book reached a record Rs 18,618 crore at 30 June 2026. This is 3.3 times the last twelve months of sales (Rs 5,642 crore).[3][4][1] Of that, only Rs 2,228 crore is scheduled for the September quarter and Rs 16,390 crore for later, so most of the book is long-dated.[6] On 1 October 2026 a subsidiary won a long-term supply agreement worth about USD 1.2 billion through December 2030 from an unnamed hyperscale customer.[9] The filing says purchase orders are released over time and that both sides carry capped liability if volumes or supply fall short, so the headline value is a ceiling on potential sales, not a booked order.[9] The watchlist note calls the order about 60 to 70% of backlog. We cannot tie that to a filing. This is because the Rs 18,618 crore book is stated at 30 June and the filing does not say how much of the agreement is already in it, so that ratio is N/A.[9][6]

Management and capital allocation

Management guided EBITDA margin for FY27 to 20% earlier in the year and raised it to 23% after the June quarter.[6] On 3 September 2026 it set a 2029 ambition of Rs 20,000 crore revenue and a 27%-plus EBITDA margin, with about Rs 1,000 crore of capital spending a year for three years to lift preform, fibre and cable capacity by 50%.[8] Crisil assumes capital spending of Rs 700 to 800 crore in FY27 and flags debt-funded capital spending as a watch item.[11] On capital raising, the Rs 1,500 crore QIP at Rs 583.01 a share added 25.73 million shares, about 5.3% more shares, and cut promoter holding to 42.29%.[2] The price is now 74% above the QIP price, which tells us institutions who bought in the QIP are sitting on large gains and may sell.[1] The FY26 annual report cycle also records that Rs 11 crore of managerial pay needs shareholder approval at the next meeting, a small governance note.[2]

Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in the last two chapters can fail, so this chapter argues against our own conclusion. It lists the strongest reasons the stock could be worth less, or more, than we think, names the single claim everything depends on, and says what evidence would change our mind.

Red-team: the fatal-flaw case

A fatal flaw is a problem large enough to break the investment on its own, not just trim the return. We looked for seven, ranked roughly by how much damage each could do, and for each we say what evidence would show we are wrong.

  • Valuation already counts the plan. At 221 times trailing earnings, 21.8 times book value and about 9.2 times trailing sales, the stock has no cushion if the quarter slips.[1] Evidence it is wrong: two more quarters at Rs 190 crore or more of profit, which would put profit at about Rs 780 crore a year and the multiple near 67. We will know on 23 October 2026 and in January 2027.[24]
  • One-off catch-up in the June quarter. Management says the orders expected for Q1 were delivered with a surge in intake, and the executable book for the September quarter is only Rs 2,228 crore, which is a little above the Q4 FY26 sales level.[6] If September sales fall back below Rs 1,500 crore, the 87% growth would look like timing.
  • Customer concentration and one big agreement. The new USD 1.2 billion agreement is with one unnamed hyperscale customer, and it is a ceiling on potential value with capped liabilities for both sides.[9] We cannot confirm the customer or the exact share of the book, so concentration risk is N/A in size but real in direction.
  • The United States legal matter. A jury found STL's US subsidiary liable to Prysmian. The court confirmed a total of USD 101.25 million in August 2025, and STL has appealed and posted a USD 41.53 million bond.[2] The company says the final effect cannot be assessed, and Crisil lists it as a watch item.[11] At an assumed rate of about Rs 88 to the dollar, a loss of the full amount would be about Rs 890 crore, roughly 16 times the Rs 56 crore FY26 profit, though only 1.7% of today's market value.[1] The exchange rate is our assumption, not a sourced figure.
  • Tariff and supply-chain shocks. About 54% of June-quarter sales were North American, and Crisil names a rise in tariffs as a key risk.[11][6]
  • Dilution and share supply. The QIP added about 5.3% to the share count at Rs 583.01, and the buyers hold a gain of about 74%.[2]
  • Capital intensity. The plan to spend about Rs 1,000 crore a year is large next to FY26 operating cash flow of Rs 520 crore. It is covered by the QIP cash for now.[8][1]

Taken together, the first two items matter most because they sit in the price today, while the legal matter and the tariff risk are outside the company's control. None of the seven alone forces a sell decision, but several arriving together would remove the cushion the current valuation lacks.

The single claim the thesis hangs on is that data-centre fibre demand will stay at today's level of intensity and pricing for several years. If hyperscaler spending slows, the 20% margin has no anchor. Evidence that would show it wrong: a cut in cloud capital-spending plans, or a margin below 17% in the September quarter.[6] Rating agencies have already moved their view the other way, with Crisil upgrading to AA in September 2026, which is a point for the bulls.[11]

Trigger: we would re-open the case if the price pulls back to Rs 806 to 816 (1.0 to 1.2 ATR above the 50-day average) on falling volume. The trailing return on capital has risen above 15% after the September and December quarters. Either alone is not enough.

Execution plan

First, do nothing before the 23 October 2026 result. Second, after the result, check September-quarter sales (we want above Rs 1,700 crore), margin (above 18%), and the order book. Third, re-read the gate in the March 2027 numbers, when four quarters of the new margin level will be in the trailing figures. Fourth, if the price pulls into the entry zone before then and the gate has not changed, the verdict stays REJECTED. Shortlist action: leave STLTECH off the shortlist and leave the watchlist row for the orchestrator to update.

Trigger to exit the idea altogether: a close below Rs 733.70, a September-quarter margin below 15%, or an adverse final ruling in the Prysmian appeal.
