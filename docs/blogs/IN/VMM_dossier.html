Vishal Mega Mart (VMM) runs 844 low-price department stores, mostly in smaller Indian towns, selling clothes, household goods and packaged food.[3] The business passes our quality checks: sales grew 19% a year for three years, it has no bank debt, and it earns twice its profit in cash before rent, and 1.1 to 1.2 times after rent.[22][1] The verdict is REJECTED for now: the ₹88.70 price (the 26 October bar, one session behind the run date) is in a downtrend, and the turnaround test fails on two of four checks, though support is holding. We could be wrong because the shares trade at 46.2 times trailing profit, against about 66 and 83 times for two larger rivals[18][20].

1. What the company does

Vishal Mega Mart is a chain of 844 shops in 582 cities across 28 states and two union territories.[3][2] Together the shops cover about 1.41 crore square feet (a crore is ten million).[3] The customers are middle-income and lower-middle-income families. Of the 844 shops, 443 are in towns of under 200,000 people, 197 are in mid-sized cities and 204 are in the largest cities.[3][6] Picture one big shop where a family buys a school shirt, a pressure cooker and a month of cooking oil in one visit, at prices close to the cheapest in town.

The company sells three kinds of goods. In the half-year to September 2026, clothing brought in 44.1% of sales, household and general goods 28.4%, and packaged food and daily-use items 27.3%.[2] About 73.9% of sales carry one of its own brands, made for it by outside factories.[2] That matters because a shop that owns its brands sets the price and keeps the profit a brand-name maker would take. Management says 18% to 20% of revenue comes from the cheapest item in each category. It has not raised those prices in the current inflation spell.[5] Sales rise when more people visit, when each customer buys more, and when new shops open.

Here is how the money works. In the quarter to September 2026 the company took in ₹3,398.5 crore. After paying suppliers it kept a gross margin — the share of sales left after paying for the goods — of 28.0%.[2] Out of that it pays staff (₹223.0 crore) and other running costs (₹315.1 crore), which leaves ₹412.0 crore.[3] That is the company's reported EBITDA, which stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It measures what the shops earn before finance and accounting charges. An accounting rule called Ind AS 116 keeps rent out of reported EBITDA. So the company also publishes an ‘operating EBITDA’ that deducts rent (₹168.7 crore in the quarter). Taking rent off leaves ₹243.3 crore, and adding back an ₹18.9 crore share-option charge that involves no cash gives the ₹262.2 crore the company reports, or 7.7% of sales.[2][3] We use operating EBITDA to judge shop economics, because a business that leases every shop pays rent in real cash.

Two newer pieces sit beside the shops. The first is quick commerce, where a customer orders on a phone and a nearby shop delivers within minutes. It runs from 781 shops in 532 cities and has over 1.5 crore registered users.[2] Brokers say it adds between 1.5% and 9% to a shop's sales, depending on how mature the shop is.[45] The second is a pilot format called Belong & Co., aimed at younger, better-off city shoppers. Its first store opened in the quarter.[4] About 18.1 crore people are enrolled in the loyalty programme, and members account for roughly 95% of sales. Most revenue therefore comes from repeat customers.[2][7] Samayat Services LLP owns 40.02% of the company. It is a vehicle of the private-equity firms Kedaara Capital and Partners Group, which sold shares at the December 2024 listing and has kept selling since.[13][23]

Seasonality shapes every quarter. Retail in India peaks around Diwali and the wedding season, and festival dates move. This year Durga Puja, Dussehra and Navratri fell in October, not September. Sales that landed in the quarter to September 2025 therefore land in the quarter to December 2026.[4] The company measures SSSG, which stands for same-store sales growth: the growth in sales at shops open for at least 15 months. Reported SSSG for the September quarter was 5.4%. Management says the festival shift cost 3 to 4 points, which puts it near 9%.[2][4] The first half of the year is therefore a cleaner test than any single quarter.

What each customer visit is worth

Management splits growth at existing shops into three parts. New customers walk in. Existing customers buy one or two more items. The average price paid rises as shoppers trade up from the cheapest goods to mid-priced ones.[29] In the June 2026 quarter, for example, the company said customer visits rose about 8% and existing customers bought about 3% more, against reported same-store growth of 10.0%. The parts are rounded and compound to about 11%, so read them as an illustration of the mix, not exact arithmetic.[32][33] The mix is healthy because most growth comes from more people, not only higher prices. That matters for a value retailer. Growth that comes from price rises alone can drive away the poorest shoppers, while growth that comes from new and repeat customers builds a larger and steadier group of regulars. It also explains why management protects the cheapest price points even when costs rise.[5] The split is not published every quarter, so we treat the June figure as an example, not a trend. 2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest part of VMM is how little it costs to run. Brokers put its monthly cost of retailing, rent included, near ₹150 per square foot. That is about a fifth below its closest rival. They also say new shops typically pay back in under two years.[25] Low costs allow low prices on the items that bring families in. The company owns the cheapest price point in most categories and sources about 74% of sales under its own brands. Two of those brands each sell over ₹1,000 crore a year and six more sell over ₹500 crore.[2][22][27] It also leases every shop and warehouse, so it spends little on land. A retailer that is cheap and asset-light can grow without borrowing. The numbers agree: no outside loans, 18.1 crore loyalty members and repeat customers behind about 95% of sales.[22][2][7]

The latest quarter shows the company holding up in a hard market, though not outpacing rivals. Its adjusted same-store growth of about 9% compares with 9.5% for the grocery chain DMart and 11% adjusted for the clothing chain V-Mart, so it is in line with peers, and its total sales growth of 14% is slower than theirs. The festival shift hit all three the same way. Other listed retailers also reported high double-digit sales growth for the quarter.[2][16][21][40][41] Management says its cheapest price points have not been raised. Price rises landed mostly on costlier items bought by better-off customers, so low-income shoppers have not been pushed away.[5] Brokers describe the economics the same way: private labels, loyal repeat customers and a lean cost structure that rivals find hard to copy.[9][7]

The weaknesses are on the cost side and in the speed of growth. Same-store sales growth has slowed every year. It was 25.2% in the year to March 2023, then 13.6%, 12.3% and 11.0% in the three years since. It was 7.7% reported in the half-year to September 2026, or about 9.5% adjusted.[22][2] That half-year was 10.0% in the June quarter and 5.4% in the September quarter.[33] The comparison is also hard. The September quarter of 2025 was lifted by an early festival season, with same-store growth of 11.3% reported and 12.8% adjusted.[34] With 21,913 permanent employees, wages are the largest running cost. Staff cost rose 26.6% in the September quarter against sales growth of 14%. The causes were higher minimum wages, new shops and share-option charges. Management says same-store growth of 8% to 10% is needed just to absorb it.[3][5][44] That is why operating EBITDA grew only 3.7% in a quarter when sales grew 14%.[2]

Competition is intense and rising. Trent's Zudio value-fashion format crossed 1,000 stores in the quarter. DMart has 518 stores and added 15. V-Mart has 613. Quick-commerce platforms and online marketplaces chase the same households. Trent's 23% sales growth was its fastest in six quarters.[19][16][21][42] In January 2026 the chief executive said competition had been intense for two to three years, from national chains, regional players and online sellers. His answer is to focus on full-year results and shop-level profit.[31] The competitive position is strong but not unchallenged. The company has a real cost and brand advantage today. The open question is whether it can keep that advantage while wages and rents keep rising.

The market VMM sells into

The company's listing prospectus, drawing on a Redseer study, sized the market for aspirational retail at ₹68 to 72 trillion in 2023. That is goods for middle-income families who want quality at a fair price. The study expected about 9% yearly growth to ₹104 to 112 trillion by 2028.[23] A broker's reading of the same industry data is that organised retail is about 20% of all retail and should reach 30% to 32% by 2028. Towns outside the biggest cities account for about 74% of retail spending and are still around 90% unorganised.[24] Redseer notes that value-first retailers win these shoppers with the lowest opening prices, a broad choice of goods and a better experience than the local market stall.[39] The opportunity is large and slow-moving. It also explains why several chains are racing to open shops in the same towns.

Ownership, management and balance sheet quality

Promoters hold 40.02% of the shares, all through Samayat Services LLP. None of those shares is pledged as security for a loan.[13][14][15] The holding was 74.6% at March 2025, 54.1% at December 2025 and 40.1% at March 2026. The owner sold about 19.6% of the company in June 2025 and about 14% in February 2026, at ₹117 a share.[14][35][43] Mutual funds now hold 31.75% and foreign investors 18.75%, so the shares have moved into institutional hands.[13] Motilal Oswal calls a further stake sale by the private-equity owner the main overhang on the stock. The 150-day lock-up that came with the February sale would have run out in late July 2026, 150 days after 27 February (our arithmetic).[9][11][36]

The chief executive, Gunender Kapur, was reappointed by the board on 21 August 2026. The new term runs five years to August 2031, and he is now called Founder, Managing Director and Chief Executive. That addresses the worry that he is about 66 years old.[37][38][9] The reappointment needed a shareholder vote at the 18 September 2026 annual meeting. We did not find the result in the sources we could read, so we show that approval as N/A.[22] India Ratings upgraded the company to IND AA+ with a Stable outlook on 1 September 2026, a sign that lenders see little credit risk.[8] One feature of the balance sheet needs saying plainly. Goodwill of ₹4,283.6 crore, left over from the private-equity purchase, is 37.41% of total assets. Reported return on capital therefore looks lower than the economics of individual shops.[22]

What this means for an owner

For an owner, the business is better than the share price suggests, and the share price is worse than the business suggests. The company has a durable cost advantage, a clean balance sheet and a good name with lenders. But same-store growth is slowing, costs are rising faster than sales this year, and a large seller still owns 40% of the shares. The practical meaning is that a buyer is not paying for a fragile business. A buyer is paying for a good business at a moment when its short-term numbers are worse than its long-term record, and when one holder has the power to move the price. That is why the rest of the report separates three questions: is the business good, is the price fair, and is the timing right? 3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 reads the accounts the way a lender would, and then the way an owner would. All figures are consolidated, in rupees crore, for years ending in March. They come from the Screener tables, which reproduce the company's filings, and we cross-checked them against the FY26 annual report.[1][22] Two accounting points help. First, the line called borrowings (₹1,988 crore at March 2026) is mostly lease liabilities. The annual report says the company has no external borrowings and that its debt is mainly lease liabilities.[22][1] Second, the operating profit line (₹1,918 crore, or 15% of sales, for FY26) is struck before rent under Ind AS 116. That is why it is higher than the 10.2% margin the company quotes after rent.[22][1]

Growth has been fast and profitable. Sales rose from ₹7,586 crore in FY23 to ₹12,906 crore in FY26. That is 19.4% a year, worked out as (12,906 / 7,586) to the power of one-third, minus one. It matches the 19.38% in the annual report.[1][22] Net profit rose from ₹321 crore to ₹839 crore over the same three years. That is 37.7% a year, which matches the 37.72% in the annual report. The Screener table rounds the same figure to 39%.[1][22] Profit grew about twice as fast as sales. Depreciation and interest grew more slowly than operating profit, and the tax rate stayed near 25%.[1]

Cash tells a more careful story. Cash from operations was ₹1,621 crore in FY26 against profit of ₹839 crore. Over FY24 to FY26 it was ₹3,850 crore against ₹1,933 crore of profit, a ratio of 1.99.[1] That ratio flatters the company. Under Ind AS 116 the rent is booked as a financing payment, below the cash-from-operations line. Deduct an annualised rent of about ₹669 crore, which is twice the ₹334.3 crore paid in the half-year to September 2026. The ratio then falls to about 1.1 to 1.2.[3] That still clears our 0.70 hurdle, and the cash is real. But the surplus left after paying landlords is much smaller than the headline suggests.

The balance sheet is simple. Net worth was ₹7,413 crore at March 2026 and ₹7,896 crore at September 2026. Lease-based borrowings were ₹2,123 crore. Investments, which are surplus cash parked in funds, grew from ₹1,119 crore to ₹1,888 crore in six months.[1] The company has said the board is looking at how to use that cash.[4] Goodwill of ₹4,283.6 crore, left from the private-equity purchase, is 37.41% of total assets. Any return on capital quoted on the whole balance sheet therefore understates the economics of a single shop.[22]

Flaw 1: Slower same-store growth may be permanent, not seasonal

Management blames the festival shift for the weak September quarter. The arithmetic supports part of that. Adjusted same-store growth was about 9% against a reported 5.4%.[2] But the longer record is a steady slowdown. Growth was 25.2% in the year to March 2023, then 13.6%, 12.3% and 11.0%. It was about 9.5% adjusted in the first half of this year.[22][2] If underlying growth settles at 8% to 9%, the aim of double-digit growth for the year fails. A multiple of 38 would then be too high for a retailer growing profit at 14% rather than 26%. The evidence that would prove this flaw is a December-quarter result below 10%, which we would see in late January 2027. The consequence for an owner would be a lower profit estimate and a lower multiple at the same time, which is the way a stock can fall by a quarter or more.

Flaw 2: Wages and rents may keep rising faster than sales

Staff cost rose 26.6% in the September quarter and other costs 15.7%. Sales grew 14.0%.[3][2] Management expects store-level cost inflation of 6% to 7% a year. It says same-store growth of 8% to 10% is enough to absorb that. This leaves little room if growth slips below 8%.[4][5] Minimum-wage increases are set by state governments and can arrive without warning. The company has said wage inflation of 5% to 7% a year is typical, and more when states revise the minimum. Rents rise about 5% a year under the lease contracts, although rent grew more slowly than shop area in the half-year because most new shops are in smaller towns.[4] If sales growth falls while these costs keep rising, the margin after rent falls for a second year, and profit estimates would be cut again.

Flaw 3: A large seller still owns 40% of the shares

Samayat Services holds 40.02%. It has sold three times since listing. The last sale was at ₹117 in February 2026, which is 32% above today's price.[13][43] Motilal Oswal names the stake sale as the main overhang. The 150-day lock-up on further sales would have expired by late July 2026.[9][36] Another block sale at a discount would likely push the price lower for a time, whatever the business reports. The opposite risk is that the owner waits for a higher price. That keeps the overhang in place and limits any recovery. Because the last sale was at ₹117 and the stock is far below that, the owner may be reluctant to sell now. Reluctance reduces the near-term risk of a block, but it does not remove the overhang, because every rally will tempt a sale.

Flaw 4: Our own valuation work may be too kind

Our central multiple of 38 comes from one broker's DCF. Brokers cut their targets five times in a week, so it is a moving target.[10][11] The ledger's reverse DCF of 13.1% a year looks undemanding. Our rent-adjusted version is 23.4% a year, which is a lot to ask for ten years.[3] We use consensus profit of ₹2.64 for FY28 even though estimates are falling. If the cuts continue, the central value falls with them. The central upside is also fragile to the multiple. At 34 instead of 38 the central value is ₹89.76, only 1.2% above the close, and at 38 on the bear profit of ₹2.35 it is ₹89.30, 0.7% above. Each cut of 5% in profit per share takes about ₹5 off the central value at a multiple of 38. We have no way to verify the multiple independently, because VMM has no three-year median to compare against, and so the central value should be read as a range.
