1. What the company does

Waaree Renewable Technologies Limited (WRTL) is an engineering contractor. A customer, usually a company that wants to own and run a solar farm, hires WRTL to design the plant, buy the panels and equipment, build it and hand it over working. The industry calls this work engineering, procurement and construction, or EPC. WRTL does not need to own the plant, so it needs little of its own money for a typical project. It earns a fee built into a fixed price, and the company has said all its contracts are fixed price[11]. The share of revenue it keeps is the operating margin. We measure it with EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) divided by revenue, and it was 19.24% in FY26[10]. WRTL was incorporated in 1999 and is listed on the NSE and BSE; Waaree Energies Limited, India's largest solar module maker, holds 74.30% and is the only promoter entity[17][29].

The company now has four activities. The largest is solar and battery EPC. In FY26 it built 2,727 MWp of plants (a MWp, or megawatt peak, is the most power a plant can produce in full sun), and the annual report says it earned Rs 3,331.42 crore of revenue[3][10]. It also earns recurring income from O&M (operation and maintenance contracts) on about 1.5 GWp of plants and owns 82.82 MWp of its own plants, which makes it an independent power producer, or IPP[3]. In October 2026 it won a Letter of Award for another 450 MWp project, to be finished in FY2027-28[25].

The fourth activity is new. On 18 June 2026 WRTL completed the purchase of 55% of Associated Power Structures Private Limited (APSPL) for Rs 1,225 crore[15][13]. APSPL, founded in 1996, makes galvanised steel transmission towers and builds high-voltage lines and substations; its current factories make 108,000 tonnes a year and another 100,000 tonnes of capacity is under construction[3]. This is transmission and distribution, or T&D, work. The CFO told analysts that T&D margins are around 9% to 10%, well below solar EPC[4], which is why the group margin has dropped.

A short way to picture the money flow: the customer pays in stages as the plant is built, WRTL pays its suppliers, and the gap between what it has billed and what it has collected sits on the balance sheet as receivables. That money, plus stock and minus supplier credit, is called working capital, and it is the main cash drain on a fast-growing contractor. WRTL buys many of its modules from its parent; related-party purchases were 28.56% of all purchases in FY26, up from 4.74% a year earlier[21]. Land and grid connection are the customer's job, so a project can stall for reasons WRTL does not control, as the CFO said when he explained the weak September quarter[4].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPC: engineering, procurement and construction, the contractor model described above.
  • Order book: the value of signed work that has not yet been billed. It tells you how many months of work the company has lined up.
  • EBITDA margin: operating profit before interest, tax and depreciation as a share of revenue. It shows how much of each rupee of sales is left after the cost of doing the work.
  • Working capital: money tied up in receivables and stock minus money owed to suppliers. In contracting it often absorbs cash when a company grows fast.
  • Stage 4: the downtrend phase of a share price, used in this report as a hard rule.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A contractor's advantage is rarely a patent. It is a record of finishing large jobs on time, access to cheap equipment, and customers who trust it with their money. On those points WRTL has real strengths. It scaled from 704 MWp executed in FY24 to 1,524 MWp in FY25 and 2,727 MWp in FY26[3]. CARE Ratings upgraded it to A+ with a stable outlook in February 2026 and noted that it sources modules from its parent, which cushions module price swings[18][19]. Its customers include large power producers, and the 450 MWp award of 27 October 2026 came from what the filing calls a leading IPP[25].

The weaknesses are just as concrete. CARE calls the segment intensely competitive and fragmented and says profit is exposed to input prices and to delays in land and clearances[18]. Concentration is high: two customers supplied Rs 2,234.94 crore, or 67.1%, of FY26 revenue of Rs 3,331.42 crore, and two customers supplied 72.2% a year earlier[21]. When a single buyer slows down, as happened with the land hand-over delay the CFO described for Q2[4], a quarter can lose half its volume. Solar execution fell to 456 MWp from 889 MWp in the previous quarter, a drop of 48.7%[4].

The market itself is not the problem. India added 20,790.76 MW of solar in April to September 2026 and now has 171.05 GW installed[23], and the government has approved the Green Energy Corridor Phase III to carry up to 135 GW of renewable power[1][3]. Demand for plants and for the lines that carry their power is real. The question is whether WRTL can turn that demand into steady profit and cash at an acceptable price, and the first half of FY27 gives a mixed answer.

The acquisition adds capacity but also dilutes quality. The company says APSPL gives it a new addressable market and in-house manufacturing[3]. The cost was Rs 1,225 crore for 55% of a business that had a net worth of Rs 339.53 crore and turnover of Rs 1,226.64 crore in FY25[14]. Most of the price is not for tangible assets: goodwill of Rs 464.02 crore and intangible assets of Rs 598.85 crore now sit on the balance sheet, together Rs 1,062.87 crore[2]. That is 95% of the Rs 1,118.43 crore of equity that belongs to WRTL's own shareholders (equity capital of Rs 20.88 crore plus other equity of Rs 1,097.55 crore)[2]. If APSPL underperforms, the write-down risk falls on the owners' equity, not on the minority partners.

Where the edge is thin

The parent relationship works in two directions. Waaree Energies gives WRTL access to modules, a credit story and a customer network, and CARE counts it as a rating strength[18]. But 28.56% of FY26 purchases came from related parties[21], which means WRTL's cost and delivery depend on one affiliate. A minority shareholder cannot judge the arm's-length quality of those transfers from the outside.

What this means for an owner: the franchise is real but the moat is operational, not structural. Competitors can bid below WRTL, margins have already fallen 527 basis points year on year in Q2[1], and the new T&D business has a different customer set and lower margins. The next chapter tests whether company-level operating measures support the strength the headline growth suggests.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 asks five questions that belong to this business, not to any company. For a contractor with an order book, the first is how much signed work is waiting. The second is whether the cash comes in as fast as the profit is booked. The third is whether the operating margin holds. The fourth is how many customers the revenue depends on. The fifth is whether the return on money invested survives the Rs 1,225 crore acquisition. Each KPI is scored PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). The company qualifies at a weighted score of 3.0 out of 5.0 with at most one FAIL.

WRTL scores 1.5 out of 5.0 with 0 PASS, 3 WATCH and 2 FAIL, so Layer 2 fails on both counts. The two FAILs are cash conversion with receivable days, and customer concentration. We chose these five KPIs because they decide whether profit turns into money you can spend and whether one lost customer can change a quarter; the scores then follow from the filings. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record is better on operations than on the balance sheet. On margins, the Q1 FY26 call guided a sustainable range of 14% to 16% and FY26 came in at 19.2%, so management under-promised and over-delivered[9]. On orders, the April 2026 call put the unexecuted book at 2.83 GWp to be executed in 12 to 15 months[11][10], and the book has since grown, with Rs 5,300 crore at June and more than Rs 5,500 crore at September including T&D[7][1].

The record is weaker where promises touched funding and timing. The January 2026 board note planned to complete the APSPL purchase by 30 April 2026[14]; the May filing moved the date to about 15 June[13], and it closed on 18 June[15][2]. The FY26 annual report showed debt-to-equity, which is borrowings divided by shareholders' equity, of 0.09 and interest cover of 47.60 times[21]. Six months later, at 30 September 2026, borrowings were Rs 1,412.99 crore[2]. Management also said in July it would 'try to maintain around 15%' for FY27[8]. That is a lower bar than the 19% delivered in FY26, and it was met in Q2 by only 12 basis points.

3. The numbers, in plain words

The five-year record is outstanding and now out of date. Revenue rose from Rs 162 crore in FY22 to Rs 3,331 crore in FY26 and profit after tax from Rs 9 crore to Rs 479 crore[29]. Return on equity averaged 70% over three years[29]. These are the numbers behind the Rs 677 share price that was Rs 1,358 at its 52-week high (session ledger). The seed note that put WRTL on this watchlist quoted ROCE (return on capital employed) of 83.2% and debt-to-equity of 0.16 (unverified): the first is Screener's FY26 figure[29] and the company's own annual report shows 62.54%[21]; the second describes a balance sheet that no longer exists.

5. What could happen next

Chapter 5 asks what growth today's price already assumes, how analysts are changing their estimates, and what the price could do in three scenarios. One input needs a word first: free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after capital spending, and the ledger's FCF yield for WRTL is negative. Screener puts the market capitalisation at Rs 7,067 crore[29]. Trailing earnings per share are Rs 44.64, which is about Rs 466 crore of profit attributable to owners (44.64 x 10.4379 crore shares), so the price is 15.2 times trailing earnings (677.30 / 44.64). On total trailing profit of Rs 476 crore, which includes the minority share, the ratio is 14.9 times. The ledger uses Rs 7,069.60 crore for its reverse DCF, a 0.04% difference. That looks modest for a company that grew profit 190% a year over five years, but the five-year history is a poor guide to the next two years because the cost of money and the business mix have changed.

Our variant perception

Our view differs from the cheap-on-earnings reading in three ways. First, 15.2 times trailing earnings is calculated on the strongest year the company has had. On our FY27 estimate of Rs 35.5 the price is 19.1 times earnings, and on FY28 it is 16.0 times (677.30 / 35.5 and 677.30 / 42.4). That is not cheap against peers at 14.4 and 14.7 times[32][33].

Second, the reverse DCF changes with the cash base. Run with the same 12% discount rate and 20 times terminal multiple on our FY27 owners' profit of about Rs 370 crore, the implied growth is about 6.1% a year, not 18.9%. The gap between those numbers is exactly the cash conversion problem: if profit turns into cash, the price implies modest growth and may be fair; if it does not, the price demands a lot. The H1 FY27 cash flow of -Rs 16.27 crore[3] argues for the harder reading until Q3 shows otherwise.

Third, the lone research note we found, dated 30 September 2026, put FY27 EPS at Rs 52.08, FY28 at Rs 61.15 and a target of Rs 1,100 at 18 times FY28 earnings[22]. It was written three weeks before a quarter in which profit fell 29.98%[1], and with H1 EPS of Rs 18.18[2] its FY27 figure needs Rs 33.90 in the second half, almost double the whole first half. We treat it as stale and use it only for the 18 times multiple in our bull case. The three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, which is the share price divided by earnings per share, is 44.6 on the Screener chart[30]. Applied to our FY28 EPS it would give Rs 1,891, but that median covers a window that began in November 2023 when profit per share was a fraction of today's and growth was above 100% a year. We reject it as an anchor.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

An estimate is only useful if you know how it could fail. This chapter takes each estimate and the verdict itself, states the weakest assumption, and names the evidence that would show it wrong and the date it should arrive. It also gives the strongest honest case against our own rejection.

Bear case against our own conclusion: the rejection is mechanical and the stock is already down 50%

The strongest argument for owning WRTL is that bad news is in the price. The stock fell 50.13% from its high, the RSI is 26.66, it trades at 15.2 times trailing profit and the order book is above Rs 5,500 crore, with fresh awards on 23 and 27 October[25][26]. Revenue grew 43.84% in the half year[1]. If the Q2 margin dip is a one-off from the first quarter of APSPL consolidation, EPS could rise to about Rs 59 and the 18-times bull case of Rs 1,060 would pay 56.5%.

We accept that this could happen, and it is why the base-case value is only 4.8% below the close and not far lower. But the Stage 4 rule exists to stop us averaging into a falling stock, and the rule does not weigh a good story. The business facts do not remove the rule's logic. Price is in a downtrend, Layer 2 fails on cash and concentration, and our expected value is negative. The cost of waiting is that we may pay more later, and we accept that cost.

Weakest assumption in the EPS estimates: a 15% margin floor

The EPS estimates rest mainly on one number: the share of revenue the group keeps as operating profit. Both estimates lean on the CFO's 'more than 15%' statement[4]. That is not formal guidance (management's official forecast), as the CFO has said in another interview[28]. The consolidated margin moved from 18.77% in Q1 to 15.12% in Q2[7][1], mostly because APSPL carries margins of 9% to 10%[4]. If the T&D share of revenue rises, the blended margin could fall below 15% even if solar EPC stays at 19%. The evidence that would show us wrong is a Q3 FY27 margin below 15.0%. The result date has not been announced (N/A).

Weakest assumption in the cash view: working capital is timing, not a trend

Working capital is the money a contractor has paid out for equipment and labour but not yet collected from customers. It matters because a company can report a profit and still run short of cash if customers pay slowly. The estimates assume the cash tied up in the first half will come back.

In the first half of FY27, operating cash flow was -Rs 16.27 crore. It came from receivables rising Rs 155.26 crore, trade payables falling Rs 167.55 crore, other liabilities falling Rs 111.43 crore and tax paid of Rs 105.76 crore[2]. Management said in April that inventory and billings reflect specific projects[11]. If that is right, receivables should fall as projects hit milestones and cash should reverse. If receivable days stay near 174, the three-year cash conversion figure of 0.84 will fall in FY27, and a hard red line (below 0.25) becomes possible over a longer view.

The test is not the Q3 FY27 results. Listed companies publish a cash flow statement only for the half year and the full year[27], so the next one is the FY27 annual accounts, expected around May 2027 (our estimate, based on the 17 April 2026 call date for FY26). What this means for an owner: until then you can watch receivables only through management comments on the Q3 call, not through audited numbers.

Weakest assumption in the balance-sheet view: which equity base for debt-to-equity

Debt-to-equity compares what a company owes lenders with the money its owners have put in. It matters here because the answer changes with the definition of 'owners'. Total equity includes the minority stake, which is the share of APSPL (45%) that belongs to its original owners and not to WRTL shareholders. 'Standalone' figures are the parent company's own accounts, before adding subsidiaries.

We treat 0.81 times as the debt-to-equity figure because total equity includes the minority partners' Rs 627.14 crore stake in APSPL[2]. An analyst who uses only WRTL shareholders' equity gets 1.26 times, which is above the 1.20 times red line. Most of the borrowing sits in the parent company (standalone borrowings Rs 984.96 crore against standalone equity Rs 1,123.31 crore[2]), so the narrower measure is not unreasonable. The decision does not depend on this point because Stage 4 already rejects the stock. It would matter if the technical picture improved.

A stricter test removes assets that cannot be sold on their own. Tangible net worth is equity minus goodwill and intangible assets. CARE's negative trigger is total outside liabilities (everything the company owes, including suppliers) above 2.5 times tangible net worth[18]. On our arithmetic that ratio is 4.58 times at September 2026: liabilities of Rs 3,123.32 crore over tangible net worth of Rs 682.55 crore. CARE's rationale predates the acquisition and no newer one was found (N/A), so a rating review is a live risk.
