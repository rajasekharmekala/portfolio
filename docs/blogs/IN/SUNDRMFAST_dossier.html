1. What the company does

Sundram Fasteners Limited was started in 1966 and is headquartered in Chennai.[22][11] It makes high-strength fasteners, which are the bolts, nuts and studs that hold engines, axles and wheels together. It also makes power train parts, sintered metal parts pressed from metal powder, cold-extruded parts, radiator caps, water pumps and oil pumps, and components for wind turbines.[22] It is part of the TVS group. TVS Sundram Fasteners Private Limited held 46.79 percent of the shares at September 2026, within a promoter group holding of 46.95 percent.[24][23] The company runs eleven plants in India (seven in Tamil Nadu, and one each in Puducherry, Sri City, Medak and Pantnagar) and owns subsidiaries in India, China and the United Kingdom.[13]

The buyers are mostly vehicle makers, which the trade calls original equipment manufacturers, or OEM (the carmaker or truck maker that fits the part at the factory). Of its domestic sales, trucks and engines take about 35 percent, cars and utility vehicles about 40 percent, and tractors 10 to 12 percent.[15] Across the whole company, about 25 percent of revenue is exports, 60 to 62 percent is sold to vehicle makers in India, and 12 to 13 percent is the aftermarket (spare parts sold after the vehicle is on the road).[15] In FY26 (the year to March 2026) exports were about 26 percent of turnover, and the annual report says no customer gives more than 10 percent of group revenue.[12] About 62 percent of revenue came from automotive customers and 38 percent from other industries in the January 2026 call.[15] On the standalone accounts for FY25, automotive sales were ₹3,557 crore and non-auto sales ₹1,674 crore.[21]

The company makes money by turning steel into precision parts and selling them under long-running supply deals. With domestic vehicle makers, the steel price is a pass-through: when steel mills and carmakers agree a new price, the company bills its customers the same change and pays its own steel suppliers.[17] That protects profit in rupees, but it also lifts revenue with no extra profit, which means a rise in sales can come with a lower margin percentage. This matters for the September 2026 quarter, when sales rose 30 percent and operating margin fell to 14.9 percent from 16.6 percent a year earlier.[2] Fasteners are about 40 to 45 percent of domestic revenue, so the fastener business sets the tone for the whole company.[15]

The growth plans sit outside the vehicle cycle. In wind energy, the company supplies fasteners to turbine makers and is spending about ₹100 crore to lift annual sales from the ₹350 crore level to ₹500 crore.[17] In aerospace, sales were about ₹50 crore last year, the target for this year is ₹100 crore, and the company aims for ₹500 crore in two to three years, with customers such as Hindustan Aeronautics, ISRO and General Electric.[17] India added a record 6.05 gigawatts of wind capacity in FY26 and 2.42 gigawatts in April to August 2026, and the government is targeting 7.5 to 8 gigawatts for FY27, which gives the wind plan a growing market.[28][27] Electric-vehicle parts for General Motors and Stellantis are expected to bring ₹200 to ₹250 crore this year, up from less than ₹50 crore last year, and electric vehicles are only 4 to 5 percent of revenue today.[17] Subsidiaries give 13 to 14 percent of consolidated revenue and 8 to 10 percent of operating profit.[13]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Fastener: a bolt, nut or stud that holds two metal parts together. The company says it is a single source for the special ones many carmakers use.
  • OEM: the vehicle maker that buys parts for new vehicles, as opposed to the spare-parts (aftermarket) buyer.
  • Sintered part: a metal part made by pressing metal powder in a mould and heating it, used for gears and engine parts.
  • Pass-through: a contract term that moves a steel price change to the customer, so the company's profit in rupees is protected but its revenue and margin percentage move.
  • Operating margin: operating profit divided by sales. Screener reports it before other income and after all running costs; the company's own EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) figure is defined slightly differently.
  • Cash conversion cycle: the number of days cash is tied up from paying for stock to collecting from customers. Fewer days is better.
  • Reverse DCF: a calculation that starts from today's share price and solves for the profit growth the price already assumes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Crisil, the rating agency, describes the company's strengths as a leading market position in the fasteners industry, a wide product range across geographies, growing revenue from non-auto customers, healthy operating profit and a strong financial risk profile.[13] We found no published market-share figure for fasteners, so the claim of leadership rests on that rating opinion and not on a number. The evidence we can count is customer stickiness: the company says it is the single source for the special fasteners at most vehicle makers, and for standard fasteners it supplies 50 to 60 percent of what each maker buys from outside, even though its overall presence is 30 to 40 percent.[17]

Three facts make the position hard to copy. First, in our judgement, switching a fastener supplier means re-testing the part in the engine, which takes time that a vehicle maker usually avoids. Second, the company has wide coverage: it supplies trucks, cars, tractors, engines and spare parts, so one weak segment is offset by another.[15] Third, India's quality control order for fasteners is pulling business from imports. Management said it has already won a few fastener orders from Hyundai. In answer to an analyst's question about Hyundai and Kia, it expected ₹100 crore or more from this customer group in two to three years.[17] The balance sheet also helps: debt is small, Crisil expects interest cover above 30 times, and its short-term rating is the top grade, A1+.[13][14]

The weaknesses are equally clear. Operating margin has settled at 15 to 16 percent, and Screener shows it at 18 percent in FY17 to FY19 and again in FY21.[2] Crisil expects the margin to stay near 16 percent over the medium term.[13] Crisil lists a large working-capital need as the first weakness: gross current assets of 165 to 180 days of sales.[13] Subsidiaries are modest, and the UK unit tracks a weak European truck market.[13] Revenue growth over three years was only 4 percent a year to March 2026 on Screener's count, before accelerating to 17 percent over the last twelve months.[2]

Trade policy is the sharpest outside risk. Management said some products faced a 25 percent US tariff and others 50 percent, depending on iron and steel content.[15] The annual report says Section 232 tariffs still apply to Indian auto-component exporters.[12][31] The industry body ACMA reported a uniform 25 percent US tariff on Indian components in January 2026, and a February 2026 trade framework promised a preferential tariff quota on auto parts.[29][30] North America was 60 to 62 percent of exports in the January 2026 call, down from about 70 percent earlier, and Europe was 25 percent.[15] In August 2026 management put Europe below 20 percent of exports, and we do not know why the two statements differ.[17] The strength of this business is therefore its customers and its balance sheet. Its weakness is that it earns a middling margin, ties up a lot of cash, and depends on the US truck cycle for its best export profit.

Weakest assumption in the earnings estimate: margin recovers to 15.8 percent

We assume operating margin rises from 14.9 percent in the September quarter to about 15.8 percent in FY28. The support is management's statement that energy-cost compensation from customers is under negotiation and that margin should improve once it is accounted.[17] The evidence against it is in the latest quarter: margin fell instead of rising, and the extra ₹463 crore of sales brought only ₹44 crore of operating profit.[2] If the margin stays at 14.9 percent, FY28 earnings per share is about ₹35.70 and the central value falls to about ₹1,180. Weakest assumption in the value: a multiple of 33 times earnings

We hold the multiple near today's level on the argument that a business with a 17.6 percent return on capital, low debt and a TVS-group owner deserves about what Endurance Technologies gets.[2][32] The risk is that growth slows. Profit grew only 5.9 percent a year over three years, and the share has traded at 35 times earnings with that record. If growth reverts to the single digits, a multiple near 28 times is more likely than 33.[2][25] Evidence that would show us wrong is a share price that stays below ₹1,000 after two clean quarters of margin recovery. We would know that by the Q3 results in late January 2027.

Weakest assumption behind the reverse DCF: that profit is the right base

The ledger solves growth from trailing profit, and that is a fair base only if most of the capex is optional growth spending.
