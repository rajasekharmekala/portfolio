1. What the company does

Polycab India makes the wires and cables that carry electricity inside homes, factories, power grids and solar farms. The company calls itself India’s largest maker of wires and cables. It also sells fans, lights, switches, switchgear, pipes and solar inverters, a group the industry calls fast-moving electrical goods, or FMEG[3]. The company was incorporated in 1996[3]. Today it runs 26 plants and 35 warehouses, sells through 3,900 or more authorised dealers and 190,000 or more retail outlets, and has served customers in 94 countries[3][10].

Three groups of customers pay. Households and small builders buy through dealers and electricians, which the company calls the channel. Large buyers such as power utilities, railways, factories and engineering contractors buy cables directly, which the company calls institutional sales. Foreign customers buy cables for power and data projects, and exports were 5.4 percent of revenue in FY26[8]. Wires and cables made about 87 percent of FY26 revenue of ₹28,884 crore, FMEG about 7 percent, and the rest, about 6 percent by our subtraction, covers an engineering arm that builds public power-distribution and broadband networks, plus any other items[1][8].

The money is made by turning metal into cable. Copper and aluminium are most of the cost, and Polycab books metal back to back with customer orders so that a price swing is passed on[10]. Management says it reprices cables about a month after a metal move and wires about every fortnight, and describes the model as cost-plus[5]. That changes how growth should be read. In the September 2026 quarter revenue rose 53 percent, but volumes grew only in the mid to high single digits, so most of the revenue rise was the higher copper price, which management says averaged 40 to 45 percent higher through the first half[5]. London Metal Exchange copper was settled at $14,415 a tonne on 23 October 2026[21]. Because metal is passed through, goods cost 76.4 percent of sales against 72.9 percent a year earlier, while EBITDA, which stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization and measures operating profit before those charges, was 14.4 percent of sales[4]. That ratio of operating profit to sales is the operating margin, and it is the main yardstick of pricing power in this report.

Plant size sets the ceiling on volume. The annual report gives wire-and-cable capacity of 6 million km a year, 75 to 80 percent of installed capacity in use, and capital spending of ₹1,480 crore in FY26[10]. A new plant for extra-high-voltage cables, used on long-distance power lines, is now due in FY28[5].

Two money ideas run through the rest of this report. The first is working capital, which is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company has not yet paid its own suppliers. The second is the letter-of-credit habit. Polycab buys metal under usance letters of credit, which are bank promises to pay the supplier later, and the resulting bills are booked as “acceptances” and not as borrowings[10]. Acceptances were ₹6,556 crore at 30 September 2026, up from ₹2,978 crore a year earlier[4]. This keeps reported debt close to zero, but each rupee of acceptances is still a bill that falls due, which is why chapters 2B and 3 show results with and without it.

Why “net cash” needs a footnote

The company reports net cash of ₹4,520 crore at 30 September 2026. Its definition is cash, investments and deposits minus borrowings, and it leaves out acceptances[4]. If the ₹6,556 crore of acceptances were counted as debt, the same arithmetic gives net debt, meaning borrowings minus cash and investments, of about ₹2,036 crore (our subtraction). At 31 March 2026 the picture was similar: net cash of ₹4,194 crore against acceptances of ₹4,266 crore, which is roughly zero[9][10]. Acceptances are ordinary trade credit for metal, and the scoring rule we apply excuses them from the debt test when interest cover is at least 3.0 times, so this is not a red flag by itself. It is a reason to read “net cash” as a statement about bank loans, not as spare cash that could be paid out tomorrow. The next chapter looks at what the business does well, and where it is exposed.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The main strength is scale in a market that is moving from small local makers to organised ones. The annual report puts the company’s share of the organised domestic market at roughly 30 to 31 percent in FY26, up from about 18 percent in FY19[10], and the Q4 deck shows a gain of about 4 points in FY26 alone, from 26–27 percent[9]. A third-party guide says organised makers held 45 percent of the market in 2018 and 78 percent in 2025[22], so part of Polycab’s gain is the whole industry formalising, and part is share taken from other organised names. The same guide puts the next largest listed makers at 10–11 percent (KEI), 8–9 percent (RR Kabel) and 6–7 percent (Finolex) of the organised market in FY25[22].

Three things make that share hard to copy quickly. The first is distribution: 3,900 dealers and 190,000 retail outlets[3] put a shop within reach of every electrician. The second is that the company makes its own cables, since the annual report says all the W&C products it sells are made in house[10]. The third is the balance sheet. CRISIL rates the company AAA/Stable and India Ratings rates it IND AAA/Stable[13], and that rating is what lets it buy metal on bank credit and bid for large power-cable orders.

The market itself is large and growing faster than the economy. The annual report cites a BCG estimate of ₹1,000 billion for FY26 against ₹350 billion in FY14, growth of about 9 percent a year or 1.5 times GDP, with demand expected to grow 1.5 to 2 times GDP through FY30[10]. On the October call management put the market at about ₹1.1 lakh crore, of which wires are about 30 percent and 30–35 percent of that is still unorganised[5]. A research vendor sizes the Indian wire and cable market at $23.13 billion for 2026, but on a different basis, so we use it only to say that the market is large[23].

Where the business is weaker: a new rival with deep pockets

On 3 September 2026 UltraTech Cement, part of the Aditya Birla Group, launched a wires and cables brand called Ultravolt with a planned ₹1,800 crore investment. It says it is already the second largest wires maker by capacity and wants a top-two position within five years[20]. Polycab’s management says wires are a small part of its sales and that it sees no pricing correction, while an analyst on the same call said channel checks showed rival wires priced 10 to 15 percent lower[5]. We cannot verify either statement. The filed results to 30 September show no price pressure, since the operating margin rose to 14.4 percent, but the entrant had been selling for under a month by quarter end, so the filings cannot yet answer the question. Jefferies noted that the shares had fallen 20 percent from their June 2026 peak amid the launch[18].

Where the business is weaker: thin spots inside the growth

FMEG revenue more than doubled, but management says it sells only solar inverters in that category and calls it largely a trading business[5]. Trading margins are usually thinner and easier to copy than factory margins, so we give the 10.1 percent FMEG margin less weight than the same number from a plant-made product. Exports are the second thin spot. They were 5.4 percent of FY26 revenue and 4.2 percent in the first half of FY27, against a target of more than 10 percent by FY30[8][3][9]. The share fell because the home market grew faster, which is a fair reason, but the target has moved further away, not closer.

Governance has two items. A tax search in December 2023 led to demands of ₹52.6 crore plus interest for the years to 2023-24, which the first appeal authority has reduced to nil, and the tax department has appealed; a further demand of ₹56.8 crore for 2024-25 is under appeal[10]. The second item is the insolvency petition from an aluminium supplier, covered in chapter 6. The Jaisinghani family holds 61.44 percent and none of it is pledged[14], and 56 percent of the board is independent[13]. Promoter holding was 65.78 percent in December 2023, so it has fallen about 4.3 points in under three years[1]. Our sources do not state the cause; with no pledge, it looks like selling or new shares being issued, and it is a modest negative for alignment.

What this means for an owner

For an owner, the strengths are real and measurable: a growing share, a distribution network, in-house plants and a top credit rating. The weaknesses are about what comes next, not what happened. A well-funded rival is entering wires, the fastest-growing part of the business, while metal prices are at record levels and the shares are already down about a quarter from their high. The next chapter turns these points into five company-specific indicators that we score, and checks whether management did what it said it would. That check matters more than usual here, because the investment case rests on management’s statements about margins that look high against its own long-run range, on cash that depends on bank credit, and on timetables for new plants. If those statements hold up, the weaknesses above are risks to manage. If they do not, the same weaknesses become reasons to avoid the stock, which is why we test the record before looking at the numbers in detail.

The court order and the exchange’s derivatives decision

Copper and the quality of revenue

Management says copper averaged 40 to 45 percent higher through the first half and that the price is passed on within a month for cables and a fortnight for wires[5]. Evidence would be the cost of goods rising or falling as a share of sales, now 76.4 percent, and the domestic volume figure management gives each quarter. We will know at the Q3 results.

Supplier credit that may have to be paid down

Acceptances are ₹6,556 crore, half of shareholders’ funds[4]. Management says it expects working capital to rise from 11 days toward its long-run 45–50 days as the letter-of-credit effect fades[5]. Evidence would be acceptances falling, operating cash flow turning negative for a second half-year, or finance cost staying high.
