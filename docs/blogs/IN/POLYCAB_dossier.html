Before you read

Polycab India is India's largest maker of electrical wires and cables, and it also sells fans, lights, switches and solar inverters. This report rejects the stock for now, because the operating scorecard passes only 3 of 5 tests and the price has not yet broken out of its flat trading range. The business itself is strong on the numbers: 33% return on capital, almost no bank debt and no pledged promoter shares[14][17]. The rejection would be wrong if volume growth returns to about 18% and the price closes above its 50-day average, or if the cash it reports proves to be real operating cash and not supplier credit. It would turn into a harder rejection if copper falls sharply or if UltraTech's new wires plant starts a price war.

1. What the company does

Polycab makes the wires that run through homes and the cables that carry power through factories, railways, wind farms and data centres. In the year to 31 March 2026 it sold ₹25,179 crore of wires and cables, ₹2,069 crore of fast-moving electrical goods (fans, lighting, switches, switchgear, pumps and solar inverters) and ₹1,666 crore of turnkey projects for governments[7]. That makes wires and cables about 87% of the company, and the rest is a smaller consumer business and a project-contracting arm. It sells through more than 3,900 dealers and distributors and about 190,000 retail outlets, and it has sold to customers in 94 countries[1].

Who pays, and how it earns its money, is the key to reading every number in this report. A cable is mostly copper or aluminium. Polycab buys the metal at the world price and sells the cable at the metal price plus a conversion margin, which is a cost-plus model[3]. Management says it revises cable prices about once a month and wire prices about once a fortnight[3]. So when copper rises, sales in rupees rise almost one for one, but the percentage margin looks thinner because the same rupee profit is divided by a bigger sale. In the September 2026 quarter, sales grew 53% while volume grew only mid-to-high single digits[1][3]. If volume grew about 7%, prices and mix explain a rise of about 43%. Most of the headline growth is metal, not extra cable.

Polycab imports all of its copper and pays for much of it with supplier credit that banks guarantee, called letter-of-credit acceptances[3]. These acceptances stood at ₹6,556 crore on 30 September 2026, up from ₹2,978 crore a year earlier[2]. They matter because they let the company hold large inventories (₹6,963 crore) and receivables (₹3,459 crore) without showing bank debt[2]. They also carry a cost: interest on letters of credit and leases was ₹78 crore in the latest quarter, against ₹30 crore a year before[2]. Later chapters test how much of the company's cash flow depends on this credit.

The mix of customers is also moving. About 32% of sales now come from consumers and retail wire buyers rather than project buyers[9]. In the latest quarter, sales through dealers grew faster than sales to project customers, and wires grew faster than cables[1]. Exports were 5.0% of sales in the quarter, and the company aims for more than 10% by FY30[2][7]. A plant for extra-high-voltage cable, the type used on the main power grid, is under construction and is now expected to start in April or May 2027[3].

What a rupee of sales is made of

In the September 2026 quarter, raw materials and goods sold cost ₹7,562 crore of every ₹9,899 crore of sales. What was left, called contribution, was ₹2,337 crore, or 23.6% of sales[2]. A year earlier contribution was ₹1,756 crore, or 27.1% of sales[2]. So the percentage fell 3.5 points while the rupee amount rose 33%. That is what a cost-plus business looks like when its main input gets 40% more expensive. It also means a falling margin percentage is not by itself a warning, and a rising rupee profit is not by itself proof of strength. Chapter 2 looks at the rupee spread per unit of volume.

Two other pieces of the profit and loss account deserve a note. Other income was ₹88 crore in the quarter, mostly gains on selling mutual-fund investments[2]. Finance cost was ₹94 crore, up from ₹48 crore a year before, because of the letter-of-credit interest described above[2]. Net profit after tax was ₹990 crore, 10.0% of sales[1].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Polycab's first strength is scale. Its own annual report says its sales are nearly twice those of its closest peer, that it holds 30–31% of the organised domestic market, and that it earns the highest margins in the industry[30]. Management says that share was 26–27% a year earlier and 18–19% in FY19, which is a gain of 3 to 4 points in FY26 alone[4]. These share figures are the company's own estimates. No independent audit of them was found. Still, the direction matches what a rating agency says: CRISIL gives Polycab its top AAA rating and names its market leadership and its 3,900-strong dealer network as the main strengths[9].

The second strength is the balance sheet. Bank borrowings were ₹112 crore against shareholders' funds of ₹13,094 crore on 30 September 2026[2]. Net cash on the company's own definition was ₹4,520 crore, up from ₹4,194 crore at the March 2026 year-end[1][31]. CRISIL puts total gearing, including letter-of-credit acceptances, at 0.37 times at March 2026, up from 0.15 times a year earlier, and expects annual capital spending of ₹1,200–1,600 crore to be paid from the company's own earnings[9]. That last figure is the honest version. The company has little bank debt, but it uses a lot of supplier credit, and that credit grew fast.

The weaknesses are real, and each one shows up in the numbers. First, the profit is tied to the copper price. Management says it has no view on copper, works on a cost-plus model, and puts the rise in copper at 40–45% on average through the first half[3]. The LME cash price was $14,595 a tonne on 16 October 2026[12]. When metal rises, a company holding about 86 days of inventory[2] tends to earn a little extra on stock bought earlier. When metal falls, the same effect runs in reverse and buyers delay orders. Second, volume growth has slowed sharply. It was about 40% in the December 2025 quarter[5], low single digits in March 2026[4], low to mid single digits in June[6] and mid to high single digits in September[3]. Third, competition is arriving. UltraTech Cement started making house wires and light cables on 1 September 2026, with ₹1,800 crore of planned investment and a plant of 10.98 lakh kilometres a year, and cable shares fell up to 6% that day[18]. Nuvama warned of a 150–250 basis point hit to sector margins, against its earlier view of 100–150 basis points[19].

How strong is the defence? A Polycab executive told Mint the company will "go all out" to keep share, and that the new entrant is focused on wires, which are less than a third of the market, while cables need approvals from utilities and contractors[20]. Indian Express reports that Nomura expects UltraTech to reach 6–7% share by FY30[22]. Motilal Oswal puts it at 5–7% by FY31[19]. The market size matters here: Equirus estimates India's wires and cables market at ₹1.07 lakh crore in FY26 and ₹1.75 lakh crore by FY30, about 13% a year[21]. The company's own annual report gives 11–13% a year through FY30[29]. If that is right, a new entrant can win 6–7% of a market that is growing 13% a year without taking a single rupee of sales from Polycab. The harm would come through price and margin, not volume.

How strong is the copper pass-through?

Pass-through is the test of whether a cable maker is a manufacturer or a commodity speculator. The evidence for Polycab is good. In the September 2026 quarter, contribution (sales minus the cost of goods) rose 33% in rupees while volume grew mid-to-high single digits and the cost of goods rose 60%[2]. EBITDA, short for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, is operating profit before depreciation and finance costs. It rose 39% to ₹1,423 crore[1]. If volume rose about 7%, EBITDA per unit of volume rose about 30%. Some of that is operating leverage, some is a richer mix of wires and exports, and some may be the gain on cheaper stock sold at higher prices. The company does not split these.

Peers show the same pattern at different margins. RR Kabel's EBITDA margin was 9.0% in the June quarter[25]. Finolex Cables' consolidated EBITDA margin was 14.2%, helped by communication cables, which earned about 30% (₹52.52 crore on ₹176 crore of sales) against ₹1.3 crore a year earlier[27]. Havells' cables revenue rose 27% in the June quarter while segment profit rose only 5%, a sign of pass-through lagging behind the metal[28]. Polycab's quick monthly and fortnightly repricing[3] looks like a real advantage in a rising market. The test is a falling one, which has not yet happened in this cycle.

Is the consumer business a drag or a driver?

The consumer business, called FMEG, was Polycab's most expensive experiment. It lost money until the March 2025 quarter[4]. Its segment profit was a loss of ₹39 crore (−2.3% of sales) in FY25 and a profit of ₹55 crore (2.7%) in FY26[7]. In the latest quarter, sales rose 124% to ₹993 crore and the margin reached 10.1%, the seventh profitable quarter in a row[1][3][33]. Management credits scale, a richer mix (33% of fans and 38% of lighting now premium) and a regional sales model[3].

The caution is the product mix. Management says solar is now the largest category and that Polycab sells only inverters in solar, which it calls largely a trading business, with no manufacturing[3]. A traded inverter normally carries a thinner margin than a made fan. A margin of 10.1% on that mix is possible, but it has been shown for one quarter. The same business was at 4.1% in the March 2026 quarter and 2.7% for FY26[4][7]. The 8–10% goal for FY30 has already been reached more than three years early[7], which is why this report treats the trend as good but the level as unproven.

Exports and the extra-high-voltage plant

Exports are the business's high-margin growth option. Export sales were ₹493 crore in the September quarter, up 17% year on year and 85% on the June quarter, but only ₹760 crore for the half year, up 4.7%[2][3]. Exports were 5.0% of sales in the quarter and 4.2% for the half, against a target of more than 10% by FY30[2][7]. Management blames shipping delays of 10–12 days and the Middle East conflict, flags a risk from US trade policy, and points to a strong order book[3]. A target that needs the share to double while domestic sales grow 50% is a large ask.

The extra-high-voltage plant is where the company can win a different kind of order. Management said in May 2026 that capacity would come on stream by the end of calendar 2026, with some revenue in FY28 and about half of domestic demand today served by imports[4]. In October it said commissioning would be in FY28, possibly starting in April or May 2027[3]. That is a slip of about four to five months, with the revenue year unchanged. KEI Industries is ahead: it has spent ₹1,722 crore of a planned ₹2,000 crore on its Sanand plant and expects EHV cable there by March 2027[24].

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A business of this kind is judged on a handful of operating numbers, not on one profit figure. Layer 2 of the underwriting framework asks for three to five measures that decide how this particular company earns money, and it asks each to be scored PASS, WATCH or FAIL. A score of PASS means the latest verified period meets or beats management's own target, which the company calls guidance when it is a published forecast, or the best peer, with the trend intact. WATCH means it meets the target in one respect but the trend, the quality of the number or the peer comparison raises a doubt. FAIL means it clearly misses.

Five measures were picked. First, the profit margin on wires and cables after copper pass-through, because that shows whether the rupee spread survives metal swings. Second, volume growth, organised-market share and exports, because rupee sales growth is mostly metal and only volume shows real demand. Third, the path of the consumer business from loss to profit, because it is the main use of fresh management attention. Fourth, capital spending, return on capital and the extra-high-voltage plant, because Polycab is in the middle of its largest investment cycle. Fifth, working capital days and cash conversion. Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, net of what is owed to suppliers, and it matters because the balance sheet's strength depends on how much supplier credit the company uses. Cash conversion is measured with free cash flow, which is the cash left after buying plant and equipment. The framework needs at least 4 of 5 to PASS to qualify. Polycab scores 3 PASS, 2 WATCH and no FAIL, which is 3 of 5 and below the bar.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record on what it promised is better than average, but it is not spotless. Project Leap, launched after FY21, promised ₹200 billion of top line by FY26[10][11]. Revenue in FY26 was ₹288 billion[7]. The company says it reached the Leap goals ahead of its committed timeline[32]. That promise was delivered with room to spare.

The promise that was missed is the consumer margin. In June 2023 the chief financial officer said the company was "targeting a 10% annualized EBITDA margin by FY26" in FMEG[11]. The segment's FY26 profit margin was 2.7% after depreciation, and about 4.7% before it, using ₹42 crore of segment depreciation[7]. The 10.1% reading came in the September 2026 quarter, about six months after the target year ended. That is a miss on timing, though the later strength is genuine. Management has since reset the long-run goal to 8–10% by FY30[7].

Working capital is the third test. Management says the long-run cycle is 45–50 days and that the current 11 days is helped by letters of credit that will normalise[3]. That is honest guidance, but it has not been delivered yet: the days went from 44 at March 2025 to 25 at March 2026 and 11 at September 2026[2][4]. The extra-high-voltage plant slipped from "by end of this calendar year" to FY28[4][3], and the dividend payout rose from 26.3% to 27.2% on the way to a stated goal above 30% by FY30[4].

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on future profit. This chapter asks three questions. What growth does today's price already assume? Are analysts raising or cutting their numbers? The first answer comes from a reverse discounted-cash-flow model. It starts from today's price and asks what yearly growth in free cash flow for 10 years would justify it, at a 12% discount rate and a 20 times exit multiple. For Polycab the answer is 21.8% a year, solved on free cash flow of ₹1,908 crore. At that growth rate, free cash flow would grow to about ₹13,700 crore by year 10. That is a demanding number, but the starting point is unusually low: free cash flow is 1.54% of the company's value because inventory and receivables swallowed cash while copper rose, and capex was ₹1,480 crore in FY26.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest one for each estimate, gives the strongest honest case against our own view, names the single claim the whole case hangs on, and says when the evidence will arrive. The point is to say in advance what would change our mind.

Weakest assumption in the base target: the multiple returns to 47.9 times

The base target of ₹11,201 needs the market to pay 47.9 times earnings again. That median was earned while earnings grew about 31% a year. Consensus for FY28 is only 12.0% growth[13][14]. A slower grower normally earns a lower multiple, not the old one. Interest rates also work against it: management told analysts that the Reserve Bank's committee raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.5% and moved to a calibrated tightening stance, which in management's words means rate cuts are not expected soon[3]. If the multiple stays at today's 39.2 times, the value is ₹9,167 and reward-to-risk is 1.53. In the expected-value table that single change cuts the expected return from +17.8% to +6.6%, and at our assumed 33.5 times it is −0.7%. Those are the numbers that show the target is wrong. It would be known by the January 2027 results if the share price fails to recover while earnings keep rising.

Weakest assumption in the expected value: the probabilities

The probabilities of 15%, 45% and 40% are judgement, and the three risks behind the bear case are not independent. A copper fall would hit margin, inventory value and working capital at the same time. That means the true chance of a bad outcome is probably more lumpy than a smooth 40%: either the bad things happen together or they do not. The expected value of ₹9,679 should be read as a central estimate with a wide error bar, not a precise figure.

The red-team bear case

The strongest case against owning Polycab starts with margin. The September quarter EBITDA margin of 14.4% is above management's own 11–13% band, and it was earned while copper was rising and stock bought earlier was sold at higher prices[2][3]. On the half-year run-rate of about ₹36,200 crore of sales, a margin of 13% instead of 14.4% would cut EBITDA by about ₹500 crore (10%), and a margin of 11% would cut it by about ₹1,230 crore (24%). After 25% tax, that is a fall of ₹380–920 crore in profit against a trailing profit of ₹3,202 crore, or 12–29%. The bear case of a 12% earnings cut is the mild end of this range.

Cash is the second leg. Without the supplier-credit float, free cash flow was negative in FY26 and in the first half of FY27, and management says working-capital days will rise from 11 to 45–50, absorbing ₹3,400–3,900 crore[7][2][3]. Competition is the third: UltraTech is targeting 6–7% share by FY30 and brokers expect margin pressure of 150–250 basis points[22][19]. A fourth, smaller leg is the Asier Metals insolvency matter and the delayed extra-high-voltage plant. The consumer margin of 10.1% on a trading-heavy solar mix is the fifth, because it has been seen once. None of these is a prediction. Together they describe how a quality company can still see its shares fall 20% from here.

The hinge claim

The claim the whole case hangs on is that Polycab's high margin is structural (scale, mix and channel) and not just copper. If it is structural, margin stays at or above 13% when copper stops rising, volume growth returns towards 1.5 times the market, and the stock should recover its median multiple. If it is not, margin reverts towards the 11–13% band, the base target is too high and the bear case becomes the central case. The December 2026 quarter is the first clean test, because copper has been rising for a year and the comparison base for volume is high (about 40% growth in the year-ago quarter)[5].

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is REJECTED, and the rule is the framework's own. A Core name needs a Stage 1 base that has broken out or a Stage 2 uptrend, and Polycab's close of ₹8,219 is below its 20-day, 50-day and 200-day averages with no breakout. It also needs at least 4 of 5 operating measures to PASS, and Polycab has 3. Either failure alone is enough. The red lines all pass, the financial floors pass on audited numbers, and the reward-to-risk ratio of 4.80 passes on a base target that assumes the multiple recovers. So this is a good business at an unproven moment, not a bad business.

Where is the price on the support ladder? Nearest support is the 63-day low of ₹7,842, and the close is 1.54 average daily ranges above it. The ladder in the scoring rules has four bands: full size within 0.6, standard within 1.2, limit-order-and-reduce within 1.6, and no-buy beyond. At 1.54 Polycab sits in the limit-order band. That means that even if the gates passed, the only way to buy would be a limit order, which fills only at your price or better and never chases, placed inside the entry zone of ₹7,842 to ₹8,135.29, not a purchase at ₹8,219.

Layer 3 is the last of the framework's three layers. Layers 1 and 2 ask whether the company is good enough. Layer 3 turns a verdict into a price to buy at, a price to sell at, and a size. The size is set in four steps. First, each position gets a tier, a conviction level from Tier 1 (largest) to Tier 4 (smallest). A tier carries a cap on how much of the portfolio the position may be at the start. Tier 3 allows 2% to 3.5%. Tier 0 means no position, which is where Polycab sits today. Second, each tier has a risk budget, the share of the whole portfolio you accept losing if the stop is hit. For Tier 3 it is 50 basis points, a basis point being one hundredth of a percentage point, so 0.5% of the portfolio, or about ₹22,870 on a net worth of ₹45.74 lakh. Third, the size is the risk budget divided by the distance to the stop. Fourth, the result is held to the tier cap. The rules also limit portfolio heat, the total loss across all open positions if every stop were hit at once, to 12% of the portfolio.

How much would we buy if it qualified? The stop is ₹7,597.59: the base low of ₹7,842 less one average daily range of ₹244.41, which is the rule for Core names. From the top of the entry zone that is a 6.61% stop distance. A Tier 3 starter would be 0.5% ÷ 6.61% = 7.56% of the portfolio, but the Tier 3 cap is 3.5%, so the weight would be 3.5% of net worth. That is about ₹1.60 lakh, or about 19 shares, and a stop-out would lose about ₹10,600, or 0.23% of the portfolio. The smaller cap is deliberate: a name that has not yet passed the chart or scorecard earns a starter size, not a full one. Today the tier is TIER_0 and the weight is 0%.

The last two rows of the table below cover what happens after a purchase. The let-winners-run rule says do not sell a rising stock just because the RSI is above 70 or because its weight has passed the 15% single-name limit. A winner may grow to 22% of the portfolio through price gains. To protect the gain, the stop trails a moving line under the price: the 21-day exponential average, which weights recent days more heavily, or the 50-day simple average. The position is sold only if the price closes below that rising line, or if the fundamentals break.

What would change the verdict? Two things must happen: the chart floor must be met, and the scorecard must reach 4 of 5. The chart is met by a weekly close above the falling 50-day average, now ₹8,624.22, on volume above its 20-day average, or by a base that holds above ₹7,842 and then lifts through the averages. The scorecard is met by one more PASS: either volume growth of about 18% (1.5 times a market growing about 12%, measured over the December quarter or the trailing 12 months) with margin staying above 13%, or two quarters of cash flow from operations that stay positive without the acceptance float. Before any promotion the full five-measure scorecard is rerun on the new data, so a PASS that is held today cannot slip unnoticed. If both happen, the verdict would move to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, and to ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP only when the price falls back to within 1.2 average daily ranges of support. One more check applies: with the stop and target as they stand, the reward-to-risk ratio drops below 3.0 above ₹8,498, so a breakout buy needs a higher earnings estimate as well.
