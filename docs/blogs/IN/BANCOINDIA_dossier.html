1. What the company does

Banco Products (India) Limited was set up in 1961 in Vadodara, Gujarat.[31] It makes two kinds of parts that keep an engine from overheating or leaking. The first is engine cooling: radiators, charge-air coolers, oil coolers and fuel coolers. A charge-air cooler chills the air that a turbo engine squeezes in. The second is sealing: gaskets and heat shields, thin layers that stop hot gas and oil escaping from the engine.[3]

Most buyers are makers of tractors, trucks, buses, digging machines, engines and locomotives. These makers are called original equipment makers, or OEMs. The rating agency CARE names JCB India, Mahindra and Mahindra, Cummins India, TAFE, John Deere India, Kirloskar Oil Engines and Ashok Leyland as key customers.[5] A second group of buyers is the aftermarket: garages and dealers who buy replacement parts.

Banco earns money by selling parts by the unit, and the price follows the price of metal. Aluminium is the main input. CARE reports that it rose from ₹2.39 lakh a tonne in April 2025 to ₹3.35 lakh in March 2026 and ₹3.63 lakh in April 2026.[5] CARE says Banco passes higher metal costs to customers with a delay. So sales rise when metal rises, but profit can be squeezed for a quarter or two. Over thirteen quarters, the profit margin swung between 9 percent and 24 percent. The yearly margin stayed between 14 and 19 percent.[1]

The group has four parts, and the legal parent supplies about one-third of its turnover. The parent, Banco Products (India) Limited, had standalone sales of ₹1,226 crore in FY26, the year to 31 March 2026. Of that, ₹882 crore was sold in India and ₹344 crore was exported.[3] The whole group had ₹3,867 crore of turnover. So about two-thirds of group sales sit outside the parent, mainly in NRF. This is an estimate, because sales between group companies are not shown separately.[3] In the June 2026 quarter the parent sold ₹342 crore, 29 percent of the group's ₹1,184 crore.[2][1] The parent had only 447 staff at the end of FY26. Its profit of ₹419 crore includes ₹264.7 crore of dividends from subsidiaries, so the consolidated figures, which add the whole group, are the right ones to judge.[3]

Running this kind of factory means paying for metal and warehouses long before cash comes back. The group held ₹1,498 crore of inventory at 31 March 2026, which is 263 days of sales.[1][3] NRF alone keeps about 15,000 different spare parts. Its annual report says parts availability is above 90 percent across its sites.[13][3] The parent can make 3.33 million radiators a year across five plants. It used 80.43 percent of that in FY26, up from 76.50 percent.[5] Demand follows farm, building and truck cycles. CARE splits parent sales into cars and trucks 31 percent, industrial and earth-moving 30 percent, farm equipment 24 percent, rail 3 percent and other cooling 12 percent.[5]

How one radiator order becomes profit

A tractor maker asks Banco to design a cooling module for a new engine. Banco builds and tests prototypes and wins an approval.[4] Once a design is approved, changing supplier means re-testing the cooling system. Buyers tend to avoid that. This is our reading of why OEM ties last, and the company publishes no retention figure. Prices follow metal costs with a delay, so a rise in aluminium lifts sales first and margin later.[5] An aftermarket sale goes through a dealer from a catalogue. In our reading, the inventory sits on shelves for months. That fits the 263 days of inventory the group shows.[1]

For an owner, this is a steady, engineering-led supplier with a heavy inventory of parts. It is not a quick-cash business. The next chapter tests whether the strengths outweigh that weight.

Why the Dutch subsidiary matters more than the parent

NRF was founded in 1927 and bought by Banco in 2010 for €17.7 million.[5] It covers more than 90 percent of the European car population in core categories such as radiators and oil coolers, says the FY25 annual report.[4] In FY26 the group says NRF grew and made a profit, widened its range from 13,000 to 15,000 parts, and recovered quickly from a fire at its French warehouse.[3] The parent also gains from NRF directly. It received ₹264.7 crore of dividends in FY26, against ₹130.8 crore in FY25.[3]

So group profit depends on European garages buying parts, on the euro-rupee rate, and on how fast NRF turns inventory into cash. Chapter 3 shows the numbers behind this.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The best sign of a good business is how much it earns on the money put into it. Banco's return on capital employed is 30.9 percent and its return on equity is 30.2 percent.[1] The FY26 annual report gives a return on equity of 32.25 percent, up from 16.8 percent in FY22.[3] Debt is small. Borrowings, including lease bills, were ₹614 crore against shareholders' equity of ₹1,685 crore. That is a ratio of 0.36. Interest was covered 28.6 times in FY26.[1][5] CARE Ratings, which rates the group, reaffirmed AA with a stable outlook on 6 July 2026.[5]

Market position is the second pillar. CARE puts Banco's share of the Indian radiator market at about 21.84 percent and calls it the market leader.[5] The parent runs five plants and makes its own radiator cores in Vadodara. It sells through more than 700 primary partners in over 230 places.[5][4] No end market is above 31 percent of parent sales. The ten largest customers took 38 percent, or ₹473.89 crore.[5]

The weak points are clear too. Metal prices are volatile. Aluminium rose about 52 percent between April 2025 and April 2026.[5] Currency swings hit exports and the European arm. Working capital is the cash tied up in inventory and unpaid customer bills, less what the firm owes suppliers. Banco needs a lot of it. CARE puts its working capital cycle at 170 days, against 164 a year earlier.[5] CARE says an upgrade needs a cycle below 100 days. CARE also lists high dividend payouts as a concern.[5] Dividends of ₹215 crore in FY26 were larger than the ₹172 crore of free cash flow (FCF) the group had left after capital spending and lease payments. Free cash flow is the cash left after the spending needed to keep the business running. Chapter 3 shows the working.[3]

A fourth point is about disclosure and cannot be scored in numbers. On 25 June 2026 Crisil Ratings cut a rating on a ₹20 crore cash-credit line to BB with the label issuer not cooperating.[6] Crisil said it had asked for information from 4 May 2026 and did not receive it. The same group held AA from Crisil as recently as 2024.[6] CARE, with the company's help, reaffirmed AA eleven days later.[5] The two ratings cover small, different facilities. The label does not mean the firm cannot pay its debts. It does mean that one agency was refused information.[6][5]

Competitive position and the evidence for it

A moat is whatever keeps customers from leaving or rivals from copying you. For Banco the evidence is a market share near one-fifth in Indian radiators, long OEM ties, and in-house design and testing. Research spending was 0.71 percent of the parent's turnover.[3] The NRF brand is the third strength. The FY25 report says it covers more than 90 percent of the European car population in core cooling parts.[4] A return on capital of about 31 percent is far above the 18 to 20 percent earned by the three peers in chapter 3. That is the number that says the moat has been real.[1][18][19][20] The weak spot is disclosure. Banco publishes no customer-by-customer data, no order book and no investor slides. So we cannot show how much of the 38 percent top-ten share is under long contracts.[30]

For an owner, the quality of the business is not in doubt on the numbers published. But the facts that would let an outsider check it are thin, and that matters more than usual here.

What could break the moat

Three things could weaken it. The first is the shift to electric vehicles. An electric car needs cooling for batteries and power electronics, not a large engine radiator. Banco says it has begun supplying a few electric and clean-energy customers, but it publishes no revenue for them.[4] Electric vehicles were 4.6 percent of Indian component supplies to vehicle makers in the first half of FY26. In the same period the aftermarket grew 9 percent, faster than the 7.3 percent for supplies to vehicle makers.[25][26]

The second is the rise of Chinese car brands in Europe. NRF says it is meeting this with a range for Chinese cars, starting with BYD.[3] The third is the age of Europe's car fleet. The average EU car was about 12.7 years old in 2024, up from 12.5.[27] Older cars need more spare parts, which helps NRF. But this is a slow tailwind, not a growth story.

So growth from here has to come from NRF's wider range and from Indian industrial demand. ACMA, the Indian component makers' body, expects industry growth of 8 to 10 percent in FY27.[24] A market rating above that would need share gains that Banco has not yet reported.
