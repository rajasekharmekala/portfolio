Tata Consumer Products (NSE: TATACONSUM) sells tea, coffee, salt and packaged foods, and the verdict is REJECTED, so the file goes to the archive. The company fails three of the five hard gates in Layer 1, the first underwriting layer. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is the profit earned on all the money the business uses, and the company earns 9.24 percent against a floor of 15.0. The technical test fails because the stock is in Stage 4, which means a downtrend, and the asymmetry test fails because no verified target exists. The balance sheet and cash tests pass, so the rejection is about returns and price, not debt.

How the verdict was reached

The underwriting rulebook sorts every stock through three layers. Layer 1 is a set of hard gates. Each gate is either a pass or a fail, and one failure is enough to reject the stock. When that happens the later layers are not run, because there is no point scoring the business on its operating measures for a company that already fails a basic return test. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five measures that drive the particular business. Layer 3 checks whether the price is close enough to a support level to buy it now.

Layer 1 has five gates. Three come from the company's reported numbers: return on capital, debt to equity, and how much of the profit turns into cash over three years. Two come from the price chart and the target: the trend test, and the asymmetry test, which asks whether the possible gain is at least three times the possible loss for this bucket. Each gate has a fixed threshold, so the answer does not depend on how a company is described in the news. The section 'The numbers, in plain words' works through the numbers, and 'Layer 1 disposition' sets out the gate table.

The verdict here is REJECTED for three reasons, each on its own enough to fail the gate. The return on capital is below 15 percent on every basis we could read. The stock is in a Stage 4 markdown with both long-term averages above the price. And no target anchored to forward earnings and a three-year median price-to-earnings multiple could be verified. That multiple is the share price divided by the profit per share, so the asymmetry test cannot be passed. Rejection means the stock is archived for now. It does not mean the company is a poor business. A large, cash-generating brand can still be a poor entry point when the returns on its capital are thin and its price is in a downtrend.

What the company does

Tata Consumer Products is part of the Tata Group and operates in food and beverages [1]. Its products include tea, coffee, salt and packaged food. On the June 2026 call, management discussed Tata Salt, Tata Sampann and Organic India as leading names [5]. Packaged foods means goods sold in sealed packs with a long shelf life, such as salt, pulses and spices, which makes them easier to sell through shops than fresh food. The company's summary on Screener says it spans packaged drinks and a diverse food portfolio, and that its 2020 purchase of Tata Chemicals' consumer business added staples such as salt, pulses, spices and oils [1]. That matters for the verdict because a broad mix of everyday brands earns its return only if each rupee of sales keeps enough profit after costs, and the margin, covered in the next section, is the measure of that.

The June 2026 quarter shows where the growth is coming from. Consolidated revenue rose about 12 percent to ₹5,349 crore, and management said the India branded business grew its underlying volume by 13 percent [3] [5]. On the call, management said its newer brands grew faster than the core: Tata Sampann grew 58 percent, ready-to-drink products grew 41 percent, and Capital Foods and Organic India grew 35 percent combined. The group's "growth businesses" grew 47 percent and now make up 36 percent of the India business [5]. Salt grew 7 percent, and the international business grew 16 percent in rupees, but only 3 percent in constant currency, which means most of that rise came from exchange rates [5].

The café business is the one that needs watching. Management said Starbucks revenue rose 11 percent in the quarter, but it also closed some cafés in the short term and relaunched its loyalty programme [8]. The company has around 11,233 employees [26]. What this means for you is that the business is broad. Its growth comes from many brands, so no single product decides the outcome, but the same breadth makes it harder to see whether the return on the whole pile of capital is improving. That question is the subject of the section 'Why the business is strong or weak'.

Why the business is strong or weak

The strength is in growth and in the brands. Sales grew from ₹13,783 crore in the year to March 2023 to ₹20,290 crore in the year to March 2026, a compound rate of 13.8 percent a year over those three years, which is close to the 14 percent Screener reports for the same period [1] [24]. A compound annual growth rate (CAGR) is the steady yearly rate that would take the start figure to the end figure. Over the four years from FY22, when sales were ₹12,425 crore, the same calculation gives 13.0 percent a year. Profit has not grown as fast. Net profit was ₹1,320 crore in FY23 and ₹1,547 crore in FY26, a rise of 17 percent over three years, or about 5.4 percent a year [1]. Screener quotes a three-year profit growth rate of 13 percent, which we could not reproduce from its own table, so this report uses the figure we can recompute. The gap is one of the source conflicts listed in 'Why these estimates might be wrong'.

The margin tells the same story from another angle. The operating profit margin (OPM) is the share of each rupee of sales left after the cost of making and selling the goods, before interest and tax. It was 14 percent in FY26 and 15 percent in FY24 [1]. In the June 2026 quarter, management said EBITDA, meaning earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, rose 19 percent and the margin widened by 70 basis points to 13.6 percent [5] [7]. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so 70 basis points is 0.7 of a point. The margin is improving, but it is not wide. The company is earning about 14 rupees of operating profit on every 100 rupees of sales, which leaves little room for a bad year of input costs.

The weakness is the return on capital. The rulebook asks whether the business earns at least 15 percent on the money invested in it. The latest figure from the consolidated table is 9.24 percent, and the March 2026 annual figure is 9 percent [1]. Return on equity, the profit earned on shareholders' money, is 7.35 percent [1]. The standalone figures, which cover the parent company without its subsidiaries, are no better: standalone ROCE is 11.9 percent and return on equity 9.37 percent [2]. The sources do not split the return by business, but the asset base is the total of plants, machines and other assets the company owns, and it explains much of the pattern. Fixed assets rose from ₹12,599 crore in March 2022 to ₹22,019 crore in March 2026, a rise of about 75 percent [1], while net profit rose by about 52 percent over the same four years [1]. The asset base has grown faster than profit, so the return has stayed in the range of 9 to 11 percent for five years. For an owner, the practical reading is that the company is getting bigger faster than it is getting more efficient with the money it has.

The balance sheet is the company's best feature. Borrowings were ₹2,820 crore in March 2026 against shareholders' funds of about ₹21,788 crore, which gives a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.13. That ratio is borrowings divided by shareholders' money [1]. Cash from operations has covered reported profit comfortably: over FY24 to FY26 the company generated ₹6,416 crore of operating cash for ₹4,049 crore of profit, a ratio of 1.58 [1]. The tables in 'The numbers, in plain words' show these figures in full, and the section 'Peer benchmarking' shows how the return compares with the other large branded consumer companies.

Ownership is stable but has been shifting. The Tata group's holding company, Tata Sons, holds 28.68 percent, and the promoter group holds 33.83 percent in total, unchanged from March to June 2026 [10]. No shares are pledged as security for loans, with the pledge recorded at 0.0 percent [10]. Foreign institutional holding fell from 20.79 to 20.08 percent in the quarter [10] [12], while domestic institutions, led by mutual funds and insurers, rose to 25.01 percent [13]. The pledge figure is the one thing in the ownership picture that a reader should check each quarter, because it can change quickly if a promoter borrows against shares.

Credit agencies are also positive about the company's ability to pay its debts. ICRA reaffirmed the company's long-term and short-term bank facilities and its commercial paper programme after an assessment dated April 27, 2026. The source describes these as high-tier ratings but does not print the grade, so this report does not name one [11]. A high-tier rating is the agency's view that the company can meet its obligations, but it is not a view on whether the company earns a good return on its capital. That gap is the central tension in this dossier.

Layer 1 disposition: the hard gates and the verdict

Layer 1 is the first of the three underwriting layers, and every gate must pass. The earlier sections 'The numbers, in plain words' and 'What the price is doing' worked through the financial gates and the technical tests. The table sets the five gates side by side and shows the disposition. Asymmetry is the last gate, and a failure is recorded as FAIL_ASYMMETRY, which means the stock failed the reward-to-risk test. It is the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss from the entry price, and the rulebook requires a verified target before the ratio can be used. A measured move is a target found by adding the height of a completed base to the point where the price breaks out above it. The momentum standard asks whether the price is in an uptrend: Core Alpha accepts either a base breakout or a Stage 2 uptrend. This stock's return on capital is 9.24 percent [1], about half the 18 percent Core expectation, so the bucket does not help it pass. How it was calculated

The stop is 945 minus 17.97, which is 927.03. The risk is 958.8 minus 927.03, which is 31.77. The minimum target is today's price plus three times the risk, which is 958.8 + (3 × 31.77) = 1,054.1.

Dividing 1,054 by 19.52 gives about 54 times, which is the three-year median P/E that would be needed for the target to clear the floor. Today's price is about 49 times the same forward earnings (958.8 ÷ 19.52 = 49.1), so the target would need a higher multiple than the stock trades on now. The consensus median target of 1,350 rupees from the same aggregator [16] is not one of the two accepted anchors, and it is not used. Its reward-to-risk ratio would be about 12.3 to 1, which is why the arithmetic looks favourable: (1,350 − 958.8) ÷ 31.77 = 12.3. On 19.52 rupees of forward earnings, 1,350 rupees would also equal a P/E of about 69. If the ledger's swing low or ATR changed, the stop and therefore the floor target would move with it. The outcome would still hinge on a verified anchor, so the gate result would not change.

What could happen next

The outcome depends on three things. The first is whether the return on capital can rise toward 15 percent without new capital being poured in, which is the question of operating efficiency. The second is whether the price can build a base and climb back above its averages, which is the question of market sentiment. The third is whether analysts' earnings forecasts hold, which is the question of the business's near-term trajectory. The sources do not give a probability for any of these, so the cases below are described without weighting.

In the upside case, the company keeps growing its faster brands and the margin keeps widening. Management said it expects double-digit growth in the coming year, and the June quarter's 12 percent revenue growth and 19 percent EBITDA growth were in line with that [5] [6]. Consensus expects earnings per share to rise from 15.78 rupees in FY26 to 19.52 rupees in FY27, a rise of about 24 percent [16]. If that happens, the return on capital moves up with profit, and the price could rebuild its averages. The signal to watch is the price closing back above the 200-day average at 1,107.73 with the 50-day average turning up.

In the base case, growth continues at the recent pace, the margin holds around 14 percent, and the return on capital stays in the range of 9 to 11 percent. The company stays a large, cash-generative consumer business that the market prices at a premium to its return. The price drifts sideways or lower until it forms a base. The rulebook would keep the file archived in this case, because neither the return test nor the trend test is met.

In the downside case, the price breaks below the 52-week low of 945. The cost of tea, salt inputs or packaging rises faster than prices, or the cafés keep closing, and the margin falls back toward the low end of its range. The stock then sits well below both averages, and the 50-day average keeps falling. The company's debt is still modest, so it would not be a balance-sheet event. It would be a valuation event, because a P/E of 57 rests on expectations that the return on capital would rise, and those expectations would be tested first.

Fatal flaws and the adversarial case

The rulebook asks for an honest challenge to the verdict. A verdict of REJECTED can be wrong in one of two ways: the company could be a better business than the return figure suggests, or the price could be closer to a base than the averages show. The three scenarios below test both possibilities. Each is a way the archive decision could be shown to be mistaken, and each is checked against the sources.

The first scenario is that the return figure understates the business. The consolidated ROCE of 9.24 percent includes the full cost of the company's acquisitions and new capacity, some of which may not yet earn a return if it was bought late in the period. The sources do not give acquisition dates, so this is a hypothesis, not a finding. If the acquired businesses reach their planned profit, the return would rise without any change in operating skill. This is a fair point, and the rulebook cannot see it. The response is that the test measures what has been earned, not what might be earned, and the standalone figure of 11.9 percent is also below the floor [2]. The point would need to be proved in reported profit before the file is reopened.

The second scenario is that the forward earnings are too high. Consensus expects 19.52 rupees per share in FY27, which is 24 percent above the FY26 figure [16]. At today's price, that is about 49 times forward earnings. If the earnings growth falls short, the premium would look even harder to justify. The response is that the rulebook does not rely on forecasts for the gate. It relies on the reported return, which is already below the floor, so the forecast cannot rescue the verdict.

The third scenario is that the price is already forming a base and the trend test is about to pass. The price is 0.77 ATR above its 63-session low, and the volume has dried up, which can come before a base but does not prove one. If the price holds above 945 and the averages flatten, the trend test might pass in a few weeks. The response is that the rulebook needs a close above falling averages, not a flat price near a low, and the averages are still falling. The trigger for this is listed in 'What we would do, and when'.

The one flaw that cannot be answered with the current sources is the target. The rulebook needs a verified target and none exists. A reader who believes the stock is cheap on the consensus target of 1,350 rupees would be relying on a number the rulebook does not accept [16]. That is the most likely way to be wrong about this stock, and it is why the verdict is archive rather than hold for later.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Every figure in this dossier is built from reported numbers, and several could move. The first source of error is the definition of profit. Net profit for the June 2026 quarter is ₹427 crore in the BSE-based summary, which shows an increase of 28.8 percent [3], and a call summary gives a 29 percent rise in net profit, which matches the BSE-based figure [6]. A third summary gives 444 crore [4]. The difference is probably a definition, such as profit before minority interests, but the sources do not explain it. This report uses 427 crore, which matches the BSE-based figure and the management figure.

The second source of error is the cash-flow column. The Screener table gives cash from operations of ₹2,422 crore for FY26, while the StockAnalysis cash-flow page prints ₹2,057 crore in the column labelled FY 2026 and ₹24,219 million (₹2,422 crore) in its trailing column [25]. The two sources disagree about which year the figure belongs to. The cash test passes on either reading, so the verdict does not depend on it.

The third source of error is the three-year profit growth. Screener quotes 13 percent. The profit figures printed in the same table give 5.4 percent a year between FY23 and FY26. The gap is large enough to matter for a growth argument, and this report uses the recomputed figure, which is the more conservative one. The fourth is the shareholding. Trendlyne and Tickjournal report foreign institutional holding at 20.08 percent, while Anand Rathi reports 9.84 percent for the same quarter [14]. The gap looks like a difference in which investor categories are counted as foreign. The promoter figure of 33.83 percent is the same in every source, so the pledge conclusion is not affected.

The fifth source of error is the EPS (earnings per share) basis. The aggregator gives FY26 EPS of 15.78 rupees, while StockAnalysis gives 15.58 rupees [16] [24]. The forward figure is used only as a reference, not in any gate, so the effect is small. The sixth is the valuation anchor. The three-year median P/E is not published in any source we could read, so the asymmetry test cannot be closed. If a reliable median becomes available, the test could be re-run, and it might change the outcome of that single gate. It would not change the return or trend results, which are sufficient for the rejection on their own.

Some source text is weaker than it looks. Several transcript sites are automated summaries, and one prints a different annual figure from another. The company's own website and the CARE rating release could not be read in this run, because the company site failed its certificate check and the rating page was rate-limited. Those two gaps are listed in the run notes. The verdict rests on sources that could be read.

What we would do, and when

The archive decision is not a forecast that the stock will fall. It is a statement that the rules are not met today. The file stays archived, with a watch on the triggers below, until one of them is met or a new filing changes the numbers. The triggers below are the operating guide. Each one names a condition, the value that would prompt a review, and the gate it would change. The verdict moves from REJECTED only when all three failed gates pass together: the return floor, the technical posture and a verified asymmetry target. Any single trigger only reopens that one check.

Reopen the file if the consolidated return on capital employed is 15.0 or higher on two consecutive reported quarters, because that would clear the financial return gate. The current figure is 9.24.

Reopen the technical check if the ledger shows a Stage 2 label, meaning the close is above both averages with the 50-day average rising. Today the close is 958.8, below the 50-day average of 1,024.1 and the 200-day average of 1,107.73, and the 50-day average is falling.

Reopen the asymmetry check if a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio can be verified from a primary source, and if the resulting target, at forward earnings of 19.52 rupees, is at least about 1,054 rupees. A target of that size would clear the 3.0 floor at today's stop, and the stop itself moves with the ledger.

Three filings matter for the next review. The September 2026 quarter results will show whether the margin holds above 13 percent and whether the return on capital moves. The next shareholding pattern will show whether the promoter pledge stays at zero and whether foreign holding keeps falling. The next ICRA or CARE review will show whether the high-tier ratings are maintained. Each filing can reopen or close the file, so the file stays archived with a watch until one of them lands. Layer 2 and Layer 3 will be run only once Layer 1 passes.

Takeaway. The company is financially sound but earns too little on its capital, and its price is in a downtrend. Those two facts, not its debt or cash, decide the archive verdict. Each trigger reopens only its own check. The verdict changes only when the return floor, the technical posture and a verified asymmetry target all pass.
