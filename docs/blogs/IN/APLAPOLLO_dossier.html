1. What the company does

APL Apollo Tubes Limited, based in Noida, makes hollow steel sections: tubes whose cross-section is square, rectangular or round. Think of them as the steel version of timber posts and beams. Builders weld or bolt them into frames for homes, factories, warehouses, hospitals, airports and railway stations. The company says it runs 11 factories with a total capacity of 5 million tonnes a year, makes more than 5,000 product varieties, and reaches buyers through more than 800 distributors in over 300 towns[2]. Ten of the factories are in India and one is in the United Arab Emirates[12]. Structural steel tubes and related products supply 99.6 percent of its turnover[12].

The people who pay are contractors, fabricators and home builders, who buy through dealers. The annual report counts over 800 distributors and dealers, more than 50,000 retailers and more than 200,000 fabricators[11]. The company's own split of revenue for the first half of the year to March 2027 shows what the tubes are used for: housing 64 percent, commercial buildings 19 percent, infrastructure 13 percent and others 4 percent[3]. Because the end use is construction, sales follow how many buildings are being built and how confident builders feel when steel prices move.

The business is a conversion business. The company buys hot-rolled steel coil, which is flat steel rolled up like a reel of paper, cuts it into strips, forms it into tubes and welds the seam. It sells the tubes at a price that follows the steel price, with a short lag. What is left after the steel and the running costs is the profit, so the number that matters most is profit per tonne. The EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is operating profit before those four items, and the company quotes it per tonne. In the September 2026 quarter that figure was ₹5,999 per tonne, up 15 percent from a year earlier[2].

The company sells under four labels. The APL Apollo brand is the premium line and made 774,751 of the 963,143 tonnes sold in the September 2026 quarter[25][2]. Roofing products added 135,255 tonnes. SG Premium is a value brand launched to fight cheap pipes made by small mills from lower-grade steel, which the annual report calls secondary Patra pipes[11]; it sold 26,910 tonnes. The UAE plant sold 26,227 tonnes[3]. Management says value-added products are now about 65 percent of its mix, and it wants that to reach 75 to 80 percent once capacity grows to 8 million tonnes[5].

The last full year was strong. In the year to March 2026 the company sold 3.49 million tonnes, up 11 percent, earned revenue of ₹23,079 crore and a net profit of ₹1,203 crore[11][1]. The first half of the year to March 2027 was uneven. Volume rose only 3.6 percent to 1,707,966 tonnes, because the war in West Asia choked the UAE business and an energy shortage cut output in India, while profit per tonne kept rising[3][5]. The September quarter then set records for volume, operating profit and net profit[2].

Ownership is stable. The promoters hold 28.18 percent, mostly through S Gupta Holding Private Limited, and none of their shares is pledged to lenders[14][29]. Foreign portfolio funds hold 34.15 percent and Indian institutions 20.82 percent, so most of the register is professional money that will sell quickly if the story changes[1].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a profit measure that ignores borrowing costs, tax and the wearing out of equipment.
  • ROCE stands for return on capital employed, the yearly profit earned on the money tied up in the business.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share, the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • free cash flow is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment; FCF is the short form.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself collects before spending on plants or paying financiers.
  • operating margin is the share of sales left as profit after the costs of running the business, before interest and tax.
  • debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' money; below 0.7 is comfortable.
  • net debt is borrowings minus the cash held; a negative figure means net cash.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share, the years of current profit you pay for.
  • working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company owes suppliers.
  • capital expenditure is spending on plants, machines and buildings.
  • guidance is a forecast the company itself publishes for a coming period.
  • consensus estimate is the average of the profit forecasts that analysts publish.
  • buyback is a company buying its own shares back, which shrinks the share count.
  • RSI stands for relative strength index, a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has moved; below 30 is oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR stands for average true range, the typical daily price swing in rupees, used to measure distance from a level.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 trading days; the 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the same idea over 50 and 200 days.
  • Stage 2 is the Weinstein stage of a confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 a confirmed downtrend; a base is a flat stretch of trading before a move, its pivot is the top of that stretch, a breakout is a move above it on heavy volume, and a retest is the pullback that checks the level from above.
  • A stop loss is the price at which we agree the thesis is wrong and sell; a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better; the entry zone is the band of prices where we would buy.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the possible gain divided by the possible loss: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). The next chapter uses these terms to judge the business.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A company is strong when it keeps earning more than the cost of the money it uses, year after year, against rivals who would like those profits. For APL Apollo the evidence has three layers: its share of the market, its power to set prices, and the way it runs its cash. Each layer has a weak spot, and the verdict depends on how much weight each deserves.

Where the strength comes from

CRISIL says APL Apollo has the highest share, about 55 to 60 percent, of the structural steel tube segment in India, and ICRA calls the group the largest maker of electric-resistance-welded (ERW) pipe in the country[16][15]. These are tubes formed from steel strip and welded along the seam. The share matters because it buys scale in steel purchasing and a distribution network that smaller makers cannot copy quickly.

The second strength is the brand. On its May 2025 call the company said that even in the most price-driven part of its range, the general category, it sold at a premium of at least 5 percent over its nearest competitor[8]. A brokerage note from January 2026 records management saying that raw-material price rises are fully passed on to customers, and that moves of up to 10 percent in a quarter pass through without hurting demand or margins[9]. If that holds, a rise or fall in steel is mostly a pricing event rather than a profit event.

The third strength is cash discipline. The company sells for cash through dealers, so customers' unpaid bills equal about 6 days of sales, stock is 27 days, and what it owes suppliers is 44 days, which leaves a cash conversion cycle of minus 12 days for the year to March 2026[1]. In plain terms, suppliers fund the factory. The company reported zero days of net working capital for the first half of the year to March 2027[3]. That is rare among steel processors and it explains how a business with 8 percent operating margins reaches a ROCE above 30 percent.

What the record profit per tonne does and does not prove

Profit per tonne rose from ₹3,796 in the year to March 2025 to ₹5,161 in the year to March 2026 and to ₹5,999 in the September 2026 quarter[19][3]. Management calls ₹6,000 sustainable because it comes from the premium brand and roofing, which together make 92 to 93 percent of volume, and it wants two quarters in a row before it believes the number itself[4]. That caution is fair.

The jump arrived while steel was getting dearer. The Ministry of Steel's data show hot-rolled coil, the raw material, at ₹70,448 per tonne in August 2026, ₹76,085 in September and ₹77,620 in October, including tax, which is 31.6 percent above a year earlier[27]. The Hindu BusinessLine reported the third monthly price rise in a row[28]. When steel rises, a tube maker's unsold stock gains value, and when steel falls the same stock loses value. An analyst asked on the Q2 call whether inventory gains lifted the quarter, and the answer in the transcript is not readable[4]. So we cannot say how much of ₹5,999 is repeatable and how much is a steel-price tailwind.

The gap between the premium brand and cheap secondary pipes is also unusually wide. Nuvama puts the spread between hot-rolled coil and Patra pipe prices at ₹14,007 per tonne, against ₹11,751 on a three-month average[22]. A wide spread lets the premium brand price high, but it also pushes some buyers to the cheap pipes, which is one reason the SG Premium volume fell 29.8 percent[3].

Where the business is weak

The company converts steel. It does not own the steel, so its profit depends on the gap between what it pays for coil and what it charges for tube. In the September 2024 quarter, when steel prices fell sharply, profit per tonne fell to ₹1,821 from ₹4,183 in the June 2024 quarter, and it recovered to ₹4,173 the next quarter; CRISIL attributes the fall to inventory loss[3][16]. The monthly pricing system and tighter inventory control are meant to limit a repeat, but they have only been tested in rising markets since then.

Entry barriers are low. ICRA writes that making this kind of pipe is not capital-intensive, so many small and unorganised makers exist[15]. APL Apollo's defence is the brand and the dealer network, not technology. A brand premium of 5 percent is valuable, and it also shows how little a rival needs to undercut before buyers switch.

The mix of the premium brand is also shifting toward light structures, the thinner and cheaper tubes. On the Q2 call, management said light structures are about 25 percent of the APL Apollo brand mix and that gross profit per tonne fell about ₹500 quarter on quarter for that reason[4]. Finally, the UAE plant, meant to be a high-margin growth leg, ran at 40 percent of capacity in May 2026 and below half in July, so the export story is on hold[7][21].

Balance sheet, ratings and governance

Two rating agencies rate the group AA+. ICRA reaffirmed [ICRA]AA+ (Stable) on 26 February 2026 and put interest cover at about 13.8 times for the nine months to December 2025[15]. CRISIL upgraded the long-term rating to AA+/Stable on 30 September 2025[16][17]. The company ended September 2026 with ₹2,546 crore of cash and bank balances against ₹918 crore of debt, a net cash position of ₹1,628 crore[3]. On the company's presentation basis, debt did rise from ₹452 crore in March 2026 (Screener's balance sheet, which groups items differently, shows ₹498 crore rising to ₹963 crore), and the interest cost for the September quarter was ₹52 crore, up 90 percent from a year earlier[3][25]. Neither number is alarming for a company with this cash, but the direction is worth watching.

On governance, the annual report for 2025-26, which the company filed with the exchanges on 21 August 2026[13], says related-party dealings were in the ordinary course and on arm's-length terms[11]. The auditors changed during the year: Walker Chandiok & Co LLP now audits, and the predecessor, Deloitte Haskins & Sells LLP, gave an unmodified opinion on the previous year's standalone statements[11]. We found no qualification in the audit report. The chairman and managing director is Sanjay Gupta[3] and the promoter group holds 28.18 percent[14], so the business is run close to its owners. That is an advantage while they allocate capital well and a risk if they do not.

What this means for an owner

An owner gets a market leader with a real brand, a net cash balance sheet and returns on capital that few steel processors match. An owner also takes three risks. The first is steel-price risk, because a processor earns the gap between coil and tube. The second is rising interest cost, which rose 90 percent from a year earlier in the September quarter. The third is a record margin that has not yet proved to be a floor. The strengths are visible in the cash flow and the ratings, and the weaknesses are visible in the missed volume promises. The next chapter turns these points into five measurable tests and checks whether management did what it said it would do. The answer decides how much of the company's 2027 and 2028 outlook we can accept.

Estimates are wrong in predictable ways. A good forecast names its weakest assumption, says what evidence would prove it wrong, and says when that evidence arrives. We do that for each of the three estimates in chapters 3 and 5. We then run the valuation stress tests that changed our verdict, and give the strongest case against our own view.

Weakest point of the FY27 estimate: second-half volume of 2.02 million tonnes

Our FY27 figure of ₹52.0 needs December and March quarters of 1.00 and 1.02 million tonnes. The September quarter was 963,143 tonnes, and the quarter before that was 744,823[3][6]. Management said July volume was up 20 percent from June and that the new Gorakhpur and Siliguri plants would help in the third quarter[5][23]. Both plants have slipped before, and the chairman said in August that the plant would start 'any time in September'[5]. Evidence against us is a December quarter below 950,000 tonnes. We learn it when the Q3 FY27 results come out. A Motilal Oswal note on the Q3 FY26 results is dated 22 January 2026, so late January 2027 is the likely date for the Q3 FY27 results, though none has been announced[9].

Weakest point of the FY28 estimate: profit per tonne of ₹5,900 when steel is 32 percent dearer than a year ago

Our central case keeps profit per tonne at ₹5,900. The Ministry of Steel puts hot-rolled coil 31.6 percent above a year ago[27]. If coil falls 10 percent, the ₹1,675 crore of stock the company held at September 2026 would lose about ₹167 crore of value, which is 29 percent of one quarter's operating profit[3]. The company says its brand lets it pass price moves through[9], and that may be true. The only test since 2024 has come in rising markets, though, and an owner should not assume the first falling market will behave like the rising ones. Evidence against us is a fall in profit per tonne below ₹5,500 in the December or March quarter. We learn it in late January 2027 and in May 2027.

Weakest point of the central value: the multiple of 45.9, which is the principal hinge with margin

The central value is where our reasoning is least secure, because it assumes the market keeps paying today's price-to-earnings figure. The multiple has been falling for two years, from 56.9 at the end of FY24 to 44.7 at the end of FY26, while profit grew, and brokers assume about 35 times forward profit[31][19]. Trailing and forward multiples are different measures, and a history of one does not prove the future of the other. If the multiple settles near 40, the central value is ₹2,344 and there is no reward in the trade at ₹2,172.82. The single tripwire is the same as in chapter 5. After the Q3 FY27 results we recompute reward-to-risk at the order price on all three lenses, and any reading below 2.0 cancels the order. Today the central lens breaks even at 41.5 times profit at ₹2,095. Valuation and margin durability are the two hinges, and either can fail alone.

Stress tests: what the other ways of valuing the shares show

We ran three further tests on our own central value, changing one input at a time and keeping every other input as stated in chapter 5. First, the brokers' multiple of 35 times on our FY28 earnings of ₹58.6 gives ₹2,051. That is 7.4 percent below the close and gives a reward-to-risk of -0.50 at ₹2,172.82, which means the target is below the entry. Second, HDFC Securities' FY28 profit per tonne of ₹5,434 in place of our ₹5,900 gives earnings of ₹53.2 and a value of ₹2,443 at 45.9 times, 10.3 percent above the close and a ratio of 1.10 at ₹2,172.82. Third, the probability-weighted value of ₹2,547 gives 1.53. Only the central case, at 2.11, clears the 2.0 floor, and it does so by 0.11. At ₹2,095 the central, weighted and HDFC-margin cases give 3.56, 2.71 and 2.08. The 35-times case does not clear 2.0 at any price above ₹1,969, which is only 2 percent above the stop. We accept that case as the one the stop is there to handle.

A risk outside the model: West Asia, the UAE and energy supply

The first-half volume miss came from a war that closed the UAE's inbound and outbound shipping and from an energy shortage in India that cut output of rust-proof pipes and roofing by 25,000 to 30,000 tonnes[5]. HDFC Securities expects UAE operating profit to halve to about ₹60 to 65 crore in FY27[19]. Another shock would hit volume again, and the company has little control over it. The UAE run rate is 100,000 tonnes a year against capacity heading to 500,000[4]. Management says the two new Dubai mills could earn ₹8,000 to ₹10,000 per tonne[4], and that the UAE could reach ₹5,000 to ₹6,000 per tonne if operations ramp up successfully[5]. We use the lower figure, because the higher one rests on mills that have not yet run. A recovery would add profit, and a second disruption would leave a half-empty plant. Evidence of recovery would be UAE volume above 40,000 tonnes in a quarter.

A risk of cash use and governance

The company holds ₹1,628 crore of net cash. It has not said how it will use the surplus beyond ₹500 to 600 crore a year of capex[7], and the transcript of the Q2 call is partly missing on this point[4]. HDFC Securities expects a higher dividend payout of 30 to 55 percent and possibly a buyback[19]. A poor acquisition or a related-party deal would harm the return on capital. The annual report found no related-party problem and the audit opinion was unmodified[11]. We found no pledge. The promoters' holding slipped from 28.25 to 28.18 percent in the September quarter, which is about 200,000 shares[29].

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule behind it is the asymmetry floor of 2.0 for a Core Alpha name, tested at the price where we would actually buy. The reward-to-risk ratio is (our target minus the entry) divided by (the entry minus the ₹1,927.98 stop). At the ₹2,213.90 close it is 1.67 on the central value of ₹2,690, so we cannot buy at the market. A limit order at ₹2,172.82, the top of the support band, would give 2.11 on the central value. We do not place one there, because the same price gives 1.53 on the probability-weighted value, 1.10 on HDFC's margin and -0.50 at the brokers' multiple. The scoring file asks for a justified target, and our central multiple is the least justified input in the report. A pass of 0.11 on one lens, with three lenses failing, is not enough to put money to work.

The ladder in our scoring file explains the lower order. The ladder measures distance above the nearest verified support, and the nearest support below ₹2,095 is the 200-day average of ₹2,007.37, 1.33 average true range away. That is the reduced-size band, where only a starter limit order is allowed. We therefore place a conditional limit order between ₹2,055.61 and ₹2,095. The lower end is where the 50-day average stood 21 days ago, and the upper end is about the highest price at which the central, weighted and HDFC-margin lenses all clear 2.0 (3.56, 2.71 and 2.08). It is a pullback of about 5 to 7 percent from the close and may never fill. If it does, the share will have slipped below its 50-day average, so it would be a Stage 3 pullback above a rising 200-day average, which our technical rule sends to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, not to rejection.

The size follows the risk-unit rule. We assign TIER_3, our third-highest of four conviction tiers, which allows a loss of 50 basis points (0.5 percent) of the portfolio if the stop is hit and caps a position at 3.5 percent. At ₹2,095 the stop is 7.97 percent below the entry, the raw size would be 6.27 percent, and the cap of 3.5 percent applies. Because the ledger classes the market as a capitulation bounce, our rules start new positions at half size, so the first purchase would be 1.75 percent. Live size today is zero. This is a pipeline status, which means the name sits on our shortlist as a candidate and is not owned, and it displaces no holding.

We considered and rejected two other verdicts. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP fails for the reasons above. REJECTED would need a failed red line, a Layer 2 failure or a central value below the entry, and none applies: Layer 1 passes, Layer 2 scores 3.5 out of 5.0, and the central value is above the close. The stop is a close-below rule. If the share closes below ₹1,927.98 we sell at the next open, so the planned loss of about 8 percent from ₹2,095 is not a hard cap, and a gap down could cost more. The sources give no way to size that gap.

The ledger is frozen at the 27 October close. A later Screener quote shows ₹2,165 at 3:28 pm on 28 October[1]. That is above our order band, so nothing changes. The market-intelligence seed in our watchlist proposed a stop of ₹2,123.23 and a target of ₹2,301.40, a ratio of 2.59. We do not reproduce that figure, because the Core rule puts the stop below the 20-day low less one average true range, and our target is anchored to earnings, not to the 52-week high. If a position is later built and works, the let-winners-run rule applies: no trim because the RSI passes 70 or because the weight crosses 15 percent, and the weight may rise to 22 percent while the stop trails the rising 50-day average.
