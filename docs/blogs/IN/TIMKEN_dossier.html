What the company does

Timken India makes bearings in plants in Jamshedpur and Bharuch, both in India. A bearing holds a rotating shaft in place and lets it spin with little friction, so almost every machine that turns depends on one. The company was set up in 1987 as Tata Timken, a joint venture between Tata Steel and the US-based Timken Company, and began production at Jamshedpur in March 1992 [1]. Tata Steel's stake was bought by Timken in 1999, and the company took its present name in July of that year [1]. Today the Singapore arm of the Timken Company owns 51.05 percent of the shares, so the parent controls the company [4].

The customers fall into a few groups, and the company reports them as separate segments. The mobile segment serves commercial vehicles and tractors, the rail segment serves railway customers, the process segment serves factories and stationary machines, and the distribution segment sells replacement parts. There is also an intercompany segment, which ships parts to other Timken plants, mainly in the United States [1][16]. The company earns money by selling these parts at a margin above what the steel, power and labour cost it, and by keeping its plants full enough to spread fixed costs.

The newest part of the business is the Bharuch plant in Gujarat, a 55,465 square metre facility that makes spherical roller bearings and cylindrical roller bearings [26][27]. Its purpose is to make in India what the company used to import, and to sell to new customers. A second project, a rail component expansion at Jamshedpur, is also under way. On 12 October 2026 the company announced a senior leadership change, with Pramesh Maheshwari named President for India and Southeast Asia [1]. The next scheduled result is for the quarter and half year to 30 September 2026, due on 29 October 2026 [1]. The company's filing index lists its results and disclosures [11][12].

The company's own plain-language summary of its business is short: it makes engineered bearings and mechanical power transmission products for many sectors, and it also provides maintenance and refurbishment services [2]. We accept that description. What we did not find is a published order book in rupees, so any statement about how many months of work the company has signed up is not verifiable from the sources in this run.

Glossary of the terms in this report

The terms below appear in later chapters. ROCE means return on capital employed, the profit a business earns on all the money it uses to run it. ATR stands for average true range, the typical size of one day's price move over the last 14 sessions, and we use it to measure how far a price sits from a support level. RSI stands for relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how quickly the price has moved; a reading below 30 is called oversold. EPS stands for earnings per share, which is profit divided by the number of shares. P/E is the price divided by one year of earnings per share, so a P/E of 50 means buyers pay Rs 50 for each Rs 1 of yearly profit. CFO stands for cash from operations, the cash a business collects from customers after paying its running costs.

Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' net worth, which is the part of the company that belongs to shareholders after debts are subtracted. A base is a sideways period in which a stock rests after a fall, and a breakout is a move above the top of that base on rising trading volume. Stage 2 is the rising phase of a stock's cycle, when the price stays above a rising long-term average, and Stage 4 is the falling phase, when the price stays below a falling long-term average. Gross margin is the share of sales left after paying for materials and direct production costs. Operating margin, shortened here to OPM, is the share of sales left after running costs but before depreciation, interest and tax.

Our rulebook is the written underwriting rulebook this report follows. Asymmetry is the upside to a verified target divided by the downside to a stop, and our rulebook requires a minimum of 3.0 for a Core holding and 3.5 for a Tactical holding. An entry zone is the price range in which a buyer would start to buy, and a measured move is a target found by adding the height of a base to its breakout price. The debtor days figure is the average number of days a company waits to be paid by its customers.

Why the business is strong or weak

A strong business earns a high return on the money tied up in it, keeps its debt low, turns its profit into cash, and faces competitors that cannot easily take its customers. Timken India meets the first two tests with room to spare. The evidence is thin on competitive position and on the timing of cash receipts (debtor days), although the cash conversion itself is solid. The company's return on capital employed is 19 percent on the consolidated figures for March 2026, against a floor of 15 percent in our gate [1]. Its net worth is large compared with its borrowings, which were only Rs 15 crore at March 2026 [1]. These are real strengths and we do not want to understate them.

The weaker evidence concerns growth and the timing of cash receipts. Sales grew 8.8 percent in the consolidated FY26 figures, but consolidated profit fell from Rs 462 crore to Rs 415 crore over the same year [1]. On the standalone series, net profit fell 11 percent in FY26, and the three-year profit growth rate is only 0.6 percent a year [3]. The company also waits longer to be paid. Debtor days rose from 81 in March 2025 to 85 in March 2026 [1]. Each extra day is cash tied up in customers' hands rather than in the company's bank account.

Competitive position is the hardest claim to test from the sources. The company's own profile describes it as a maker of anti-friction bearings and power transmission products [2], and its parent reports to investors as a global bearings maker [35]. The commercial case rests on two things we can check. First, the rail and commercial vehicle customers keep buying Timken parts. Second, the Bharuch plant lets the company supply rail bearings made in India rather than imported. The first point is unverified in detail because the company does not publish order numbers: an analyst summary of the third-quarter call says that management does not disclose order book figures [22].

Ownership and the promoter's position

Promoter holding is the stake held by the founding group or the controlling shareholder. In Timken India the controlling shareholder is the parent. The company's promoter holding has fallen in steps, from 75.00 percent in March 2017 to 57.70 percent in March 2024 and 51.05 percent from March 2025 onwards [1]. Two aggregators show the same 51.05 percent holding as of June 2026 [9][10]. The shareholding tables do not say why the parent sold, and we did not find a reason in the pages we read. What they do show is that the pledge figure is zero: none of the promoter's shares are pledged as security for loans, which is the usual warning sign in India [4].

Institutions have filled part of the gap. Mutual funds hold 26.59 percent and foreign portfolio investors hold 7.32 percent as of June 2026 [4]. The shareholder base is therefore now more domestic and fund-driven than before. That matters for the chart, because funds tend to sell in the same direction when a stock falls below its moving averages, so a weak chart can feed on itself.

The business is sound on returns and debt, weaker on cash conversion and on the order picture, and expensive against its peers. Chapter 3 tests these views against five years of numbers.

The momentum test

The momentum gate is a separate test in our rulebook that asks whether a stock is either already in a strong uptrend or is starting one. It has two routes, and a stock must pass one of them. The result is computed by code from the frozen ledger snapshot, so no judgment is involved. The gate returned NO_MOMENTUM, which means the stock fails both routes and receives no allocation.

The first route, called Test A, failed on two conditions. The structure test failed because the price is below the 50-day average and the 50-day average is below the 200-day average, which is the wrong order for an uptrend. The relative strength test also failed. Relative strength compares the stock with the Nifty 50 over 63 sessions, and Timken India has lagged by 0.19 percentage points. The second route, Test B, passed four of its five conditions, but failed on volume. The volume multiple is 0.55 times, while the test needs at least 1.0 times on expansion. The stock is quiet, and the test wants a sign of buying interest.

The target anchor and the asymmetry test

Asymmetry compares the upside to a verified target with the downside to a verified stop. Our rulebook sets the minimum at 3.0 for a Core holding and 3.5 for a Tactical holding. The target must be anchored, which means it has to come from forward earnings multiplied by a three-year median P/E, or from a measured move on a verified base. Neither anchor could be verified, so the asymmetry gate fails and the ratio is marked N/A.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report has a weak point, and the decision is only as good as the weakest of them. The estimate that matters most is the rejection itself, which rests on the chart and on the absence of a verified target. A reader should know which of these would change the answer, and when that information will be available.

The weakest assumption

The weakest assumption is that the chart will stay in its present downtrend. The technical test is computed from closing prices, and a single strong session can change the momentum readings within days. The momentum gate also relies on volume, and the volume multiple is currently 0.55 times, which is a quiet market. A sudden rise on heavy trading would change the volume test first, and the structure test second, so the rejection could reverse quickly once buyers return.

The second weakest assumption is the absence of a target. Our conclusion relies on the fact that the three-year median P/E and forward EPS were not published on the sources we could read. If a primary filing or a credible data provider later publishes both, the target could be computed, and the asymmetry gate could pass. That outcome is possible but is not the base case, because the stock would still need to clear the technical gate.

Run notes and gate exceptions

The citation gate allows a stale date only as a disclosed exception, and this run has several. The exceptions are summarised here, with the reason each cannot be cured now, how it lowers confidence, and what would cure it.

Stale as-of dates. The checker flags every found record whose as-of date is more than 60 days before 12 October 2026. These are the annual FY26 figures (as of March 2026), the June 2026 quarter and shareholding figures, the May 2026 call target, the August 2026 broker and results items, and the May 2026 tender report. The checker's line reads "EXCEPTION AS-OF" with the record key and the as-of date. They cannot be cured now because the newer periods are not yet reported; the October 2026 results will refresh the quarterly figures. Confidence is lower on the FY26 annual figures than on a current quote, but the direction of the numbers agrees across two aggregators.

The FY24 consolidated cash line. The consolidated three-year cash test needs the March 2024 consolidated cash from operations. The pages we could read show two years only, and the primary results PDFs were compressed binary files that the tool could not read as text. We used the standalone three-year series from Trendlyne instead and disclosed the basis. The result is a ratio of 0.94 against a floor of 0.70, so the conclusion does not depend on the basis, but the figure is aggregator-sourced rather than primary-sourced. It would be cured by reading the FY25 annual report's comparative consolidated cash line, which we could not do in this run.

Three-year median P/E and forward EPS. Both are not published on any page we could reach, and each was searched with three distinct queries. The target anchor therefore cannot be formed, and the asymmetry gate fails. This is a gate failure rather than an exception, but it lowers confidence in any upside view. It would be cured by a published consensus EPS series or by a three-year P/E history from a primary or licensed source.

Order book and credit rating. The company does not publish an order book, and no credit rating appears on the Trendlyne or MarketScreener pages [6][33], after four searches for the rating and three for the order book. These are recorded as not found. They are not gates in our rulebook, but they remove two useful cross-checks. They would be cured by the rating rationale if the company holds a rated facility, and by an order disclosure in the investor presentation.

Source quality. One fetched page for the smart-investing P/E history belonged to a different company and was rejected. The Trendlyne consensus target and the broker's Rs 3,812 target are third-party views and are not used to set any number in the decision. The Bharuch utilisation rate and the rail tender are reported as company plans or press reports, not as confirmed results.

With these exceptions disclosed, the verdict stands on the chart and the missing target, and the financial gates are the least uncertain part of the analysis.
