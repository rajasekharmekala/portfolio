1. What the company does

A bearing is a ring of steel with small rolling parts inside, and it lets a shaft turn with little friction. Timken India makes engineered bearings, and its best known product is the tapered roller bearing. A tapered roller bearing can carry weight from the side and from above at the same time, which is why it sits inside railway wagon axles, truck and tractor hubs, mining machines and the gearboxes of steel mills and wind turbines[8][9]. The company was set up in 1987 as Tata Timken and is now a subsidiary of the US Timken group: Timken Singapore Pte Ltd holds 51.05% of the shares[21][24].

Five groups of customers pay the company. In the June 2026 quarter, revenue was ₹929 crore. Rail customers (wagon, coach and locomotive makers) paid about ₹200 crore, or 22%. Mobile customers (commercial vehicles, tractors and off-highway machines) paid about ₹184 crore. Distribution, which sells to repair shops and traders, brought ₹154 crore. Process industries (steel, cement, wind, mining) brought ₹186 crore. Exports, which are mostly sales to other Timken group companies, brought about ₹200 crore[14]. For the full year to March 2026, India supplied ₹27,405 million of the ₹34,193 million of revenue, so about four rupees in five come from Indian customers[6].

The company makes money in two ways. It manufactures bearings in its own plants, and it also buys finished bearings from group companies and sells them in India. Management says the split between made and traded goods is about 75 to 25[15]. Purchases of goods from Timken group companies came to ₹11,909 million in FY26, which is 34.8% of revenue (our sum of the six related-party rows)[7]. The company also pays a royalty to The Timken Company for the brand and technology, about 2.5% of revenue, or ₹843.56 million in FY26[7]. In the June 2026 quarter the company kept 39.9% of each rupee of sales after paying for materials, a measure called gross margin, which was 100 basis points better than a year earlier[15].

Timing matters. Railway and wagon builders finish their orders in the last quarter of the financial year, so January to March is always the biggest quarter: it brought more than ₹1,000 crore in FY26[13]. Quarter by quarter, profit swings widely around the year, as the quarterly table in Chapter 3 shows. One customer group alone paid ₹6,251 million, or 18.3% of FY26 revenue[6], so the loss of that customer would be felt.

The company is in the middle of a large building programme. A new plant at Bharuch in Gujarat, which makes spherical roller bearings and cylindrical roller bearings, cost about ₹750 crore and began commercial production in the second quarter of FY26[3][17]. A ₹120 crore rail component line at Jamshedpur is due in the third quarter of FY27, and a ₹38 crore plain-bearing line at Bharuch is due in the second quarter of FY27[3]. In November 2025 it also bought Timken GGB Technology Private Limited, a group company, for ₹1,288 million in cash[3][7]. These projects explain why profit has lagged revenue, because the new plants carry costs before they carry sales.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Tapered roller bearing: a bearing whose rolling parts are cone shaped, so it carries sideways and downward loads together.
  • Spherical roller bearing (SRB) and cylindrical roller bearing (CRB): two other heavy-duty bearing types that the new Bharuch plant makes.
  • Front-end: the company's own word for a customer group, such as rail, mobile, distribution, process and exports.
  • OEM: original equipment manufacturer, a company that builds trucks, wagons or machines and fits bearings as it builds.
  • PPAP: production part approval process, the customer's sign-off before a new bearing can be sold in volume.
  • 80/20: a Timken group method that ranks customers and products so the best 20% get most of the effort.
  • Crore, million: one crore is 10 million; ₹100 crore is ₹1,000 million.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A good bearing business is hard to enter for three reasons. A bearing in a wagon or a mine must last for years, so buyers prefer a name that has done it before. A new bearing must pass the customer's production part approval process before it can be sold in volume. And the maker needs steel, heat treatment and grinding skill that takes years to build. Timken India has all three, backed by the global research of the Timken group[9][14]. The CMD says the company leads in rail freight bearings[15], and it has just won a BIS quality mark for its cylindrical and tapered rollers, which supports sales to Indian buyers who now insist on local standards[13].

Pricing power is the best test of such a moat, and the evidence is mixed. Steel prices rose by about ₹5,000 a tonne between January and April 2026. In heavy trucks and tractors, management says it has passed most of that on, and the gross margin of 39.9% in the June 2026 quarter was 100 basis points above a year earlier[15]. In rail and public-sector buying, contracts are fixed or run on annual rate contracts, so higher costs can be passed on only when a new contract is signed[15]. A moat that cannot reprice a quarter of the business quickly is a narrower moat than the margin history suggests.

The weaknesses are plain in the filings. Low-cost Asian makers press on price, and the company itself lists counterfeit bearings and global price pressure among its threats[9]. The company depends on its parent: 35% of revenue is matched by purchases of goods from group companies, including ₹4,795 million from Timken Wuxi in China, up from ₹3,728 million a year earlier[7]. Exports to the United States fell 15% in FY26 while other export markets rose 57%, so the export story is a swap of one market for others rather than broad growth[6].

Growth has been slow for a company priced as a compounder. Revenue rose 6.8% a year over the three years to FY26, and profit after tax was almost flat, up only 0.6% a year[1]. India's bearing market itself is growing at over 9% a year and was worth about USD 2.9 billion in FY26[8], so the company has grown more slowly than its market over the last three years even though FY26 was a record revenue year[9].

Cycles and seasons

Rail, commercial vehicles, steel and cement all move with government and industrial spending. Management itself calls the bearing business cyclical, and says rail will never be a hockey stick and should grow only in the high single digits[13][18]. In FY26 coach output reached a record 9,900 against a plan of 8,700, and commercial vehicle sales passed one million units, yet Timken's rail revenue grew only about 2% in the June 2026 quarter[8][14]. The consequence is that a good year for the customer industry does not automatically become a good year for Timken.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management made specific promises over the last year and a half, and the record shows mostly on-time projects with late ramp-ups. The Bharuch plant was promised for the first quarter of FY26 in the FY25 annual report[10], and the FY26 report says commercial production began in the second quarter[3]. The first cylindrical line was capitalised in late June 2025 and invoicing started in July[18]. Against a first-quarter promise that is a slip of a few weeks, small for a plant of this size.

The utilisation promise is the weak spot. In August 2025 the CMD said the plant would exit FY26 near 45% utilisation, and the company would "stay course"[18]. In February 2026 the CFO said utilisation was about 30% and would be a little above 50% by the June 2026 quarter[17]. In August 2026 he said the spherical line had run at 40% to 45% and should reach 70% in August to September, with the cylindrical line lower[14]. Utilisation slid by about one to two quarters, and each new date is credible but unproven.

Capital allocation is the second test. FY25's final dividend was ₹36 a share, far above the usual ₹2.50, and it was paid in FY26 as ₹2,707.87 million[10][4]. The parent received ₹1,382.40 million of it[7]. In the same year the company paid ₹1,288 million to buy a group company, cut the FY26 dividend back to ₹2.50, and saw its cash and investments fall from ₹5,114 million to ₹2,682 million[3][2]. Minority holders received their share, but cash left the company at the moment it was funding the largest capital programme in its history, and the acquisition was a purchase from the controlling shareholder's group.

The strongest bear case has four parts. Trigger 2, the order: no order is placed until a complete bar after the results shows that the momentum gate no longer returns NO_MOMENTUM, meaning Test A, B or C passes on that bar. Test C needs all four of its conditions: the close above the 63-day low with the 20-day swing low holding (C1); a close at or above the 20-day average together with a positive 28-day relative strength or a positive two-week gain (C2); support within 2.0 ATR (C3); and RSI at or above 32 (C4). A one-day bounce, or RSI above 32 on its own, is not enough. On the qualifying bar we recompute support, stop and reward-to-risk and place a limit order for half the planned size (1.75% of the portfolio) only if reward-to-risk is still 2.0 or better. The zone, stop and targets are then reset to that bar and may differ from today's. On today's levels there is no chase above ₹2,855.

Trigger 3, upgrade: when Test C passes on all four conditions as set out in Trigger 2 and the September quarter meets the tests in Trigger 1, the verdict moves to ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP.

Trigger 4, stop: a close below ₹2,597.92 voids the order and tells a holder to sell, on its own, without waiting for any other test.

Trigger 5, reject: a 200-day average that falls below 3,247.60 while the close is under a falling 50-day average with Test C failing, or a promoter pledge of 20% or more, turns the verdict to REJECTED.
