1. What the company does

Aeroflex Industries Limited was incorporated on 19 October 1993 and is based near Taloja, Navi Mumbai[2]. It makes flexible pipes of stainless steel that carry liquids, gases and powders through plants and buildings and bend where a rigid pipe would crack. It began as Suyog Intermediates, became a public company in 2006, and has been owned since 2018 by a listed parent, now Aeroflex Enterprises Limited and formerly Sat Industries Limited[3]. Asad Daud is chairman and managing director.

The product list has widened in three steps. The core is the corrugated hose, with or without a braid of steel wire. Next is the assembly, a hose cut to length with end fittings welded on, which sells at a higher price and margin: management says 16 to 20 percent for a bare hose and 22 to 26 percent for an assembly[4]. The newest step, since late 2025, is the liquid-cooling skid, a ready-built frame of pipes, hoses and joints that moves coolant between chillers and server racks[3].

In FY26, hoses were about 43 percent of consolidated sales, assemblies and other products about 52 percent and skids about 5 percent, with 617 skids worth ₹21.2 crore sold in four months[10][5]. In the June 2026 quarter skids sold 1,040 units for ₹32.4 crore, 22 percent of the quarter's ₹145.38 crore of sales; the call rounds this to 23 percent[3][4]. Exports were 69 percent of FY26 sales. The Americas took 59 percent of exports, Europe 27 percent and Asia 11 percent, and the United States alone 49 percent of exports, which is about 34 percent of total sales, our arithmetic. In the June quarter exports rose 43 percent but fell to 58 percent of sales, as skids sell only at home.

Management names Senior and Parker as skid rivals[4] and calls Aeroflex the only India-based maker of its scale, a claim we did not verify[6]. The company has plants at Taloja, Palava and Chakan, about 760 staff and more than 3,300 product variants[3]. The table sets out each line.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has moved. ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is operating profit before depreciation. EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares. ROCE (return on capital employed) is operating profit as a share of the money tied up in the business.
  • Free cash flow, or FCF (free cash flow), is cash from operations less spending on plant. Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills, less what the company owes suppliers.
  • Operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales. Gross margin is sales less the cost of materials, as a share of sales. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by profit per share. A consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow a stock.
  • A base is a flat stretch of trading after a fall where a stock builds a floor, and a pivot is its top. A breakout is a close above the pivot on rising volume, and a retest is a later return to see whether the level holds. Stage 2 is a rising trend, with the price above rising 50-day and 200-day averages. Stage 4 is a falling trend, with the price under a falling 50-day average that is under a falling 200-day average.
  • Guidance is the forecast a company gives of its own results. A limit order is an order to buy at a stated price or lower. A stop loss is a price at which we would sell to cut a loss; it is an exit instruction and not a cap on the loss. The entry zone is the range where a limit order may sit. Asymmetry and reward-to-risk mean the same thing: the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, and the Tactical Momentum floor is 2.5 to 1. A hurdle is a minimum yearly return; the 12 percent used here is this dossier's own judgment. Chapter 2 turns next to why the business is strong or weak.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business rests on steady growth, a rising margin and a clean balance sheet. Total income grew from ₹269.5 crore in FY23 to ₹443.3 crore in FY26, 18.1 percent a year, our arithmetic[3]. The operating margin rose from 20 percent in FY23 to 22.6 percent in FY26 and 23.0 percent in the June 2026 quarter[1][5]. Borrowings were ₹1.07 crore at March 2026 against ₹69.65 crore of cash and bank deposits, and no promoter share is pledged[36].

The case against is just as concrete. The June quarter's 72 percent growth followed a weak year-earlier quarter hit by tariffs, so part of it is a low comparison[8]. One buyer takes all the skids, and the biggest overseas buyer takes about a quarter of sales; the filings do not say whether the two are linked[6]. Profit grew only 5.8 percent in FY26 on 17 percent more sales because depreciation rose from ₹11.27 crore to ₹26.08 crore[5]. Return on capital employed slid from 36 percent in FY22 to 19 percent in FY26[1]. And ₹51 crore of tax and duty demands arrived in March and April 2026[10]. The sections below test each side.

Evidence for the moat: approvals, engineering and a niche

A moat is something that protects profit from rivals, as a moat protects a castle. Here the protection is qualification. Management says it took 10 to 11 months from first talks to the first sale with its skid partner, and that data-centre designs need drawings approved before production starts[4]. Customer audits and supply checks are a must before it supplies high-end industries[6]. In October 2025 the company announced a ₹7.8 crore bellows order, under an existing global agreement, for liquid-cooling products for the subsidiary of a United States company worth more than US$50 billion[24]. It runs an NABL-accredited research laboratory and has 58 products in development[3].

The market is real but modest. The annual report cites studies that value the global stainless-steel flexible hose market at US$2,182 million in 2025, growing 7.2 percent a year, and data-centre liquid cooling at US$5.65 billion in 2024, growing to US$48.4 billion by 2034[10]. We did not test them. Aeroflex's core lines, without skids, grew about 21 percent over the last four quarters, three times the hose-market rate, so the company is taking share or moving to higher-value assemblies[1][5][3].

The limit is that this is an engineering and approvals moat, not a patent or a brand. The managing director said in May 2026 that there is no patent on the skid product[6]. Rivals including Senior and Parker have started skid lines[4]. The machines can be reused for other piping work if skid demand fades, which lowers the cost of being wrong. For an owner the moat is real but narrow, and it protects the older hose lines better than the skid line. Next, the margin record shows how much reaches profit.

Cost pass-through: what the margin record shows

Pass-through is a company's ability to raise prices when its costs rise. The filings we read describe no contractual steel price clause, so the margin is the evidence. Materials net of the change in stock were 56.8 percent of sales in FY26 and 55.5 percent in the June 2026 quarter, our arithmetic, so the gross margin rose from 43.2 to 44.5 percent[5][3]. The operating margin still slipped from 23.86 percent in March to 23.04 percent in June, because of more staff for the skid ramp and the new Chakan plant and higher freight after the West Asia crisis[4]. Over four quarters the margin stayed between 23.0 and 23.9 percent[1]. We read that as moderate evidence of pass-through, tested by tariff and freight shocks, but the sample is four quarters.

Two warnings apply. Skid prices run from ₹1.10 lakh to ₹5.50 lakh a unit and the average fell from ₹4.99 lakh in the December 2025 quarter to ₹3.11 lakh in June; management says price follows design and declined to give the skid margin beyond calling it about the company average[3][4]. And the margin target is 25 percent overall in a few years, while we hold our central margin at 23.0 to 23.5 percent. The next subsection turns to what could go wrong.

The weak spots: one buyer, aged bills, spending and demands

Buyer concentration is the first. The skid line sells to one supplier under an exclusive agreement for India. Aeroflex sees a yearly pipeline, quarterly purchase orders and a dispatch plan only about two months ahead[6][4]. The annual report says there is no concentration of credit risk because customers are widely spread, which is hard to square with a skid buyer at 22 percent of sales and an overseas buyer near 25 percent[10]. Chapter 2B scores concentration.

Receivable quality is the second. Bills over one year old rose from ₹3.93 crore to ₹10.61 crore, from 3.4 to 8.1 percent of receivables, our arithmetic from the ageing table[10]. Inventory days were 122 and the cash conversion cycle 112 days in FY26, against 64 days in FY20[1]. The third is the legal demands: ₹41.76 crore of income tax, under appeal, ₹8.55 crore of goods-and-services tax, on which the annual report says the company is in the process of filing an appeal, and a ₹0.72 crore property-tax notice[14][15]. A fourth is the plan to spend ₹202 crore by March 2028, partly from new equity[12][13]. Chapter 2B now turns these points into five scored measures.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 asks why the estimates might be wrong. Each has a weakest assumption, and the strongest honest case against our view follows. For each risk we give the evidence that would show it and the date we will know. The first two risks lower our profit estimate; the third could raise our value, and it gets equal weight.

Weakest assumption in the profit estimate: that skid sales follow the exit-rate plan

Our central FY27 profit per share of ₹6.63 needs skid sales of about ₹40.3 crore in the September quarter, ₹58.9 crore in December and ₹69.8 crore in March, after ₹32.4 crore in June. That path leans on one buyer, on design approvals floor by floor, and on a machine supplier that has already been late[4]. Management said in May 2026 that the main bottleneck was the skid design work, which the buyer was meant to supply and Aeroflex's own team now does[6]. The skid price per unit has also fallen, from ₹4.99 lakh to ₹3.11 lakh[3]. If the skid line is 3,000 units short in FY28, value falls about 15 percent. A cut in the buyer's orders brings the poor case. The evidence would be skid sales under ₹40 crore on 28 October 2026, or a skid share above 30 percent without matching core growth. Management said overseas skid business would come this year; we found no filing announcing it.

Weakest assumption in the cash view: that suppliers keep financing the stock

FY26 cash from operations of ₹65.84 crore leaned on ₹23.88 crore of extra supplier credit, with payables up 42 percent[10][5]. If suppliers ask for faster payment, the cash cycle returns toward 129 days. The three-year ratio then falls from 0.91 toward 0.75 on FY26 alone. The ₹202 crore plan will draw on equity, a bridge loan and internal cash in proportions not published[12]. Net cash of ₹68.58 crore is about one-third of it. The finance-cost stress in Chapter 5 moves value little. The larger risk is timing, since a raise at a weak price costs holders more. The evidence would be a negative half-year cash flow, a bridge-loan drawdown or a resolution for new shares. The 28 October filing carries the half-year cash flow.

Weakest assumption in the target: that the multiple falls back to its three-year median

Our central value uses a median multiple of 54.36. The median over the last twelve months was 79.4 times, and for 2026 so far it is 95.5. It was 59.3 in late 2023, 54.4 in 2024 and 48.1 in 2025. If the market keeps paying 79.4 times FY28 profit of ₹7.87, the value is about ₹624, 38 percent above the close. The ratio at the top of the zone would then be about 9.0. This is the most important way our view could be wrong, and it works in the stock's favour. We still use the median because the rule says so, because the premium multiple dates only from 2026, and because the price fell 21 percent in the six sessions after the capital plan. A close under ₹414.03 would show the opposite.
