Before you read

DB Corp publishes Dainik Bhaskar, India's largest newspaper group, and earns most of its money from advertisers[10][11]. We reject the stock for now, because its price is still falling and the business scores only 2.0 out of 5 on the checks that matter for a newspaper[38][3]. The company is sound: it has almost no debt, about 1,120 crore rupees in cash, a top credit rating and a price of about 8 times a year of profit[3][10][1]. The pressure is newsprint, the paper the news is printed on, which costs about 21 percent more than a year ago[5][3]. The idea comes back for review if the price stops falling, if the January 2027 results show the festival advertising that slipped out of September, and if paper stops getting dearer[5][39].

1. What the company does

DB Corp (the Dainik Bhaskar Group) is the largest newspaper group in India by circulation. Its flagship is Dainik Bhaskar, a Hindi daily with 43 editions across 11 states, joined by Divya Bhaskar in Gujarati (8 editions), Divya Marathi in Marathi (6 editions) and Saurashtra Samachar.[11][1] In all it runs 61 main editions and 211 district editions, prints at 51 centres on 78 machines with a capacity of about 3.2 million copies an hour, and reaches 14 states.[11][10] The Audit Bureau of Circulations ranked the group number one overall and number one in Hindi for 2025.[10] The group also owns the MY FM radio network, now 37 cities after seven new stations opened in FY26, and a set of news apps that reached almost 18 million monthly users in August 2026.[9][2]

Three kinds of customers pay the company. Advertisers are the biggest: education institutes, builders, jewellers, car dealers, consumer brands, banks and governments buy space in the papers and airtime on the radio. In FY26 the company booked 1,668 crore rupees of advertising revenue against 475 crore of circulation revenue, so advertising is about 68 percent of the revenue the company reports as total, 2,441 crore.[3][10] Readers are the second customer: they pay a cover price of about 5 rupees, and the company says a household spends about 150 to 170 rupees a month, or about 1,200 rupees a year after selling the old papers, which is why it has not raised cover prices.[5] The third customer is the government, whose share of advertising fell from 24 percent to 17 percent of revenue in the first nine months of FY26 once the election advertising of the year before disappeared.[16]

The model is simple to describe and hard to run. Selling prices are set by the market: advertising rates move slowly and the cover price is frozen. The main cost is a commodity. Newsprint was 34.8 percent of total cost in FY26 and 37.8 percent in the September 2026 quarter, when personnel was 24.3 percent and everything else 37.9 percent.[3] About 70 percent of newsprint is bought in India and the rest is imported at a premium of about 15 percent, from Russia, Malaysia, Canada and Europe.[10] Average newsprint cost was 47,549 rupees a tonne in FY26 and 56,903 rupees in the September 2026 quarter, a rise of 20.8 percent on the 47,117 paid a year earlier.[3][5] When paper gets dearer faster than advertisers will pay, profit shrinks; when advertising demand is strong, profit grows even with dear paper, as it did in the June 2026 quarter.[15][6]

Profit needs one definition before the numbers start. EBITDA, short for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, is operating profit before the cost of machines wearing out and before financing. The company's own EBITDA includes other income, mostly interest on its cash, while the stock-data site Screener shows operating profit without it. For the September 2026 quarter that is 91 crore of operating profit plus 33 crore of other income, equal to the 124 crore EBITDA the company reported; 13.39 crore of that other income was a one-off gain from ending the Ahmedabad office lease.[1][2][5] Both views are used in this report and each is labelled, because a reader comparing a news headline with a data site will otherwise see two different "profit" figures for the same quarter.[3]

The Agarwal family owns 74.50 percent of the shares, up from 71.93 percent in December 2023, and none of it is pledged to lenders.[1][20] At the May 2026 call a promoter said buying would stop at 75 percent, the legal ceiling for a listed company in India, so a takeover-and-delisting bid is not the plan.[7] The subsidiaries are too small to matter to the group numbers, according to the rating agency.[10]

How a rupee of revenue turns into profit

Take the September 2026 quarter as a worked example. Of every 100 rupees of company revenue, about 67 came from advertising and 19 from circulation, with radio and other items making up the rest.[2] Newsprint took 37.8 percent of costs, which were 490 crore against 476 crore a year before. Revenue fell 3.2 percent, so costs rose while revenue dropped and EBITDA fell from 158 crore to 124 crore.[3] The EBITDA margin, which is EBITDA divided by total revenue, was 20.2 percent against 25.0 percent a year earlier. Net profit was 69 crore against 93 crore.[3][2]

This is why the newsprint price matters more than almost any other number. At about 185 crore a quarter, newsprint is the largest single cost, and the 14 percent rise in newsprint cost in the quarter, about 23 crore, equals about half of the 47 crore fall in operating profit from 138 crore to 91 crore.[3][2] Management cannot fix this through price: it holds cover prices still, and the industry body's 15 percent advertising surcharge from August 2026 is a recommendation that each paper may ignore.[24][25]

Who runs the company and how it is paid

Sudhir Agarwal is managing director, Pawan Agarwal is deputy managing director and Girish Agarwal is a promoter director. All three hold 3.68 percent of the shares.[20] The annual report shows that managerial pay rose 15 percent in FY26 while staff pay rose 9 percent in a year when group profit fell 10.5 percent, a gap worth watching but not a red line.[9] Price Waterhouse Chartered Accountants LLP and Gupta Mittal and Co. audit jointly, and the annual report discloses pending defamation and other legal cases against the company and its directors in Note 37.[9]

  • capital expenditure is money spent on buildings, machines and other long-lived assets.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

DB Corp's strength is local dominance in a large, under-served region. It is number one by circulation in India and number one among Hindi papers, leads in Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Haryana, Chandigarh and Rajasthan, and claims number one in the top four cities of Bihar.[11][10] A local advertiser in Bhopal or Jaipur who wants to reach readers across a whole region has few alternatives, and that gives the company a reach advantage a national paper cannot copy city by city. The group also runs about 12,000 journalists, including stringers, which is the cost of that hyperlocal depth and a barrier for a newcomer.[11]

The second strength is the balance sheet. Borrowings were 225 crore at September 2026 against cash and bank balances of 1,120 crore, so the company holds about 895 crore more cash than it owes.[3][1] CARE Ratings reaffirmed CARE AA+; Stable on 25 September 2026, with overall gearing of 0.11 times and interest cover of 18.89 times in FY26. It names a fall in operating margin below 13 percent as a downgrade trigger, a level the company has not touched in the last five years.[10] Cash flow has been strong: cash from operations was 579, 414 and 365 crore in FY24, FY25 and FY26, which is 1.20 times the profit of those three years.[1]

The weaknesses are about direction, not today's strength. Circulation was about 40 lakh copies a day in 2025, about 39 lakh in March 2026 and about 37.68 lakh in September 2026, and circulation revenue fell 3.9 percent in the latest quarter.[7][8][14] The company says it is choosing to hold copies in its core markets and to accept losses in non-core ones, and that a cover price rise would speed the decline. That is a candid statement of a shrinking product, even if the pace is slow.[5] The print advertising market is 13 percent of India's total ad spend and is forecast to grow 3 percent in 2026, while digital grows 18 percent, so the company must keep taking share just to stay level.[27]

Cost control is a third weakness, in the sense that it has run out of room. Management said at the Q2 call that other costs are well controlled and that newsprint is the only major input it cannot control.[14] The industry data agree: newsprint prices rose almost 25 percent over four to five months because freight, a stronger dollar and mill closures in Canada squeezed supply, and Indian mills have asked the Prime Minister to intervene over coal supply.[23][26] The rating agency also flags stretched receivables: collection took 71 days in FY26, and about 27 percent of debtors were more than six months old, part of it government agencies that pay late.[10]

What caused the fall in the share price

The stock has lost about 47 percent from its 52-week high of 280.55 and 44 percent over one year, and the cause is a chain of events, not one shock.[38] In January 2026 the Q3 result showed advertising down 7.8 percent because election advertising and festival timing had flattered the year-earlier quarter. Elara Capital cut its rating to Accumulate and its target to 280 rupees.[8][16] From March the Middle East conflict and the disruption of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz pushed freight, crude oil and the dollar higher. Industry sources forecast a 15 percent rise in newsprint, and the real rise to the September quarter was 20.8 percent.[26][28][3] The June 2026 quarter was a strong result, with profit up 24.6 percent, and the stock rose as much as 7.5 percent in the day, but management's promise that newsprint would soften did not hold.[15][6]

Two things then hit together. The Indian market had its longest weekly losing streak in 25 years, eight straight weeks, as crude oil stayed near or above 100 dollars a barrel and foreign investors sold.[28][29] And on 15 October the company reported September-quarter profit down 26.6 percent to 68.62 crore with advertising down 6.4 percent. The share fell 6.48 percent that day to 166.75.[13][12] It kept falling to a new 52-week low on 23 October and closed at 149.04 on 28 October, 1.02 average true ranges above its low of 143.01 on a volume of only 0.75 times average, which is selling that has not yet turned into panic.[37][38]

What did not cause the fall matters just as much. We found no 2026 regulatory order, auditor resignation, promoter pledge or dividend cut: the promoter stake rose to 74.50 percent, pledge is 0.0 percent, and the Rs 5 interim dividend was declared in July.[20][31][5] The only enforcement item is the July 2021 income tax search of the group, which is five years old and a reminder that the group has had disputes with the state.[30] The company's FY26 dividend payout was 37.6 percent of profit against 58 percent in FY25, so the cash is being retained, and management says it is looking for "a tax-efficient manner" to use it.[9][7]

What the market is worried about, and what it may be overdoing

The sensible worry is that newsprint stays dear and advertisers do not follow with price rises. Management itself expects only a 2 to 3 percent fall in the next two quarters, and the Q3 price will still be 18 to 20 percent above last year.[5][14] The less comfortable worry is structural: the company has paid for more than a year to hold copies and the base is still falling, and the app audience fell from about 20 million to 18 million after marketing was cut.[5][2]

The possible over-reaction is in the price. At 149.04 the stock costs about 8.1 times the last twelve months of profit and about 1.06 times book value, and it paid 7 rupees of dividend last year, a 4.7 percent yield on the ledger close.[1][38] Five-year average price-to-earnings was 14.8 on Trendlyne, and quarter-end trailing multiples were 14.41 in September 2025 and 10.56 in June 2026, so the market has cut the multiple by about 27 percent by June and by about 44 percent now, to 8.1.[21][22] Cheapness is not a verdict, because a falling-earnings business should be cheaper. Chapter 5 asks whether 8 times is low enough for the earnings we expect.[39]

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A newspaper company is an advertising business with a reader subscription attached and a commodity input. So the five measures that run the business are advertising revenue growth, the price of newsprint and the margin it leaves, the size of the readership, cash conversion with dividend cover, and the progress of the non-print businesses.[10][3] Each measure is scored PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0), and the line between the scores was set before the number was read. The Layer 2 rule then applies: a name qualifies when the score is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 and at most one measure fails.[39]

The result is 2.0 out of 5.0: one PASS, two WATCH and two FAIL, which is below the 3.0 line, so Layer 2 reads FAIL. The first FAIL is advertising growth, where four quarters of revenue are 0.2 percent lower than the four before and the rule says negative growth fails. The second FAIL is the newsprint measure, where the price is up 20.8 percent and the EBITDA margin is down 480 basis points.[3] Both FAILs can change with the January 2027 results, and the closing paragraphs of this chapter say what each would need.[5]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management speaks to investors every quarter, and the question is whether what it said last time came true. We checked eleven statements from the January 2026, May 2026 and July 2026 calls against the figures that followed.[8][7][6] Four were delivered (the radio stations, the property purchases, the fourth-quarter advertising growth and the 75 percent promoter ceiling), four were partly delivered and three were missed. Two of the misses and one of the partial deliveries were forecasts about newsprint, and each was too hopeful.[3][10]

This matters for the base case. The company said in January that newsprint would stay range bound, in May that the rise was temporary, and in July that prices would fall from the third quarter; in October it said they would fall only 2 to 3 percent, "but not more than that as what we hear from our suppliers".[5] Our estimates therefore do not assume newsprint relief beyond what the October call allows, and the bull case, not the base case, is the one that needs prices to fall.[39]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue was 1,769 crore in FY22, 2,129 crore in FY23, 2,402 crore in FY24, 2,339 crore in FY25 and 2,356 crore in FY26, with 2,366 crore over the last twelve months.[1] That is growth of 7.4 percent a year over four years but only 3.4 percent a year since FY23, and nothing in FY25 and FY26: the FY25 year carried election advertising, and the FY26 year did not.[1][10] Screener shows compound sales growth of 9 percent over five years and 3 percent over three, which fits the story of a business that recovered from the 2020 and 2021 shutdowns and then stopped growing.[1]

Profit rose and then fell back. Operating profit was 324 crore in FY23 and jumped to 623 crore in FY24 because sales rose by 273 crore while costs fell by 27 crore, then slipped to 546 crore, 490 crore and 467 crore over the last twelve months.[1] The operating margin was 15 percent in FY23, 26 percent in FY24, 23 percent in FY25, 21 percent in FY26 and 20 percent now. The September quarter alone was 16 percent. Net profit went from 426 crore in FY24 to 332 crore in FY26 and 327 crore over twelve months, and earnings per share from 23.89 rupees to 18.63 and 18.35.[1] Screener's three-year profit growth of 25 percent a year starts from the weak FY23 base. Measured from the FY24 peak, profit is falling about 11.7 percent a year.[1]

Cash conversion is the strong part. Cash from operations beat profit in every year: 371, 244, 579, 414 and 365 crore from FY22 to FY26, which is 2.59, 1.44, 1.36, 1.12 and 1.10 times profit, so the earnings are real cash and not accounting.[1] Free cash flow was 194 crore in FY26, down from 366 crore. Screener defines it as cash from operations less spending on fixed assets, so the 171 crore gap between 365 and 194 is that spending, and the annual report agrees: payments for property, plant and equipment, right-of-use assets and intangibles were about 172 crore, including about 120 crore on buying out rented properties. The separate cash-from-investing row of minus 173 crore also nets purchases and sales of investments, which is why the two rows do not give free cash flow by simple subtraction. Borrowings are 225 crore, largely lease liabilities, working-capital borrowings and import credit, against cash and bank balances of 1,120 crore.[1][10][3] Return on capital employed (ROCE) has fallen from 25 percent in FY24 to 21 percent and 18 percent, and the company's own figure for FY26 is 17.1 percent.[1][3]

Two features of the numbers need a warning. First, other income of 84 crore in FY26 and 99 crore over twelve months, mostly interest on the cash, is about 19 to 22 percent of profit before tax, so earnings per share is partly a bank-deposit yield that moves with interest rates and with how the cash is used.[1] Second, the company reports "EBITDA" with that other income inside it, while data sites show operating profit without it. Chapter 1 showed the bridge. This chapter uses operating profit from Screener for the five-year tables and company EBITDA only where the source gives no other measure.[2][1]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in this report rests on a weakest assumption, and the case against the conclusion should be made by someone who wants it to fail. This chapter does that for the FY27 estimate, the FY28 estimate, the 9.0 times multiple and the rejection itself, then names the single claim everything depends on and dates the checks.[39]

The weakest assumption behind each estimate

FY27 earnings per share of 19.2 rests on the festival revenue coming back. About 40 crore of September-quarter advertising was pushed into October by the Navratri calendar, and we have counted all of it as arriving in the December quarter.[5] The evidence for it is management's statement and the "good" first seven or eight days of the festival. The evidence against it is the monsoon, which was below normal in parts of the country, and a market under pressure from crude near 100 dollars. If only half returns, FY27 earnings per share falls to about 18.3 rupees.[2][28]

FY28 earnings per share of 19.2 rests on newsprint at 56,500 rupees a tonne. The weakest part is that Indian mills price off imports, and the Storyboard18 report says coal supply, freight at about 18 percent of landed cost (8 percent before) and the closure of Canadian mills are all still pushing the other way.[23] Management's own record on newsprint relief is two missed forecasts and one partly wrong, so a flat price is not a cautious assumption; if the price reaches 60,000 rupees and advertising does not grow, earnings per share falls toward 14 rupees.[7][6]

The 9.0 times multiple rests on the idea that the market will pay more for DB Corp than for Jagran Prakashan, which is at 7.15 times. The weakness is that the two print peers we checked trade at 7.15 and 4.41 times earnings, so DB Corp's current 8.1 times may simply be the going rate for print.[32][33] The market gave it 14.41 times a year ago; whether that was the right price for a growing business or an erroneous one for a flat business is the question we cannot settle.[22]

The red-team bear case against our base case

The strongest case that our base value of 172 is too high runs as follows. Copies fell about 6 percent in nine months, the company refuses to raise cover prices, advertisers can move to digital where costs are lower, and the advertising surcharge the industry body recommended is voluntary; so the revenue line is weaker than 4 percent growth.[5][24][27] Newsprint is 38 percent of cost and rising; each 1,000 rupees a tonne costs about 12.6 crore a year. The company has 4,776 employees whose pay rises about 9 percent a year; and the cost cuts management describes as done leave no cushion.[3][9] If advertising grows only 1 percent, circulation falls 5 percent and the paper price is 58,000 rupees, FY28 operating profit is about 416 crore, earnings per share is about 15.8 rupees, and 8 times that is about 127 rupees, 15 percent below the price and below the 137.10 stop.[39]

The reverse case, that rejection is too harsh, is also real. The stock is at 8 times earnings and 1.06 times book with 34 percent of its value in net cash, a 4.7 percent dividend yield and an AA+ rating, and the promoters have bought up to 74.5 percent, which is a mild positive signal.[1][10][20] If September-quarter profit was the trough, the market may be paying for the worst. We do not dismiss this. We simply decline to buy before the chart shows a floor, because the rule that rejects is a rule about timing, and an investor who waits a few weeks for confirmation loses only the first part of any rebound.[39][38]

The single hinge claim

The hinge claim is that newsprint stays at or below about 57,000 rupees a tonne while advertising grows at least 4 percent, so that the company-reported EBITDA margin returns to at least 22.5 percent in Q3 FY27 and about 20 percent in operating terms in FY28.[5][3] If that claim fails, the base case becomes the bear case; if it holds, both Layer 2 FAILs can move to WATCH and the score can reach 3.0, which leaves the technical test as the main gate. We will know the answer at the Q3 FY27 results, expected in the middle of January 2027 (the Q3 FY26 results came on 15 January 2026, so that date is inferred and not announced).[8][9]

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, and we would place no order. The rule that rejects it is the technical routing rule. Only a Stage 4 breakdown disqualifies a name that otherwise passes the red lines. That means a close below a falling 50-day average, which is below a falling 200-day average, with Test C failing. The ledger shows exactly that, with Test C failing on C2 and C4.[38][39] Two more grounds stand beside it. Layer 2 scores 2.0 of 5.0 with two FAILs, below the 3.0 needed. Reward-to-risk at the close is 1.92 against the 2.0 Core floor. The base value of 172 is 15 percent above the price, and the stop reference of 137.10 is 8 percent below it.[39]

We do not say the business is bad. It has almost no borrowing and 1,120 crore of cash. It holds a CARE AA+ rating, and three-year cash conversion is 1.20 times profit. Every red line on solvency passes.[10][1] The reason for rejection is a mix of three things. The price decline is unfinished. The operating score is under the line. The base value needs the market to pay 9 times earnings instead of 8.1. If the stock were held, this rejection alone would not force a sale. The stock is not in the portfolio, so the question does not arise.[39]
