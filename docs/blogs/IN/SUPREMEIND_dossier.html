What the company does

Supreme Industries makes things out of plastic, and most of its money comes from pipes. Think of the pipe that carries water into a house, the drain under a kitchen sink, or the pipe that feeds a farm well. Pipes are sold through dealers and channel partners [11], and they are fitted by plumbers and builders. That fitting step is a general description of the industry, not a company fact. Sales therefore follow building activity and the price of the plastic powder used to make them. Plastic piping products made up about 69 percent of FY26 revenue: ₹7,776 crore of ₹11,218 crore [2] [3]. The 69 percent is our division of the two numbers, not a reported share. A filing summary says plastic products made up 97.67 percent of standalone turnover, but that figure counts all plastic goods, not pipes alone [4].

The rest of the business is smaller. Packaging products, industrial moulded parts, consumer furniture and storage products, and composite gas cylinders make up the remainder [3]. The company says it is India's largest manufacturer and processor of plastics [5]. It lists more than 14,000 products, which the data provider calls SKUs, or stock-keeping units, meaning separate items with their own codes [3].

The company also owns part of an associate, Supreme Petrochem Limited. An associate is a company that is not fully owned but is large enough to affect the owner's reported profit. In the June 2026 quarter the associate added ₹73.05 crore to group profit [6]. That number lifts reported profit without adding a rupee of sales, so we will come back to it in chapter 3.

The terms below are the ones this report uses. Each is defined once, here, at first use.

  • return on capital employed (ROCE) means the profit a company earns on all the money tied up in its business, shown as a percentage. This report uses 15 percent as the floor.
  • debt-to-equity means total borrowings divided by shareholders' money. A low number means the business runs mostly on its own money.
  • earnings per share (EPS) means the profit that belongs to each share.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio means the share price divided by the profit per share over the past year. A higher number means investors pay more for each rupee of profit.
  • operating margin means the share of each rupee of sales left after running costs, before interest and tax.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is the operating profit before interest, tax and the wear-off of assets. It is a rough measure of day-to-day earnings.
  • relative strength index (RSI) is a score from 0 to 100 that compares recent gains with recent losses. Below 30 means the stock has fallen unusually fast, and above 70 means it has risen unusually fast.
  • average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions, measured in rupees.
  • Stage 2 means a stock in a sustained uptrend, with the price above averages that are themselves rising.
  • Stage 4 means a stock in a sustained downtrend, with the price below averages that are falling.
  • base means a sideways period in which a stock rests after a fall, before its next move.
  • pivot means the price level a stock must close above to confirm a move up.
  • breakout means a close above a known price level, such as a pivot, after a quiet period.
  • asymmetry means how much a stock could gain compared with how much it could lose from the entry price.
  • stop loss means the price at which a holder sells to cap a loss.
  • entry zone means the price range in which buying is allowed.
  • guidance means what management says it expects for the coming year.

Why the business is strong or weak

Scale is the first strength. The company describes itself as the largest plastics maker in India [5], and a range of more than 14,000 products [3] lets a dealer buy many items from one supplier. Scale helps on cost, because a large plant spreads its fixed costs over more tonnes of output. We cannot measure the cost advantage from the pages we read, so we treat scale as a claim the numbers should support.

The returns on money in the business are the second strength. Return on capital employed was 20.7 percent on the consolidated numbers and 22.6 percent on the standalone numbers [3] [7]. Astral, another listed pipe maker, reports 19.2 percent [8]. Finolex Industries reports 11.8 percent [9]. Supreme therefore earns the highest return of the three listed peers on the consolidated numbers, and well above the weakest. Growth is the weak side. Sales grew about 7 percent a year over three years, and profit grew about 3 percent a year [3]. Profit after tax was ₹954 crore in FY26, a little below ₹961 crore in FY25 [3]. The FY26 annual report summary reads more brightly. It reports total income up 7 percent and pipe volume up 15 percent [10]. That summary gives a different profit figure, which chapter 3 explains.

Pipe prices follow the cost of PVC resin, the plastic powder the pipes are made from. In April 2026 polymer prices fell sharply, and channel partners cut their stock, which hit demand [11]. Management says the policy changes help prices steady. They include removing a customs duty exemption and a minimum import price for suspension-grade PVC [12] [13]. A minimum import price stops very cheap imports from undercutting domestic makers, so it supports local prices, but it does not guarantee them.

Market forecasts disagree. One research firm puts India's plastic pipe market at USD 2.24 billion in 2026, growing 6.30 percent a year to 2034 [14]. Another puts India's PVC pipe market at USD 5.8 billion in 2026, growing 8.40 percent a year to 2035 [15]. Neither is a company forecast. The gap between them is a reason to read market growth as a range.

The balance sheet is a clear strength. Borrowings were ₹91 crore at March 2026, against shareholders' money of about ₹6,169 crore [3]. The data provider notes the company is almost debt free [3]. That leaves room to fund the new plants management is building in Bihar, Malanpur and Jammu [16]. Management puts FY27 capital spending at around ₹1,000 crore [17].

The credit rating was reaffirmed at CRISIL AA+/Stable, with a short-term rating of A1+, in December 2025 [18] [19] [20]. That date is older than the 60-day window our research standard applies, so we cite the rating as context only and do not rely on it for any gate.

Taken together, the strengths are scale, high returns on capital and a balance sheet with almost no debt. They weigh against growth that has been slow for three years. A business with those strengths can still be a poor buy if its profit stops growing, and that is the question chapter 3 takes up.

What the price is doing

The momentum test asks whether the price is already in an uptrend on the rule's measures. Our result is NO_MOMENTUM, which means the price is not yet in an uptrend by those measures. The rule has two buckets. Core names are longer holdings, and they need a Stage 1 breakout or a Stage 2 uptrend. Tactical names are shorter trades, and they need a Stage 2 uptrend, with the price above averages that are rising. The test has two parts. Part A checks the trend itself: the price must sit above rising averages, relative strength must lead the benchmark, and the price must not be stretched too far from its support. Part B checks whether a stock is entering an uptrend: its 50-day average must hold, relative strength must turn up, the price must sit near its pivot, and volume must dry up and then expand. A stock that fails both parts gets no allocation, however good its business. The code runs the test on the 9 October 2026 ledger snapshot [1].
