1. What the company does

In plain words: Supreme turns plastic granules into pipes, packaging, moulded parts and furniture, and sells most of it through about 7,000 distributors. When the price of the raw material jumps or drops, its sales and its profit swing with it, and that swing is the main story of this report.

The Supreme Industries Limited, founded in 1942, runs 35 plants across India, sells nine groups of products and exports to 55 countries. [2] Plastic piping is the largest group. It covers PVC pipes and fittings for plumbing and farm water, CPVC (a heat-resistant plastic) pipes for hot water, polyethylene pipes for farms and gas, and water tanks. In FY26 it sold 7.54 lakh tonnes of plastic goods, up about 12 percent, for ₹11,217.67 crore of sales. [5] [21] It reports four segments. These are piping, industrial products (parts for appliance and car makers, pallets and composite gas cylinders), packaging (films and protective packaging) and consumer products (mainly moulded furniture). [2]

It makes money from the gap between the price of the plastic it buys and the price of what it sells. The plastics include PVC, polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene and ABS; the chairman said 'We are not PVC company only.' [3] Management says India imports 68 percent of its PVC resin, so the import price, the rupee and import rules reach every pipe it sells. [6] Management says finished prices follow raw-material prices, but the company holds about two and a half months of stock, so stock bought at a high price loses money when prices fall, and the reverse is also true. [3] Chapter 2 puts numbers on both.

Capacity is large and still growing. Plastic piping capacity reached about one million tonnes a year at 31 March 2026, including three plants bought from Wavin. The company plans to add about 1.10 lakh tonnes in FY27, to reach about 1.35 million tonnes in all. [8] [5] The Wavin business came for USD 30 million plus working capital on 1 August 2025, with a licence to Wavin's technology. [20] The company also owns 30.78 percent of Supreme Petrochem Limited, a listed polystyrene maker. It counts its share of that company's profit in its group results: ₹101.20 crore in FY26 and ₹73.05 crore in the June 2026 quarter alone. [4] [8] Chapter 3 treats that share separately, as it is more volatile than the core.

  • Inventory gain or loss is profit or loss from selling plastic bought at a different price from today's; rising resin prices create gains and falling prices create losses.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

In plain words: This is a large, well-known business with no debt and a good name, whose profit has stopped growing for five years. The share price has been paying for a recovery that the latest quarter has not yet shown in volumes.

The strengths are scale, brand, reach and a clean balance sheet. The weaknesses are a flat growth record, a dependence on a raw material whose price can swing by a third in a month, and a group profit line that now leans on a cyclical associate. This chapter sets them against each other and then covers the policy facts that move the pipe business, because this year they matter more than any company-specific event.

Where the strength is

The company sells through more than 7,000 distributors, runs 35 plants so that bulky pipe travels short distances, and holds a Crisil rating of AA+ with a stable outlook, reaffirmed on 24 December 2025. [2] [19] That letter is 303 days old at the 23 October session, as Run notes lists. Management put its cash at ₹542 crore on 30 June 2026 and calls itself debt free. [2] Borrowing at 31 March 2026 was nil: it raised ₹295 crore of commercial paper and ₹500.70 crore of working-capital loans during FY26 and repaid all of it. [8]

Water-programme buyers pay quickly: in October 2025 management said '17 days of the sale remain outstanding' on dues from the central water programme, low against its own standard. [13] Value-added products grew 15 percent in FY26 and 22 percent in the June 2026 quarter. [5] [1] The company's own return on average capital employed was 22.59 percent in FY26 against 24.55 percent in FY25. [8]

Where the weakness is

Profit has not grown. Screener's compounded figures show sales up 12 percent a year over five years but profit down 1 percent a year, and ROCE falling from 42 percent in FY21 to 21 percent in FY26. [32] ROCE is return on capital employed, the profit earned on the money the business uses. Part of the fall is a high starting point, because FY21 had a 20 percent operating margin, the highest in the series, and part is the cost of building plants ahead of demand: FY26 plant spending was ₹814.99 crore against depreciation of ₹428.28 crore. [8]

Depreciation is a growing drag: it rose 31.5 percent in the June 2026 quarter to ₹122.33 crore. [2] The industrial segment earned only about 6 percent EBIT in June, and the composite cylinder plant ran at 25 to 35 percent of capacity because it depends on oil-company orders. [2] [3] The Q1 FY27 profit jump is also flattered by the associate: of ₹280.72 crore group profit, ₹73.05 crore came from Supreme Petrochem, up from about ₹24.94 crore a year earlier (consolidated pre-tax profit of ₹265.18 crore less standalone ₹240.24 crore). [1] [4] Chapter 3 separates the two.

Policy and demand: import duty, the price floor, the water mission and housing

Trade press reports three decisions on PVC imports this year. On 18 November 2025 the government decided not to apply the anti-dumping duty that its trade-remedies authority had recommended. [24] The company says a customs-duty exemption on suspension-grade PVC resin ended on 16 July 2026, while ChemAnalyst gives 30 June; we use the company's date. [1] [23] And trade press reports that on 24 July 2026 the resin moved from free to restricted imports with a minimum import price of US$0.766 a kilogram, for six months; the notification itself was unreadable. [23] ChemAnalyst treats the anti-dumping case as still open. On that report, a floor stops cheap imports, so it supports the price of resin and raises Supreme's cost. Management said the floor does not stop producers from charging more, only from charging less than US$766 a tonne, and that resin had already risen about ₹9 a kilo by 28 July. [3] The floor lapses in about January 2027 unless extended, and management said 'Extension part you can only take from the Government of India.' [3]

The Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM), the central programme that pays for rural piped water, is a large buyer of pipe but a slow one. The Cabinet extended it to December 2028 with an outlay of ₹8.69 lakh crore, and the Union Budget for 2026-27 proposed ₹67,670 crore for it. [25] [26] The Comptroller and Auditor General's report on its implementation was tabled on 24 March 2026. [27] Management said in April 2026 that 'whatever budget they announced in February, they could spend only one-third amount' in each of the last two years, and it was 'not sure this year also'. [6] We give the mission no part of our central volume case.

Housing is steadier, though no filing quantifies it: management said 'You can't complete building without plumbing', with 'no demand disruption from there', which is a statement and not data. [6] Industry pipe volumes fell 9 percent in FY26, and suppliers expected about 8 percent growth in FY27, on management's account. [6]
