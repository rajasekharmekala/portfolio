Before you read

KRBL makes basmati rice and owns India Gate, the biggest packaged basmati brand in India.[1][7] We reject the stock for now, because the chart is in a Stage 3 pullback, the asymmetry (the reward-to-risk ratio) is 1.06 to 1 against a floor of 3.0, and only 2 of 5 KPIs pass. Debt is not the issue: borrowings are ₹170 crore against ₹5,807 crore of net worth, and the promoters have pledged nothing.[1][18] Trust and price are the issue: the auditor has qualified its opinion at every year-end since March 2021 over a court case, and ₹394 leaves little room for error.[6] We would buy if forward earnings per share reach about ₹42 or the price falls to about ₹372 as the trend mends, and we would drop the idea below ₹364.

1. What the company does

KRBL grows its own supply chain from the seed up. It develops seed, runs contract farming, buys paddy from farmers, stores it, mills it, packs it and sells it under its own brands.[1] Paddy is rice in its husk. Basmati is a long, thin, aromatic grain grown in a defined belt in north India.[26] Its price follows the size of the crop and the appetite of Middle Eastern importers.[9] KRBL's flagship label is India Gate, which the company calls the world's number one basmati brand.[1] Its five plants have a combined milling capacity of 207 metric tonnes an hour, and Screener describes the Punjab plant as the largest rice mill in that state.[3][1]

In the year to March 2026, KRBL earned ₹6,098 crore of revenue.[3] Of that, ₹4,444 crore (73%) came from selling rice in India, ₹1,555 crore (25%) came from exports and ₹99 crore (2%) came from selling wind, biomass and solar power to state electricity boards.[3] The power business is small. It sells at fixed contract prices, so it adds steady but flat profit.[13] The rice business is where all the risk and the return sit.

The money comes from a spread. KRBL buys paddy between October and January, when the new crop arrives, then ages the rice for a year or more because aged basmati cooks better and sells at a premium.[17] That is why inventory is huge. At 31 March 2026 the company held ₹3,714 crore of paddy and rice, which is about 61% of a year's sales (our calculation).[10] Screener reports 329 inventory days on its own basis, which is not the same measure as stock over a year's sales, so we use that series later only to show direction.[1] The business therefore carries price risk: if paddy prices fall after KRBL buys, the aged stock is worth less than it cost.[17]

Who pays depends on the channel. In India, India Gate reaches about 1.2 crore urban households through roughly 3.3 lakh retail outlets, and its share of packaged basmati was 37% in general trade, 38% in modern trade and 41% in e-commerce in June 2026.[7][9] Abroad, the company sells to over 90 countries.[7] India as a whole shipped 6.52 million tonnes of basmati worth ₹50,138 crore in 2025-26, and the top buyers were Saudi Arabia (16.6%), Iran (13.9%) and Iraq (11.2%).[26] The Chairman said on the Q1 FY27 call that the Middle East takes close to three quarters of Indian basmati exports, which is the reason the Hormuz shipping crisis of 2026 hit the whole industry.[9]

KRBL is also trying new things beside rice. Branded non-basmati rice reached ₹271 crore in FY26, edible oil under the Uplife brand reached only ₹12 crore, and masala products are at an annual run rate of about ₹9 crore.[10][9] The cash flow statement shows something else: ₹407 crore went into investment property (land) in FY26, which the annual report says "is not related to the rice business".[3] That choice matters for how we judge management in chapter 2B.

What this means for an owner

An owner of KRBL owns three things at once: a brand, a stock of aged rice, and a pile of cash. The brand earns the steady profit. The rice stock is the source of the risk, because its value moves with the paddy price. The cash and the new land purchase are where management's choices matter most. Chapter 2 asks how strong the brand really is and what the auditor's qualification means for trust in the numbers.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Paddy: rice grain still in its husk; the raw material KRBL buys from farmers.
  • Aged rice: rice stored for 12 to 24 months so it cooks longer and fluffier; it sells at a premium.
  • Branded versus bulk: branded rice is sold in packs under India Gate; bulk or private-label rice is sold in large lots to other companies.
  • Strait of Hormuz: the narrow sea lane at the mouth of the Persian Gulf that most Gulf-bound cargo uses.
  • Crore and lakh: Indian units. One crore is 10 million; one lakh is 100,000; ₹100 crore is ₹1 billion.
  • Net cash: cash and treasury investments minus bank borrowings; net debt is the same sum with the opposite sign.
  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization; it is profit before those four items, and KRBL quotes it as a share of total income.
  • ROCE stands for return on capital employed; it is the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share; it is the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow, which is the cash left after running the business and buying plant and equipment.
  • gross margin is revenue minus the cost of goods sold, as a share of revenue, and operating margin is revenue minus all running costs, as a share of revenue.
  • debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' money, and working capital is the cash tied up in stock and customer bills minus supplier credit.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit; a low P/E means the market pays little for each rupee of profit.
  • guidance is the company's own forecast for the coming period, and the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry and reward-to-risk mean the same ratio: the possible gain divided by the possible loss, or (target − price) ÷ (price − stop).
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • RSI stands for relative strength index; it is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has moved, and below 30 is oversold while above 70 is overbought.
  • ATR stands for average true range; it is the typical daily price swing in rupees, and we use it to measure how far the price sits from support.
  • SMA and EMA: a simple moving average (SMA) is the plain average of the last N closing prices, and an exponential moving average (EMA) is a similar line that gives more weight to recent days.
  • A base is a sideways pause the price builds before a move, the pivot is the top of that pause, and a breakout is a move above it on rising volume.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The moat here is a brand plus a distribution network plus a supply chain that is hard to copy. India Gate leads every channel it measures: 37% of general trade, 38% of modern trade and 41% of e-commerce in packaged basmati, about ten points ahead of the next e-commerce rival.[7][9] The company owns the largest rice-milling plant in Punjab and runs the largest contract-farming network in the sector, which gives it access to paddy at harvest.[1][7] ICRA rates the company's long-term bank facilities [ICRA]AA (Stable) and cites the brand, the distribution network and the location of the mills next to the paddy supply.[17]

The moat is real but not widening. On the Q1 FY27 call management said its traditional-trade market share fell about 2 percentage points from a year earlier, and that its share in parts of modern trade needs to improve.[9] Meanwhile the closest listed rival, LT Foods, grew revenue 26% in the same quarter to ₹3,161 crore while KRBL's revenue fell 6% to ₹1,496 crore.[29][7] LT Foods earns a higher return on capital (17.6% against KRBL's 15.2%) and trades at a higher price-to-earnings multiple (22.6 against 11.9).[28][1] The market is pricing KRBL as the slower of the two.

The business is exposed to four forces it cannot control. First is the paddy crop: the 2025 Punjab floods cut basmati output in the worst districts by 20% to 25%, and a below-normal 2026 monsoon is now a risk for the crop that arrives in October.[9] Second is the Middle East: in Q1 FY27 KRBL's export revenue fell 50% to ₹244 crore because shipping through the Strait of Hormuz was disrupted.[9] Third is trade policy: the Aug 2025 call noted a 25% US tariff, and an analyst on the Q1 FY27 call referred to an 18% US duty.[12][9] Fourth is its own working capital, because ICRA describes the operations as working-capital intensive and says high inventory exposes the company to price swings.[17]

The seed text for this report called the removal of the basmati minimum export price a fresh catalyst. It is not. The Ministry of Commerce removed the ₹950 per tonne minimum export price on 14 September 2024, more than two years ago, and the removal is already in the numbers.[27][13] The live catalyst today is shipping access through Hormuz, and that depends on diplomacy and not on any Indian policy.[9]

The industry backdrop: India dominates supply, but the route to market is narrow

The Chairman said on the Q1 FY27 call that India accounts for roughly 85% of world basmati trade.[9] Pakistan is the other large source. In the year to June 2026 its rice exports fell about 26% in volume and 32% in value, even though its basmati sold at a premium of about USD 100 a tonne to comparable Indian grades.[9] That is good news for Indian pricing power. Indian rice is not losing on price. The 2026 problem has been the route and not the price.

Indian basmati exports in Q1 FY27 were about 1.5 million tonnes against 1.7 million a year earlier, and the Middle East was down about 11%.[9] India shipped 6.52 million tonnes in 2025-26 in total.[26] KRBL's own export revenue fell about 50% in the quarter, which is a much larger fall than the industry's. Our reading is that KRBL leans harder on the Middle East than the industry average and that its own Saudi route is still unbuilt, but the filings do not give a split by country, so we cannot prove it.[9]

What this means for an owner: the brand's pricing power is intact, and the cause of the weak export quarter is access and not demand. That supports a recovery case. It does not by itself support a price of ₹394, because the recovery date is unknown and depends on shipping lanes and not on KRBL. The next subsection covers the part of the business that does not depend on shipping at all: governance.

Governance: the part of the moat the auditor will not sign off on

The auditor, Walker Chandiok, has given a qualified opinion on the FY26 accounts. A qualified opinion means the auditor accepts the accounts except for one matter it cannot verify. Here the matter is a case brought by the Enforcement Directorate, which is investigating the company's Joint Managing Director under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act for alleged involvement in the AgustaWestland case.[3] The ED has also filed a criminal complaint naming the company, its subsidiary KRBL DMCC and the Joint Managing Director, and it alleges that USD 24.62 million of proceeds of crime reached KRBL through KRBL DMCC.[5] The company disputes the allegation, and an independent firm hired by the board found no conclusive evidence of an effect on the accounts, so the auditor says it cannot say whether any adjustment is needed.[5] The company's own filing says the qualification has continued since 31 March 2021, so it has appeared at every year-end audit since then.[6]

A second signal came in September 2025, when independent director Anil Kumar Chaudhary resigned and wrote that "dissent is suppressed or sidelined".[21] He listed inconsistent board minutes, withheld information, a write-off of export receivables, questions about CSR spending, arbitrary variable pay and a change to the company's object clause.[21] The shares fell as much as 12.8% to ₹387.05 in one day.[20] The company later disclosed a ₹58 crore write-off of a 2023 African export receivable and ₹37 crore of unspent CSR money.[22] ICRA reported in December 2025 that an independent third-party review found no irregularity with a material bearing on the accounts, and a replacement independent director joined on 4 December 2025.[17] We have not seen the review report itself.

What this means for an owner: the numbers in the filings look clean, because debt is tiny and cash is large, but the auditor's qualification and the board resignation mean a buyer is relying on management's word for one unresolved criminal matter. ICRA still rates the company's long-term bank facilities [ICRA]AA (Stable) and CARE rated its commercial paper A1+, the top short-term grade, so the risk is a reputation and legal one, not a solvency one.[17][16] That risk should be paid for with a lower price, and today the price is not low enough to pay for it, which leads into the KPI scorecard in chapter 2B.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A branded rice company is judged on five things: whether it sells more rice year after year in its home market, whether it holds its share of shelves, whether it keeps a healthy gap between the price it sells at and the price it paid for paddy, whether its cash keeps up with its profit despite huge inventory, and whether its export arm can ship when asked. We picked these five KPIs because each one maps to a way this business can fail. We scored each PASS, WATCH or FAIL against a stated threshold, and the rule is that a stock needs at least 3 of 5 passes to qualify.

KRBL scores 2 of 5. The margin and cash-conversion KPIs pass. Domestic volume and market share are on WATCH because they are slipping from good levels. The export KPI fails for a clear reason: export revenue fell 38% in Q4 FY26 (₹279 crore against ₹450 crore, our arithmetic; the CFO cited 33% on the call) and 50% in Q1 FY27.[10][9] Two passes out of five is below the bar.

Management's record is mixed. 5. What could happen next

Chapter 5 asks what the price already assumes, what the experts expect, and where we differ. A reverse discounted cash flow works backwards from the share price. It asks what growth rate, over ten years, makes the stock worth today's price if you discount future profit at 12% and assume the business is sold at the end for 20 times profit. The ledger solves that at −0.21% a year.

In plain words, today's price assumes KRBL's profit never grows over ten years. The basis is trailing profit of ₹758 crore, derived as market capitalisation (₹9,024 crore) divided by the trailing price-to-earnings of 11.9.[1] That basis includes the unusually strong June 2026 quarter. We reran the ledger's formula on other profit figures: at ₹733 crore (about ₹25 crore less to strip some one-off other income, our estimate) the implied growth is +0.24% a year, and at the FY26 audited ₹648 crore it is +1.88% a year.[3] So the price assumes growth of about zero to 2% a year.

Realistic growth is above that. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 attacks our own work. Each estimate has one assumption that matters most, and each can be tested against a dated event. We state the bear case against our rejection, the weakest assumption in each estimate, the one claim our conclusion depends on and the date we will know.

Weakest assumption in the earnings estimate: a 17.5% margin on ₹6,400 crore of income

The estimate leans on the CFO's guide of 17% to 18% EBITDA margin and on total income rising about 4%.[9] The margin guide could be wrong in either direction. It could be too low, because management guided about 1% to 2% of Q1's margin to one-offs, which means most of the Q1 margin came from higher rice prices, and if prices hold, profit per share would run above our estimate. It could be too high, because paddy for the 2026 crop is still being bought and rice prices have already gone up as far as management says they can.[9] We will know when the Q2 FY27 result is filed, which should be in mid-November 2026 (the Q2 call was on 14 November in both 2024 and 2025, and the trading window has been closed since 1 October 2026).[13][11][1]

Weakest assumption in the multiple: 13.3 is a fair median

The 3-year median of 13.3 comes from one data source and covers a period in which the multiple ranged from 10.2 to 20.1.[2] If the market now treats the governance issue as permanent, the right multiple is lower than 13.3 and our base target of ₹441 is too high. If the audit qualification is lifted, the right multiple is higher. We will see this at the next audit report, due with FY27 results in about mid-May 2027, because the FY25 and FY26 audit reports were dated 16 May 2025 and 14 May 2026.[4][3]

The red-team bear case against rejecting the stock

The strongest case for owning KRBL runs like this. The balance sheet has ₹1,841 crore of cash and investments and almost no debt, so solvency is not the question.[7] The brand leads the market. Global rice stocks are tightening and a below-normal monsoon points to firm paddy and rice prices.[9] Hormuz is reopening, and importers in the Gulf have run down stocks, so a restock wave could drive exports sharply higher.[9] At 11.9 times earnings and a reverse DCF that assumes zero growth, the price already looks cheap, and a stock with the market's growth expectation this low can rerate fast when profit surprises.

We accept all of that and still reject, for two reasons. The first is the rules: a Stage 3 chart and a reward-to-risk of 1.06 fail the gates, and the scorecard fails at 2 of 5. The second is arithmetic: only the bull case, at a 20% weight, gets the ratio above 3.0 (3.47), and it still misses the 3.5 floor for a turnaround. Our rejection is therefore a rejection of this price, not of the business.
