1. What the company does

Data Patterns (India) Limited designs and builds electronic systems for defence and space: radars, electronic warfare units (equipment that listens to, or jams, enemy radio and radar signals), flight electronics, military radios, small satellites and the automated test equipment that proves each of these works.[4] It was incorporated in 1998 and works from one campus at Siruseri, near Chennai, with about 200,000 square feet built up; it had 1,112 engineers at June 2026.[4]

The buyers are almost all arms of the Indian state. The company names the Ministry of Defence, the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO), defence public sector companies such as Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) and Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL), and the space agency ISRO, plus some private and overseas customers.[4][3] The audited accounts do not name the three customers that each paid more than a tenth of FY26 revenue.[1]

Revenue comes in three kinds. Production orders ship finished units. Development orders pay the company to design a new system, usually in milestones as the customer's inspectors accept each stage. Service orders cover annual maintenance. In FY26, 58 percent of revenue was production, 37 percent development and 5 percent service.[4] Revenue is booked when a customer accepts work, so it arrives in lumps: the fourth quarter carried 37 percent of FY26 revenue and 55 percent of FY25 revenue.[6] The chairman says the company is 'a yearly business' with long projects and not a month-to-month producer, and that revenue can slip when customer inspectors do not arrive on time.[5]

That lumpy timing explains the cash story that decides this report. Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what suppliers are owed. Data Patterns holds parts and part-built systems for many months and is paid on acceptance, so its working-capital cycle, the days between paying for parts and receiving customer cash, was 365 days in FY26 and 428 days in FY25.[4][6]

Concentration and geography. In FY26 India supplied ₹862.97 crore of revenue (93.3 percent) and the rest of the world ₹61.80 crore (6.7 percent), down from ₹106.71 crore (15.1 percent) in FY25.[1] Three customers each paid more than a tenth of revenue, together ₹509.12 crore (55.1 percent), against two customers and ₹337.06 crore (47.6 percent) in FY25, and they owed ₹414.17 crore, 56.9 percent of trade receivables.[1] Radar and electronic warfare brought ₹487.06 crore (52.7 percent), flat on FY25.[1] What this means for an owner: profit rests on a few state buyers, exports are a plan and not yet a revenue line, and chapter 2 asks how strong the franchise is.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit.
  • EPS is earnings per share, profit divided by the number of shares; the price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share. The operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales and the gross margin is sales less the cost of materials as a share of sales.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash a business earns after tax and before spending on plant. Free cash flow (FCF) is that cash less money spent on plant, equipment and capitalised development. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' funds.
  • Guidance is what management says it expects to deliver.
  • RSI is the relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has moved. ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price swing, which we use to measure chart distances. The 20-day, 50-day and 200-day averages are the average closing prices of the last 20, 50 and 200 sessions.
  • Stage 2 is a rising trend above rising averages, and Stage 4 is a falling trend below falling averages. A base is a flat stretch of trading after a decline, its pivot is the top of that stretch, and a breakout is a close above the pivot on higher volume. A retest is a pullback to a level the price just broke through.
  • A stop loss is the price at which we would sell to limit a loss. A limit order is an order to buy at a stated price or lower. The entry zone is the price range in which we would be willing to buy. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop; the Tactical Momentum floor is 2.5 to 1. Chapter 2 now asks how strong the franchise is.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The franchise is strong where it is hard to copy and weaker where it depends on a few state decisions. Data Patterns builds the boards, software and finished systems itself, so a buyer of a radar or jammer pod deals with one supplier that carries the design risk.[4] The company says it has spent more than ₹135 crore on product development and about ₹185 crore on plant in five years, with ₹150 crore more planned.[4] The qualification barrier is real, but it protects a short customer list governed by government budgets.

Competitive position: what is proven and what is claimed

Proven by filings: a 40.1 percent operating margin in FY26, a 63.2 percent gross margin, a 20.8 percent return on capital and no borrowings.[4][2] Proven by third parties: Crisil rates the company's bank facilities A+ with a positive outlook (revised from stable on 7 September 2026) and ICRA rates them A+ with a stable outlook (upgraded on 24 December 2025); both cover ₹725 crore of bank guarantees, which are bank promises to pay a customer if the company fails to deliver, and a small cash credit line.[17][18] Claimed by management: a move from parts to complete systems and single-vendor orders for equipment already developed.[5][6] This is plausible, since nine precision approach radars went to the Army and Navy and one was exported to Europe, but it is a statement about the future.[4]

What this means for an owner: the weakness is not credit risk but the timing of cash, which chapter 3 tests.

Demand and the indigenisation tailwind

Indigenisation, the policy of buying defence equipment from Indian makers instead of importers, is the main demand driver. On 7 September 2026 the Defence Acquisition Council approved about ₹1.10 lakh crore of purchases and the Defence Minister said about 98 percent would come from Indian industry; the Times of India reported this (secondary).[26] India's defence exports reached a record ₹38,424 crore in FY26 (secondary).[27]

Data Patterns has not yet shared in the export surge. Its audited overseas revenue was ₹61.80 crore in FY26 against ₹106.71 crore in FY25, and its international order book was ₹39 crore at 30 June 2026, about 4 percent of the ₹928 crore firm book.[1][4] Management says it is building an export group and expects 'a multimillion dollar business' within months, which we treat as a plan; the only filed proof is one radar delivered to a European customer.[5][4] What this means for an owner: the home market is the case today, and exports are a bonus the numbers do not yet show.

Order book and execution

The firm order book was ₹927.7 crore at 30 June 2026, up from ₹730 crore a year earlier but level with March, and about 0.99 times trailing-twelve-month revenue of ₹941.47 crore on our arithmetic.[4][3][35] Orders received in FY26 were ₹1,121 crore against about ₹355 crore in FY25, which gives a book-to-bill ratio, orders received divided by revenue, of 1.21; first-quarter FY27 inflow was only ₹117 crore, down 36.1 percent from a year earlier.[4] A ₹585.76 crore Bharat Electronics radar order, announced in August, is not in the June book.[14]

Management also quotes a larger figure of ₹2,654 crore that includes orders it has negotiated but not received; subtracting the firm book leaves about ₹1,726 crore of negotiated orders on our arithmetic.[3][35] These have slipped. On 6 February 2026 the chairman was asked whether ₹1,100 crore of negotiated orders, plus another ₹500 crore, would arrive that quarter and replied 'It should happen this quarter'; by 31 July he said the programme had been 'stretched by more than 1.5 years' and the customer had asked for 'another 2 months'.[7][5] He also said the negotiated figure excludes a HAL contract, reported at about ₹1,300 crore, where the company is lowest bidder (media, secondary).[5][25] Until signed, these are hopes, not backlog.

What this means for an owner: the firm book covers one year of revenue, against two years in FY23 and FY24, and the larger figure depends on customers' approval calendars.[4]

Where the strength is thin

Second, costs: first-quarter FY27 staff cost rose 16.9 percent and other expenses 63.5 percent while revenue rose 16.8 percent, so the operating margin fell to 27 percent from 32 percent.[4][3] The company cites plant-upgrade repairs and about ₹2 crore of provision against old receivables.[5] Third, an independent director left on 9 September 2026 at term end, citing other commitments, which is not a resignation.[16]

The promoters hold 42.41 percent, unchanged for thirteen quarters, with no pledge in any filing (chapter 3).[20][21] On 25 August 2026 the company agreed to buy ST Advanced Composites for about ₹10 crore; completion is not confirmed.[15] What this means for an owner: the strengths are real, costs are rising faster than revenue, and chapter 2B scores the operating measures.
