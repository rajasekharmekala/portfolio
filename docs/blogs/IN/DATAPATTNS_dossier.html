What the company does

This chapter describes the business before any judgment, because the verdict depends on what kind of revenue the company earns. Data Patterns designs and manufactures electronics for defence and aerospace customers. Its own profile says it works across radars, electronic warfare suites, communication systems, avionics, satellites and test equipment, and that it supplies products for the Tejas light combat aircraft, the Light Utility Helicopter and the BrahMos missile.[7] The company was incorporated in 1998 and reports 1,599 employees, of whom 1,096 are engineers.[1] That mix of engineers to staff matters, because the product is design-led work rather than volume manufacturing.

The business in one paragraph

Think of Data Patterns as a specialist design house that builds the electronic brain of a larger defence system. A radar, an electronic warfare suite or a missile seeker is assembled by a larger integrator, but the sensing and processing boards inside it are often designed and tested by a firm like this one. The company's own filings describe a single business segment, defence electronics, covering design, development, manufacture, sale and service.[25] That structure means revenue arrives in lumps as orders convert, and margins depend on how much of the work the company does in-house.

The business is also long-cycle. ICRA's December 2025 rating rationale describes long gestation periods, because the research and development cycle for defence products can run for several years, and it notes that projects can be deferred by procedural delays.[4] For a reader, the practical point is that a strong order book does not guarantee revenue this quarter. The company must first build, test, qualify and deliver the product, and the customer must accept it. Each of those steps has its own delay risk, and the rest of this dossier returns to that point more than once.

The ownership structure is concentrated. The promoter group holds 42.41 percent of the shares as of June 2026, and none of that holding is pledged, with a pledged share of 0.0 percent.[8] Foreign portfolio investors hold 12.50 percent in the same quarter, up from 12.47 percent in March 2026.[7] A pledge-free promoter stake removes one common warning sign, which is that the founders have borrowed against their shares. It does not remove the cash risk, which is the subject of the second chapter.

Who pays the company

The customers are a short list of public-sector and government bodies. ICRA names the Ministry of Defence, the Defence Research and Development Organisation, BrahMos Aerospace, Bharat Electronics, Hindustan Aeronautics, Electronics Corporation of India and units of the Indian Space Research Organisation as major customers.[4] The company's own release says it works closely with defence PSUs such as Hindustan Aeronautics and Bharat Electronics, and with DRDO and ISRO.[3] Concentration like this has two faces. A few large customers give steady demand and repeat orders. But a single customer that delays acceptance can move a whole quarter's revenue, which is what happened in the first quarter of FY27.

The company's own explanation for the weak quarter was that revenue was modest because of temporary delays in customer approvals. The gates in the next chapter test whether this order book turns into cash, which is the real question. The chairman said those delays were expected to normalise in the coming quarters.[3] Treat that statement as the company's view. It is a reason to watch the next two quarters closely, not a fact that the quarter will recover.

The order book and the latest order

The order book is the total value of contracts the company has in hand and has yet to deliver. It is the closest thing this business has to a revenue pipeline. The annual report puts the order book at ₹926 crore on 31 March 2026, and the company's first-quarter release puts it at ₹928 crore on 30 June 2026.[1][3] When orders that have been negotiated but not yet placed are added, the company reports ₹2,654 crore.[3] Those are three different numbers, and the difference between them matters. The first two are firm orders. The third includes negotiated work that could still slip.

The company disclosed a new order intimation dated 21 August 2026.[14] Press reports put its value at ₹585.76 crore, for radar electronics from Bharat Electronics Limited.[15][16] Against FY26 total revenue of ₹953 crore, that one order equals about 61.5 percent of a full year's revenue, which is a large single contract by any standard. Read that as evidence of demand, and also as evidence of concentration: one customer's schedule will drive a large part of the next year's sales.

New programmes and the acquisition

The company has been expanding into missile electronics. Its chairman told the trade press that the company expects orders for its indigenous seeker systems for the BrahMos missile before the end of FY2026-27, and that the seeker products are ready while qualification, including flight trials, is still under way.[17] The same report says the company had first expected the process to move faster, and that delays had pushed the order timeline back. A seeker is the sensor at the front of a missile, so a successful programme would be high value. It is also a programme where timing has already slipped once.

The company also agreed to buy ST Advanced Composites, a Chennai composite parts maker, for a consideration the company puts at ₹10 crore.[3] The Screener announcement list dates the share purchase agreement to 25 August 2026 at about ₹10 crore.[7] A MarketScreener news line gives a figure of ₹15 million, which is ₹1.5 crore, for the same company.[9] The two figures differ by a factor of about six. The purpose is clear, because the company says it will bring composite parts in-house for its radar work. The price does not change the verdict, but the conflict is recorded in the run notes and it is a reason to check the filing before relying on either number. Financial health: return on capital and debt

Return on capital employed, or ROCE, measures how much operating profit the company earns on all the money tied up in the business. A ROCE of 15 percent is the rulebook's floor, and 18 percent is the preference for core names. The FY26 annual report states ROCE of 23 percent, up from 19 percent in FY25, and a return on net worth of 17 percent.[1] The FY25 annual report, by contrast, printed a ROCE of 22 percent for the same year.[2] The later report restates FY25 at 19 percent, so the company's definition appears to have changed. Either figure clears the gate. The FY26 figure is used here because it is the latest audited number.

The debt gate is the easiest to pass. The company reports a debt to equity ratio of 0 for FY26 and says it has remained debt free as of 31 March 2026.[1] Debt-to-equity is borrowed money divided by the owners' capital, so zero means no borrowing. An aggregator reports the same zero debt-to-equity figure in its statistics.[10] A company with no borrowings cannot be squeezed by lenders, which is the point of the gate. The company's net worth reached ₹1,736 crore at the end of FY26, up 15 percent in the year, which is mostly retained profit.[1] Strength on this gate is not in doubt. The question is whether the strength shows up in cash, which is the next gate.

Cash conversion: the gate that fails

Cash conversion asks whether reported profit becomes cash the company can use. The rulebook takes three years of operating cash flow and divides it by three years of profit, and requires at least 0.70. A ratio near 1.0 means the profit is backed by cash. A ratio near 0.2 means most of the profit is still sitting in unpaid bills or stock. For a defence supplier that is paid on delivery and acceptance, a low ratio is a warning rather than a surprise, but the rulebook does not make exceptions for industry reasons.

The audited figures are: operating cash flow of ₹139.38 crore in FY24, negative ₹89.88 crore in FY25 and ₹80.15 crore in FY26. Profit after tax was ₹181.69 crore, ₹221.81 crore and ₹271.37 crore in the same years.[2][1] Summing the three years gives ₹129.65 crore of cash against ₹674.87 crore of profit, so the cumulative ratio is 0.19. The aggregator's cash-flow table shows the same three operating cash flow figures in millions, and matches the audited numbers once converted.[11] The prior record also showed 0.19, so the figure is not in dispute. Only the gate result changes.

The gate fails by a wide margin. The FY24 year alone clears the floor at 0.77, but the FY25 year was negative and FY26 reached only 0.30. The reason is in the balance sheet. Trade receivables rose from ₹596 crore to ₹728 crore in FY26, which is 22 percent growth against 26 percent growth in total revenue, and the cash flow statement shows a receivables build of ₹136.18 crore in FY26 and ₹198.79 crore in FY25.[1] Net working capital is the cash tied up in receivables and inventory, less the bills the company has not yet paid. The rating agency reports that it runs at 105 percent of operating income, and projects it to stay between 90 and 100 percent in the medium term.[4] That is the mechanism. The company books revenue at milestones, holds large inventories of components, and waits for customer acceptance before cash arrives.

Technical posture: the trend test

The technical gate asks whether the price is in a confirmed uptrend. For a tactical candidate, that means Weinstein Stage 2: the price is above a 50-day average that is rising, and above a 200-day average that is also rising. A Stage 3 reading means the uptrend has stopped and the stock is topping or pulling back. A Stage 4 reading means it has broken down, which the rules treat as a hard exit. The ledger puts DATAPATTNS in Stage 3 as of 9 October 2026.

Two of the three conditions are checked directly. The close of ₹4,084.8 is above the 200-day average of ₹3,769.59, and that average has risen from ₹3,242.32 sixty-three days earlier, so the long trend is intact. The close is below the 50-day average of ₹4,494.35, and that average has moved from ₹4,490.77 twenty-one days ago to ₹4,494.35 now, a change of 0.08 percent. It was ₹4,522.92 ten days ago, so the average has been flat to falling. The rule requires the 50-day to be rising, so this condition fails. There is no Stage 4 breakdown and no death cross, because the 50-day average is still above the 200-day average. That is why the stock fails the posture gate rather than being classed as broken.

Asymmetry and the target anchor

The asymmetry gate is the reward-to-risk test. The target must be at least 3.5 times the distance to the stop for a tactical name. The rulebook also says the target must be anchored, which means it must come from one of two accepted methods: forward earnings multiplied by the company's three-year median price-to-earnings ratio (the share price divided by earnings per share), or a measured move from a technical base. A target without one of those anchors is unverified, and the rulebook says an unverified target is an automatic FAIL_ASYMMETRY. The method is strict on purpose, because a target picked to look good is the most common way a bad idea gets a good score.

The run could not complete either method. Forward earnings are available as consensus estimates: ₹62.36 per share for FY27 and ₹82.33 for FY28.[9] But no source in the run gave a historical price-to-earnings series that would allow a three-year median. The chart on the MarketScreener page is not in the cached text, and the aggregator statistics give only current ratios.[10] The second method needs the height of a base, which is the gap between the base's low and its breakout pivot, the price a stock must clear to leave the base. The ledger does not supply that. Its base measure runs from the 63-day low upward, which is a distance, not a target. The asymmetry line is therefore set to N/A and the gate fails. The arithmetic is shown in the asymmetry chapter, for information only.

The analyst consensus is a reasonable reference, but it is not one of the two anchors. Eleven analysts average a target of ₹4,578.45, which is about 12 percent above the ledger close.[10] Against the stop of ₹3,972.3, that would be an asymmetry of about 4.4 to 1, which clears the 3.5 floor. The rules do not accept it as the anchor, so the gate is still a FAIL. The point is that the candidate is close to passing on reward-to-risk, but the rules require a verified target, and the run could not supply one.

The momentum gate

The momentum gate is a second, separate test. Every candidate must pass Test A (already in momentum) or Test B (entering momentum), computed by code from the frozen price snapshot. Failing both means no allocation, however good the business. Test A requires the price above the 50-day, the 50-day above the 200-day, the 50-day rising, and the price within 1.2 ATR (average true range) of its support anchor. DATAPATTNS fails the first and last of those, because the close is below the 50-day and sits 1.24 ATR above the 63-day base low.

Test B requires the 50-day to be holding, the relative strength against the benchmark to be rising over 28 days, the price near a base pivot, a volume dry-up, and a volume expansion on the breakout. The stock passes the 50-day holding condition, with a change of 0.08 percent against a 0.5 percent allowance, and it shows a volume dry-up with a ratio of 0.23. It fails the rising relative strength, which is minus 4.83 percent over 28 days, the pivot distance of 1.24 ATR against a maximum of 1.0, and volume expansion, where the 20-day volume multiple is 0.61 against a minimum of 1.0. Both tests fail. The gate verdict is NO_MOMENTUM, the label for a name that fails both tests, and the rules allocate nothing.

What would change this view: momentum

  • Test A passes if the close rises above ₹4,494.35, the 50-day average turns up over 21 sessions, and the price stays within 1.2 ATR (about ₹179.9) of its support anchor.
  • Test B needs the 28-day relative strength to turn positive and the 20-day volume multiple to reach 1.0. It also needs the close to sit no more than 1.0 ATR above the 63-day base low of ₹3,899.0, which is about ₹4,048.9.
  • Either test passing alone does not allocate capital. The stock would still need the cash gate and a verified target before a buy.

Glossary

These terms come up in the dossier. Each is defined once in plain words, so a newcomer can follow the reasoning without a finance background.

ATR (average true range). The typical size of a stock's daily move over the last 14 days, in rupees. A stop placed half an ATR below a level gives the stock room to wobble without triggering a sale on normal noise.

CFO (cash flow from operations). This is the cash a business generates from its core activity in a year, after paying suppliers and staff and after tax. Unlike profit, it cannot be inflated by accounting entries alone.

Consensus. The average of the forecasts from several analysts. It shows what the market expects, not what will happen.

Death cross. A chart pattern in which the 50-day average falls below the 200-day average. The rulebook treats it as a warning sign when it occurs on heavy volume.

Forward P/E. The share price divided by the expected earnings for the next financial year. It tells you how much you pay for next year's profit.

NWC / OI (net working capital to operating income). The money tied up in receivables and inventory, net of payables, as a share of annual operating income. A high figure means the company must fund a large part of its sales before collecting cash.

ROCE (return on capital employed). Operating profit divided by the capital used in the business, including borrowings. It measures how well the company uses the money invested in it.

ROIC (return on invested capital). A similar profit-to-capital measure, shown for peers because it is available on the same basis for all of them.

Stage 2, Stage 3, Stage 4. Weinstein's cycle labels: the markup, the top or pullback, and the decline. Only Stage 2 qualifies for a tactical buy under this rulebook. The run notes below list every exception behind the numbers in this dossier, so the reader can see what the verdict rests on.

Holdings assumption and the position conflict

Why this matters: the holding rules and the challenger rules give different answers, and the difference is large. The fix is outside this run. It requires either a corrected transaction file or a written explanation of where the 35 shares sit, and the holdings log remains the authoritative record until one of those exists.

Stale as-of dates and the 60-day rule

The FY26 annual figures are as of 31 March 2026, which is 192 days before the 9 October data date. The FY25 figures are 557 days old, and the FY24 figures are older still. The first-quarter FY27 results and the June 2026 shareholding are 101 days old. Cure dates: the next quarterly figures come with the Q2 FY27 results, which an aggregator estimates for 30 October 2026 but the company has not confirmed. The next audited figures come with the FY27 annual report, after the 31 March 2027 year-end, and no date for it has been announced. Using the FY26 report is therefore the only way to produce a current verdict, and the gate result would not change with a later quarter unless the cash figures moved sharply. The verdict is marked as based on the latest available filings, which is what the reader needs to know.

Cache and source hygiene

Several cache entries that existed before this run did not hold the text of the page they named. The annual report entry held a short hand-typed excerpt, and the PDF entries held raw file bytes. Each of those entries was replaced by a fresh fetch, and the PDFs were converted to text and cached under their original addresses using the cache script's local-file option. No cache file was edited by hand, and every record's quoted text was pulled by code from the cached page. The run used 25 distinct sources in the records and cites them in the notes below.

Why this matters: a number that cannot be traced to a page the run actually read is a number nobody can check. The cache rule exists to prevent a verdict resting on a remembered figure. The three replaced entries were the only ones whose content did not match their address, and the fresh copies were checked against the quoted figures in the records. A reader who wants to repeat the check can start from the record file, follow each source address, and confirm the quoted sentence appears in the page text.
