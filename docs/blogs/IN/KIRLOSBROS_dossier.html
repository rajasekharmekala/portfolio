Kirloskar Brothers (KBL) is India's oldest pump maker, founded in 1888. It builds pumps, valves, motors and the engineered systems around them for irrigation, power stations, oil and gas, buildings, defence and nuclear plants. About half of what it sells is standard pumps made to stock, and the rest is built to a customer's drawing.[5] This page decides whether the stock earns a place on the shortlist as a Core Alpha holding. Ten ideas carry the rest of the page. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, a trend line that smooths out daily noise; the 50-day average is the medium-term line and the 200-day is the long-term line. A Weinstein stage is one of four seasons in a stock's life: Stage 1 is a sideways base (late winter), Stage 2 is the advance (spring and summer), Stage 3 is the top (autumn) and Stage 4 is the decline (winter). RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer from 0 to 100; below 30 means the stock has fallen unusually fast, above 70 means it has risen unusually fast. ATR, the average true range, is the stock's normal daily wobble in rupees, and we use it to place stops so that ordinary noise does not knock us out. Volume is how many shares changed hands, a count of how many people voted with their money. A stop-loss is an exit price you decide before you buy. Risk and reward compares what you could win with what you could lose; dividing the first by the second gives the asymmetry ratio. Position size is how much of your portfolio you put in one stock, which decides whether a wrong call is a nuisance or a wound. ROCE is the operating profit earned per rupee of capital tied up in the business. CFO/PAT compares cash the business actually generated with the accounting profit it reported.

The rulebook has three layers. Layer 1 is three financial floors that every candidate must clear: ROCE of at least 15%, debt to equity below 0.70, and three-year operating cash flow of at least 70% of profit. Layer 2 is three to five numbers specific to this company that make or break its story. Layer 3 is the chart: a Core Alpha candidate must be in a Stage 1 base breakout or a Stage 2 advance, pass a momentum test, and offer at least three rupees of reward for every rupee risked. Fail any hard gate and the action is REJECTED. The labels you will see are IMMEDIATE_BUY, ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP, WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT and REJECTED; a rejection on price grounds is filed as REJECTED_BELOW_HURDLE, which means the company is fine but today's price does not pay for the risk.

Company history, ownership and governance

The Kirloskar family business began in 1888 and KBL was incorporated in 1920. Its first indigenous centrifugal pump came in 1926, at a time when India imported almost all of its pumping equipment.[5][11] A centrifugal pump moves liquid by spinning it outward inside a casing, and it is the workhorse of water supply, irrigation and industry. Owning that product line for a century gives the company a brand, a dealer network and a service business on top of an installed base, which is why spare parts and service appear as their own line in the order book.

The growth in scale came by acquisition. SPP Pumps in the United Kingdom was bought in 2003 and SPP in the United States in 2004. The Dutch group KBI B.V. followed in 2007, the same year as the Kolhapur Steel castings business, and Rodelta in the Netherlands in 2015.[11][13] These purchases explain why about 36% of FY26 revenue (₹1,628 Cr of ₹4,538 Cr) now comes from the international subsidiaries, and why those subsidiaries earn lower margins than the Indian parent.[13] The group operates ten plants in India and seven overseas, so it carries the fixed cost of a global footprint on a mid-sized revenue base.

Promoters hold 65.95% and that figure has not moved for years. Foreign investors hold 6.41%, domestic institutions 10.23% and the public 17.40% across 64,065 holders (June 2026).[1][3] About a third of the shares are therefore free to trade, which is thin for a company worth ₹13,421 Cr. A thin float means price moves on small volume; the session of 28 September 2026 saw about 457,358 shares trade and an intraday high of ₹1,936.10.[36] The rulebook treats such a session as a signal only when the volume repeats on the way up, and it has not.

The statutory auditor is Sharp & Tannan Associates, with an unmodified opinion on the FY26 accounts. The consolidated audit report does note exceptions in the audit-trail (edit-log) feature of the accounting software at the holding company, two subsidiaries and a joint venture, which is a control point to watch rather than a restatement.[7] The key audit matter is revenue recognition on long-term contracts, about ₹686 Mn in FY26, because revenue on those contracts is booked as work progresses and depends on management's estimate of completion.[7] A promoter share pledge is N/A: none is disclosed in the annual report or on Screener, but the quarterly BSE shareholding pattern was not read in full, so it is recorded as not verified rather than as zero.

Recent governance filings are routine. A postal ballot notice of 15 September 2026 proposes an independent director, Dr. Madhuri Kanitkar; a Regulation 74(5) certificate was filed on 10 October 2026; and the trading window closed on 26 September 2026 ahead of the Q2 results.[27][28][29] One inconsistency deserves a flag: page 29 of the annual report describes an "AA+" rating from CRISIL, while the CRISIL rating rationale itself says AA with a Positive outlook. The rating document is the primary source, so AA/Positive is used throughout.[7][24]

What would change this view

  • If the BSE shareholding pattern for the September 2026 quarter shows any promoter pledge, the governance reading drops from clean to cautious and the position cap in any future file is cut.
  • If promoter holding falls below 60% from 65.95%, treat it as a signal to re-read the filings, because a stake that stable for years rarely moves without a reason.
  • If the auditor's report for FY27 repeats the audit-trail exceptions at the same entities, upgrade the concern from a note to a control-quality finding.

Ownership and governance are not the obstacle to this stock. What the business earns, and from where, is the next question.

What this means for you: a stable family-controlled owner with no pledge disclosed is a positive. The thin float is a risk to your exit, not to the thesis, so any future position should be sized with the knowledge that it could take several sessions to build or sell without moving the price.

Operations and revenue model

KBL makes money in four ways. It sells catalogue pumps through dealers (made to stock, about 50% of FY26 revenue), builds pumps to a customer's specification (made to order, 27%), engineers large custom pumps and systems (engineered to order, 19%), and runs turnkey projects (3%).[15] Made-to-stock earns a steady margin and turns over quickly because inventory is sold from shelves. Engineered pumps and projects are lumpy, but they sit in a written order book that gives visibility months ahead. The ratio between the two explains why the company's standalone order book is a poor measure of half the business.

Sector dynamics

Pump demand follows four public and private spending cycles: irrigation and water supply (government budgets), power (thermal, nuclear and pumped-storage capital spending), oil and gas, and buildings. KBL's standalone order book is led by irrigation at ₹1,013 Cr, a government-paced business with long approval cycles. Power follows at ₹599 Cr, then buildings at ₹233 Cr.[12] A company with this mix does not sell to one cycle. When irrigation budgets slow, power and oil and gas orders can fill the gap; when power slows, the reverse can happen. That spread is the main reason the order book has been stable even when reported profit has not.

The rating agencies publish the clearest outside view of where the sector is expected to go. CRISIL expects revenue growth of 10 to 12% a year, EBITDA margins of 13 to 14% and capital spending of ₹150 to 200 Cr a year, with cash accruals above ₹500 Cr.[24] CARE's December 2025 rationale looked backward and found a revenue CAGR of about 13% and an EBITDA CAGR of 24% for FY21 to FY25.[21] Both documents describe a company in an upcycle with a wide margin of safety on debt service. The question for an equity buyer is different: whether profit keeps compounding from here, because the price already reflects a 33.2 times multiple on trailing earnings.

Glossary

ATR (average true range)
The typical daily price range of a stock in rupees. KBL's is ₹58.76, and the stop is placed one ATR below the base low.
Asymmetry ratio
Reward divided by risk. Reward is target price minus entry; risk is entry minus stop. The Core floor is 3.0.
Base low
The lowest price in the recent sideways range. For KBL it is ₹1,640 over 63 sessions.
CFO/PAT
Cash from operations divided by profit after tax. A ratio near or above 1 means profit is turning into cash.
Distribution day
A session when an index or stock falls on higher volume than the day before, a sign that large holders are selling.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation; a measure of operating profit.
Gate
A pass-or-fail rule. A failed hard gate rejects the stock.
Moving average
The average closing price over a set number of days (20, 50 or 200 here), which smooths daily noise into a trend line.
OPM
Operating profit margin: operating profit divided by sales.
P/E
Price divided by earnings per share. KBL's is 33.2 on Screener's figure.
ROCE
Return on capital employed: operating profit divided by the capital used in the business.
RSI
Relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of recent price momentum.
Stage
The Weinstein cycle: 1 base, 2 advance, 3 top, 4 decline. KBL is in Stage 1.
Stop-loss
A predetermined exit price. KBL's would be ₹1,581.
TTM
Trailing twelve months, the most recent four quarters added together.
