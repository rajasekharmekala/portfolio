Saregama owns about 180,000 songs outright and earns most of its money by licensing them to streaming services, brands and films[3]. Our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, because the business is sound but the ₹536 price already assumes 17.9% to 26.6% yearly growth for ten years[1][3]. We would buy only on a pullback to between ₹478 and ₹490, with a stop at ₹453.29, where the reward is 2.29 times the risk; at today's price it is 0.46 times. The hinge is the twelve-month profit margin on music after paying for new content, which was 46.3% in FY26: if it falls below 42% in any quarterly report, we are wrong; management plans ₹300 to 350 crore of new music this year, which is what puts the margin at risk[3][4][6]. 1. What the company does

Saregama is India's oldest music label. It began in 1902 as the Gramophone Company of India, sold records under the HMV name, and today belongs to the RP-Sanjiv Goenka (RPSG) group.[1][2] The company now describes itself as an entertainment intellectual-property business. That phrase matters because it owns the rights, not just the copies: more than 180,000 songs, kept in perpetuity and worldwide, plus 70 films, 55 digital series and more than 10,000 hours of television.[3] Think of it as a landlord who owns a large block of buildings that never wear out. Every time someone streams a song, plays it in a film, or uses it in an advertisement, Saregama collects rent.

The rent comes from three kinds of payers. The largest is the platform: services such as Spotify, YouTube, Netflix and JioHotstar pay licence fees. The second is the brand: companies such as Myntra, Unilever and Hero pay to put a song into an advertisement. The third is the direct customer, who buys a Carvaan music player, a vinyl record or a concert ticket.[3] By revenue, music (licensing, artiste management and retail) was 83% of FY26, live events 6% and video 11%.[3][6] The investor deck splits FY26 revenue by buyer into platform, brand and direct-customer groups; the text we could extract prints those percentages ambiguously, so we do not quote them. One analyst on the August 2026 call put the brand share at 19%. The company declined to give the share from paid subscriptions, calling it competition-sensitive.[3][4]

The accounting for new music is the key to reading this company. When Saregama buys the rights to a new film album, the cash leaves on day one, but the cost is charged against profit over about ten years. Management says a new song pays back its cost in about five years and then keeps earning for 60 to 80 years, and it quotes a 26% internal rate of return on new music.[3] In plain words, profit lags cash. In years when the company buys a lot of music, reported profit looks healthy but cash left over is thin. That is exactly what happened in FY26, and it is why this report tracks cash after content spending as closely as it tracks profit.

Beside the music engine sit three smaller businesses. Artiste management represents 309 singers, comedians and online creators with a combined reach above 440 million followers; Saregama earns a share when they perform at weddings, concerts or in brand deals.[4] Live events cover concerts, devotional shows and a festival called UN40. Video covers television serials for Sun TV and Udaya TV, digital series and short clips made with the Pocket Aces team, and a film business that is being wound down.[3][6] Carvaan, the pre-loaded retro-music speaker, is now sold almost entirely through e-commerce and was about 7% of FY26 revenue.[15] The company also put ₹325 crore into Sanjay Leela Bhansali's Bhansali Productions in January 2026, in return for first rights to its film music.[13][14]

The owners are the RPSG group companies, which hold 60.84% of the shares, with no pledge against those shares as of September 2026.[18] The market value is about ₹10,344 crore at ₹536 a share. The September 2026 quarter results and a call with analysts are scheduled for 4 November 2026.[1][5]

A short vocabulary for the rest of the report

Price terms come first. The RSI is a meter from 0 to 100 that shows how fast a price has been moving; its full name is the relative strength index. The ATR is the typical daily swing of a stock in rupees; its full name is the average true range. A base is a sideways stretch of trading where a stock rests before its next move. The pivot is the top of that base. A breakout is a close above the pivot on heavy volume. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.

Order terms come next. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, so it never chases a rising stock. The entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy. The asymmetry, also called the reward-to-risk ratio, is the gain we expect to the target divided by the loss we would take at the stop. A ratio of 2.0 means two rupees of expected gain for each rupee at risk.

Accounting terms finish the list. The operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue, and the net margin is final profit as a share of revenue. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying the assets it needs. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. The price-to-earnings ratio is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit. Guidance is the company's own forecast for the coming period. Working capital is the cash tied up in day-to-day operations, such as unpaid customer bills and stock.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Catalogue: the set of songs a label owns. Saregama's is about 180,000 songs.
  • Licensing: letting someone use a song for a fee while the label keeps ownership.
  • Content charge: the yearly cost taken against profit for newly bought music, spread over about ten years.
  • Music net margin: music profit left after the content charge, the best measure of whether new music is paying for itself.
  • Adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization): Saregama's own profit measure before the content charge. Screener's operating profit is after it, so the two differ.
  • Carvaan: Saregama's pre-loaded retro-music speaker, now a small part of sales.
  • Rolling 12 months: the last four quarters added together. Management asks to be judged this way because releases move between quarters.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest feature is ownership. Saregama holds the master recordings and the underlying works of its songs in perpetuity, so a hit song keeps earning for decades without being bought again.[3] CARE Ratings, which rates the company AA- with a stable outlook, lists the largest music archive in India among its strengths, and notes that 56% of licensing revenue now comes from songs of the 21st century.[15] Management says 60% of FY26 music revenue came from music released after 2000, and 45% of that from after 2020.[4] That cuts both ways. It proves the company can buy hits. It also means Saregama is less of a stable old-song annuity than its image suggests, because it must keep winning new albums to keep growing.

The second strength is the shift to paid streaming. Management cites a Goldman Sachs estimate that paid streaming reaches under 3% of internet users in India, against 57% in the United States, and says each extra percentage point is about 14 million subscribers.[8] Platforms share about half of subscription income with labels, so the owner of a large catalogue gains when more users pay.[10] The honest limit is evidence. Saregama does not disclose its subscription income, so this tailwind is a management claim we can only see indirectly in the growth of licensing revenue.

The weaknesses are concentration and cyclicality. Streaming and video platforms are the main buyers of the catalogue, and the company does not disclose any single platform's share, so a small number of buyers can set the price.[3][4] The FY25 shutdowns of Airtel Wynk, Hungama and Resso, and the move of Gaana behind a paywall, removed free-service payments and held music growth to about 12% that year.[9] Music revenue fell to ₹166 crore in the June 2025 quarter from ₹187 crore in the March 2025 quarter, which is also why the June 2026 growth looks so large.[3] Hit-driven spending adds a second kind of swing: a film album can be pushed from one quarter to another, as the Bhansali film Love and War was, and the revenue moves with it.

Competition comes from other labels such as Tips Music, T-Series and global majors, which compete to buy the same film albums. Saregama says it will fight for 25% to 30% of new music and has refused to overpay: it declined the second Dhurandhar album because the price did not fit its five-year payback rule.[4][8] The Bhansali agreement is meant to take pressure off the Hindi film bidding by giving first rights to a marquee studio, with the aim of sourcing 30% to 40% of Hindi film music that way.[17] Management admits one gap, Punjabi music, and says it is rebuilding it.[8] A last structural weakness is that the company owns the audio of its 1950s to 1980s songs but not the original music videos, which limits how well the old catalogue sells on YouTube.[4]

Why the three obvious risks are real but manageable

Obsolescence of listening formats, piracy and the high price of good content are the three risks CARE names.[15] Management argues that human-made, rights-cleared music becomes more valuable as artificial-intelligence music spreads, and says it is exploring licences with generative-AI companies rather than spending on its own models.[8] That is a reasonable stance, but it is unproven. We treat AI as a risk to the price of a licence, not as a certainty either way, and we look for it in the growth rate of licensing revenue.

Balance sheet risk is small. Debt-to-equity was 0.06 in the standalone accounts and interest cover 127.9 times, and the borrowings on the June 2026 balance sheet are described as temporary use of working-capital limits.[3][16] What this means for an owner: the business can survive a bad year, so the question is price, not survival. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

The walk-the-talk audit compares what management promised in May and November 2025 with what the filings and calls show a year later. The pattern is a team that delivers on profit margin and discipline, slips on timing and on the size of content spending, and reverses itself on video.[9][10]

On spending, management promised over ₹1,000 crore of new music between FY25 and FY27. FY25 delivered ₹316 crore. In November 2025 it cut the FY26 outlook to ₹320 to 330 crore because film albums were pushed, and FY26 ended at ₹235 crore of new music plus ₹105 crore of catalogue. CARE counts ₹339 crore for FY26, so the total is about on plan, though the mix moved to bought catalogues.[9][10][8][15] On video, management said in May 2025 that the vertical would grow 25% a year for five years. By December 2025 it announced the Bhansali investment, and FY26 video revenue fell about 43%; the films business is being wound down.[9][13][17]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue rose from ₹576 crore in FY22 to ₹985 crore in FY26, a three-year compound growth of 10% a year and a four-year rate of 14% a year from the FY22 figure.[1] That gap is the main fact in the numbers. The path was not smooth. FY25 revenue of ₹1,171 crore included the Diljit Dosanjh concert tour, which lifted the December 2024 quarter to ₹483 crore; FY26 therefore fell 16% even though the music business grew.[1][15] Profit grew far more slowly than revenue: net profit went from ₹153 crore to ₹206 crore, a three-year compound growth of only 4%, because the yearly charge for acquired music and other amortisation rose from ₹13 crore to ₹82 crore.[1]

Two profit measures appear in this report, and they are not the same. Screener's operating profit is taken after the content charge and shows a 34% margin for FY26. Saregama's own adjusted EBITDA is taken before the content charge and shows 41%.[1][3] The first is the better guide to what the business keeps. The second shows how well the existing catalogue earns. We use Screener's definition in the five-year tables. We use the company's definition when quoting management guidance. We label which is which.

Cash tells a more careful story than profit. Over FY24 to FY26 operating cash flow added up to ₹524 crore against profit of ₹608 crore, a ratio of 0.86, which comfortably passes our 0.70 floor.[1] But Saregama counts the purchase of new music as an investing outflow, not an operating one: ₹265.3 crore in FY26 and ₹213.4 crore in FY25.[7] Take that spending out of the operating cash flow. The cash left over in FY26 was then negative. A reader who looks only at profit would miss this.

The balance sheet is simple. Borrowings were ₹73 crore at March 2026 against reserves of ₹1,673 crore, and the borrowings are described as temporary use of working-capital limits.[1][3] The company raised ₹750 crore from investors in a share issue after FY21; that money sits partly in cash and deposits, which is one reason return on capital employed has fallen from 34% in FY21 to 17% in FY26.[1][3][15] The next tables show these five years line by line. The number

Cash left after content spending in FY26 was a negative ₹110 crore.[7]

How it was calculated

Operating cash flow of ₹155.3 crore less ₹265.3 crore spent on new content, which the company records as an investing outflow.[7] Screener's own free cash flow measure for the year is −₹114 crore.[1]

The inputs

Operating cash flow of ₹1,553 million and new-content spend of ₹2,653 million from the cash flow statement in the Q4 FY26 deck; FY25 comparison ₹3,854 million and ₹2,134 million; Screener free cash flow −₹114 crore for FY26 and ₹170 crore for FY25.[7][1]

The range

If FY27 content spend is ₹300 crore, the middle of management's ₹300 to 350 crore guide for new music, and operating cash flow repeats FY26's ₹155.3 crore, cash left is −₹144.7 crore. If operating cash flow returns to FY25's ₹385.4 crore, cash left is +₹85.4 crore.[4][7]

If the number is off

If cash left stays negative, profit growth is being funded from the balance sheet, which would cut the value of earnings per share and move us further from a buy. If cash left turns positive while spending rises, the quality case improves sharply.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one weakest assumption. This chapter names each one. It says what evidence would show it wrong. It says when we will know. It also gives the red-team bear case. That is the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion.

Here is the red-team bear case in one paragraph. The stock costs 45 times trailing earnings. Three-year profit growth is 4%. Cash after content spending was negative last year. Music margin after content charge was 43% in the latest quarter against 46% a year earlier. The finance chief has just changed. If music growth merely slows to the low teens, the multiple could fall toward the 25 times a cautious broker uses. On ₹16.4 that gives ₹410, about 24% below today's price. That ₹410 is a multiple-only stress. Our ₹338 bear case is deeper because it also assumes profit stalls at ₹13.5.[1][3][4][17]

Fatal flaw 1: new music costs more than it earns

The base value assumes bought music pays back inside five years. The evidence is mixed. Music net margin fell from 46% to 43% in the June 2026 quarter while spending rose, and management plans ₹300 to 350 crore of new music in FY27, up from ₹235 crore in FY26.[3][4][8] Two of the biggest albums, Love and War and Paradise, slipped past their planned FY26 releases, and Love and War has now moved to January 2027. If they are released and underperform, the content charge would rise ahead of revenue for years, because it is spread over about ten years.[4][10][17] Our single tripwire is the twelve-month music net margin (music profit after the content charge, over the last four quarters, as a share of music revenue) falling below 42% in any quarterly report. One weak quarter on its own does not cancel the order. The quarterly figure is lumpy (46%, 43%, 40%, 55% and 43% over the last five quarters, so 40% has already happened once without breaking the twelve-month figure of 45.5%). Music revenue growth is not part of the tripwire.

Fatal flaw 2: the platforms change the price of a licence

Streaming and video platforms are the main buyers of the catalogue. In FY25 the closure of Airtel Wynk, Hungama and Resso, and Gaana's move to a paywall, cut free-service payments and held music growth to 12%.[9] The growth case now leans on paid subscriptions, but Saregama does not disclose its subscription income, so we cannot verify it directly. Generative-AI music is a second unknown: management says it will fight unlicensed use and will license where terms are right.[4][8] We would know from licensing growth staying near or above 20% on a rolling twelve months, and from any platform announcing lower per-stream payments.

Fatal flaw 3: cash accounts, film inventory and the Bhansali bet

Saregama put ₹325 crore into Bhansali Productions in January 2026, with a stake of between 28% and 49.9% to be fixed in 2028 by the studio's performance, and has an option to reach 51% after March 2030 at a price still to be determined.[13][14] Inventories, which include films and series in production, were ₹251 crore at June 2026, and management says it will release the films on its books over three to four quarters, which leaves a risk of write-downs.[3][4] A new chief financial officer joined in 2026 and an analyst's question about a content-spend difference was taken offline.[4] We would know from the September 2026 and March 2027 results, from the cash flow statement and from any change in the content-spend line between decks.
