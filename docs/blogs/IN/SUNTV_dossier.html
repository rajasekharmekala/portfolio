Before you read

Sun TV Network is South India's largest television broadcaster. It also owns the Sunrisers Hyderabad cricket team, a film studio and about ₹7,400 crore of cash and investments[1]. The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: the company has almost no debt and passes our safety tests, but at ₹619.90 the shares already price in the good news, so we would consider buying only at ₹512-520, after the results due on 30 October 2026 are checked[42]. Four-fifths of last quarter's pre-tax profit growth came from cricket, advertising income fell 11.5% in FY26, and family-linked distributors supply 56% of subscription income[2][1]. We are wrong if the Q2 FY27 results due on 30 October 2026 show advertising and subscription income both growing[9], or if the shares fall below ₹504.37.

1. What the company does

Sun TV Network Limited is a Chennai broadcaster. It runs satellite television channels in four southern languages (Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam) and three northern ones (Bangla, Marathi and Hindi). It also airs FM radio stations, produces films under the Sun Pictures name, and runs the Sun NXT streaming service[2]. Its newest business is cricket: it owns three teams, Sunrisers Hyderabad in the Indian Premier League, Sunrisers Eastern Cape in South Africa's T20 league, and Sunrisers Leeds in The Hundred in the United Kingdom[2].

The company earns money in four ways. Advertising income comes from brands that pay for slots between programmes. Subscription income comes from cable and satellite distributors that pay a monthly fee per household to carry its channels. Movie income comes from selling the rights to films Sun Pictures makes, such as theatrical, digital and overseas rights. Cricket income is the team's share of the league's media and sponsorship pool, plus its own sponsors and ticket sales[1]. Of the ₹4,102.13 crore the parent company booked as revenue in FY26, subscription was 48%, advertising 28%, cricket 14% and movies 10%[1].

Think of it as a toll road with a film studio and a sports team attached. The channels collect a toll every month from distributors that serve many households, whether or not a hit show airs. That is why the margin on the channel business is so high. The studio and the team are lumpier: one film can add hundreds of crores in a quarter, and a cricket season lands mostly in a single three-month window.

Most of the money stays in the company. Sun TV pays almost no interest and has no loan funds at the parent company (the group balance sheet shows ₹106 crore of borrowings). At 31 March 2026 the parent company held ₹7,395 crore in mutual funds, bonds and bank balances, against a market value today of ₹24,393 crore[1][36]. Roughly three of every ten rupees you pay for a share is therefore backed by cash and investments. The rest buys a broadcaster whose growth has stalled, a cricket franchise whose value the market keeps trying to price, and a film slate that is expensive to make.

Why quarters do not compare with each other

Sun TV reports four very different quarters. The Indian Premier League opens in late March and runs into May, so most cricket income lands in the quarter to June[21][2]. The Hundred falls in the quarter to September: the Leeds team booked ₹94.52 crore of revenue in that quarter last year[33]. South Africa's league and each film release fall in other quarters. A film's income arrives when it opens in theatres. Its cost is written off in two ways: distribution rights on theatrical release, and satellite rights over four years in graded steps from first telecast[1]. Income and cost can therefore land in different quarters.

The result is a wide swing in profit per share. On a consolidated basis the last five quarters earned ₹13.43, ₹8.99, ₹8.22, ₹5.89 and ₹15.71 per share (Screener rounds the last to ₹15.70)[36][2]. Anyone who annualises the latest quarter, or reads a single quarter's year-on-year jump as a trend, will draw the wrong conclusion. This report therefore compares like with like, the same quarter a year earlier, and checks which line produced the change.

What this means for you

As an owner you hold three things at once: a high-margin channel network, a cash pile and a sports-and-film business. The channel network sets the floor for profit. Cash cushions the downside and pays a dividend of ₹12.50 per share in FY26[1]. Cricket and film set how much profit rises above the floor in any year. Chapter 2 tests how strong the floor is, and Chapter 3 shows the numbers behind each part.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is operating profit before those four items.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. A high figure means each rupee invested earns a lot.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is the cash left after the spending needed to keep the business running. For Sun TV that spending includes buying films and programme rights.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a share price has moved. Readings above 70 mean a fast rise, and readings below 30 mean a fast fall.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing, here measured over 14 days. We use it to measure distances: a stop set one ATR below a level leaves room for normal noise.
  • Operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue.
  • Net margin is final profit as a share of revenue.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. A figure of 0.01 means almost no debt.
  • Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share. It tells you how many years of current profit you pay for.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates from its day-to-day activity, before investing.
  • Working capital is the money tied up in unpaid customer bills and stock, less what the company owes suppliers.
  • Capital expenditure is spending on long-lived assets. For Sun TV that includes film rights and content.
  • Guidance is a forecast or target that management gives to investors.
  • Consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who cover the company.
  • Stage 2 is the rising phase of a price cycle, when the price is above a rising 50-day average and 200-day average.
  • Stage 4 is the falling phase, when the price is below falling 50-day and 200-day averages.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 sessions, a short-term trend line. The 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the same idea over longer spans, and support is the level where buyers have stepped in before.
  • Base is a flat stretch of trading after a decline, where the price stops falling.
  • Pivot is the price level at the top of a base that a rise must clear to confirm a new advance.
  • Breakout is a close above that pivot on rising volume.
  • Retest is a pullback to the breakout level to see whether it holds.
  • Stop loss is a price at which we sell to cap the loss if we are wrong.
  • Limit order is an order to buy only at a set price or lower.
  • Entry zone is the price range where we are willing to buy.
  • Asymmetry is the gain we expect if right divided by the loss if wrong. The related phrase reward-to-risk is the same ratio.
  • Measured move is a price target found by repeating the height of an earlier pattern.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of this business is easy to state and harder to measure. Sun TV says its flagship channel is the most watched in India and that it owns its recent fiction, so it can sell the same show on television and on its streaming service[1]. The evidence for pricing power is in the margin. In FY26 the group kept an operating profit margin of 50.0% of sales, and in the June 2026 quarter the parent company's EBITDA was 51.5% of revenue[36][2]. Zee Entertainment, the nearest listed rival, reported an EBITDA margin of 4.1% in the same quarter[30]. A gap of that size is not an accident of accounting. Zee's EBITDA had fallen 65% in that quarter, which exaggerates the gap. Over a full period the gap is also wide: Zee's return on capital is 2.72% against Sun TV's 16.4%[37][36]. The filings do not give cost per viewer, so we do not claim that.

Three facts temper that praise. First, the margin has fallen. It was 62% in FY24, 53% in FY25 and 50% in FY26, and return on capital fell from 26% to 16% over the same span[36]. Second, the channels earn from two payers that are both under pressure: households leaving pay television and advertisers moving to digital media. Third, the largest source of subscription income is a set of distribution companies that the promoter family controls, which is a strength in negotiation and a weakness in governance. The three sections below take these points in turn.

Audience strength and where it shows

Audience strength shows up in subscription income. Domestic subscription income rose 9.69% in FY26 to ₹1,891.68 crore[5]. Pay television is shrinking, though. The FICCI-EY report for 2025 counts about 11 million fewer pay-TV households in a year, a drop of 8% in industry subscription revenue[17]. Sun TV grew against that tide, but the growth slowed to 3.3% in the June 2026 quarter, from 9.7% for the year[2][5].

Advertising tells the opposite story. The same report says television advertising fell by more than 10% in 2025 and that this was the fourth year of decline for the industry[17][18]. AdEx ad-tracking data show ad volumes down another 7% in January to July 2026[19]. Sun TV's advertising income fell 11.5% in FY26, from ₹1,304.69 crore to ₹1,154.24 crore[1]. Zee said its domestic advertising was hit by the Middle East crisis and cricket in the June 2026 quarter[30], while Sun TV's advertising income fell 2.6%. Sun TV is holding up better than the industry, but it is still shrinking. The company does not publish a split of advertising by language, so we cannot say whether Tamil, Telugu, Kannada or Malayalam is weaker.

Who really pays: the promoter-linked distributors

The related-party note in the annual report changes how the subscription number should be read. In FY26 six distribution companies in which the Chairman's family has control or influence (one of them, Sun Direct Go, paid only ₹0.09 crore) paid Sun TV ₹1,111.05 crore of subscription income. That is 56.1% of the ₹1,980.48 crore total, up from 20.8% a year earlier[1]. Much of the jump comes from TVCH Distribution Services, which became a related party on 28 March 2025, and from Sun Direct TV and Kal Media Services[1]. Sun TV also paid those distributors ₹253.22 crore in pay-channel service charges[1].

The company states that these deals are on arm's-length terms benchmarked against unrelated parties[1]. We have no evidence to the contrary. The risk is structural rather than a finding of misconduct. When the buyer and the seller share an owner, the price can move profit between the listed company, where outside holders own 25%, and unlisted companies where they own nothing. Related parties also owed Sun TV ₹675.46 crore at 31 March 2026, which is 48.6% of all trade receivables[1].

The balance owed by TVCH halved during the year, from ₹516.63 crore to ₹257.54 crore, but the filing warns that the earlier figure includes amounts from before TVCH became a related party[1]. The point is that the quality of the subscription line depends on one family relationship, and that is why we score subscription as WATCH in Chapter 2B.

Politics and family governance

Kalanithi Maran is Executive Chairman, his wife and daughter are executive directors, and he holds the promoter stake of 75% in his own name with no pledge[1][7]. He and Mrs Kavery Kalanithi were each paid ₹87.50 crore in FY26, a total of ₹175 crore that equals 12.6% of the parent company's profit of ₹1,393.52 crore[1]. Pay at that level is a deliberate choice by a controlling family and a cost to every outside holder.

In June 2025 the Chairman's brother, Dayanidhi Maran, sent a legal notice questioning share allotments from 2003, and the shares fell more than 5% in early trading[15]. On 11 August 2025 the company said the notice had been withdrawn and the issues resolved, but it did not disclose the terms[14]. The shareholding pattern at 30 September 2026 still shows one promoter holding 29.56 crore shares, or 75%, with none pledged[7].

Politics is the third strand. Mr Maran is a relative of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (DMK) leadership. When early counts on 4 May 2026 showed the new Tamilaga Vettri Kazhagam leading and the DMK trailing, the shares fell as much as 9.5% to ₹547.55 in the day[16]. Analysts quoted in the same report noted that the stock had not created meaningful wealth during five years of DMK rule, so political closeness had not translated into higher earnings[16]. We treat politics as a risk to sentiment and to Tamil Nadu cable distribution, not as a driver of earnings, and we do not forecast the outcome.

What this means for you

Sun TV has a real franchise, shown by margins that its peers cannot match. The franchise is eroding at the edges, and the way profit is shared between the listed company and the family's other companies is a risk that you cannot diversify away. The next chapter turns these observations into five measurable tests and checks what management said it would do.

We compared the FY25 annual report, published in August 2025, with what the FY26 accounts show.

The record is mixed. The company delivered on the Leeds acquisition and on debt and dividends. It did not deliver growth in cricket in FY26, although the June 2026 quarter has now done so. It also described its pricing power in language that the advertising line does not support.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter lays out five years of results in plain figures. Revenue grew from ₹3,585 crore in FY22 to ₹4,335 crore in FY26, a rate of 4.9% a year, and the trailing twelve months to June 2026 stand at ₹4,502 crore[36]. Profit did the opposite. It peaked at ₹1,926 crore in FY24 and fell to ₹1,441 crore in FY26, because depreciation and amortisation more than doubled from ₹306 crore to ₹723 crore while the operating margin slid from 64% to 50%[36].

Two lines hold the profit up. Other income, which is mostly interest and gains on the investment pile, was ₹493 crore in FY26, a quarter of pre-tax profit of ₹1,939 crore[36]. It fell from ₹642 crore in FY25. In the parent company's cash flow, interest income fell from ₹276.56 crore to ₹201.60 crore and fair-value gains on investments fell from ₹340.02 crore to ₹288.80 crore[1]. And cash flow from operations stayed between ₹1,665 crore and ₹2,171 crore in the last three years, so the cash side of the business is steadier than its reported profit.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could fail. This chapter names the weakest, argues the bear case as strongly as we honestly can, and says when we will know. The single most important claim is stated at the end.

The base value: advertising and cricket must both hold

The weakest assumption behind the ₹615 base value is that advertising income stops falling and cricket margin stays near ₹300 crore a year. Both are less certain than the five quarters of data suggest. Advertising was flat for five quarters, but the FICCI-EY report says industry television advertising has now fallen four years in a row, and the AdEx tracker shows volumes down 7% in January to July 2026[18][19]. Sun TV's own decline has slowed mostly because it is now comparing with an already lower year.

The red-team bear case is that the June quarter flattered the picture. Cricket produced 79% of the pre-tax profit rise, and media rights are 75% of franchise revenue[2][20]. Media Partners Asia expects the 2028-32 rights cycle to be flat at $5.4 billion, a 13% drop per match, which would cap the franchise margin that is now 34% across the league[20]. If the next IPL season is weaker, or the 2028 reset lowers the pool, the cricket margin could fall by a third, which would remove about ₹100 crore of pre-tax profit. In that case earnings of ₹36 at 13 times, or ₹470, is a fair reading. We know the first answer when Q2 is reported on 30 October 2026 and the second in August 2027, when the quarter to June 2027 reports IPL 2027.

The stressed value: the multiple of 14.3 may not hold

The stressed value of ₹599 removes our earnings uplift but keeps the 14.3 multiple. That multiple is MarketScreener's three-year average of the forward ratio, dated 4 June 2026, and it was earned while profit was higher. A business whose profit is flat or falling can lose the multiple as well as the earnings[27]. The harder test keeps earnings unchanged at ₹43 and applies the 13 times already used in our bear case. That gives ₹559. At a ₹535 buy with the ₹504.37 stop, the reward-to-risk is only 0.78. With the Street's ₹41.90 at 13 times (₹544.70) it is 0.32. To reach 2.0 at ₹535 the value must be at least ₹596.26, which needs 14.23 times on ₹41.90, almost no allowance for a lower multiple.

This test sets the top of the buy zone at ₹520 and not at the ₹535 that the base value alone would allow. At ₹520 the ₹43-at-13-times value of ₹559 gives 2.50. At ₹520 the Street's ₹41.90 at 13 times still gives only 1.58, because 2.0 would need a price of ₹517.8 or lower. We state that residual shortfall rather than hide it. The cash is the argument for a floor under the multiple: net of treasury, the shares trade at 7.4 times trailing EBITDA. It is not a proof, because owner cash flow was only ₹778 crore in FY26 and related parties supply much of the income. The analyst view that the stock is 'sideways to negative' on weak advertising and viewers moving to streaming is the evidence against[16].

The bull value: the demerger is a rumour and the controller decides

The bull value of ₹790 assumes the IPL team is priced like a scarce sports asset. The company denied the demerger report on 30 September 2026 and the shares closed 9.35% higher at ₹605 that day, not the 20% intraday high[11]. Sale comparisons such as RCB at $1.78 billion are for control of a whole franchise, and a listed minority holder cannot force such a sale[21]. The controlling shareholder holds 75%[7], and the denial gives no sign that a split is planned. We treat the break-up as a possible event, not a plan.

Governance, politics and the film slate

Three risks do not show in the price-to-earnings ratio. First, 56.1% of subscription income and 36.9% of FY26 movie distribution income (₹149.39 crore of ₹405.32 crore) came from related parties, and the Chairman and his wife were paid ₹175 crore between them[1]. Second, the Tamil Nadu election of May 2026 cost the stock as much as 9.5% in a day, and the company's links to the previous ruling party remain a source of headline risk[16]. Third, ₹856.99 crore of content is still under development, and the accounting policy writes satellite rights off over four years from first telecast in graded steps and distribution rights on theatrical release; a weak film or a delayed series would add to amortisation or bring an impairment[1]. Jailer 2 has grossed ₹765.15 crore worldwide in 15 days[22], which favours the first film of the batch, but Sun TV's income from the film is what it received for selling the rights (₹45 crore for Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, for example), not the box office gross, and it has not yet reported the total[24].

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule behind it is the technical routing rule: Sun TV passes every Layer 1 red line, qualifies as a compounder on levels and scores 3.5 out of 5 on Layer 2. At the ₹619.90 close the reward-to-risk is -0.04, and the close is 2.24 ATR above support, in the NO_BUY band of the ladder explained in Chapter 4[42]. We do not buy at market. We also do not place a standing order today.

A conditional order exists, but it activates only after four checks, in this order. First, the Q2 FY27 results of 30 October 2026 must be out and both channel screens must pass: advertising of at least ₹270 crore and domestic subscription of at least ₹470 crore. Second, we rebuild the earnings bridge from the filing: advertising, subscription, cricket profit, film income after amortisation and cash conversion. Third, we recompute value and reward-to-risk on the new numbers, and it must reach 2.0 on the base value, on the stressed value and on the 13-times stress, meaning earnings of ₹43 at 13 times (value ₹559). Fourth, we rerun the price gate to confirm support and a fresh stop. If any check fails, there is no order.

On today's numbers the buy zone is ₹512 to ₹520. The multiple-compression stress sets the zone; a simple rule on the base value alone would give a higher zone near ₹535. With earnings unchanged at ₹43 and a multiple of 13, the value is ₹559. At ₹535 that gives a reward-to-risk of 0.78, and at ₹520 it gives 2.50. The highest price that keeps 2.0 on that test is ₹522.58. On the Street's ₹41.90 at 13 times (₹544.70) the ratio is 1.58 at ₹520 and the highest price for 2.0 is ₹517.8, so the zone top carries a stated shortfall on that cross-check. The reward-to-risk of 6.08 on the base value at ₹520 looks large only because the stop is close: ₹504.37 is 3.0% below the entry and about half of one day's normal range of ₹29.44. A stop-out on noise is likely, and a gap to the bear value of ₹470 would lose 9.6%, three times the planned loss.

The zone also sits below the 50-day average of ₹533.81, by 0.47 ATR at ₹520 and 0.74 ATR at ₹512. The order therefore waits for the 50-day average to fail as support, and buys on value rather than on a technical retest. That is why the fourth check reruns the price gate, and why the stop of ₹504.37 is recalculated at activation, since the 50-day average moves each day. The base value is ₹615, the bull value ₹790 and the bear value ₹470.

Position size follows a conviction tier. A tier is a class that caps how much of net worth one idea can hold. TIER_4 is the lowest class and allows 1% to 2%. Each tier also has a risk budget in basis points (bps), where one basis point is one hundredth of a percent. TIER_4's budget is 35 bps, so the most a stop-out may cost is 0.35% of net worth, or ₹0.35 on each ₹100. Here the tier cap holds the position to 2.0%, so the planned loss is about 0.06%.

Sun TV is not a holding: the Zerodha transaction log has no entry for it, and the ledger lists only a watchlist role. It enters the shortlist as a pipeline challenger in the AUTOWRITTEN class and would not displace any holding at this time. Waiting for Q2 results removes only the unreleased-print risk. It does not remove the cricket concentration, family-linked income or political risks, so the position stays small.
