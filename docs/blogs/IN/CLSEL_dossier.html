1. What the company does

Chaman Lal Setia Exports Limited is a basmati rice miller and exporter based in Amritsar, Punjab, with its main mill at Karnal in Haryana. Chaman Lal Setia founded the business in 1974, it became a partnership in 1983 and a public company in 1994, and it listed on the Bombay exchange in 1995 and on the National Stock Exchange on 12 May 2021.[3] The government recognises it as a three-star export house, and the founding family still runs it: Vijay Kumar Setia is chairman and managing director, Rajeev Setia is joint managing director and chief financial officer, and Ankit Setia and Sankesh Setia are executive directors.[10][2] The family holds 74.05 percent of the shares.[19]

The company buys paddy and semi-processed rice from the farmers' markets of the basmati belt, cleans and sorts the grain with colour-sorting machines, packs it in sizes from half a kilogram to one tonne, and ships it to buyers in more than 95 countries.[10] The flagship brand is Maharani, with Mithas and Begum as the other basmati brands and Green World for non-basmati aromatic rice.[3] Most of the volume does not carry these names. The company packs more than 300 private labels. That means the rice reaches the shelf under the name of the importer or the supermarket chain.[10][1] Management counts 440-plus distributors and the annual report counts more than 900 buyers.[10][3]

The plant is built for sorting and packing more than for milling. Colour-sorting capacity is 880 tonnes a day, the silos hold 18,750 tonnes, and warehouses hold 82,500 tonnes, which gives the company room to store a season's purchases.[10] Packing averages about 500 tonnes a day and can reach 800, and a unit at Mundra in Gujarat lets cargo leave from a port close by.[11][10] Most output comes from buying semi-finished rice and converting it to finished rice, which keeps the factory cycle short and the fixed assets small: net fixed assets were Rs 167 crore against sales of Rs 1,440 crore.[10][1] The small fixed-asset total explains why the return on capital looks good even though the margin on sales is modest.

Customers are importers, supermarket chains and regional distributors. They pay on credit, and the company's receivables were 59 days of sales in FY26. So a bill is collected about two months after the cargo ships.[1] Exports supplied 88 percent of revenue from operations in FY26, up from 84 percent a year earlier.[3] Management quotes 91 percent because it divides export sales of Rs 1,268 crore by product sales that exclude Rs 39.6 crore of other operating income, and the two numbers describe the same business.[11][2]

The company earns a spread. It buys rice or paddy at one price, processes and packs it, and sells it at another. So the price of raw rice drives profit more than any overhead does. Paddy and rice cost 75 to 80 percent of revenue, according to the rating agency CRISIL. That means a one-rupee move in the raw material moves profit far more than a one-rupee move in wages or power.[18] Operating profit as a share of sales has ranged from 9 percent to 14 percent over the last decade and was 11 percent in FY26 on revenue of Rs 1,440 crore.[1] Management buys heavily when prices are low: it filled its warehouses from the start of the 2025 season until mid-January 2026, and prices then rose about 30 percent.[11]

The calendar of the business follows the harvest. Basmati comes off the fields in autumn. So the company buys a large share of the year's rice between October and January and sells it over the next nine months. That is why the balance sheet carries Rs 427 crore of inventory. Screener measures this as 147 days on its own basis, and against sales alone it is about 108 days. Management says 30 to 45 percent of that stock is still paddy and the rest is rice.[10][1][11] The company says it does not age rice for long, which lowers the risk that stock loses value in storage.[10]

Two policy facts matter. India holds the geographical indication for basmati and exported 6.52 million tonnes worth Rs 50,138 crore in 2025-26, with Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, the United Arab Emirates and Yemen the largest buyers.[24] In September 2024 the government removed the minimum export price, a floor that had been $1,200 a tonne from August 2023 and $950 from October 2023.[23] The sources we fetched report that removal and nothing newer about export duties on basmati. So the current state of curbs beyond September 2024 is unconfirmed here and remains a standing risk for a food staple.[23][26]

How the brand business fits in

Sources disagree about how large the branded business is, and the gap comes from definitions. Screener, which summarises company filings, puts the brand at about 10 percent of revenue, management said in May 2025 that branded sales were 14 to 15 percent, and CRISIL gave 23 percent for FY25.[1][16][18] We treat the brand as a minority of revenue, between one-tenth and one-quarter, and we note that CRISIL names higher brand share as a key factor for a future rating upgrade.[18] In Q1 FY27 the company reported a brand realisation of Rs 92 a kilogram, against Rs 98 for exports and Rs 64 for domestic sales.[10][11]

Management has said repeatedly that India is the next market to build. On the February 2026 call the chief financial officer spoke of a new brand called Teasan, and on the August 2026 call he said a single phone call to an Agra distributor produced an order for 100 tonnes.[13][11] That is encouraging, but domestic sales are only about Rs 131 crore. So even a fast-growing brand would move total revenue slowly.[2]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position has three real strengths and one structural weakness. The strengths are procurement skill, a long list of customers and a clean balance sheet. The weakness is that the company is a price-taker in the rice market: it has no patent, no unique grade of rice and no brand strong enough to set prices. So profit depends on buying well rather than on charging more.[18] That is why the margin on sales has swung between 9 percent and 14 percent while the return on capital has swung between 17 percent and 46 percent over twelve years.[1]

Procurement is the most visible skill. The company says most procurement is paid in cash to earn a cash discount, which one manager put at 2 percent. Another manager said that rice bought in the morning can be fumigated and packed within 24 hours. The company also bought heavily from the start of the 2025 season. That left a large stock of low-cost rice when prices rose 30 percent from January 2026.[10][11] The proof is in the margin: operating margin was 12 percent in each of the last two quarters of FY26 and 12.6 percent in Q1 FY27, against 9 to 10 percent in the first half of FY26.[1][10] This is real skill, but it is also a bet on the price of rice, and the same bet would have lost money if prices had fallen after the purchase.

The customer list is the second strength. The company says it serves 900-plus buyers in more than 95 countries. The largest ten customers were about 35 percent of revenue in June 2026. Management told investors in August 2026 that it shipped nothing to Iran in the June quarter and had no stuck containers.[3][12][11] A broad customer base protects against the loss of one buyer. CRISIL, the rating agency, called the company's operations diversified across clients, geographies and products when it raised the rating to A+ in August 2025.[18] The limit is that a diversified customer base is not the same as a diversified product: basmati rice is 90 to 95 percent of sales.[18]

The balance sheet is the third strength. Net worth was Rs 838 crore at 31 March 2026, interest cover (profit before interest and tax divided by interest) was 18 times on our calculation, the current ratio was 6.9 and CRISIL rates the Rs 350 crore of bank limits at A+ with a stable outlook.[10][17][3][2] Bank borrowings fell from Rs 61 crore to Rs 8.9 crore in a year, and the company holds Rs 128.6 crore in other bank balances, mostly fixed deposits.[5] One caution: most of the Rs 82.7 crore of total borrowings is not bank debt. It is Rs 72.6 crore of unsecured deposits from the directors, on which the company paid Rs 6.13 crore of interest in FY26.[5][6] That is cheap and stable money. But it ties the company to its owners, and CRISIL itself treats 75 percent of such a loan as equity.[18]

The structural weakness is that the competition has more scale and more brand. KRBL, which owns India Gate, earned an operating margin of 21 percent in the June 2026 quarter against 12 percent a year earlier, and LT Foods reported quarterly revenue above Rs 3,200 crore.[31][32] CLSEL's quarterly revenue was Rs 346 crore.[1] The larger rivals can spread marketing and distribution costs across a much bigger sales base, and CLSEL's own managers concede that brand visibility in India is low.[12] The market therefore pays CLSEL a similar price-to-earnings multiple to KRBL's and half of LT Foods'.[31][32]

Risks to the export model

The first risk is the Middle East. Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, the United Arab Emirates and Yemen took about 56 percent of India's basmati exports in 2025-26, and a war between the United States, Israel and Iran has disrupted shipping since February 2026.[24][26][12] Exporters said that about 0.5 million tonnes of rice was stranded at the Gujarat ports in July 2026, and an industry blog counted roughly 400,000 tonnes stranded in early March.[25][27] CLSEL reports that it shipped nothing to Iran in the June quarter and that no cargo was stuck. It has filed claims with ECGC for extra war charges on cargo shipped before the conflict.[11][12] It does not publish the Middle East share of its own sales. So the reader cannot check how much of the business depends on the region.[2]

The second risk is the new sanctions pressure on Iran. Policy Circle reports that the United States launched a campaign on 24 August that widens the risk of secondary sanctions for firms and banks that deal with Tehran, and that Iran bought 13.9 percent of India's basmati exports in 2025-26.[26] CLSEL says it did not sell to Iran in the last quarter, which limits direct exposure. But a general freeze on Gulf payments or shipping would still affect its main customers.[11]

The third risk is price. New-crop basmati paddy was selling for Rs 4,200 to Rs 4,300 a quintal in Punjab mandis in October 2026, against roughly Rs 2,800 a year earlier.[29] Paddy and rice are 75 to 80 percent of revenue. So a rise of that size lifts the cost of this autumn's stock sharply.[18] If export prices follow, the spread holds. If they do not, or if they fall after the company has filled its warehouses, the company loses money on inventory. FY26 showed the good side of this pattern, when the company filled its warehouses at low prices before the rise.[11]

What the competitive evidence adds up to

Taken together, the evidence describes a well-run, financially conservative trader-miller whose profit swings with the rice price. The stable parts are the balance sheet, the customer list and the cash discipline in buying. The unstable parts are the margin, the Middle East exposure and revenue that has not grown in three years: the three-year sales growth rate is 1 percent a year.[1] A reader should expect good years when raw rice prices rise after the purchase and poor ones when they do not. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of results show a business that grew once and then stood still. Revenue rose from Rs 932 crore in FY22 to Rs 1,387 crore in FY23, an increase of 49 percent, then moved between Rs 1,356 crore and Rs 1,495 crore for three years and was Rs 1,440 crore in FY26.[1] Net profit followed the same shape: Rs 65 crore, Rs 118 crore, Rs 116 crore, Rs 103 crore and Rs 115 crore.[1] The three-year growth rate in sales is 1 percent a year and in profit minus 1 percent a year, although the last twelve months show profit of Rs 125 crore, up 23 percent on a year earlier.[1]

Earnings per share rose a little faster than profit because the company bought back shares. The share count fell from 51.7 million in FY24 to 49.7 million in FY26, a drop of 3.9 percent.[22] Earnings per share were Rs 23.08 in FY26 and Rs 25.17 over the last twelve months.[1] At the ledger close of Rs 260.00 the shares trade on 10.3 times those last-twelve-month earnings.[35][1] That is cheap against LT Foods and level with KRBL. But it is above CLSEL's own three-year median of 9.1 times.[32][31][22]

The balance sheet tells the second half of the story. Reserves rose from Rs 399 crore to Rs 828 crore over five years. So the company has kept most of what it earned. Other assets, which are mostly stock, receivables and deposits, rose from Rs 562 crore to Rs 872 crore.[1] In other words, the retained profit sits in the warehouse and in customers' unpaid bills. Only Rs 35 crore of operating cash flow came in across the five years, against Rs 517 crore of profit. So the question the next tables answer is how much of that profit is cash in a different pocket.[1]
