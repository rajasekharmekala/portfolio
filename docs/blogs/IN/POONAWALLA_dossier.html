Before you read

Poonawalla Fincorp is a non-bank lender owned mainly by the Poonawalla family group behind the Serum Institute vaccine maker.[1][9] It makes consumer, small-business, gold, education and used-car loans, which grew 55 percent in a year to ₹74,008 crore, and its profit reached ₹375 crore in the September quarter.[1] We still reject the stock at ₹402.80, because our central value of ₹430 is only 7 percent higher and we need 12 percent a year.[34] The chart is building a floor near ₹395 but has not turned up.[34] The valuation case would clear the 12 percent hurdle if our profit forecast rose from ₹25.3 a share to about ₹27.6 or more, or if the same upside came from a price near ₹364, and the verdict could then be re-opened once the chart also passes the momentum gate.

1. What the company does

Poonawalla Fincorp lends money. It is a non-deposit-taking non-bank finance company (NBFC) regulated by the Reserve Bank of India, which means it borrows from banks, bond investors and mutual funds and lends the money on to households and small firms at a higher rate.[18] It was called Magma Fincorp until 2021, when a holding company owned by Adar Poonawalla, chief executive of the vaccine maker Serum Institute of India, bought control and renamed it.[9] That holding company, Rising Sun Holdings, owned 59.01 percent of the shares at 30 September 2026, with no shares pledged.[18][19] Mr Poonawalla chairs the board, and Arvind Kapil, who earlier ran the mortgage banking business at HDFC Bank, is managing director and chief executive.[1]

Who pays, and for what? Borrowers pay interest and fees. The largest group is small-business owners and self-employed people who borrow against property or take business loans. A second group is salaried people who take personal loans, many of them through a fully digital process. A third, newer group borrows against gold, buys consumer durables on instalments, finances a used commercial vehicle, or pays for study abroad. The table below shows how the loan book was split at 31 March 2026.[8] Since then the company has pushed the newer lines hard. They made up 28 percent of disbursements in the September 2026 quarter and 18 percent of loans under management (AUM) at 30 September 2026, up from a standing start two years ago.[1] AUM is the total loans the company has made and still manages, and it stood at ₹74,008 crore, up 55.1 percent in a year.[1]

How the company earns its money

A lender makes money on the gap between what it charges and what it pays. In the September 2026 quarter the portfolio yield was 14.08 percent and the cost of borrowing was 7.76 percent, a gap of 6.32 points.[1][12] The company reports a net interest margin (NIM), which is the interest it keeps after paying its own borrowing costs, measured against its loans; including fees it was 9.26 percent, up from 9.10 percent in the June quarter.[12] Think of it as a shop that buys money at one price and sells it at another. The shop then pays for staff and branches, and sets aside money for customers who will not repay.

The September 2026 quarter shows where each rupee goes. Net income including fees was ₹1,589 crore. Running costs took ₹712 crore, which is 44.8 percent of it. Expected loan losses took ₹376 crore, or 23.7 percent. Tax took ₹126 crore, or 7.9 percent. The remaining ₹375 crore, 23.6 percent, was profit after tax.[1] The four slices add to 100 percent. The point to hold on to is that fewer than one rupee in four of net income reaches shareholders, so a small move in running costs or loan losses changes profit a lot.

A lender also carries far more borrowing than a factory does. Its debt-to-equity ratio is total borrowings divided by shareholders' money, and it was 4.30 times at 30 September 2026, meaning ₹58,865 crore of borrowings against about ₹13,703 crore of shareholders' funds.[1] For a lender this is normal, because borrowed money is its raw material. The limit that matters is the regulator's capital rule, and the company's capital ratio was 18.68 percent against a 15 percent minimum.[1][13]

What changed in the last two years

Under chief executive Arvind Kapil the company rebuilt itself. It took a one-time ₹666 crore provision on an old short-term personal loan book in FY25, which is why that year shows a loss of ₹98 crore, and then launched six new products: prime personal loans, gold loans, consumer durable loans, commercial vehicle loans, education loans and shopkeeper loans.[9][7] It opened gold-loan branches (550 by September 2026), signed up about 20,000 dealer points for consumer durable loans and says it runs 143 artificial-intelligence projects, of which 84 are live.[1] Management's stated aspiration is to grow AUM five to six times over the five years from FY25, and its near-term target is AUM growth of 35 to 40 percent a year.[1][12]

The September 2026 quarter results were announced on 9 October 2026, so they are out and priced in. The next results, for the December quarter, have not been scheduled yet.[1][13] Last year the December-quarter call was held on 16 January 2026, which suggests mid-January 2027, but that is an inference, not a filing.[5] The rest of this report asks whether the business that these results describe is worth owning at the current price, and the answer depends on the numbers in Chapters 3 and 5.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPS stands for earnings per share (EPS): the company's profit divided across all its shares. We state it in rupees per share.
  • ROCE stands for return on capital employed (ROCE): the profit earned on the capital the business uses. It suits factories; for a lender, return on equity and return on assets replace it.
  • GNPA means gross non-performing assets (GNPA): loans whose borrowers are more than 90 days overdue, before the safety provisions, as a share of all loans. NNPA means net non-performing assets (NNPA): the same figure after those provisions.
  • free cash flow (FCF) — the cash left after running the business and buying assets. For a lender it is not a useful measure, because loans issued count as cash spent.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash a business generates from its own activities before financing. A lender's figure turns sharply negative when its loan book grows.
  • guidance is the company's own published forecast or target for a coming period.
  • consensus estimate means the average of analysts' forecasts for a number such as profit per share.
  • dilution is the fall in each shareholder's slice when a company issues new shares.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit; price-to-book (P/B) is the price paid for each rupee of shareholders' funds.
  • stop loss — the price at which the thesis is wrong and the position is cut.
  • limit order — an order that fills only at your price or better and does not chase the market.
  • entry zone — the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the possible gain divided by the possible loss: (target price minus buy price) divided by (buy price minus stop loss).
  • RSI stands for relative strength index (RSI): a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been moving; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR stands for average true range (ATR): the average daily price swing in rupees, used to measure distances on the chart.
  • base is a sideways stretch of trading where a price builds a floor; its top is the pivot, and a breakout is a close above the pivot on heavy volume. A retest is a pullback to a level the price has just broken or left.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend; Stage 1 is the basing phase between them.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Poonawalla Fincorp is a good and improving lending franchise with a strong sponsor and cheap, steady funding. It is not yet a proven one. Most of its loan book is less than two years old, it has not been tested by a credit downturn, and the profits that make the stock attractive are only a few quarters old.[9][11] This chapter separates what the evidence supports from what it only suggests.

The sponsor and the funding edge

The strongest asset is the parent. Crisil and CARE both rate the company's long-term debt AAA with a stable outlook, and both say the rating rests on the expectation of timely support from Rising Sun Holdings and, behind it, the Serum Institute.[9][11] Rising Sun put in ₹1,500 crore of fresh equity in September 2025, and the company raised another ₹2,500 crore from institutions in April 2026.[11][1] A AAA rating means lenders treat the company almost like a bank, so it can borrow at 7.73 percent on average (the figure for FY26) while lending at 14 percent.[9][12] Crisil adds that the funding mix is wide: banks, bonds, commercial paper and foreign-currency loans, and its 9 October 2026 bulletin still lists the bank facilities as AAA/Stable, including ₹5,936.29 crore of proposed long-term bank lines.[9][10] The company's own investor page shows the same AAA/Stable ratings from both agencies.[2]

The funding is also well matched to the loans. Bonds (including subordinated debt) were about 31 percent of ₹58,865 crore of borrowings at 30 September 2026, up from 7 percent in September 2024 (the share of bonds alone, as reported on the October 2025 call), and the company's own repayment schedule shows more cash coming in than going out in every period up to five years.[1][6] The cumulative surplus was ₹4,894 crore in the one-to-three-year bucket and ₹433 crore in the three-to-five-year bucket, so there is no near-term funding cliff.[1] The limit is the rating itself. CARE lists two downgrade triggers: gearing above 5.5 times on a sustained basis and net bad loans above 2 percent of loans. Gearing is now 4.30 times and net bad loans are 0.61 percent.[11][1]

Distribution, products and technology

A lender wins by finding good borrowers cheaply. The company has built several doors: 550 gold-loan branches, about 20,000 consumer-durable dealer points, more than 1,250 commercial-vehicle channel partners, 700-plus education consultants and a digital prime personal loan line that is 42 percent of its personal-loan disbursements.[1][12] The pitch is that each door brings customers who can later be sold a second product, and cross-sell income did rise from ₹68 crore to ₹118 crore in a year.[14] Management also says the bureau data shows consumer-durable customers who finished their loans are about 40 percent better in credit quality than the industry.[12]

These are claims about quality, and the proof is time. Crisil and CARE both name the lack of seasoning as the main weakness, because a large share of the book was written in the last 15 months.[9][11] A summary of the October 2026 call puts the share of the book that is less than 12 months old at 60 to 65 percent, a figure we could not confirm in the company's own presentation.[15] Seasoning matters because losses often appear only after a loan has aged, so today's low bad-loan ratio partly reflects a book that has not yet had time to go wrong.

Where the business is weak

Three weaknesses stand out. First, the company has not earned a good return on equity yet. Screener shows return on equity of 5.86 percent for FY26, and Crisil's return on managed assets was 1.08 percent for the year.[18][9] The September 2026 quarter was far better, with return on assets of 2.18 percent, but one quarter is not a record.[1]

Second, the cushion against bad loans is thin. The provision coverage ratio, meaning the share of bad loans already provided for, was 49.14 percent, and the provision on loans that are not yet overdue (stage 1 and 2 together) was only 0.76 percent of those loans.[1] That is acceptable while bad loans are falling. It leaves little room if they rise.

Third, the environment has turned. The Reserve Bank of India raised its policy rate by 0.25 points to 5.50 percent on 7 October 2026, its first rise since February 2023, and said rate cuts are off the table.[17] Management says about one-third of its funding is at fixed rates and it has already raised its own lending rates by 0.25 points.[15][12] Separately, on 21 October 2026 Bajaj Finance and Piramal Finance both flagged early stress in gold loans, the fastest-growing segment in the sector, and an insurance-commission cap is being discussed that could cut the fee income that supports cross-selling.[16][13][15] None of these hurts the September numbers. All of them bear on the next four quarters.

How the evidence adds up

On evidence, the franchise earns a pass on funding, growth and early asset quality, and a watch on seasoning, capital and cost. There is no cost advantage in the lending itself that a rival with the same AAA rating could not copy, so the lasting edge is the sponsor's support and the data and distribution the company is building.[9][11] That edge is real, but its value depends on whether profit growth continues, which is what the numbers in Chapters 3 and 5 test. For an owner, the practical meaning is simple. The business deserves respect, and the sponsor lowers the chance of a funding accident. Respect for the business is not the same as a reason to pay any price for it, so the next chapters turn to the numbers and the price.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 takes each estimate from Chapter 5 and asks what its weakest assumption is, what evidence would show it wrong, and when we will know. The point is not to hedge. A reader should leave knowing which single belief carries the verdict.

Estimate 1: our FY28 profit per share of ₹25.3

The weakest assumption is that return on average AUM stays near 2.2 percent while AUM grows 35 percent a year. It could be too low, because management's own aim is 3.0 to 3.5 percent by June 2028 and three profit levers, spread, credit cost and operating leverage, are all pointing the right way.[3][12] It could be too high, because running costs per rupee of loans rose in the latest quarter, the cost of borrowing is rising, and a book that is mostly under two years old has not met a downturn.[1][15]

The evidence will arrive with the December-quarter results, expected around mid-January 2027 though not yet scheduled. We will be looking at return on assets (2.4 percent or more is the upside signal), credit cost (2.0 percent or lower), running costs (4.0 percent of AUM or lower) and gross bad loans (1.5 percent or higher is the warning).[1][5]

Estimate 2: what growth and return the price asks for

The weakest assumption is the cost of equity of 12 percent and the long-run growth of 6 to 8 percent used to turn price to book into a required return on equity. With a cost of equity of 11 percent and growth of 8 percent, the required return on equity at 2.58 times book falls to 15.7 percent (8 + 2.58 x 3), which is within reach.[35] The reverse DCF has the opposite weakness: it treats profit as cash that can be paid out, which overstates what owners receive while the company is growing fast. The two readings bracket the truth, and neither is a precise measure.

Nothing will settle this by a date. The test is practical: whether return on equity moves past 14 percent by March 2027 and toward 17 percent by March 2028, which the quarterly results will show.

Estimate 3: the central value of ₹430 and the probabilities

The weakest assumption is the 17 times multiple. It is a judgement, set 20 percent under the peer median trailing P/E of 21.1, because Poonawalla has no published 3-year median P/E of its own to anchor on and its 5-year average of 44.2 is distorted by a loss year.[23][24][25][26][27][21] The probabilities of 25, 50 and 25 percent are also judgements. Shifting 10 points from the bear case to the bull case raises the weighted value to about ₹434, which is 8 percent above the price and still below the hurdle.

We will learn more from how the peer group is re-rated in the December-quarter results season, when large lenders report again, and from the Reserve Bank's next meeting on 2 to 4 December 2026.[17]

The next heading, Layer 3, turns the verdict into an entry zone, a stop and a position size. Risk-unit sizing means risking a fixed small slice of the whole portfolio on each idea, so a wider stop gives a smaller position. The slice is measured in basis points, or bps, where 100 bps is 1 percent of the portfolio. Portfolio heat is the total of those risk slices across all open positions. Each idea gets a tier, from TIER_1 with the largest risk slice to TIER_4 with the smallest, and TIER_0 means no risk is allocated.
