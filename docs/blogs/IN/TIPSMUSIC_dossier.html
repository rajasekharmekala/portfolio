Before you read

Tips Music owns the rights to more than 38,000 songs in over 25 languages and earns money each time they are streamed on YouTube, Spotify or Instagram.[1] A team of only 94 people runs it.[1] It clears every red line we test: no debt, no promoter pledge, three-year cash conversion of 1.08 and an operating profit margin of 73% in FY26.[2][3] We rate it WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT because the ratio of possible gain to possible loss is only 0.91 at the ₹741.60 close, against a floor of 2.0, but 2.62 in a buy zone of ₹650 to ₹667. The call is wrong if the YouTube Shorts and Warner Music terms improve sharply, because then we are too cautious, or if they reset lower, because then even the zone is too high[4].

1. What the company does

Think of Tips Music as the owner of a very large jukebox. It paid for the rights to film soundtracks in the 1990s and 2000s and keeps adding new ones. Whenever someone plays one of those songs on a phone, a platform pays Tips a small amount. Management says the company was founded in 1988 and now holds about 38,000 songs in more than 25 languages.[1] In FY26 the company reported revenue of ₹375.51 crore and profit after tax of ₹216.75 crore.[2]

The money arrives from several routes. About 73% of first-half FY27 revenue came from digital platforms such as YouTube, Spotify and Instagram; management guides a 70% to 75% range.[1][4] Paid subscriptions are only 10% to 15% of that digital pool.[4] The rest comes from licences to television, films, advertisers and live shows. Warner Music Group distributes the whole catalogue and negotiates the global platform deals, and management puts Warner at 25% to 30% of revenue.[5][4] The annual report shows that ₹257.49 crore of the ₹375.51 crore, or 68.6%, was booked as international revenue in FY26.[2]

Tips pays out almost all its profit. It returned ₹166.18 crore as dividends in FY26, which was 99.8% of FY25 profit, and it has promised to return last year's profit of about ₹217 crore again in FY27.[6][7][2] The promoters, the Taurani family, own 64.32% and none of it is pledged.[8] The listed company has no subsidiaries.[2] One related company, Tips Films Limited, is controlled by the same family and supplies about one-third of the content it takes in.[7][2]

How the accounting differs from most music companies

Tips records the whole cost of new music as an expense in the quarter the songs are released.[10][1] Most peers spread that cost over several years. The effect is that Tips' quarterly profit swings with the release slate: the operating profit margin was 50.3% in the June 2026 quarter and 70.5% in the September quarter.[11] It also means the balance sheet holds almost no music asset, so a flop leaves no write-off behind.[4][12]

This choice flatters one ratio and understates another. Return on capital employed looks extreme because there is almost no capital on the books. Margins look lower than a peer that capitalises its content spend, so you cannot compare Tips' margin line by line with Saregama's.[3][13] We therefore lean on cash flow and payout, not on the headline return figure.

Who runs it and who owns it

Kumar Taurani is Chairman and Managing Director, and Girish Taurani, from the same family, is an Executive Director.[2] The chief executive, Hari Nair, left on 30 April 2026; an analyst on the April call described him as instrumental in the Warner and YouTube Shorts negotiations. Girish Taurani and the chief financial officer, Sushant Dalmia, are running the company jointly, and as of the October call the search for a successor was still open.[14][15][4] That gap sits at the centre of the risks in chapter 6.

  • A base is a sideways stretch of trading that comes before a move. The pivot is the top of that base, the price level a breakout must clear on heavy volume.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength is the catalogue. About 85% of revenue comes from music acquired more than three years ago, and the old film songs keep finding new audiences: one older track, Tera Naam Doon, passed one billion views on Instagram.[4][11] The company has 164.5 million YouTube subscribers and more than 54.3 billion total views.[11] Because the songs were bought long ago and expensed, each extra stream is almost pure profit. Revenue per employee is about ₹4 crore, from 94 staff.[1][2]

The second strength is growth with a clean base. Revenue grew from ₹135.59 crore in FY22 to ₹375.51 crore in FY26, and profit after tax from ₹64.56 crore to ₹216.75 crore.[16][2] Management says streaming volumes rose 30% to 35% over the last year, mostly from more plays rather than higher prices.[4] The Indian music segment grew only 10% in 2025 and digital licensing just 2%, so Tips is gaining share, and it estimates its share of industry revenue at 8% to 10%.[17][4]

The weakness is pricing power. Management says the per-stream rate will stay at about ₹0.05 however many plays rise, and Indian labels earn 4 to 10 paise a stream against 50 to 90 paise globally.[4][1] Growth must therefore come from more plays, a shift to paid plans, or short-video payments. The paid base is small: 14.4 million paid music subscribers in 2025, forecast to reach 28 million to 30 million by 2028.[17][18] A fixed-fee YouTube Shorts licence ran out in June 2026 and the renewal, first promised for June, was still unsigned on 15 October.[19][10][4]

YouTube views fell 8% in FY26, from 228.3 billion to 210.4 billion, while revenue rose 21%.[1][2] Management says the fall came from Shorts, which is not yet a significant contributor to revenue or profit, and the Shorts licence was a lump-sum deal.[20][21] That explanation fits the revenue data, but it also shows that the most-watched format earns little for Tips until the new deal is signed.

The competitive position in one view

Tips is one of several large Indian film-music labels. It competes with Saregama and with labels owned by global majors, and the company gives no ranking, only the 8% to 10% share estimate.[4] Its advantage is price: it bought most of its library for little, so it can accept a lower return on new music than a buyer paying today's prices. Its disadvantage is that film-music rights are now bid for by well-funded rivals, and the chairman says intense competition has pushed content prices very high.[4] Management's answer is to refuse bidding wars, give producers one or two recreated catalogue songs, and sign artists song by song instead of by long contract.[7][4]

For you as an owner, this means the quality of the business is real but the growth rate depends on two negotiations, the Warner renewal in FY28 and the YouTube Shorts deal, that management does not control alone.
