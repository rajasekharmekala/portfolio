1. What the company does

Sedemac Mechatronics Limited is a Pune company that was started in 2007 by three engineers from the Indian Institute of Technology, Bombay.[13] It designs and makes small electronic control units, called ECUs. An ECU is a sealed box of electronics and software that tells a machine how to behave. In a motorcycle, one ECU decides how the engine starts. In an electric scooter, another decides how the motor spins. The company sold more than 3.9 million of these units in the year to March 2026, over 60% more than the year before.[6]

Its best-known product is the integrated starter generator controller, or ISG ECU. In a scooter or small motorcycle fitted with an ISG, one electric machine starts the engine and then turns into the generator that charges the battery. The ECU runs that machine. SEDEMAC's version needs no position sensor inside the machine, because its software works out where the spinning part is from the electrical signals alone. The company calls this sensorless commutation. Fewer sensors mean fewer parts that can fail, which matters most in dusty, wet or hot conditions.[6][7] About 8.4 million Indian two- and three-wheelers were built with an ISG in FY26, against 5.1 million three years earlier, and management says more than 80% of the FY26 growth in that count came from SEDEMAC.[6]

The people who pay are vehicle makers, not the riders. TVS Motor alone supplied 75.48% of revenue in the nine months to December 2025, 80.46% in FY25 and 83.46% in FY24.[8] The rating agency ICRA puts the largest customer at 77% of FY26 revenue.[13] The three biggest customers made 91.22% of revenue and the ten biggest made 98.67% in the nine months to December 2025. Bajaj Auto and Kirloskar Oil Engines are among the named buyers.[8] None of them has promised to buy a minimum quantity, so the company earns its place again with every order.[8]

The money mechanics are simple to state and hard to change. Each ECU is priced roughly by the power of the motor it controls, and management says automotive prices become hard to move after long talks with a customer.[7] The largest cost is parts. In the June 2026 quarter the cost of materials consumed was ₹200.20 crore against revenue of ₹309.77 crore.[14] Most of the parts that matter, the semiconductors, are imported: ICRA says about 75% of raw material needs come from abroad.[13] The company also spends heavily on engineering. HSBC puts research and development at about 7% of revenue in FY25 and 9% in the first nine months of FY26, and the restated accounts show ₹258.93 million (₹25.89 crore) of product development capitalised in those nine months.[23][10] Capitalised means the cost is put on the balance sheet and written off over time instead of hitting profit at once, which makes reported profit look slightly better than cash profit.

Why a start-stop controller needs software that is hard to copy

A starter generator is an old idea. What is new is running it without sensors. Most competing systems have sensors inside the machine, and the control box reads them. SEDEMAC's controller reads the electrical behaviour of the machine instead, which removes a part that can break and a wiring step that costs money. Management says very few companies in the world make this kind of controller, and the offer document names Shindengen and Denso as the other ISG makers, most of whose designs still use sensors.[11][7] An independent industry study quoted in the offer document puts SEDEMAC at about 35% of domestic ISG controller volume in FY25.[11] In October 2024 the company launched a combined controller that does both the starter-generator job and the fuel-injection job in one box. Cumulative sales of that combined box passed one million units in September 2026, 23 months after launch.[18] Combining two controllers into one saves the vehicle maker money, which is a reason to switch even without caring about sensors.[6]

Newer products use the same control technique. Electric-vehicle motor controllers are already selling: about 7.4% of FY26 two- and three-wheeler revenue came from electric products.[6] An aftertreatment controller for commercial vehicles is due to start production in the second half of FY27, and a first motor controller for a power tool is expected to start production within four to five quarters of May 2026.[6] Management gives no revenue figures for any of these, so they are options on future growth and not yet part of our numbers.

Who pays, and why that shapes everything else

Because a handful of vehicle makers buy nearly everything, the company's fortunes follow theirs. If TVS Motor sells more scooters that carry the ISG, SEDEMAC sells more controllers. If TVS Motor decides to buy part of its supply from someone else, SEDEMAC's volume falls. The offer document says as much in plain terms: customers can change order volumes or choose another solution at any time.[8] Management argues that concentration is normal at this stage, because an anchor customer adopts a new technology first and the others follow, and it shows a metric that has fallen over three years.[6] The facts support part of that story. The share from TVS Motor fell from 83.46% in FY24 to 75.48% in the nine months to December 2025, and Bajaj Auto and others have been added.[8] But ICRA's FY26 figure of 77% shows the share did not keep falling.[13] Outside India, the company sold about US$10 million of generator controllers in FY26, and management says all of its exports are in that generator business.[7]

How the company reached the stock market, and what the listing did not do

SEDEMAC listed on 11 March 2026. The issue price was ₹1,352, the shares opened at ₹1,535, and the offer raised ₹1,087.35 crore.[26][37] The offer was entirely a sale of existing shares by earlier investors, called an offer for sale. The company itself received no money from it.[9] The largest seller was A91 Emerging Fund II, which offered up to 2,410,650 shares at an average cost of ₹368.28 each. Two promoter-group members offered only 45,000 and 67,500 shares.[40] That matters for two reasons. First, the growth plan, including new factories, has to be paid for from the company's own cash flow and loans, which we test in Chapter 3. Second, the sellers were investors who had put money in before, and several of them came back to the market in September 2026. A block of about 11.05% of the shares, worth ₹1,469 crore, changed hands between five sellers and new buyers, according to a news headline carried on a filings site.[15] The people who run the company, the promoters, still hold 26.23% and none of it is pledged to lenders.[21][22] Chapter 6 returns to what the larger free float means for the share price.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong in one narrow place and exposed everywhere around it. The strength is technical. ICRA describes a niche in powertrain controllers where SEDEMAC faces limited competition for its principal product, the sensorless ISG, because of the patented nature of its products.[13] HSBC, which started coverage in July 2026, quotes the view that SEDEMAC was the first company globally to develop the technique. It also says that, apart from Honda, which uses its own sensor-based design, it sees no structural barriers to other two- and three-wheeler makers adopting SEDEMAC's ISGs.[23] Those two statements fit together: the technology is hard to copy, and the market for it is still open.

The evidence that the strength is real sits in the numbers. Operating margin, which is the share of each rupee of sales left after running costs, rose from about 12.8% in FY23 to 19.0% in FY25 on the company's own measure of operating profit, and ICRA puts it at 21.5% for FY26.[12][13] Return on capital employed (ROCE), meaning profit before interest and tax divided by the money tied up in the business, was 35.3% on the latest Screener reading and 33.79% for FY25 on the offer document's definition.[2][10] Few auto parts makers earn that on a fast-growing set of assets. The four listed peers we compare in Chapter 3 show ROCE of 15.1% to 22.9%.[29][30][31][32]

The weakness is that almost all the strength is carried by one customer and one product. ICRA says a single product supplied roughly two thirds of revenue in FY25 and FY26, and its largest customer supplied 77% in FY26.[13] More than 80% of revenue comes from Indian two- and three-wheelers, a market that the offer document says grew at only 3% a year from FY20 to FY25.[13][11] About 75% of raw materials are imported, so a chip shortage or a weaker rupee hits cost directly.[13] Management itself says no supplier keeps a monopoly forever, and that people have been trying for seven or eight years to crack the problem.[7]

Taken together, the right word is narrow rather than weak. The company has a moat, which is a lasting advantage, but the moat is a single technique and a single main buyer. That is why this book scores the customer-concentration test as a failure in Chapter 2B even while it scores growth, cash conversion and product pipeline as passes. It is also why the price paid for the shares, discussed in Chapter 5, matters more here than it would for a company with ten equal customers.

What protects the business

Four things protect the profits. The first is patent-backed technology that ICRA says leaves no material competition in the sensorless category.[13] The second is switching cost. A vehicle maker that fits an ISG controller into a model has tested it for reliability across millions of units, and changing supplier means retesting. Management says it has now sold more than 13 million control-intensive ECUs in total, which is the track record that vehicle makers buy.[16][7] The third is reach. Three of the four largest Indian two-wheeler makers use SEDEMAC's sensorless ISG, according to management.[6] The fourth is cost. The combined ISG+EFI controller saves the maker money compared with buying two boxes, and adoption of the combined box is fast: one million units in 23 months.[6][18] Each protection is real, but each also depends on vehicle makers continuing to choose SEDEMAC, which they are free to stop doing.

What could break the protection

The first risk is that TVS Motor, or another large buyer, brings in a second supplier. The offer document warns that customers can adjust order volumes or pick alternatives at any time.[8] Management's own answer is that ISG adoption has been under way for seven or eight years and that nobody has cracked the problem, but it also says that some of the more technically competent players may solve it in time.[7] The second risk is cost. A tightening chip supply raised raw material cost in the June 2026 quarter, and the share of sales spent on materials rose from about 59.6% to about 62.6% in a quarter.[17] The third is the vehicle cycle. A weak monsoon cuts two-wheeler demand, and a quiet US hurricane season cuts genset demand, and management named both in its FY27 outlook.[6] The fourth is an execution risk. New factories are being built, and if demand falls short of capacity, the fixed costs show up in margins.

Governance and ownership signals

Promoters hold 26.23% of the shares and none of that stake is pledged, which means it is not offered as security to lenders. That is the clean position a long-term holder wants.[21][22] The list of shareholders changed fast after the lock-in periods ended: foreign institutions rose from 4.27% to 10.30% between June and September 2026, and mutual funds from 15.33% to 20.57%.[22][38] The company granted 82,400 stock options on 13 October 2026 at ₹2,700 each and received approval to list up to 9,75,000 shares for its option plan, which is a small amount of dilution, the rise in share count that thins each holder's slice.[2] The sellers in the September block deal were earlier investors rather than the promoters, since the promoter holding stayed at 26.23%.[15][21] ICRA upgraded the company's bank-loan rating to [ICRA]A with a Positive outlook on 15 September 2026.[13][36] Small tax items exist, for example GST show-cause notices totalling ₹2.35 crore, which are not material against ₹104 crore of annual profit.[17]

Why the moat matters to your position

A moat protects profit only while customers keep choosing the product. SEDEMAC's moat is real. It rests on a patent-backed technique, more than 13 million units sold and about 35% of the domestic ISG controller market in FY25.[16][11] For you, a price dip is less likely to come from a rival beating the product. It is more likely to come from a customer decision or a change in market mood. The picture would change if a second supplier won a large share at TVS Motor, or if a rival showed a working sensorless design.[7] Until then the moat supports holding the 200 shares. It does not support buying more at 114 times earnings.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Chapter 2 described the business in words. This chapter turns it into five numbers that matter for this particular company. It then checks whether management did what it said it would do. We pick five operating measures because a general screen of profit and debt cannot tell a controller maker with a patented technique from a plain parts assembler. Each measure gets a score of 1.0 for a pass, 0.5 for a watch and 0.0 for a fail. The house rule counts the company as qualified when the total is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 and no more than one measure fails.

The five measures are chosen for these reasons. Volume growth shows whether the main product is spreading. The share of sales from the largest customer shows how much of the business can be lost to one decision. The operating margin shows whether the technical edge turns into pricing power as the company scales. Cash conversion over three years shows whether reported profit turns into cash. Pipeline conversion shows whether the large engineering spend produces products that sell. Two more items matter. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after buying plant and equipment, and it was negative last year. Receivable days are the days customers take to pay. We discuss both after the table. They are not scored, because the new plants are not yet running. Cash conversion compares cash flow from operations, the cash the business generates before spending on plant, with its profit after tax. The scoring file's rule of 3.0 of 5.0 with at most one failure is the test we apply, and this five-measure scorecard stands in for the verdict taxonomy's 'at least 3 of 4' wording.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter gives the numbers behind the story and then runs the three gates that decide whether the company deserves a place in the shortlist. Layer 1 asks two things. Are the static levels, such as debt, profit rates and cash conversion, safe and strong? And is the direction of travel, the rate of change, still upward? A company can pass the first and fail the second, or the reverse, so both need to be tested.

SEDEMAC has a short record as a listed company but a longer one as a business. Sales rose from ₹283 crore in FY22 to ₹1,058 crore in FY26. So the three-year profit growth rate of 128% measures a low starting point and not a normal pace.[1] We therefore lean on the direction of margins, cash and debt more than on the profit growth rate.
