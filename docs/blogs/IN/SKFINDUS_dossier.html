1. What the company does

SKF India (Industrial) Limited is a company that sells bearings. A bearing is a ring of steel with balls or rollers inside it. It lets a shaft, a wheel or a gear spin with much less friction and wear. Without bearings, motors, gearboxes, rail wagons, wind turbines and steel-mill rollers would heat up and fail. The company also sells seals, lubrication systems, condition-monitoring sensors and repair and re-manufacturing services. [9] It is the industrial half of the former SKF India Limited. A court-approved plan, the Scheme of Arrangement, moved the industrial business into this new company on 1 October 2025. [16] [14] Every shareholder of the old company received one share of the new company for each share held, 49,437,963 shares in total, and the shares began trading on the NSE and BSE on 5 December 2025. [14] The Swedish parent, AB SKF, holds 52.58 percent. [24] [15]

Three kinds of customers pay the company. Original equipment manufacturers, called OEMs, build machines and buy bearings to put inside them. In the June 2026 quarter OEMs were 54 percent of revenue, and recent wins include a ₹140 crore order from a global wind-gearbox maker and a ₹35 crore contract with a tractor maker. [20] [21] Distributors are the second group, 34 percent of revenue. More than 120 distributors across India sell bearings to factories that need replacements, and this aftermarket business is the higher-margin one, described by the CFO as 'high double-digit' against 'high single-digit' for OEM sales. [9] [21] The third group, exports and sales to the automotive sister company SKF India, was 12 percent of revenue. [20]

The end markets are broad. In the June 2026 quarter, general machinery (pumps, motors, gearboxes) was 26 percent of revenue, railways 20 percent, wind 16 percent, agriculture and electrical 15 percent, heavy industry 12 percent and metals 11 percent. [20] The company serves more than 40 industrial segments. [9] That spread is useful because the segments do not all turn at once, but all of them depend on capital spending, which is the weakness of any industrial supplier. India's industrial bearings market was valued at about ₹66,000 crore in 2024 and is forecast to grow only 4.33 percent a year to 2033, according to a market study quoted in the annual report, so growth above that rate has to come from share gains, wind and rail. [8]

How the company makes money is not what the word 'manufacturer' suggests. In FY26 it bought ₹1,812 crore of finished goods for resale and used ₹564 crore of raw materials, so about 76 percent of what it purchased came in as finished bearings that other SKF companies had made. [2] It manufactures in Pune and Bengaluru and says 60 percent of what it sells is now localised, with a target of 75 percent. [14] [10] That matters for profit. Imported goods bring currency risk, and the company pays group fees for royalty, trademark and IT services. In the six months to March 2026 those three fees came to ₹73 crore, about 4.1 percent of revenue in those months. [13] [18] [17]

How the group relationship works

SKF India (Industrial) is a subsidiary of AB SKF in Sweden, and the other SKF companies in India are its sister companies. [14] Sister companies make many of the bearings it sells, lend it technology and share services. This is why the company has a long list of related party transactions, which are deals between the company and firms controlled by the same owner. Such deals are legal and need approval from the audit committee and from shareholders when they are large, but they can move profit from one pocket to another. [12] A related party deal is only as fair as its price. The owner of 52.58 percent decides the terms, and minority holders have limited ways to check them.

At the AGM of 13 August 2026 shareholders were asked to approve ceilings for FY27 with six group companies: ₹640 crore with SKF GmbH Germany, ₹1,207 crore with SKF Engineering and Lubrication India, ₹413 crore with SKF Sverige, ₹465 crore with AB SKF, ₹1,818 crore with SKF India Limited and ₹382 crore with SKF Asia Pacific, a total of ₹4,924 crore. [12] A ceiling is the most the company may do, not what it will do, and the ceilings count sales and purchases both ways. Even so, the total is about 1.4 times FY26 revenue of ₹3,440 crore, which shows how tied the business is to its group. [2]

The demerger in plain words

A demerger splits one company into two and gives shareholders shares in both. Here, the auto bearings stayed in SKF India Limited, now a pure automotive company, and the industrial business went to the new company. The group's stated reason is that each business can set its own plan, with its own capital. [14] The cost of the split was real. The new company booked ₹196 crore of one-off charges in FY26, of which ₹164 crore is an estimated stamp duty and transfer premium for land moved from the old company, ₹29 crore is IT and professional fees for separating the systems and ₹3.5 crore is a gratuity charge from new labour codes. [5] The one-time IT cost continues: the CFO said it will run for two more quarters from the August call, tapering by the end of the year. [21]

For an investor the split has two consequences. First, the record is short. The company was incorporated on 17 December 2024, its first financial year ran only to 31 March 2025, and the first full year is FY26, so there are no earlier annual accounts for this entity. [15] [2] Second, the figures for the first half of FY26 were carved out of the old company's books by management with assumptions and were not audited or reviewed by the auditors, so year-on-year comparisons are approximate. [17] [20] Chapter 3 shows what can be said with the data that exists, and says plainly where it cannot be said.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Bearing: a steel part that lets one surface rotate against another with little friction.
  • OEM: original equipment manufacturer, a company that builds machines and buys parts like bearings to fit into them.
  • Aftermarket: sales of replacement parts to owners of machines already in use.
  • Related party transaction: a deal between the company and another firm under the same owner.
  • Demerger: a court-approved split of one company into two, with shareholders receiving shares in both.
  • Exceptional item: a one-off cost or gain shown on a separate line, so the usual profit is easier to see.
  • PBT margin: profit before tax divided by revenue; it includes other income such as interest.
  • Localisation: the share of what the company sells that is made or bought inside India.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A bearing is a small part in a large machine, and a failed bearing can stop a steel line or a wind turbine. Customers therefore pay for reliability and not only for price. SKF has made bearings since 1907, and the annual report describes long relationships with railways, public-sector buyers and wind and metals customers. [18] [9] That is the first source of strength: a respected brand in applications where failure is costly. The second is reach: more than 120 distributors and a direct sales team cover about 40 industrial segments. [9] The third is access to the global SKF group's technology and its range of products, for which the company pays royalty, trademark and IT fees. [13]

The balance sheet is the fourth strength. At 31 March 2026 the company held ₹569 crore of cash and had no bank borrowings, only ₹4.6 crore of lease liabilities, against equity of ₹1,477 crore. [3] Return on capital employed (ROCE) was 28.2 percent before one-off items, and the debt service cover was 45 times. [7] A company with this much cash can fund the new Pune plant, a ₹900 crore to ₹950 crore project that management says will start producing in 2028, without borrowing. [21] The question is whether it spends the cash on that plant, on dividends, or on loans to group companies, which Chapter 2B examines.

What protects the business

Switching costs protect the aftermarket. A plant maintenance team that has fitted one bearing brand in a gearbox usually keeps buying that brand, because an unplanned shutdown costs more than any price difference. That is why the CFO described distribution as the higher-margin channel. [21] Solutions, meaning remanufacturing, condition monitoring and plant-level maintenance contracts, are a small start: about 6 to 7 percent of the India business, with management saying it expects them to grow faster. [21] Remanufacturing used rail bearings with a three-year warranty is the clearest example, and it is also a way to keep customers tied to the brand.

Demand has a long runway. India added a record 6.05 GW of wind capacity in FY26, against 4.15 GW a year earlier, taking installed wind capacity to 56.09 GW, and the budget gave Indian Railways a record ₹2,78,000 crore. [8] Wind and rail together are 36 percent of the company's revenue in the June 2026 quarter. [20] Management also says steel, cement and mining companies plan to double capacity by 2030, which should lift demand for pumps, motors and conveyors, and therefore for bearings. [21]

What weakens it

Profit per rupee of sales is the weak point. The company's operating profit, defined as revenue minus operating costs and before other income, was about 11.3 percent of revenue in FY26, and the quarterly profit before tax margin has fallen from 14.4 percent in the September 2025 quarter to 9.0 percent in June 2026. [1] [17] [19] The nearest peers earn more: Timken India and Schaeffler India each report an operating margin of about 18 percent. [25] [26] The gap exists partly because the company buys three-quarters of its goods ready-made from group firms and then adds only its own distribution margin, which is a thinner business than making the part. [2]

Mix is moving the wrong way. OEM sales are growing faster than distribution, and in the quarter to March 2026 the railway share rose, which management said lowered margins. [20] [22] Currency is a second pressure. In the June 2026 quarter the rupee's fall against the euro and other currencies cost the company ₹14.7 crore on imported goods, about 1.5 percent of revenue, and management says it expects that to depend on how the rupee moves. [21] A third is the annual report's own list of weaknesses, which names residual dependence on imported components, exposure to cyclical, capital-spending-driven sectors, and an organisation still in transition after the demerger. [9]

Governance deserves separate attention. The Managing Director who led the company through the demerger, Mukund Vasudevan, stepped down from that post on 31 August 2026 to take a wider group role, and Sujeeth Pai became Managing Director from 1 September 2026 for five years. [23] [21] That is a change of leader nine months after listing. The company has also lent ₹157.6 crore to a sister company, SKF Engineering and Lubrication India, and asked shareholders to approve a loan of up to ₹250 crore to it in FY27. [11] The loan is secured on that company's fixed assets and a guarantee from AB SKF, and it pays interest at the seven-year government bond yield plus 1.5 percentage points, but it is cash the minority shareholders cannot spend or receive as dividends. [11]

Where the business sits against its peers

The company's market position is best judged by what its customers and rivals show in numbers. Timken India has an operating margin near 18 percent and return on capital employed of 19.0 percent, Schaeffler India 18 percent and 27.3 percent, and NRB Bearings 18 percent and 18.4 percent. [25] [26] [27] SKF India (Industrial) reports a higher return on capital employed, 28.2 percent, but on a lower margin and with a balance sheet whose book values came from a carve-out, so the ratio comes from asset-light trading and a large payables balance more than from high prices. [7] [3] Our reading is that the moat is real but narrower than its brand suggests: strong in distribution and in rail and wind, weaker where price decides, as in OEM supply. Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks two questions. The static question is whether the business passes each fixed rule today. The trajectory question is whether each measure is improving or worsening, because a cheap company with a rising trend can be worth owning even if one level misses. The table applies all of the India hard red lines in our rule book, then the three archetype tests, and then the technical routing rule. Several terms recur. Debt-to-equity compares borrowings with shareholders' money. Cash flow from operations is the cash the core business collects. Price-to-earnings compares the share price with profit per share. Chart terms follow a stock through four stages: Stage 1 is a flat base, Stage 2 a rising trend, Stage 3 a top and Stage 4 a falling trend. The strongest case against our own rejection

A fair review of a rejection asks what the best argument for buying would be. It would run like this. The company has ₹569 crore of cash and no debt, a return on capital employed of 28.2 percent, revenue growing 18 percent, and the SKF brand. [3] [7] [19] Management says about 3 points of the 9.0 percent margin are temporary, which puts the underlying margin near 12 percent. [21] [30] If consensus FY28 earnings of ₹86.77 are met and the market pays 36 times, the stock would be worth about ₹3,124, a gain of 29 percent.

Our answer is arithmetic. Analysts' FY27 earnings of ₹72.03 a share need a profit before tax margin of about 13.1 percent in each of the next three quarters, against 9.0 percent in the last one, on our revenue path. FY28 consensus needs 13.0 percent for the year, above the 12.5 percent we expect. [21] The rejection costs little if we are wrong: the stock can be reviewed again on 4 November, and a rise before then is not a loss, only a price we would not pay with a reward-to-risk of 1.0.
