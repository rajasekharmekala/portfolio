Before you read

Kirloskar Ferrous makes pig iron, engine castings, alloy steel and seamless tubes at seven plants in India, and it belongs to the Kirloskar Group[2][3]. Our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: the company passes every red line in our rulebook, but the share price has not yet shown a turn[1][2]. We would buy only on a pullback to ₹428 to ₹437, and only after the 30 October 2026 results. The case rests on one claim, that profit margins recover from 12 percent toward 13 percent as cost projects start[3][4]. The idea fails if the share closes below ₹411.25 or if the December-quarter margin is below 12.5 percent.

1. What the company does

Kirloskar Ferrous Industries was incorporated in 1991 and sits inside the Kirloskar Group, a Pune-based manufacturing family that dates to 1888[2][3]. It began as a maker of foundry-grade pig iron, which is crude iron cast into small blocks that foundries melt to make engine blocks, pump housings and pipes. It added ferrous castings, which are the iron parts themselves, such as cylinder blocks, cylinder heads and transmission housings[2]. In the 2021-22 financial year it bought the insolvent ISMT Limited through the bankruptcy process and gained alloy steel and seamless tubes, which are hollow steel pipes with no weld seam[10]. In September 2023 it bought Oliver Engineering, a foundry in Punjab. Oliver and Adicca Energy Solutions have since been merged into the listed company, and the August 2026 presentation restates earlier numbers after that merger[10][3][29].

Four businesses make up the sales line. Pig iron sells to foundries and steel mills. Castings sell to tractor, truck and car makers and to engine builders. Alloy steel from the Jejuri plant goes mainly to the auto and bearing industries. Seamless tubes go to boiler makers, oil and gas users, traders and engineering firms[3]. The company runs plants at Koppal, Hiriyur, Solapur, Rajpura, Jejuri, Baramati and Ahilyanagar[3].

Pig iron and castings together bring in about 60 percent of sales, and tubes bring in another 27 percent. That matters because the three businesses react to different prices: pig iron to coking coal and iron ore, castings to tractor and truck demand, and tubes to oil, gas and boiler orders. Castings are the steadiest and tubes are the most volatile.

How the four businesses fit together

Think of the company as one chain with four links. Two mini blast furnaces at Koppal and one at Hiriyur turn iron ore, coke and coal into liquid iron[4][6]. Some of that iron is sold as pig iron. Some is poured straight into the company's own foundries to make castings. Some feeds the steel plant that makes the blooms, which are thick steel bars, that the tube mills roll into seamless tubes[10]. Management calls this a natural hedge: when pig iron prices fall, the company's casting and tube plants buy cheaper metal from its own furnaces[3].

The cost side is where the company has invested. A coal-injection and oxygen system cuts coke use, a 40 megawatt plant turns waste heat into power, and the company's own iron ore mine now supplies part of its ore[8][10]. A 70 megawatt solar plant in Jalna already runs, and another 35 megawatts of solar and 25 megawatts of wind are being commissioned[6][3]. These projects explain why management keeps saying that margins can rise even if prices stay flat.

What drives profit in each business

Pig iron is a commodity, so the company is a price taker. Profit equals the selling price minus the cost of coking coal, iron ore and power. Coking coal is mostly imported, so the rupee exchange rate and global prices matter. The pig iron price moved from ₹40,600 a tonne in FY25 to ₹38,032 in FY26 and then to ₹42,383 in the June 2026 quarter[5][3]. Castings are priced per tonne on contracts, with raw material price changes passed on to customers after a delay. The company holds an estimated 19 to 20 percent share of large castings and acts as the only supplier for many parts[10]. Tubes sell in a market where Chinese imports, oil and gas orders and product mix move profit. In the June 2026 quarter the company sold more low-priced line pipe, and tube volume fell 14 percent[3][4].

Each product therefore asks a different question. For pig iron the question is the spread, which is the gap between selling price and input cost. For castings it is volume and the share of complex parts. For tubes it is orders, and for steel it is how fast the new rolling capacity fills. Chapter 2B scores the company on exactly these questions.

Who owns it, and the listing history

Promoters hold 50.73 percent at September 2026. Of that, Kirloskar Industries Limited holds 45.86 percent of the equity, and none of the promoter shares is pledged, which means no shares are held as collateral for loans[14]. Mutual funds raised their stake from 12.09 percent to 13.30 percent in the September 2026 quarter, with small-cap and mid-cap funds the largest holders[14]. Promoter holding stood at 56.44 percent in June 2024 and 50.93 percent in September 2024. The reason is not stated in the sources we read, so we treat it as an open question and do not rely on it[2].

The stock trades on the National Stock Exchange again after a long gap. In August 2025 management said the exchange had allowed trading and then withdrawn it, and in May 2025 it promised to work on a relisting[8][9]. Our price ledger holds only 136 daily bars for the NSE series, which is about six months, so the 200-day average and the trend stage cannot be computed[1]. Analysts asked about the relisting again in November 2025, and management passed the question to the finance chief[32]. The exchange notice that restored trading was not found, and the ledger's thin history is the reason Chapter 4 reads technical signals with care.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is the profit from running the plants before finance costs, tax and the yearly write-down of equipment, and in this report it is the same as operating profit.
  • operating margin is operating profit divided by sales. An operating margin of 12 percent means ₹12 of operating profit for every ₹100 of sales.
  • ROCE means return on capital employed, which is the profit earned on each rupee of money the business uses. A figure of 15 percent means ₹15 of profit a year for every ₹100 put to work.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share, which is the company's profit divided across all its shares.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and paying for plants and equipment. Free cash flow is what could be paid out or used to cut debt.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates, before it pays for new plants or repays loans.
  • debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' money. A figure of 0.27 means ₹27 of loans for every ₹100 of owners' money.
  • net debt is borrowings minus the cash held. working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills, net of what the company itself owes suppliers.
  • price-to-earnings is the share price divided by profit per share. It tells you how many years of current profit you pay for.
  • consensus estimate is the average of what analysts forecast for a number, and guidance is the company's own published forecast.
  • capital expenditure is spending on plants and equipment, shortened to capex in this report.
  • RSI is the relative strength index, a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been moving. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR is the average true range, which is the typical daily price swing in rupees. We use one ATR as a yardstick for how far the price sits from support.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days, and a 50-day moving average and a 200-day moving average work the same way over 50 and 200 days. These are slow lines that the price often bounces off.
  • Stage 2 is the phase of a confirmed rising price on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the phase of a confirmed falling price. Stages 1 and 3 are the sideways phases at the bottom and the top.
  • base is a sideways consolidation that a price builds before a move. The pivot is the top of that base. A breakout is a move above the pivot on strong volume, and a retest is the pullback that tests the pivot from above.
  • stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better and never chases the market. An entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain from entry to target divided by the loss from entry to stop: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).
  • A basis point is one hundredth of a percent. A risk budget of 50 basis points means we accept losing 0.5 percent of the whole portfolio if the stop is hit.
  • TIER_3 is our confidence class for a position. Each tier has a risk budget and a tier cap, which is the largest weight the position may take: 3.5 percent of the portfolio for TIER_3.
  • A capitulation regime is a market washout, where selling looks exhausted but a recovery is not yet proven. In it we start with a pilot, which is half the normal size, and add only after the price confirms.
  • FULL_SIZE is the band of prices within 0.6 ATR of support, where the stop is closest and full size is allowed. STANDARD is the band from 0.6 to 1.2 ATR above support, where standard size is allowed once a test confirms the turn.
  • WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT is our verdict for a name that passes the business tests but is not yet at a buyable price: no purchase now, limit orders later. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP means buy in steps on pullbacks to support. REJECTED means the idea is dropped for this research cycle.
  • breadth is the share of tracked stocks that trade above their 50-day average, and a low figure means a weak market. A distribution day is a day of heavy selling in the benchmark index. Many of them in a short window warn that big investors are selling.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong where it has scale and cost control, and weak where it has no pricing power. It is a mid-sized, integrated iron producer with a credit rating of AA with a stable outlook from ICRA, an Indian rating agency[10]. The rating agency credits its position in foundry-grade pig iron and castings, the backward integration into coke, power and ore, and its long relationships with engine and vehicle makers[10]. It also warns that profit margins swing with raw material prices because pig iron is a commodity[10]. Both statements are true, and our view sits between them.

What the company does better than most

Management says the company holds 22 to 25 percent of foundry-grade pig iron and 19 to 20 percent of large castings, meaning parts above 50 kilograms for tractors, commercial vehicles, utility vehicles and diesel engines[10]. ICRA notes that the company is the single source for many of these parts, which leads to repeat orders[10]. Two cost levers support the margin. Pulverised coal injection and oxygen enrichment cut coke use, and the carbon rate was about 535 kilograms per tonne in May 2025, down 60 to 70 kilograms from earlier years[8]. The company's own iron ore mine saves roughly ₹700 to ₹800 a tonne on ore, by management's account[8]. Castings volume also gave an honest signal in the June 2026 quarter: sales rose 18 percent to 41,345 tonnes while the price per tonne rose 5 percent, so the growth was not bought with discounts[3].

Demand in the end markets is mixed but supportive. Alloy steel use rose 25.6 percent to 3.0 million tonnes in the June 2026 quarter on auto and engineering demand[3]. India's tractor sales grew 8 percent to 6.1 lakh units in the first half of FY27 but fell 18 percent in September on a patchy monsoon and a festive season that shifted into October[20]. Mahindra's domestic tractor sales fell 23 percent in September to 50,208 units against 64,946 a year earlier[21]. Tractors are 40 percent of the company's casting volume, so that September weakness is the main near-term demand risk[3].

Where the business is weak

The return on capital is only 13.4 percent, below the 15 percent we want from a compounder[2]. Profit has not followed sales. Revenue grew from ₹6,417 crore in FY23 to ₹6,889 crore in FY26, which is 2.4 percent a year, and operating margin fell from 13 percent to 12 percent over the same period[2]. Reported FY26 profit of ₹507 crore looks like a 72 percent jump, but it includes a one-time tax credit from the merger. Without it, profit was about ₹364 crore, which Chapter 3 shows in detail[11][12][5].

Three other weak spots stand out. First, about three quarters of casting volume goes to tractor and commercial vehicle makers (40 percent plus 34 percent in the June 2026 mix), and ICRA puts the share of casting sales near 80 percent, so the business is cyclical[3][10]. Second, the company imports coking coal, so costs jump when coal or the dollar rises. In the June 2026 quarter, other expenses rose ₹105 crore, or 26 percent, to ₹505.0 crore, and power cost reached 9.3 percent of revenue against 6.2 percent a year earlier[3]. Third, Karnataka's 8 percent forest development fee has been argued since 2016. It is shown as a contingent liability of about ₹350 crore, and the Supreme Court has reserved its order[4][26].

How it compares with listed peers

No listed company matches this mix exactly, so Chapter 3 compares four partial peers. Maharashtra Seamless makes seamless tubes and earns a 14.3 percent return on capital with almost no debt[23]. Jindal Saw and Electrosteel Castings make ductile iron pipes and earn 10.4 and 5.0 percent[22][24]. Kirloskar Ferrous sits second on return on capital and first on return on equity among the five companies in the Chapter 3 table. That is why we study it closely, but it is not a standout compounder.
