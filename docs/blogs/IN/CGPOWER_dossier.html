Before you read

CG Power and Industrial Solutions makes the heavy electrical equipment that moves and uses power: transformers, circuit breakers, motors, railway signalling and, new this year, chip packaging.[1] The Murugappa Group owns 56 percent of it, and the company has almost no debt.[15] The business is strong and still improving: sales rose 30 percent and profit 44 percent in the September 2026 quarter, and signed orders stand at Rs 23,646 crore.[1] Our verdict is WAIT FOR SUPPORT, because the share price of Rs 835.40 already assumes roughly a decade of growth above 20 percent a year, and it sits above the Rs 790.54 to Rs 821.03 band where we would place a buy order. The case would be wrong if the very high transformer margins fade, or if the premium price shrinks faster than profit grows.

1. What the company does

CG Power was founded in 1937 as the Crompton Parkinson Works Private Limited.[13] The Murugappa Group, through Tube Investments of India, took control in November 2020 and still holds 56.29 percent of the shares.[14][15] The company called itself debt-free from FY23 and still has almost no debt: borrowings were Rs 169 crore in September 2026, up from Rs 17 crore in FY24.[13][21] Its FY26 sales were Rs 12,418 crore.[8] It runs 22 factories in India and one in Sweden, with more than 4,900 employees.[1] So you are looking at a mid-sized industrial group with a family-controlled parent, not a start-up.

Seven money terms come up again and again, so here they are in plain words. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses. EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided by its number of shares. The operating margin is the share of each rupee of sales left after running costs. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, so 0.02 means almost no debt. Working capital is the money tied up in unpaid customer bills and stock, less what the company owes its suppliers. Capital expenditure is money spent on factories and machines. FCF (free cash flow) is the cash left after that spending.

Several price terms follow. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by profit per share, so 94 means you pay Rs 94 for each Rs 1 of yearly profit. Book-to-bill is new orders divided by sales; above 1 means the order book is growing. RSI (relative strength index) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has moved. ATR (average true range) is the usual daily price swing in rupees. A Stage 2 stock is in a confirmed uptrend, and a Stage 4 stock is in a confirmed downtrend. A base is a sideways stretch of trading that a stock builds before a move, a pivot is the top of that base, and a breakout is a move above the pivot on heavy volume. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better. The entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the possible gain divided by the possible loss.

The company has two main business lines and one new one. Power Systems sells power transformers, which step grid voltage up and down, and distribution transformers for the last mile. It also sells extra-high-voltage circuit breakers, instrument transformers and gas-insulated switchgear, which is switching equipment sealed in gas so it fits in a small space. The buyers are state and central utilities, the national grid operator, renewable-energy builders, data-centre builders and exporters. Management described recent orders as coming from "utility companies to your AI to your data centers", in India and abroad.[2] A single 765 kV transformer order from the national grid operator was worth about Rs 641 crore, and one export order for a United States data centre was worth Rs 900 crore, the largest in the company's history.[8]

Industrial Systems sells electric motors, drives that control motor speed, railway traction and signalling equipment, and a small range of consumer fans, pumps and water heaters. Low-voltage motors are the largest piece, and management said their margin has moved into double digits.[2] Railway work is bid for in tenders at thin margins, and the company said it will keep that business flat or smaller rather than chase sales that lose money.[2] The consumer line is close to break-even, with profits put back into marketing.[2] This segment grows more slowly and earns about a third of the margin of Power Systems, which matters for the profit mix discussed in chapter 2.

The third business is semiconductors, held in two subsidiaries. CG Semi builds an outsourced semiconductor assembly and test plant (OSAT), a factory that cuts finished chip wafers, packages each chip and tests it before it goes to a phone or a car. The first plant (G1) in Sanand, Gujarat, started commercial production on 4 July 2026 at a peak of 0.5 million units a day; a second, much larger plant (G2) is planned for about 14.5 million units a day.[3][8] The central and state governments support the project: of a Rs 7,584 crore project cost, central assistance is Rs 3,501 crore and the state adds Rs 1,400 crore.[8] Axiro, the second subsidiary, designs radio-frequency chips and was at break-even in the second quarter.[2] The semiconductor work costs money today: it cut consolidated profit before tax by Rs 35 crore, or 91 basis points of sales, in the September 2026 quarter.[1]

Power Systems and Industrial Systems now sell almost the same amount each quarter, but Power Systems earned Rs 443 crore of operating profit against Rs 164 crore for Industrial Systems, which is about 73 percent of the segment total.[1] That profit split is the single most important fact about this company, and chapters 2 and 6 return to it.

How an order becomes profit

Heavy electrical equipment is sold order first and built later. A distribution transformer can ship in about six months, while a 765 kV transformer takes about 18 months from order to delivery.[6] The company books revenue as it delivers, so the order backlog works like a visible queue of future sales. At 30 September 2026 that queue stood at Rs 23,646 crore for the group, which is about 1.7 times the last twelve months of sales.[1][21] The gap between orders arriving and sales leaving is what the market is paying for.

Metal prices matter because copper, steel and transformer oil are large costs. In Power Systems, management said most contracts carry a price variation clause, so a change in commodity prices is compensated by the customer.[5][4] Motors are different: the company raised prices by more than 30 percent over twelve months to offset commodity inflation, and it says price rises only cancel the cost increase, while its own efficiency programme (called CG Edge) produces the margin gain.[2] Railway contracts also have a price formula, but it lags by one or two quarters.[6] So margins in Power Systems are protected by contract terms, while margins in Industrial Systems depend on how fast the company can raise prices.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Order backlog: signed orders not yet delivered, so sales the company can expect without winning anything new.
  • PBIT: profit before interest and tax for a segment, the cleanest read of how profitable one business line is.
  • Transformer (MVA): a machine that changes electricity voltage; its size is measured in megavolt-amperes (MVA), so a plant rated 65,000 MVA can build that much transformer capacity a year.
  • OSAT: a factory that packages and tests finished chips. It earns fees for the work and does not design or sell chips itself.
  • Kavach: India's train collision-avoidance system, made by CG's subsidiary G.G. Tronics.[13]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The company's strength shows most clearly in Power Systems. The segment's operating margin rose from 20.7 percent to 25.0 percent in a year, and management attributed the gain to "stronger price realisation and better operating leverage", meaning prices rose and fixed costs were spread over more output.[1] Sales in that segment grew 41 percent and orders grew 137 percent in the quarter, and the backlog is 81 percent higher than a year ago.[1] A business that can raise prices while orders double is usually short of supply, not short of customers. That points to a real shortage of large transformer capacity in India and abroad, and CG has been adding capacity quickly.

The evidence on capacity is specific. Management said in January 2026 that transformer capacity had been 17,000 MVA a year earlier and would reach 65,000 MVA within a quarter, and in October it described the existing Bhopal-area plant (called T3) as about 65,000 MVA.[5][2] The plant now runs at its maximum, and the board approved a Rs 68 crore project to add 10,000 MVA within the same site over 12 to 15 months, taking it to 75,000 MVA.[1] A new plant at Sehore in Madhya Pradesh began operating on 4 September 2026, 13 months after the work started, and rolled out its first transformer in October, built for 220 kV to 1,200 kV equipment.[1] The company also commissioned a second extra-high-voltage breaker factory in June 2026 that adds 80 percent to breaker capacity.[3] Fast, well-timed expansion is the main sign of a competent operator in this industry.

Several features protect the franchise. Utility customers approve a supplier product by product, and the company owns a 1,600 kV ultra-high-voltage test centre at Nashik where it can run the tests those approvals require.[13] Management said winning US utility approvals is "a long-term work" that runs in parallel, and that approval needs for the Sehore plant depend on the customer and the country.[2] That slow approval cycle keeps new suppliers out for a while, but it also slows CG's own entry into new markets, so the benefit is real and temporary. The balance sheet adds to the strength: borrowings were Rs 169 crore against shareholder funds of about Rs 8,739 crore, and the credit rating is IND AA+ from India Ratings.[21][15]

The weak spots are just as clear. Industrial Systems earned a 9.9 percent margin in FY26 against 12.1 percent in FY25, because of commodity costs and what the company called competitive pricing in railways.[8] The semiconductor build costs about Rs 35 to 43 crore a quarter before it earns anything meaningful.[1][3] And profit is concentrated: close to three-quarters of segment profit comes from one segment at a 25 percent margin. If transformer prices flatten as the new capacity of CG and its rivals arrives, that margin is the first thing to give.

Where the strength comes from

Three things work together. First, demand: the group added Rs 8,485 crore of orders in one quarter, up 78 percent from a year earlier, with transformer export inflow up about 200 percent from the previous quarter.[1][2] Second, supply: capacity went up quickly, helped by a Rs 3,000 crore share sale in mid-2025 rather than by debt.[8] Third, contract terms: most Power Systems contracts pass commodity swings to the customer.[5] Together these explain why profit grew faster than sales. They also tell you what to watch, because each leg can weaken independently.

Where it is thin

Industrial Systems is a lower-quality business. Its 6-month operating profit grew 2 percent on 15 percent more sales, and its margin of 9.4 percent is below the 9.7 percent of a year ago.[1] Motors have recovered to double-digit margins, but railway work is a drag, and the company has said it will let railway sales stay flat or fall.[2] Kavach, the train protection system, has an order worth Rs 433 crore from Chittaranjan Locomotive Works, and it only received the final regulator approval on 26 October 2026, three days after the earnings call said it had not started.[19][2] So a hoped-for lift in railway margins is still an expectation, not a result.

Who else competes

Power equipment in India is contested by global groups and local specialists. In our peer table in chapter 3 we compare CG with ABB India, TD Power Systems, Transformers and Rectifiers (India) and Bharat Heavy Electricals. Management itself said that competition "will continue to be there with more and more players coming in".[3] New entrants and rival capacity additions are the reason we treat today's 25 percent Power Systems margin as a peak-like number in our bear case, not as a floor.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management does not give forecasts. The chief executive said so directly on the July 2026 call: "we don't give any forward -looking statements".[3] So the test of its word is how it handled the plans it did state, such as capacity dates, project starts and margin intentions, across the calls from July 2025 to October 2026. We lined each statement up against what happened.

On factories and capacity, the record is good. In January 2026 the chief executive said he expected to finish transformer capacity "a minimum of 1 year ahead of the time", against a plan the board had approved through 2028.[5] Capacity reached about 65,000 MVA at the Bhopal site, and the Sehore greenfield plant came up in 13 months.[2][1] On dates, the record is mixed. In May 2026 the company expected to commission Sehore between July and August; it began on 4 September and its first transformer came out in October.[4][1] In July 2025 it said the large OSAT plant would "start production in '27"; in October 2026 it said the second plant's qualification starts around the middle of 2027 and commercial production comes at the end of 2028 or in 2029.[7][2] That is a slip of one to two years on the semiconductor project that carries the long-term growth story.

On capital, the record is slower. The company raised Rs 3,000 crore in July 2025, mostly for the OSAT subsidiary, a transformer plant, acquisitions and general use. By 30 September 2026 it had spent Rs 660.39 crore, which is 22 percent, and Rs 2,339.61 crore was sitting in bank fixed deposits earning about 6.5 percent.[18] Spending in step with construction is sensible, but idle cash lowers the return on capital, which the company reports as 27 percent on a standalone basis against 35 percent in the first quarter of FY26 before the share sale.[1][10] The company also changed statutory auditors in the September 2026 quarter, from S R B C & Co to Price Waterhouse Chartered Accountants, in line with a rotation at its parent; we treat that as routine.[18]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue, profit, cash and debt tell one story at three speeds. Sales have grown fast and steadily: from Rs 5,484 crore in FY22 to Rs 12,418 crore in FY26, a three-year compound growth rate of 21 percent and a five-year rate of 33 percent.[21] Profit grew more unevenly because FY24 included a Rs 557 crore gain from a discontinued business, which is why the three-year profit growth figure of 10 percent understates the trend.[21][17] Strip that gain out and profit rose from about Rs 870 crore in FY24 to Rs 1,199 crore in FY26.[17][21] The operating margin has been steady at 13 to 14 percent for four years.[21]

Cash is the part that needs attention. Cash from operations was Rs 1,028 crore, Rs 916 crore and Rs 702 crore in FY24, FY25 and FY26, while free cash flow fell from Rs 805 crore to minus Rs 72 crore as capital spending rose to Rs 777.76 crore in FY26.[21][15] Customers owe the company more: trade receivables grew 45.5 percent in FY26 against sales growth of 25.3 percent, and tax paid jumped to Rs 511.86 crore from Rs 196.44 crore.[15] Customer advances rose too, from Rs 410.17 crore to Rs 706.40 crore, which helps offset the receivables.[15] So the cash gap is mostly a timing effect of a fast-growing, order-driven business, but it has to close for the earnings to be trusted.

The balance sheet is almost debt-free. Borrowings were Rs 169 crore in September 2026 against shareholder funds of about Rs 8,739 crore, and interest cost was Rs 4 crore a quarter.[21] The 2025 share sale of Rs 3,000 crore built a large cash pile that is still largely unspent, and it inflates other income: Rs 84 crore in the June 2026 quarter and Rs 82 crore in September.[21][18] That cash lifts profit today and depresses return on capital, so two readings of the same company are possible, and the Layer 1 table below shows both. The company's own stand-alone return on capital shows the effect: it was 34 percent in the December 2024 quarter, 32.8 percent for FY25 and 35 percent in the June 2025 quarter, before the share sale, and it is 27 percent in the September 2026 quarter, after it.[12][11][10][1] Order intake in FY25 was Rs 13,526 crore, up 35 percent, so the demand story began well before the share sale.[11]

The number

We estimate three-year cash conversion at 0.74 of profit as the headline, with a range from 0.56 to 0.87.

How it was calculated

We added cash from operations for FY24, FY25 and FY26 (Rs 1,028 crore, Rs 916 crore and Rs 702 crore) and divided by net profit for the same years (Rs 1,428 crore, Rs 973 crore and Rs 1,199 crore): 2,646 divided by 3,600 is 0.74.[21]

The inputs

Cash from operations and net profit come from the Screener consolidated tables, which agree with FY26 and FY25 figures in the annual report (Rs 702.45 crore and Rs 916.10 crore).[21][15] The FY24 cash figure was Rs 396.99 crore when first reported and was restated to Rs 1,027.79 crore in the FY25 report, and FY24 profit includes Rs 557 crore from a discontinued business.[17][16]

The range

If FY24 cash is taken as first filed, the ratio is 0.56; if the discontinued-business gain is removed from profit, it is 0.87. The bear case is 0.56, which would sit between the 0.50 floor for growing capital-goods firms and the standard 0.70 floor.

If the number is off

If the true figure is 0.56, the company still clears the capital-goods floor of 0.50 but loses its clean pass on the standard floor, and we would lower the Layer 2 cash reading from WATCH to FAIL if cash from operations is below 60 percent of profit in the half-year statement for September 2026 (filed with the results, not retrieved by us) or in the FY27 annual statement (March 2027).

The number

We estimate that treasury income on idle cash supplies roughly 8 to 17 percent of recent profit.

How it was calculated

Other income was Rs 84 crore and Rs 82 crore in the last two quarters, Rs 166 crore in total. After 27 percent tax that is about Rs 121 crore, or 17 percent of the Rs 717 crore half-year profit. Taking only the unspent share-sale money (Rs 2,339.61 crore at 6.50 percent for half a year, about Rs 76 crore, or Rs 56 crore after tax) gives 8 percent.[21][18]

The inputs

Other income and half-year profit come from the Screener quarterly table and the company's results release; the unspent amount and deposit rate come from the CARE monitoring report; the tax rate of 27 percent is the quarterly rate shown by Screener.[21][1][18]

The range

Low case 8 percent (only the share-sale money earns the income); high case 17 percent (all other income comes from cash). As the money is spent on plants, this income fades and operating profit has to replace it.

If the number is off

If the share is 17 percent and the cash is spent by FY28, that profit has to be replaced by operations. Our FY28 estimate of Rs 12.5 per share already assumes other income falls, so a higher share means a larger drag than we have modelled and a lower target.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 can be wrong, and the honest way to say so is to attack our own conclusion. This chapter takes the strongest bear case against the WAIT FOR SUPPORT verdict, which is that the stock is too expensive to own even after a pullback, and it names the single claim the verdict depends on.

Flaw 1: the 25 percent Power Systems margin is a price effect that can fade

Almost three-quarters of segment profit comes from Power Systems, whose margin rose 430 basis points in a year mainly because of "stronger price realisation".[1] Price is the easiest part of a margin for rivals to compete away. Management said competition would continue with "more and more players coming in", and CG itself is adding capacity that brings it into direct contest for the same orders.[3][1] The weakest assumption is that margins hold for eight more quarters. The evidence that we are wrong would be a Power Systems margin below 22 percent in any quarter, against 21.4 percent in Q3 FY26; we will know when Q3 FY27 results arrive, which we expect around late January 2027 (date not announced).

Flaw 2: the price already pays for the good news

At 95.8 times trailing earnings and about 80 times analysts' FY27 earnings, small changes in sentiment move the price a lot. Our estimate box shows that a multiple of 70 times FY28 earnings, which is above the transformer pure-play and the state-owned group but below ABB India and TD Power, produces a value of Rs 875 and kills the reward-to-risk margin. Even with base earnings, 60 times FY28 earnings gives Rs 750, which is 10.2 percent below the close. The multiple is not an input we can verify; it is a market mood. The evidence of error would be a close below the 200-day average (Rs 808.29) followed by a lower low, and we would know within weeks.

Flaw 3: cash may not follow profit, and idle cash may be inflating profit

Cash from operations fell to Rs 702 crore on profit of Rs 1,199 crore in FY26, working-capital days rose from 16 to 70 and receivables grew 45.5 percent.[21][15] An estimated 8 to 17 percent of recent profit is interest on cash that has not yet been spent.[21][18] The weak point is that we did not retrieve the half-year cash flow statement for September 2026, so the latest trend in cash is unknown to us (N/A). Our single trigger is this: cash from operations below 60 percent of profit, in the September 2026 half-year statement (already filed with the results) or in the FY27 annual statement (due after March 2027), moves our cash measure from WATCH to FAIL.

Flaw 4: semiconductors may need more money and more time

The first OSAT plant started in July 2026, but the larger plant is now expected to reach commercial production only at the end of 2028 or in 2029, and the total project cost is Rs 7,584 crore.[3][2][8] Government support covers Rs 4,901 crore of that (Rs 3,501 crore central and Rs 1,400 crore state), and the share sale earmarked Rs 1,062.85 crore for the subsidiary, leaving a gap of about Rs 1,620 crore that we could not trace to a funding source (N/A).[8][18] A fresh equity raise would dilute you, meaning each share would own a smaller slice of the company. We would see the answer in the next quarterly capital expenditure update.
