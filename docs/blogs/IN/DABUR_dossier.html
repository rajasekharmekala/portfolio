Before you read

Dabur India makes herbal consumer goods such as Dabur Amla hair oil, Dabur Red toothpaste, Chyawanprash and Real juices, and the Burman family owns 66.25% of it.[14][20] This report rates the stock REJECTED today. The frozen price record puts it in Stage 4, which is a confirmed downtrend, and the recovery test called Test C fails.[28][29] The business itself passes every solvency and quality test, with a return on capital of 20.3%, low debt, strong cash flow and no pledged promoter shares.[18][19][20] The rating would be wrong if the stock closes back above its 20-day average after the results on 4 November 2026 and holds there; until then the price has to prove that it has stopped falling[1][2].

1. What the company does

Dabur India is a consumer goods company that describes a legacy of 141 years.[1] It makes everyday products, and many of them draw on Ayurveda, the traditional Indian system of herbal medicine. Shoppers buy its hair oil, toothpaste, health tonics, honey, juices, spices and cooking oils under brand names such as Dabur, Vatika, Real, Hajmola and Badshah. The company says its products are used in 8 out of every 10 Indian households, that it reaches 8.5 million retail outlets and that it has three brands above ₹1,000 crore of yearly sales (Dabur Amla, Dabur Red toothpaste and Real), three above ₹500 crore and 16 between ₹100 crore and ₹500 crore.[1] One crore is ten million, so consolidated revenue of ₹13,193 crore in the year to March 2026 is about ₹132 billion.[13]

The people who pay are shoppers, but Dabur's direct customers are distributors, wholesalers, supermarket chains and online platforms. Dabur books revenue when goods leave its warehouses, not when a shopper picks them off a shelf. That gap can move a quarter's numbers. In 2024-25 management described a one-time inventory correction in the India business as a drag on growth.[12] In the quarter to December 2025, Chyawanprash (a herbal jam sold mostly in winter) was flat for the company because the trade held extra stock, even though shop sales rose 11%.[9]

Out of every ₹100 of net sales, materials took about ₹51.70 in 2025-26. That leaves a gross margin, which is the share of sales left after paying for materials, of 48.3%.[13] Staff costs took 10.4% of revenue, advertising 6.7% and other costs 12.5%. What remains is the operating margin, which is the profit from running the business before interest, tax and other income, and it was 18.6%.[13] Advertising is the lever that managers adjust most often. In Q1 FY27 it grew 13.6%, faster than revenue at 10.6%.[5]

A second source of profit is interest earned on cash and investments. At 31 March 2026 Dabur held ₹9,489 crore of cash and investments against ₹1,063 crore of borrowings, so it had net cash of ₹8,426 crore.[13] Other non-operating income was ₹599.8 crore in 2025-26, against an operating profit of ₹2,451.8 crore and a pre-tax profit of ₹2,420.4 crore.[13] So about one rupee in four of pre-tax profit comes from treasury income, not from selling goods. This matters for valuation in Chapter 5, because part of what an investor pays for the share is cash that earns a bank-like return.

The four engines of the business

Home and Personal Care earned ₹4,591 crore in 2025-26 and supplies 51% of India FMCG sales (FMCG means fast-moving consumer goods, the cheap items people buy again and again). Inside it, hair care is 20% of India sales and oral care 19%, followed by home care at 8% and skin care at 4%.[14] These are the cash earners. Hair oil is where Dabur's Amla brand sits, and toothpaste is where it ranks number two in India by volume.[14]

Healthcare covers health supplements (14% of India sales), over-the-counter and doctor-prescribed Ayurvedic medicines (8%) and digestives (6%).[14] Crisil puts Dabur's share of the Chyawanprash market at over 61%.[17] This is the part of the portfolio most tied to the weather and the season: Glucose is a summer product that sells mostly in rural Bihar, West Bengal and Orissa, and unseasonal rain in March 2026 cut its sales by about a quarter in Q4 FY26.[6][7]

Foods and Beverages holds Real juices (Crisil estimates a share of about 61% of fruit juice), the Hommade cooking range and Badshah, a spice maker that Dabur bought when it made about ₹220 crore a year and says is now heading toward about ₹400 crore.[6][17] International sales of 27% come from 120-plus countries, a manufacturing presence in eight, and the acquired Hobi group in Turkey and Namaste in the United States. The Middle East is 27% of that international total.[16]

Seasons, weather and raw materials

Weather moves the numbers. Q4 (January to March) is a beverage and summer-products quarter, and it is Dabur's lowest-margin quarter: the operating margin was 15.2% in Q4 FY26 against 19.7% in Q1 FY27.[8][5] Unseasonal rain in April and early May 2026 hurt Glucose and juices in Q1 FY27, and then May and June brought mid-teens growth back.[5][6]

Raw materials follow oil prices and farm prices. Hair oil depends on liquid paraffin, which is linked to crude oil. Dabur raised prices on its hair oils after crude moved up.[6] Management put the input cost inflation it faced in Q1 FY27 at about 8%, and in May it had spoken of about 10% across most portfolios, excluding beverages and healthcare.[5][7] It announced a 4% price increase across parts of the business and planned a second round.[7]

How the goods reach shoppers

General trade, meaning small neighbourhood shops, still carries most of the volume. Management says rural general trade grew about 550 basis points faster than urban in Q1 FY27 (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point), helped by a go-to-market programme called Project Saksham.[6][1] Modern trade (supermarket chains) grew at double digits and e-commerce and quick commerce (rapid delivery apps) grew strongly in the September 2026 quarter.[1] Dabur calls itself the second most widely distributed FMCG company in India.[1] Reach matters because rural shops depend on the monsoon. In late July 2026 the chief executive said the rainfall deficit had narrowed to about 14% to 15% and that rural demand was still resilient, with rural growth for the category 170 basis points ahead of urban in NielsenIQ data.[6] A weak monsoon is the event that would slow the channel that is now helping Dabur the most.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Dabur's strength is that shoppers trust its brands and that its distribution is wide. Few Indian consumer companies sit in as many homes. The company calls itself the second most distributed FMCG company, reaching 8.5 million outlets, and in the September 2026 quarter it said rural markets kept outpacing urban ones.[1] That wide reach is hard to copy quickly, because each new outlet needs a distributor, a salesman visit and working relationships built over years.

The second strength is a set of leadership positions in narrow categories. Crisil estimates Dabur's share at over 61% in Chyawanprash, about 19.1% in hair oil and over 16% in toothpaste, and at about 61% in fruit juice.[17] Odonil holds 43.7% of the air freshener market by volume, and Dabur became the number two toothpaste brand in India by volume in 2025-26.[14] In the quarter to December 2025 its hair oil volume share touched an all-time high of 20%, up 193 basis points over the year.[9] In Q1 FY27 management claimed another gain of about 100 basis points in hair oil and said herbal toothpaste outgrew non-herbal toothpaste by 550 basis points.[6] Herbal is a long-running consumer preference, and Dabur leads it.

The weakness is slow growth. Over three years sales grew at 5% a year and profit at 4% a year, and over five years sales grew at 7% and profit at 2%.[20] Revenue grew 5.0% in 2025-26 and 1.3% in 2024-25.[13][17] Part of the reason is that the largest categories are mature. Hair care and oral care together are 39% of India FMCG sales.[14] A shortfall in one of them, or a loss of share, shows up in group growth quickly.

Where the moat is deep

A moat is a lasting advantage that protects profit from rivals. For Dabur it has three layers. The first is brand trust in Ayurvedic and herbal claims that shoppers have known for generations. The second is distribution through millions of shops. The third is scale in procurement of amla, honey and coconut oil, and scale in advertising: advertising and publicity spending was ₹888.1 crore in 2025-26.[13] The credit agency Crisil, which rates Dabur at its highest long-term level, AAA with a stable outlook, names the strong position in India's FMCG industry as the main strength.[17]

Brand-building spend is turning into measured gains. Hair oil grew 18% in Q1 FY27 on an 8% volume gain, and shampoo grew in the early twenties.[6][5] Honey gained 150 basis points of share and the premium juice and coconut water ranges grew above 40% and 70% respectively.[6] The Siens nutraceutical brand grew threefold in the quarter, and management expects an annual run-rate of about ₹50 crore by year-end.[6]

Where the moat is thin

Competition is intense. Crisil lists exposure to intense competition as the main offset to the company's strengths.[17] In hair oil, Marico said its value-added hair oils grew in the twenties for the sixth quarter in a row in the September 2026 period, so Dabur's gains come against a rival that is also taking share.[26] In beverages, management said in May 2025 that it had slightly raised margins for out-of-home sellers to compete with what it called the Campa Cola war.[12]

Prices are rising, and volumes may pay for it. The industry data from NielsenIQ showed FMCG volumes down 2% in April to June 2026 while value rose 0.8%, helped by 2.8% price growth.[27] Dabur did better than that, with India FMCG volume up 5% in Q1 FY27.[6] But the chief executive said on the same call that volumes would not reach double digits and "will be under pressure" because inflation was too high.[6] So the growth now being reported leans more on price than it did a year ago.

International is 27% of sales and growth there is uneven. The Q1 FY27 numbers show the Middle East region up 5.2% in rupees but down 5.4% in constant currency.[5] Rupee growth gets a lift from currency translation, because Middle East sales are in dollar-linked currencies, while the war in the region disrupted supply.[6] In Q4 FY26 total international growth in rupee terms was only 2.5%.[8]

Legal, tax and governance points

Three items deserve a place in any underwriting. The first is a US lawsuit that accuses hair-relaxer products sold by the subsidiary Namaste Laboratories of causing cancer. Crisil says the relaxer portfolio is under 1% of consolidated revenue and that the outcome is unlikely to have a material financial effect.[17] The second is that Dabur's annual report names tax claims on excise, GST and sales tax, mostly about how products are classified, as a potential financial exposure.[15] The third is a management change: Herjit Bhalla, the chief executive of the India business, said on the Q1 FY27 call of 29 July 2026 that he was closing in on three months in the role.[6]

Ownership is stable. The promoters held 66.25% at September 2026, and none of their shares is pledged.[20][18][19] Foreign institutions cut their holding from 16.49% at December 2023 to 9.60% at September 2026, while domestic institutions raised theirs from 11.66% to 18.12%.[20] That means Indian funds have absorbed the foreign selling. If they stop, the price has no natural buyer.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 asks which few numbers run this particular business, and then scores each one as PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). The rule is that the company qualifies when the weighted score reaches 3.0 out of 5.0 and at most one KPI fails.[29] For a branded consumer goods company with a large treasury, we chose five: India volume growth with share gains, operating margin against input inflation, cash conversion, the three-year growth trend, and the return on capital together with the use of cash. Volume is the cleanest sign of real demand. Margin shows pricing power. Cash conversion shows whether profit is real. The growth trend explains the multiple. The last one shows whether the cash pile is working.

The thresholds are stated here so that the reader can check them. Volume growth of 4% or more with share gains scores PASS, 1% to 4% scores WATCH and a fall scores FAIL. An operating margin that holds or rises while inflation is above 5% scores PASS, one that is flat scores WATCH and one that falls by more than 100 basis points scores FAIL. Cash flow of at least 0.9 times profit over three years scores PASS. Three-year sales growth of 8% or more scores PASS, 4% to 8% scores WATCH and under 4% scores FAIL. A return on capital above 20% with a visible plan for surplus cash scores PASS, and a falling return or an idle cash pile scores WATCH.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A company's guidance is its own published forecast for a coming period. The audit below sets what Dabur's management said one and two years ago against what it reported. Management is open with the market: the Q2 FY27 update came on 6 October 2026, nearly a month before the results.[1] That openness is helpful, because we can test it.

The record is mixed on the year-ahead promise and strong on the quarter-ahead promise. In May 2025 the chief executive said Dabur should end 2025-26 with high single-digit growth, if not double-digit.[12] It reported 5.0% for the year, because the first half was weak: Crisil put first-half operating income growth at 3.4%.[13][17] By contrast, the half-year forecast made in October 2025 (mid to high single-digit growth) was met, with growth of 6.1% in Q3 and 7.3% in Q4.[10][9][8] On margins, management said in July 2025 that it saw no pressure, and the operating margin edged up 15 basis points.[11][13]
