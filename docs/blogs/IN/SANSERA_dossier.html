1. What the company does

Sansera Engineering Ltd, based in Bengaluru, turns steel and aluminium into the hard, precise parts inside engines, gearboxes, suspensions and aircraft. Its best-known products are connecting rods (the arm that links a piston to the crankshaft), rocker arms, crankshaft assemblies and gear shifter forks. A carmaker or motorcycle maker is the customer, and the company is a tier-one supplier. That means it sells directly to the vehicle maker rather than through another supplier.[7] In FY26 it sold ₹3,498 crore of goods across 18 integrated plants to more than 100 customers in 27 countries.[3]

The company is vertically integrated, which means it does the forging (shaping hot metal with presses), the heat treatment, the machining and the assembly under one roof. That is why it can win parts that need tight tolerances and why a customer that has qualified a part does not move it easily: a new capacity line takes 18 to 24 months to build and prove, according to the group chief executive.[4] About 65 percent of FY26 sales came from India, 19 percent from Europe, 9 percent from the United States and 7 percent from other countries, and exports from India were 26.6 percent of sales.[3]

Money comes in as a price per part, agreed with each vehicle maker, multiplied by the number of parts delivered. Steel and aluminium are the main costs, and contracts let the company pass raw-material moves through to the customer. But with a delay: in August 2026 management said the pass-through of higher steel, aluminium and consumables costs was still being negotiated and had not yet reached the profit and loss account.[4] Gross profit was 41.3 percent of FY26 sales and 42.5 percent in the June 2026 quarter. Operating profit before depreciation, called EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), was 18.1 percent of FY26 sales. Employee costs were 14.0 percent and depreciation 5.9 percent of FY26 sales. That shows how much of the model is people and machines rather than materials.[3]

Three engines of growth

The first engine is the old business, engine parts for Indian two-wheelers and cars, where Sansera says it supplies about 75 percent of the volume of the components it makes for Maruti, and has gained share with TVS, Yamaha and Suzuki.[4] Indian demand is strong: car dispatches rose 21.8 percent and two-wheelers 13.5 percent in September 2026. Management says its existing plants are running at peak utilisation and the supply chain is stretched.[19][9][4] The company is adding crankshaft and connecting-rod capacity at Pantnagar, Manesar and Bengaluru because two-wheeler makers are outsourcing more crankshaft work.[4]

The second engine is parts that do not care what powers the vehicle, plus electric-vehicle parts. These grew from 5.0 percent of sales in FY21 to 13.5 percent in FY26.[3] A 60:40 joint venture with Japan's Nichidai Corporation, with Sansera investing about ₹50 crore this year, adds cold and warm forged parts for differentials and compressors.[9][3]

The third engine is ADS, which means aerospace, defence and semiconductor equipment. Sales were ₹145.4 crore in the June 2026 quarter. The five-year order backlog was ₹4,436.8 crore on 30 June, rising to about ₹5,750 crore after a further semiconductor-equipment order of about ₹1,250 crore in the following weeks.[3][4] Management guides ADS sales of ₹550 to 600 crore for FY27 and aims for about ₹3,500 crore of ADS capacity by FY31.[3][4] Our job is to price this engine without paying for orders that have not yet become sales.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when customers cannot easily leave, when it earns more than its cost of money, and when growth does not need new debt. Sansera shows the first and third and is on the way to the second. ICRA, the rating agency, calls it the largest supplier of gear shifter forks for two-wheelers in India. It is also one of the largest suppliers of connecting rods and rocker arms for two-wheelers and light vehicles. Sansera is the single source for several parts.[7] Single-source means the vehicle maker has no second supplier for that part, which gives the company pricing steadiness and makes a lost contract unlikely during a model's life.

The proof in the numbers is that the company kept its operating margin (operating profit as a share of sales) at 16 to 18 percent for five years while sales grew 18 percent a year over five years and 14 percent a year over three.[1] The balance sheet also changed: borrowings fell from ₹891 crore at March 2024 to ₹407 crore at March 2025 after the ₹1,200 crore QIP, and stood at ₹591 crore at March 2026 against reserves of ₹3,075 crore.[1][7] ICRA rates the debt [ICRA]AA with a stable outlook. It names total debt above 1.5 times operating profit on a sustained basis as one trigger for a downgrade.[7]

Where the strength is

Qualification is the moat. A forged engine part must pass a first-article inspection, then a long trial, before a customer lets it into mass production. The company's own order table shows how slowly that converts. The order book of new business (peak annual revenue, on the company's older definition that included ADS) was ₹1,920 crore at March 2026, after ₹790 crore of new wins and ₹730 crore moved into mass production during FY26.[3] The same table shows that order book growing every year from ₹1,320 crore at March 2023. So the pipeline is not shrinking.[3]

Customer concentration is falling. The top five customers were 44.5 percent of sales in FY26 against 59.2 percent in FY21. The largest single customer fell from 20.7 percent to 13.0 percent.[3] ICRA still counts more than 50 percent of sales from the top five in the first half of FY26 on its own basis. That is why we score customer concentration as a watch item and not a pass in Chapter 2B.[7]

Where the weakness is

The old engine is tied to the petrol and diesel engine. ICE parts were 65.4 percent of June 2026 sales. ICRA warns that 43 percent of H1 FY26 sales came from Indian two-wheelers and that electrification could cut demand for connecting rods and crankshafts over time.[3][7] Electric two-wheeler retail sales rose 88.7 percent in September 2026 to 2,07,314 units. That shows the direction of travel, even though Sansera's own xEV parts are growing about twice as fast as its tech-agnostic parts.[19][3]

Capital is a second weakness. Capex was ₹591 crore in FY25 and ₹510 crore in FY26, against cash from operations of ₹377 crore and ₹387 crore. So free cash flow, the cash left after capex, was negative at ₹219 crore and ₹123 crore. Management plans a similar ₹510 crore for FY27.[1][3][9] The company has contracts for another ₹212 crore of capital work not yet paid for at March 2026, and has given corporate guarantees of ₹64.5 crore for credit facilities in its own accounts.[6] That is manageable at this debt level. But it means profit is being reinvested faster than it is being turned into spare cash.

A third weakness is the cost-and-tariff squeeze that shows up when growth is not smooth. When the United States raised tariffs on Indian goods to 50 percent in 2025, group sales grew only about 6.5 percent in the half to September 2025 and management cut its tone from a growth guide of high teens to a cautious wait-and-watch.[11][12] Growth recovered to 33.3 percent in the June 2026 quarter, but the episode shows how fast the export engine can stall.[3]

The cycle, and what the stock is paying for it

Indian vehicle demand is at a high. Management said in February 2026 that post-GST momentum was strong. The September 2026 SIAM figures were records for the month for cars, two-wheelers and three-wheelers.[10][19] A high point in a cycle is when a business looks best and a price for it looks easiest to defend. So we weight what does not depend on the festive season. That is the multi-year ADS backlog and the new customers in crankshaft outsourcing.[4] What the stock is paying for the cycle is visible in the multiple: at 66.7 times trailing profit against a three-year median of 40.2, the market is already paying for a good cycle, so a normal year would not be enough to lift the price and a weak one would hurt it.

Governance and promoter selling

Governance is clean on the main tests. The auditor, Deloitte Haskins and Sells, was appointed for five years at the September 2025 annual meeting and gave unmodified opinions for FY26. The Board's report discloses one matter. An ex-employee misappropriated ₹1.22 crore over several years, and the company is recovering it.[6] No promoter shares are pledged.[14] The promoters, however, sold: the promoter group held 18,777,811 shares at September 2025 and 18,123,240 at September 2026. That is 654,571 fewer shares, and one promoter's sale of 10,000 shares at ₹4,537.50 on 30 September 2026 is on record.[15][16] Selling by insiders after the stock rose 181 percent from its 52-week low of ₹1,414.90 is common and not proof of trouble. But it is a reason not to treat the share price as a bargain.[18] What this means for an owner: the governance record does not block the idea, but the promoter selling removes one argument for paying a high multiple, so the decision rests on price and results, not on insider conviction.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 sits on assumptions that can be wrong. This chapter takes each one, says which part is weakest, what evidence would show it wrong and when we would see that evidence. It also states the strongest case against our own decision to wait, written as a red team would write it.

Weakest assumption in the profit estimate: that the margin rises to 19.2 and then 20.0 percent

The margin guide is 'inch towards 19 percent'. The June quarter's 19.2 percent was helped by an unusually high 39.9 percent of sales from abroad, a favourable mix and currency gains, which management itself calls elevated.[4] Raw-material price rises in aluminium, steel and consumables have not yet been passed through to customers.[4] If the pass-through takes longer, or if labour costs in the northern plants keep rising, the margin could stay near 18 percent. The evidence would be the September quarter's operating margin before one-offs: under 18.5 percent would count against us in the cautious direction, at or above 19.5 percent would count in the company's favour.

Weakest assumption in the target: that the multiple falls to 40.2 times

This is the hinge. The three-year median of 40.2 comes from a period in which trailing profit per share rose from ₹29.70 to ₹59.82 on Screener's series. The stock has not traded below 39 times since October 2025. The one-year median is 53.2.[2] The sector also trades at 50.8 to 95.4 times. So a stock priced at 66.7 is not out of line with its peers.[20][21][22][23] If investors treat ADS as a lasting re-rating, a 53 times multiple on our FY28 profit gives ₹4,575.84, which is a reward-to-risk of about 0.56 to 1 at the close on the ₹2,918.92 stop, still under the 2.0 floor. The multiple alone would not make the stock buyable at the close; it would lift the value, and a lower price would still be needed. The evidence would be the multiple itself after the next two results, with profit per share still rising.

Weakest assumption in cash and capital: that the capacity pays back at 2 times sales

The company plans ₹510 crore of capex again in FY27 and a further ₹250 crore for ADS, with an asset turn of 2.0 to 2.2 times, meaning each rupee of new plant should produce about two rupees of annual sales.[9][4] Last year's capex ran 46 percent above the guide, and cash from operations has not covered capex in two years.[13][3][1] If the new hangar, surface-treatment line and defence plant ramp slowly, depreciation arrives before the revenue and return on capital employed stalls near 15 percent. The evidence would be depreciation above ₹70 crore a quarter without a matching sales rise.

The strongest bear case against our own conclusion is that we are anchoring on a stale multiple. The business has re-rated because its mix changed: ADS grew from 3.8 percent to 14.2 percent of sales in five years, and a backlog of ₹5,750 crore against ₹336 crore of ADS sales is a visibility that few auto suppliers have.[3][4] Estimates are rising.
