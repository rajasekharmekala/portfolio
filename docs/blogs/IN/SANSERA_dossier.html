Terms used in this dossier

Several terms recur through the report, and most people meet them for the first time in a company analysis. Each one is explained once here, with an everyday comparison, so the later sections can use them without stopping to define them. The definitions follow the way this dossier uses each term, which is not always identical to the way a broker or a data site uses it.

Think of the terms in three groups. The first group describes the business: return on capital employed, return on equity, and cash flow to profit. The second describes the price: moving averages, the Weinstein stage, RSI (relative strength index), and ATR (average true range). The third describes the decision rules: asymmetry and the momentum gate. A reader who understands one term from each group can follow every verdict in this report.

  • ROCE (return on capital employed). Operating profit before interest and tax, divided by the money the company has tied up in the business (debt plus equity). Think of a rental flat that earns 15 a year for every 100 invested in it. The Layer 1 floor is 15.0%.
  • ROE (return on equity). Net profit divided by shareholders' own money. It is affected by debt, so a company that borrows heavily can show a high ROE with a weak underlying business.
  • CFO/PAT (cash flow to profit). Cash generated from operations, divided by reported net profit, summed over three years. A ratio near 1.0 means reported profit turns into cash. The Layer 1 floor is 0.70.
  • Stage 1 to 4 (Weinstein). A simple map of a share's life. Stage 1 is a base after a fall, Stage 2 is a sustained uptrend, Stage 3 is a top where the trend flattens, and Stage 4 is a downtrend. Only Stage 2 qualifies for the Tactical book.
  • DMA (daily moving average). The average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 trading days. A price above a rising average is in an uptrend. The 50-day and 200-day averages are the two trend lines the rules watch.
  • ATR (average true range, 14 days). The typical daily price swing in rupees. It is the ruler the rules use: a level that is 1.0 ATR away is one normal day's move away.
  • RSI (relative strength index, 14 days). A momentum reading from 0 to 100. Above 70 is usually called overbought, and below 30 oversold. It describes speed, not direction.
  • Core and Tactical books. The two strategy books a company can be assigned to. Core is for high-return businesses held for three years or more, and it needs a Stage 1 base breakout, meaning a move above the top of the base, or a Stage 2 uptrend. Tactical is for shorter moves held for three weeks to six months, and it needs a Stage 2 uptrend with a rising 50-day and 200-day average.
  • Asymmetry. The upside to the target divided by the downside to the stop. The formula is (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). The Core floor is 3.0 and the Tactical floor is 3.5.
  • Momentum gate. A code test that asks whether the share is in a trend and close enough to a support level to buy. Test A is for shares already in an uptrend, and Test B is for shares about to enter one. Failing both means no allocation.

How the verdict was reached

The process has three layers, and a company must clear each one in order. Layer 1 is a set of hard gates. A company that fails any gate is rejected at once, and the later layers are not run. A weak business or a poor setup should not be rescued by a good score elsewhere. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five operating measures specific to the business. This dossier uses four measures for this business, and at least three of them must pass. Layer 3 maps the distance from the nearest support onto a set of entry bands, which decide whether a buy is immediate, deferred, or not allowed.

The momentum gate sits beside these layers rather than inside them. It is computed by code from the price file. The scoring rules say that a share failing both of its tests may not be allocated, whatever the business looks like. For this company the gate returned NO_MOMENTUM, so it is reported as a code result and not recomputed here.

For SANSERA, Layer 1 was run in full. Its result was a fail on the asymmetry gate, because no target met the rule. The target has to come from forward earnings multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, from a measured move on a technical base, or from a discounted cash flow. None of these could be verified from the sources available in this run. Because Layer 1 failed, Layer 2 and Layer 3 were not run, as the rules require. The layer-by-layer disposition is set out in the Layer 1 section below. The sections on Layers 2 and 3 say what would have been measured and what the price bands would have said.

What the company does

Sansera Engineering makes parts that are forged from metal and then machined to very tight tolerances. Screener summarises the company as an engineering-led maker of complex precision components for automotive and non-automotive sectors. It also says the company has been one of the top 10 global suppliers of connecting rods in light-vehicle and commercial-vehicle segments (Screener, consolidated profile, as of 12 Oct 2026). In plain terms, the company sells the hard, heavily tested parts inside an engine or a gearbox. Auto parts are usually approved by a customer before production, and the order book is the forward view of the business. That general industry background was not verified in this run, so no figure depends on it.

The business has two halves that are growing at very different speeds. The first is the traditional automotive business, which includes internal-combustion engine parts and newer parts for electric and hybrid vehicles. The second is the Aerospace, Defence and Semiconductor (ADS) division, which makes precision components for aircraft structures and equipment. In the first quarter of FY27, which ended 30 Jun 2026, management said ADS revenue was Rs 1.45 billion. That is roughly triple the year-earlier level. The internal-combustion automotive segment grew 20.8% and non-automotive grew 129.9% (Indian Stock Alerts, summary of the Q1 FY27 earnings call, filed 20 Aug 2026). The same summary put the executable order book at Rs 57.5 billion, to be delivered by FY31. Quarterly revenue crossed Rs 10.2 billion on the same call, so ADS was about 14% of the quarter (1.45 divided by 10.2).

What this means for an owner is that the company is becoming a supplier to aerospace and defence customers. Buying on longer contracts is general industry background, not a figure from this run. The other 86% of the quarter, mostly automotive, is still the larger share. The transition is also capital-intensive. Cash used in investing activities was Rs 957 crore in FY25 and Rs 439 crore in FY26 (Screener, consolidated cash flow, as of 31 Mar 2026). The question for a buyer is whether the new business earns enough on the capital it uses, which is the subject of the next section.

Why the business is strong or weak

The strong side of the case is growth backed by cash. Consolidated sales rose from Rs 2,338 crore in the year to March 2023 to Rs 3,498 crore in the year to March 2026. The three-year compound growth rate of sales is printed at 14% (Screener, as of 31 Mar 2026). Net profit grew faster, from Rs 148 crore to Rs 327 crore over the same three years. Cash from operations also rose, from Rs 374 crore in FY24 to Rs 387 crore in FY26. Summed over three years, cash from operations is Rs 1,138 crore against profit of Rs 732 crore, a ratio of 1.55 times. A ratio above 1.0 means reported profit is being turned into cash. That is the main reason this company clears the cash-flow floor.

The balance sheet is also comfortable. Consolidated borrowings were Rs 591 crore at March 2026, against reserves of Rs 3,075 crore and equity capital of Rs 12 crore. Borrowings are therefore roughly one-fifth of the shareholders' money. That is a very different position from March 2024, when reserves were Rs 1,337 crore. The company has also grown its operating margin: the operating profit margin for FY26 is printed at 18% (Screener, as of 31 Mar 2026).

The weak side is capital efficiency and working capital. Return on capital employed is the measure that matters most for a manufacturer that is spending heavily on plants, and the sources disagree about it. Screener's headline figure is 14.5% (as of 12 Oct 2026). A third-party summary of the FY26 annual report says it improved to 16.7%, while another article says it moderated to 14.1%. The Layer 1 section sets out the disagreement. Inventory days rose from 126 in FY24 to 169 in FY26, and cash from operations as a share of operating profit fell from 91% in FY24 to 75% in FY26 (Screener, consolidated ratios, as of 31 Mar 2026). Free cash flow after capital spending was negative in both FY25 and FY26, at minus Rs 219 crore and minus Rs 123 crore.

Ownership has also moved. Promoter holding fell from 35.04% in March 2024 to 29.21% in June 2026. Institutions hold most of the rest: foreign investors held 21.52% and domestic funds 31.04% at 30 Jun 2026 (Screener, shareholding as of 30 Jun 2026). The promoter stake is not pledged: the latest shareholding data show 0.0% pledged (Trendlyne, shareholding as of 30 Jun 2026). The public pages do not say why the promoter percentage fell, and this dossier does not assume a reason. For the owner, the business is good at turning sales into cash. It is still spending that cash faster than it earns a return on it, and that is the trade-off the valuation has to pay for.

Layer 2 scorecard

Layer 2 was not run for this company because Layer 1 failed. The rule is that a company failing any hard gate is rejected at once, and the operating scorecard is not evaluated. A strong operating picture cannot offset a missing valuation anchor. The scorecard would have been built from three to five measures specific to this business, drawn from its own operations: the order book and its conversion for the ADS division, inventory days and cash conversion for working capital, and the mix between automotive and non-automotive revenue. This dossier uses four of those measures, and at least three must pass.

The company's own figures give a preview of how such a scorecard would look, although they are not a scored result. Cash from operations as a share of operating profit fell to 75% in FY26 from 91% in FY24. Inventory days rose from 126 to 169 over the same period (Screener, consolidated ratios, as of 31 Mar 2026). The order book of Rs 57.5 billion is a company-reported figure from the Q1 FY27 call, and its conversion schedule to FY31 is not verified in this run. The ADS revenue tripling is also company-reported. A scorecard built on these figures would show growth and order strength alongside working-capital drag and a weaker cash conversion. That is a mixed picture rather than a clear pass.

What this means for the decision is simple. Layer 2 can only confirm or deny a company that has already cleared the hard gates, and this company has not. Its operating picture is worth following, because the ADS growth and the order book are the story a future valuation would have to price. The rules do not allow that story to make up for the missing target. If a verified target is established in a later run, Layer 2 will be run then, on the same operating figures and with the same working-capital and conversion measures. The scorecard result will be recorded at that time.

Layer 3 entry posture

Layer 3 was not run for this company because Layers 1 and 2 did not both pass. It would have mapped the distance from the nearest verified support onto the single support-distance ladder in the scoring file, in units of the 14-day ATR. The bands are: a full-size buy within 0.5 ATR of support, a standard-size buy within 1.0 ATR, a limit-order-only reduced buy within 1.2 ATR, and no buy beyond that. The ledger's measured distance is the base pivot distance of 5.88 ATR, far beyond the 1.2 ATR limit.

On the ledger band alone, the posture would be NO_BUY, which is the rule's wait-for-support state. The action would be WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, meaning do not chase. The reason is not the trend, which is in place. It is the distance. Buying now would mean paying about 5.9 daily swings above the base the code measures from, and the rules do not allow that without a pullback or a new base. That posture is recorded here as what the ledger band would give, not as an entry instruction, because Layer 3 is not a valid stage for this company in this run.

What this means for an owner is that waiting is the rule's answer, not a view on the company. The levels that would move the price into a buyable band are set out in the price section. A close at or below about Rs 3,331.1 would clear the Test A distance condition on the arithmetic, and a close at or below about Rs 3,292.7 would clear the Test B pivot condition. Both levels are far below the current price, and the price section explains why a fall to them is not a realistic route. For practical purposes, the company would need a long sideways base before any buy could be allowed under these rules.

Fatal flaws and the red-team case

The strongest honest case against buying SANSERA is not that the business is poor. It is that the price already assumes a great deal, and the company has not yet shown the return on capital that would justify that assumption. The first flaw is valuation. At a price to earnings of 71.0, the market is paying about 71 rupees for each rupee of trailing earnings. Endurance trades at 34.6, Uno Minda at 51.7 and Sona BLW at 67.6. The average analyst target of Rs 4,106 on 12 Oct 2026 sits about 2.9% below the ledger close of Rs 4,228.2. StockAnalysis prints -3.08% against its own price (StockAnalysis, forecast page, as of 12 Oct 2026). Taken at face value, the consensus of 13 analysts sees no upside from here.

The second flaw is capital efficiency. Screener's headline ROCE is 14.5%, already 0.5 points under the 15.0% floor. The sources disagree between 14.1% and 16.7%. The company is spending heavily on new capacity. Free cash flow after capital spending was minus Rs 219 crore in FY25 and minus Rs 123 crore in FY26, so the company funded its growth from outside its operations. Cash from operations as a share of operating profit fell to 75% in FY26 from 91% in FY24, and inventory days rose from 126 to 169. If the new plants earn less than the old ones while the cost of building them rises, the headline ROCE would fall further below the floor.

The third flaw is the price itself. The stock is 5.88 ATR above its base low, far beyond the 1.0 to 1.2 ATR the rules allow for a buy. Its momentum gate fails both tests. Volume is thin, at 0.41 times its 20-day average, which is the wrong signal for a breakout. A stock that has risen so far so fast can fall a long way when the trend pauses. A close below the 50-day average at Rs 4,020.81 would be the first sign of that.

The fourth flaw is execution risk in the new business. The ADS division tripled in the first quarter of FY27 from a small base, and it was about 14% of quarterly revenue. The Rs 57.5 billion order book runs to FY31. Those orders are company-reported, and their conversion is not verified here. Aerospace and defence programmes can slip, and the capital spent on them is front-loaded. The case for owning the shares rests on a future that has not yet been delivered, and the price already pays for much of it. The business may well deliver. At this price, though, a buyer is paying in advance for delivery that the rules require to be shown first.

What would change the verdict

The verdict is REJECTED because the asymmetry gate failed, so the first thing that would change it is a verified target. The rule accepts three anchors: a forward earnings estimate multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, a technical measured move, or a discounted cash flow value. For a Core position the reward-to-risk ratio must be at least 3.0. With the stop placed by the scoring rule, a target of Rs 4,106 would give no upside at all, because it sits below the close. For the Core stop of Rs 2,634.5, the risk per share is 4,228.2 minus 2,634.5, or Rs 1,593.7. Three times that is Rs 4,781.1 of required reward, so the target would need to be at least about Rs 9,009 (4,228.2 plus 4,781.1). The analysis would then need to explain why the market has not already priced it.

Reopen the asymmetry gate with a verified target that gives a reward-to-risk ratio of at least 3.0 on the Core stop rule. Reopen the momentum gate only on its own price conditions, which are listed separately below.

  • Asymmetry gate: if a primary source (the FY26 annual report or a company investor presentation) confirms a return on capital employed at or above 15.0%, the ROCE gate moves from unresolved to pass. This alone does not change the verdict, because the asymmetry gate still fails. Check: next re-test, once the annual report page is readable.
  • Asymmetry gate: if a forward earnings estimate and a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, each sourced to a primary filing or a named data provider, produce a target that gives a reward-to-risk ratio of at least 3.0 on the Core stop rule, the asymmetry gate passes. Layer 1 would then pass if the ROCE gate also passes, and Layers 2 and 3 would be run.
  • Momentum gate, Test A: a close at or below about Rs 3,331.1 would clear the distance condition on the arithmetic, but it would also break the trend condition, so the gate would not pass there. The route that works is a sideways base in which the 63-day base low rises. The code would need to rerun to confirm any pass. A momentum pass is necessary but not sufficient for a buy.
  • Momentum gate, Test B: a base near its pivot within 1.0 ATR, which on today's base low means a close at or below about Rs 3,292.7, together with volume at or above 1.0 times its 20-day average, would clear the pivot and volume conditions. Entry would still need a confirmed breakout or a retest, which means a fall back to the broken level that holds.
  • Trend check: if the close falls below Rs 4,020.81, the 50-day average, the short-term trend is broken. The verdict would not change, because it is already REJECTED, but the trend would need to be watched for a Stage 3 top.

Each of these changes has a date attached in practice: the next run of the price ledger and the next annual report or results release. The price levels can be checked on any trading day. The ROCE and target inputs need a fresh primary source before a rerun. Until one of these events happens, the verdict stands. It should be read as a rule outcome, not as a judgement that the company is poor.
