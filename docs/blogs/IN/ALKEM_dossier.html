1. What the company does

Alkem makes and sells medicines. It was incorporated in 1973, has its head office in Mumbai, and reported revenue from operations of ₹14,712 crore (₹147,123 million) for the year to March 2026.[15][8] Of the regional sales inside that revenue, roughly 67.8 percent came from India, 20.5 percent from the United States and 11.7 percent from the rest of the world. The regional split leaves out ₹1,800 million of other operating income, so India is 67.0 percent of total revenue.[8] Most Indian sales are branded generics, which are medicines whose active ingredient is no longer under patent but which carry a brand name that doctors know and trust. Think of it as selling the same salt under a label that a doctor writes on the prescription, so the buyer asks for the brand and not for the cheapest copy.

The Indian money chain is simple to follow. A doctor prescribes a brand, the patient buys it at a chemist, the chemist buys from a stockist, and the stockist buys from Alkem. Alkem therefore earns its margin by keeping doctors loyal, which it does with a field force of more than 14,500 representatives.[5] It ranks sixth by value in the Indian pharmaceutical market and third by doctor prescriptions, and it has held the number one place in antibiotics (anti-infectives) with a 13.3 percent share of that therapy market.[5] Brands such as Clavam, Pan, Pan-D, A to Z NS, Xone and Taxim-O are among the top 100 brands in India.[15] The weak side is that over 80 percent of domestic sales came from acute medicines, which treat short illnesses such as infections and pain, and about 30 percent of the products sit under government price control.[15][16] Chronic medicines, which treat long-running conditions such as diabetes, are the faster-growing but smaller part.

The United States business sells generic copies of out-of-patent drugs through Alkem's subsidiary Ascend Laboratories.[15] As of 30 June 2026 the company had filed 192 abbreviated new drug applications (the US filing for a generic) and held 170 approvals, 18 of them tentative.[5] US sales were ₹7,439 million in the June 2026 quarter, up 6.5 percent, and non-US export sales rose 34.5 percent to ₹4,785 million.[8][4] One fact shapes the risk: management said about 45 percent of US revenue comes from the Daman plant, the same plant the US regulator has now rated Official Action Indicated.[11][23] That works out to roughly 9 percent of group sales (45 percent of the 20.5 percent US share).

Alkem is also building three newer businesses. Its subsidiary Enzene makes biosimilars (near-copies of biological drugs) and offers contract development and manufacturing to other drug firms, and the seven biosimilars launched in India bring in about ₹150 crore a year.[11] The US contract unit costs about ₹60 crore a quarter to run and is not expected to break even within twelve months.[11] The medical-device arm bought a majority stake in the Swiss heart-device maker Occlutech, completed in mid-July 2026 for up to €99.4 million.[30][7][31] Alkem spent 4.2 percent of sales on research in FY26 and held net cash of ₹57.64 billion at 30 June 2026, which means it owed the banks far less than it held.[8][5]

How a branded generic reaches the patient

A branded generic is a long-running contract with doctors. Alkem does not win by owning a patent. It wins when a doctor remembers a name and writes it again next month, which is why the size of the field force and the age of the brands matter more here than the research budget. In the June 2026 quarter, IQVIA prescription data show Alkem growing 13.2 percent against 12.2 percent for the whole Indian market, a lead of 100 basis points (one basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point).[4] The lead was 220 points in acute medicines and 250 points in chronic ones.[4]

The trade generics business works differently. It sells unbranded medicines to distributors on price and credit terms, so growth depends on how loose the credit is. Alkem moved this business into a separate subsidiary, Alkem Wellness, on 30 September 2025, and then tightened how many days customers may take to pay. Sales were flat in the June 2026 quarter as a result, and the managing director said he would be happy with late single-digit growth there for the next one to two years.[9][11] This is why Alkem's reported Indian sales growth of 10.3 percent trailed the 12.2 percent market growth even while its branded business grew 12 percent.[11] The next subsection turns to the US side, where the regulatory risk sits.

Why the US business matters more than its size

US generics compete mainly on price, so price erosion is a constant. Management said price erosion has "bottomed out" for Alkem, but also that it has no volume growth in the US and that the 6.5 percent rise in the June quarter came mostly from the weaker rupee.[11] The full-year US outlook was trimmed from high single digits to a mid-to-high single-digit range.[11]

Concentration makes this sharper. A plant inspection by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) ended with seven written observations on 1 May 2026, and in August the FDA rated the Daman plant Official Action Indicated, the category where the regulator has found conditions it may act on.[23] Alkem says approved products continue to ship without interruption, and management hopes to clear the status in six to twelve months.[11] An Official Action Indicated rating means the regulator may consider regulatory or administrative action,[23] so a worse outcome would put the 45 percent of US sales at risk. Chapter 2 weighs how much that risk should count in the verdict.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Alkem's competitive position is good but not widening. Its strength is distribution and brand habit in India, where it is the sixth-largest company by value and holds the top place in antibiotics.[5] Its weakness is concentration: most Indian sales are in acute medicines that face price control, and a large slice of US sales comes from one plant that is now under regulatory scrutiny.[15][11] Each claim is cited so that you can test it, and the ownership signals in the third subsection matter more than usual this quarter.

A useful test of a moat is whether a company can earn well above its cost of money without borrowing heavily. Alkem passes. Screener reports a ROCE of 21.2 percent and a return on equity of 18.9 percent, and the company's own figure for pre-tax ROCE was 29.6 percent for FY26.[1][5] Debt is small: CRISIL puts adjusted debt at 0.14 times net worth and interest cover at 22.3 times, which means operating profit covers interest more than twenty times over.[15] Two rating agencies, CRISIL and CARE, hold the company at AA+ with a stable outlook.[15][16]

Strengths: brands, reach and a clean balance sheet

The strongest asset is the brand portfolio. Alkem has 17 brand families with sales above ₹1,000 million, and 15 brands above ₹250 million rank in the top three of their covered market.[5] Its anti-infective franchise has held the number one rank in India for more than 15 years, with a 13.3 percent market share and a rank of 1.[5] That position is hard to copy because antibiotics are prescribed by habit and trust, and the sales force of more than 14,500 people keeps those habits fresh.

The second asset is the balance sheet. Net cash was ₹57.64 billion at 30 June 2026, and CRISIL expects cash accrual above ₹2,000 crore a year, enough to cover capital spending of ₹1,600 to ₹1,700 crore in FY27 including the Occlutech purchase.[5][15] A company that can fund its own growth does not need to dilute shareholders or borrow at high rates. The cost of that comfort is visible, though: investments stood at ₹1,869 crore against fixed assets of ₹3,871 crore in March 2026, and part of the surplus was held in real-estate investments that were written down by ₹747 million.[1][8]

Weaknesses: acute mix, price control, one large US plant, and thin volume growth

CRISIL names two weaknesses outright: high dependence on acute therapies, where over 80 percent of domestic revenue came from in FY25, and exposure to price control, with about 30 percent of products covered by the national list of essential medicines.[15][16] Acute medicines grow more slowly than chronic ones and the government can cut their prices, so they cap how fast earnings can rise. Alkem is trying to shift: its chronic business grew 17.9 percent in the June 2026 quarter against 12.3 percent for acute.[4] Even so, chronic medicines were only about 15 percent of the sales force.[15]

The US business has no volume growth, by management's own account. It said the rise in US sales is more currency than volume, that the products launched in the last 12 to 24 months have not added volume, and that the Daman plant, which supplies 45 percent of US revenue, is under an Official Action Indicated rating.[11] Alongside this, employee costs rose more than 16 percent in the quarter as 1,200 more representatives were hired, and the new CDMO and medical-device units are still loss-making.[11] The effect is visible in the operating margin: 20.5 percent in the June 2026 quarter against 21.9 percent a year earlier.[5]

Ownership and governance signals

Promoters (the founding family) held 56.74 percent of the shares in March 2024 and 49.70 percent in September 2026, a fall of 7.04 points in two and a half years.[1][17][18] The latest step was a block sale on 2 June 2026, when 17,88,220 shares (1.49 percent) were sold at an average of ₹5,200 for ₹929.87 crore, taking promoter holding from 51.20 to 49.71 percent.[21] The buyers were large mutual funds and global banks, and mutual fund ownership has risen to 17.79 percent, so the shares moved from insiders to institutions.[21][22][17] Pledged shares are zero percent of the promoter holding, which clears the pledge red line in our rules.[17]

Three governance events came in the last three months. The chief financial officer, Nitin Agrawal, resigned on 30 July 2026, effective 30 September, and the company disclosed it on 24 September.[27][28][29] On the August call the managing director said the company is still looking for a chief executive, "a critical hire".[11] On 21 October 2026 the audit committee approved a forensic audit by Grant Thornton Bharat LLP into suspected misappropriation of funds by a former employee in procurement; the filing does not give the amount or the name.[24][25][26] None of this proves wrongdoing at the top. It does mean three of the usual signals of a calm company, a settled finance chief, a named chief executive and a clean audit trail, are missing at once, which is why Chapter 6 treats the audit as the hinge of the case.

5. What could happen next

Chapter 4 told us where the chart stands. This chapter asks what the price is already assuming, how the people who forecast profit are changing their minds, and what we believe differently. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in this report rests on an assumption, and the honest way to use it is to find the weakest one and say what would prove it wrong. This chapter does that for each estimate, then gives the red-team case against our own conclusion, names the single claim the whole verdict hangs on, and says when we will know. Our verdict is REJECTED, so the red team argues the opposite case: the best reasoning for buying now.

Two features of this case make it harder than usual. The biggest risk, the forensic audit, has no published size, so it cannot be put in a model. And the second, the Daman rating, has no published end date, only a management hope of six to twelve months.[24][11] Unknowns of that kind are why we cap the probability of the base case at one in two. Our scenarios also assume both risks are settled within twelve months. That is an assumption, because the audit has no deadline and Daman is management's hope. A prolonged overhang is not a separate scenario in our table. It sits between the base and bear cases: at 25.4 times, the lowest multiple of the last three years, our FY28 EPS gives ₹5,131.

The weakest assumption in each estimate

For FY27 earnings of ₹177, the weakest assumption is the 31 percent tax rate. For FY28 earnings of ₹202, the weakest assumption is that Daman clears and the US CDMO unit stops losing about ₹60 crore a quarter by FY28.[11] A warning letter or import alert at Daman would remove a large part of US sales for a time, and a CDMO delay would leave about ₹240 crore of annual cost in place, which is about 8 percent of FY27 pre-tax profit. For the ₹5,400 base value, the weakest assumption is the 26.7 times multiple. That is below the three-year median of 28.9, but the stock has not traded below 25.4 times in three years, and a governance overhang can push it there.[3]

The red-team case: the best argument that rejecting Alkem is a mistake

The strongest case for buying is that the business is far better than the chart suggests. Return on capital is 21.2 percent, debt is 0.14 times net worth, the company holds ₹5,764 crore of net cash, and two rating agencies hold it at AA+.[1][15][16][5] The tax shock is a one-time reset and not a fall in the business: pre-tax profit rose 1.8 percent and sales rose 10.9 percent in the June quarter.[4] The forensic audit was started by the company itself, which suggests a working control system, and it concerns one former employee. At 26.6 times earnings, the stock sits 24 percent below the median of five large peers, and four of those five also saw quarterly profit fall.[2] If the audit stays small and Daman clears, earnings could reach consensus (₹224 in FY28) and the stock could reach ₹6,450, a gain of 27 percent.

We weigh that case and keep our verdict for three reasons. The probability-weighted value of ₹5,145 offers only 1.3 percent upside, so the case requires at least two good outcomes at once. A price that is 1.96 ATR above its 52-week low in a Stage 4 downtrend gives no sign yet that other buyers agree. And reward-to-risk is 0.80 at the close, far under the 2.0 floor. Stress-testing the expected-return math: if the bear case were 20 percent instead of 30, the weighted value would be ₹5,405 and the ratio at the close using that value as the target would be 0.81, still far under 2.0, so the rejection does not rest on the bear probability. Two sensitivities show the limits of the economic case. If consensus FY28 EPS of ₹223.77 were delivered at 26.7 times, the value would be ₹5,975 and reward-to-risk at the close would be 2.23. If our EPS were delivered at 25.4 times, the value would be ₹5,131, only 1.0 percent above the close. So worth and timing are separate questions. The 1.3 percent expected gain is an assumed figure built from our probabilities. The rejection is a current decision driven by the chart, and not a verdict that the business is worth little.
