The order book is a lead, not the answer

You and I can be optimistic about demand and still refuse to confuse bid wins with realized economics. The proof chain runs from specification to factory slot, passed test, dispatch, milestone invoice, collection and retained margin. Each broken link delays cash.

The bull case is a sequence, not a slogan. First, India’s power transmission, renewable integration, industrial electrification and data-centre loads require more equipment. Management kept FY27 standalone guidance at 25% revenue growth, 16% EBITDA margin and 9–10% PAT margin. Those are ambitious and measurable promises. We should reward delivery, not capitalize the guidance as if it were already audited earnings.

The bear case begins where the bull slides often end: cash. In FY26, the audited standalone cash-flow statement reported ₹395.77 crore of operating profit before working-capital changes, but receivables, inventory and other working-capital movements left cash generated from operations at only ₹39.76 crore before tax; tax paid was ₹98.32 crore and operating cash flow became negative ₹58.56 crore. Backlog growth therefore brings funding and execution risk alongside revenue visibility.

CHAPTER I

How the Business & Stock Got Here — Past Cycles & Pivots

"A factory is a balance sheet that has learned to speak in steel, copper and test reports."
— Research Desk maxim

If we start at the beginning, you and I should not imagine a giant utility contractor appearing fully formed. TARIL traces its operating roots to 1981, when founder Jitendra Mamtora began with transformer-repair work and then moved into manufacturing 33–66 kV units. The early lesson was close to the customer: repair exposes the failure modes, maintenance constraints and urgency of equipment already on the grid. The company was incorporated as a public limited company in Gujarat in 1994. That distinction matters: the operating story began before the listed-company story, and the first decades were about engineering capability and customer trust rather than rapid scale.

The next transition was from repair and lower-voltage manufacture toward larger systems. Changodar enabled production up to 110 kV and later 245 kV. Between 2001 and 2006, the company records a series reactor supplied to Danieli in the United Kingdom, its first 100 MVA / 220 kV power transformer, and approvals from NTPC and PGCIL. These approvals are not a marketing ornament. For a utility-grade transformer, type tests, manufacturing quality, vendor approval, reference installations and service support reduce a buyer’s perceived risk. The supplier does not merely sell copper and steel; it sells confidence that a costly unit can survive fault conditions and remain available for decades.

TARIL listed on the NSE and BSE in 2007. Later milestones included a 315 MVA, 400 kV transformer, the 1,200 kV pilot autotransformer dispatched through Power Grid to the National Test Station at Bina, technical collaborations with ZTR Ukraine and Fuji Electric for higher-voltage work, and 765 kV-class reactors. In FY26 the annual report also recorded a Power Grid repair order for a 397 MVA, 400 kV converter transformer as an entry point toward HVDC. The strategy is visible across time: climb the voltage and complexity ladder, build references, then compete for a deeper pool of tenders. The counterweight is that every rung asks for more design, testing, working capital and reputation at risk.

You should give credit for qualification only once. A 1,200 kV reference can lower future friction, but it does not erase price competition, customer concentration or the time required to turn a drawing into an accepted installation.

The financial timeline is not a straight line. The consolidated sales series rose from ₹821 crore in FY17 to ₹716 crore in FY18, ₹855 crore in FY19 and ₹701 crore in FY20; the subsequent years included a pandemic-era contraction in some customer projects, then a recovery to ₹1,158 crore in FY22 and ₹1,396 crore in FY23. You can see the cyclical signature even inside the growth story: a transformer order is commissioned around a grid or industrial investment cycle, not bought every week like a consumer staple. FY25’s 56% sales increase and margin step-up deserve attention, but they should not be projected as a stable run rate without cash evidence.

The market’s stock cycles have been more dramatic than the sales chart. Screener’s ten-year price CAGR is around 33%, its five-year price CAGR around 84%, and its one-year change negative about 20%; current TradingView data places the share 15.95% below its 52-week high. The 2021–25 rerating reflected the market’s discovery of high-voltage capability, order-book expansion and India’s infrastructure theme. The 2025–26 retracement reminds you that forward expectations move faster than a factory’s output: a richly valued cyclical name can correct even while reported revenue and orders are growing. We should not assign exact past peaks from memory. The relevant cycle lesson is the sequence—earnings acceleration, multiple expansion, growth scrutiny, then a reset when execution or valuation outruns cash.

The capability ladder

For each new voltage class or product, ask four things: qualification, repeat orders, factory yield and cash terms. Technical milestones matter when they improve the next three—not when they appear only as a one-off trophy.

Across the Atlantic, GE’s grid-equipment lineage reminds us that a company can change corporate form while preserving customer relationships and technical capability; Eaton’s history shows a broader electrical portfolio can absorb more of the customer’s system value. India’s CG Power is a closer market analogy for a business whose investor narrative changed when execution, governance and capacity utilization improved—but its diversified industrial systems make it an imperfect comparator. In Europe, Siemens Energy’s separation from Siemens and the rapid growth of its Grid Technologies backlog demonstrate how a strategic reset can meet a strong electrification cycle, while the stock still depends on margin and delivery. TARIL’s own history is narrower: one operating segment, concentrated in transformer manufacture, with a growing component ecosystem.

The corporate timeline also explains why today’s strategic language emphasizes India-made components. TARIL’s facilities and subsidiaries cluster in Gujarat, near an established industrial ecosystem for fabrication and electrical steel. The company has built a presence through plants at Moraiya, Changodar and Odhav, with technical capability spread across large power units, reactors and industrial applications. Its 2026 UK subsidiary is a new commercial foothold, but the Q2 results explicitly said it had not yet contributed capital or begun operations. That is a useful discipline for us: a subsidiary can mark the beginning of an option, while revenue, local staff, assembly and recurring service are later milestones. The listed story is therefore changing from a Gujarat-centered transformer manufacturer toward a broader component and service platform, but the transformation remains in progress.

The decade also teaches us not to confuse accounting profit with a new cycle’s permanent margin. Operating margins in the consolidated record moved from 11% in FY17 to 6% in FY22, then 10% in FY24, 16% in FY25 and 15% in FY26. FY25 and FY26 profit growth benefited from a stronger order environment, product mix and utilization, but cash flow did not move in lockstep: FY25 free cash flow was negative ₹78 crore in the screening series and FY26 negative ₹215 crore. The industry can deliver operating leverage when volumes rise through a factory, but that leverage has a mirror image: the cost of underutilized capacity and the financing cost of unfinished work. History tells us the margin turn is meaningful; it does not yet certify a high-quality, cash-rich annuity.

A useful investor maxim for this history is to separate “new capability” from “new earnings power.” The 1,200 kV pilot unit, 765 kV reactor references, global furnace installations and dynamic short-circuit tests are evidence that TARIL can design and manufacture demanding equipment. The next questions are recurrence, capacity economics and customer terms: how often does the capability win a tender, what margin remains after input escalation, and how quickly is cash collected? In the United States, GE Vernova’s grid-equipment business operates within a larger global power ecosystem; in India, CG Power’s revival shows the market rewards a visible operating turnaround; in Europe, Siemens Energy’s grid technologies grew into a much larger strategic business after corporate restructuring. Each analogy says execution can change a company’s category, but none says capability alone guarantees per-share returns. The annual report captured the philosophy in a short line: “We built our order book to last, not to impress.”

One final historical distinction matters. TARIL’s own site dates the company’s roots to 1981, its public listing to 2007 and the move into high-voltage work across the following decades. The stock’s modern rerating is therefore layered over a much older engineering franchise; investors are paying for both accumulated references and the possibility that a new capacity cycle broadens the earnings base. Those are different assets and should be valued differently.

Start with the large power transformer, the economic center of the present backlog. Imagine a transmission utility connecting a new generation source to a high-voltage corridor. Its engineers issue a technical specification—voltage ratio, MVA, impedance, vector group, cooling, losses, short-circuit withstand, accessories and delivery milestones. TARIL’s design team maps the specification to a core-and-winding design; copper or conductor, electrical steel, insulation, tank and bushings are assembled and tested. Factory acceptance testing precedes dispatch; installation and energization follow at the utility’s site. The actual purchase price depends on rating, material clauses and specification, so we will not invent a universal price per transformer. There are named, real order examples: NPCIL’s Kaiga Units 5 and 6 generator-transformer award, the APTRANSCO LOI, and DVC’s 500 MVA and 200 MVA units. The reactor and autotransformer example is equally concrete. GETCO placed a large order in October 2026 for 80 MVAR and 125 MVAR shunt reactors and a 500 MVA autotransformer, with delivery scheduled over 24–30 months. A shunt reactor absorbs reactive power and helps control voltage on a lightly loaded or long transmission line; the customer is not buying a generic box but a grid-stability component matched to a specific network. DVC separately awarded 500 MVA and 200 MVA transformers for delivery over 15–28 months. The buyer’s acceptance process, transport constraints, site readiness and testing all affect when the manufacturer can book milestones and collect. These large orders reinforce the engineering case while reminding us that the order value is recognized over time, and that a long delivery clock also lengthens the cash-conversion clock.

For furnace transformers, picture a steel producer operating an electric-arc furnace. The furnace’s short, intense, variable load needs very high secondary current, low-voltage control, cooling and arc stability. The plant’s metallurgical team needs a custom transformer coordinated with electrodes, furnace controls and the upstream grid. TARIL’s presentation cites a 132 MVA, 33 kV furnace-duty transformer installed in Mexico, a 70 MVA / 36 kV unit installed at Yazd, Iran, and a 156 MVA unit at Novoross Steel in Russia; the FY26 annual report records successful delivery and factory testing of a 175 MVA EAF transformer to a customer in Mexico. That is a credible global reference set. The pricing remains project-specific: conductor, steel, thermal design, duty cycle, testing, logistics and warranty terms matter more than a single headline price.

CHAPTER III

How It Is Sold & Delivered — Distribution & Unit Economics

"In project manufacturing, the invoice can be earned long before the invoice is collected."
— Research Desk maxim

You and I should start with how a transformer reaches a customer, because “distribution” here does not mean a supermarket shelf. TARIL is a business-to-business engineering manufacturer. The buyer may be a central or state utility, an EPC contractor, a generator, a renewable project developer or an industrial plant. Sales generally begin with a tender, vendor qualification or technical inquiry; engineers reconcile the specification, voltage class, MVA, losses, performance standards and delivery window; commercial teams price the unit, negotiate escalation clauses and agree on milestones. The FY26 order book was concentrated in state utilities and EPC players, while the September call described a roughly even government/private inquiry pipeline and 80% domestic / 20% export bidding. There is no public disclosure of dealer or distributor share, so we should not describe a dealer-led network that the evidence does not show.

The route from inquiry to cash has several gates. First, the buyer qualifies a design and manufacturer; then the order is awarded and material is reserved. Core steel, copper or conductor, insulation, bushings, tank fabrication and accessories must arrive in sequence. The core and windings are built, dried, assembled and tested; a factory acceptance test may be witnessed before packing and transport.
