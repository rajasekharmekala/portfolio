1. What the company does

Westlife Foodworld Ltd (NSE: WESTLIFE, BSE: 505533) owns and runs McDonald's restaurants across West and South India through its wholly owned subsidiary, Hardcastle Restaurants Pvt Ltd. The watchlist note that put it on our list called it a McDonald's supplier. That is wrong. The company is the operator: it holds the master franchise from McDonald's Corporation and pays McDonald's a royalty, so it buys from suppliers and sells to the public.[1][3] The Screener page shows the legal name as Westlife Foodworld Ltd, and the filings use the same name, so we use it here.[1][3]

The company was set up in 1995 as a joint venture with McDonald's Corporation.[1] McDonald's Corporation sold its 50 percent stake in 2011, and the company has held the master franchise under a 2010 agreement since then.[1] At 30 September 2026 the network was 492 restaurants in 80 cities, up from 450 a year earlier. The company says it serves over 200 million customers a year and employs over 10,000 people directly. Sales per comparable restaurant over the last twelve months were ₹62.3 million, up 3 percent.[2][14][3][32] Most restaurants have an in-house McCafé coffee counter, and drive-through, 24-hour and delivery formats sit alongside the standard restaurant.[3]

Customers pay in four ways: at the counter, at a self-ordering kiosk, in the app, or through delivery. In the September 2026 quarter, dine-in and takeaway made up 59 percent of sales and the rest came from delivery, on-the-go pickup and drive-through. Digital channels, meaning the app and kiosks, brought in more than 75 percent of sales. The app has passed 58 million downloads and about 4.2 million people use it each month.[2][34][35][33][36][37] Delivery runs on the company's own riders in some cities and on third-party platforms elsewhere. The seed note also mentioned quick commerce. We found no company disclosure that links the business to quick-commerce apps, so we treat that part of the seed as unverified.[7]

Each rupee of sales follows a fixed path. In the September quarter, food and paper took 32.4 percent, payroll 10.8 percent, royalty 5.6 percent and other restaurant costs 32.0 percent. That left a restaurant operating margin — the share of revenue left after operating costs — of 19.3 percent. Head-office costs took 6.1 percent, leaving an operating profit of 13.1 percent of sales.[2] The table below shows the same split and what each line means.

Lease accounting: why every ratio here is shown twice

Most restaurants sit on long leases of over 20 to 25 years, according to ICRA.[23] Under the Ind AS 116 accounting rule, a company no longer charges rent as a cost. It books a right-of-use asset and a lease debt instead, and then charges depreciation and interest. Reported operating profit rises, and so do depreciation and finance costs. For the September 2026 quarter the company reports operating EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) of ₹98.05 crore on the reported view and ₹57.92 crore on the older view. The gap of ₹40.12 crore is the quarter's rent.[2][38]

This matters for three tests in our rules: debt-to-equity, interest cover and return on capital. Including the lease debt, the company looks heavily indebted. Leaving it out, the company has little bank debt. Neither view is wrong, but they answer different questions. Chapter 3 shows both and says which one we rely on for each test.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Same-store sales growth (SSSG): sales growth at restaurants open for a full year, which strips out the effect of new openings.
  • Ind AS 116: the lease accounting rule that moves rent out of operating costs and into depreciation and interest.
  • Pre-Ind AS EBITDA: operating profit after charging rent, the older and tougher view of the same business.
  • Restaurant operating margin: restaurant-level profit as a share of sales, before head-office costs.
  • Master franchisee: the company that holds the exclusive right to open and run a brand's restaurants in a region.
  • Cash PAT: the company's own measure, profit after tax plus depreciation, which is not the same as free cash.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong in three ways. First, it has the McDonald's brand and an exclusive right to use it in a large region, and ICRA rates the company [ICRA]AA- with a stable outlook for that reason.[23] Second, it has scale: 492 restaurants and a digital channel that brings in more than 75 percent of sales.[2] Third, it has a value offer, an entry-price meal at ₹99, that is now bringing people through the door. Management says guest counts grew in double digits in each month of the September quarter. Its own survey of penetration and frequency in key cities points to both new customers and more visits.[7]

The recovery is recent and it is steep. Same-store sales growth was -2.8 percent in the September 2025 quarter, -3.2 percent in December, +1.5 percent in March, +4.3 percent in June and +10.1 percent in September 2026.[2][6] Because the comparison quarter was weak, the two-year view is gentler: the two-year total is about +7 percent. The reading is still good, but it is one very strong quarter, not a long record. Management calls it its strongest in 13 quarters. It says October ran at a similar pace, with high single digits as the floor it aims to hold.[7]

The business is weak in three ways. First, almost all of the profit that rises with sales is absorbed by lease costs, depreciation and interest. Reported profit after tax was a loss of ₹2.04 crore in the September quarter, and a profit of only ₹32 crore for FY26, which reached that level because of exceptional gains.[1][40][6] Second, the company depends on one brand and one agreement, and ICRA lists compliance with the master franchise agreement and the royalty paid to the principal as credit risks.[23] Third, costs are rising: the company absorbed over 190 basis points of inflation in oil, chicken, power and wages in the September quarter.[7]

Competition is real. ICRA names Dominos, KFC and Burger King as organised rivals and also points to unorganised players. Its industry note carries the headline that operating leverage remains elusive.[23][24] Several listed rivals exist, and we compare four of them in Chapter 3. What sets Westlife apart is the combination of scale, a global brand and low bank debt. What does not set it apart is profitability: its returns are no better than those of weaker peers once leases are counted.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 asks which few numbers run this business and whether they are healthy. A burger chain earns money from traffic, from how many restaurants it can open, from the margin each restaurant keeps, from cash left after rent and building costs, and from the return on the capital it ties up. We picked five measures, one for each, and score each as PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). The rule in our scoring file qualifies a company at 3.0 out of 5.0 or more with at most one FAIL. It rejects a company below 3.0 or with two FAILs.

We state the thresholds for measures four and five here. Cash after rent and capital spending is a PASS at 2 percent of sales or better, a WATCH between -2 and +2 percent, and a FAIL below -2 percent. Pre-Ind AS return on capital is a PASS at 12 percent or better, which is the discount rate we use elsewhere, a WATCH from 8 to 12 percent, and a FAIL below 8 percent. We define that return as follows, and we made the definition explicit during review: it is the latest half-year's operating profit after depreciation on the rent-as-cost view, excluding other income and exceptional items, doubled to a yearly figure, divided by equity plus bank borrowings at the half-year end. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has made many specific promises in the last two years. Some were delivered, some were beaten, and some are running late. The pattern matters more than any single item: the traffic and cost promises were kept, while the expansion and margin promises are slipping.

Expansion is the clearest slip. In July 2026 ICRA wrote that it expects 110 to 120 openings by December 2027. The Vision 2027 target was 580 to 630 restaurants by December 2027, and on the October call the target became 580 or more by March 2028. This chapter takes each estimate and finds its weakest point. The red-team case is the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion. It says we are too strict. Layer 2 fails by about ₹0.9 crore of operating profit, and the latest quarter on its own annualises to 8.3 percent, above the 8 percent floor. The company has just posted its best quarter in 13 quarters, and a rule that rejects it on a half-year average may be catching the old business, not the new one.[7][2] We take that seriously, which is why the rejection comes with a dated reopen test.

Weakest assumption: the return-on-capital line in Layer 2

The FAIL rests on the half-year average, 7.9 percent. The September quarter alone is 8.3 percent, and the third quarter is seasonally strong: Q3 FY26 pre-Ind AS margin was 9.2 percent against 7.5 percent in the March quarter.[14][2] A different stated measure, such as the latest quarter or a trailing year, could flip the score to 3.0. We define the measure on the half-year basis, as stated in Chapter 2B, and a different measure would be a different test, so it would not by itself reopen the name. If the Q3 FY27 results lift the half-year-annualised figure to 8 percent or more, the Layer 2 test is met.

Weakest assumption: the margin path

Our central case needs the pre-Ind AS margin to rise from 7.6 percent in the first half of FY27 to 9.4 percent in FY28. The first half delivered about 68 basis points of gain against a guided 100 to 150. For FY27 to reach 8.7 percent, the second half must run near 9.7 percent against 8.4 percent last year.[2][5][11] Wage rises in Karnataka and higher oil and chicken costs are still working through.[7] If the margin stays near 7.6 percent, FY28E operating profit would be about ₹268 crore and our central value falls toward ₹450.

Weakest assumption: the durability of traffic

The 10.1 percent is measured against a base of -2.8 percent, so it flatters the underlying trend. Management says value and guest count drive the growth and that price rises have been avoided.[7] That is the stronger kind of growth, but it also means the company has not yet shown it can raise prices without losing guests. Elara expects 7.5 percent for FY27 and 3.5 percent in each of FY28 and FY29, which implies growth fading back toward the level of the stronger comparisons.[15] If the festive season runs below high single digits, the story weakens quickly.

We use 25 times pre-Ind AS EBITDA because it is the Street's own number and close to the FY23 trough of 25.6 times. We will know the answer for FY27 when the Q3 FY27 results arrive in early February 2027 and the second-half margin can be compared with the 9.7 percent that an FY27 margin of 8.7 percent needs.[2][14][11]

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is REJECTED for now, and the rule behind it is the Layer 2 score. Our scoring file rejects a company whose five operating measures score below 3.0 out of 5.0, and Westlife scores 2.5 because its return on capital is 7.9 percent against an 8 percent floor. The price adds a second reason. Our tactical archetype needs an asymmetry, meaning a reward-to-risk ratio, of 2.5 to 1, and at ₹600.70 the central value of ₹589 gives -0.11 to 1. The chart does not cause the rejection. The stock sits 0.67 ATR above its nearest verified support, which is the standard buying band, but that ladder measures chart distance, not value or quality.

This is a narrow rejection with a dated reopen test, not a verdict that the business is poor. The name can be reopened only if all three of these are true. First, on the half-year or later results, return on capital on the stated basis is 8 percent or more, which needs about ₹75.5 crore of annualised operating profit against ₹74.6 crore now, and the Layer 2 score is back to 3.0 or more. The Q3 FY27 results in early February 2027 are the first chance. Second, lease-adjusted interest cover is still above 2.0 times. Third, the price is at or below the level where the then-current central value gives 2.5 to 1. On today's numbers that level is ₹522. It moves with the model: if only the FY29E margin stays at 9.4 percent, the value is about ₹563 and the level is about ₹515, as Chapter 5 shows.

Lease basis. Our hard rule on debt-to-equity and interest cover fails on the reported Ind AS 116 basis (2.92 times and 0.98 times) and passes on the lease-adjusted basis (0.48 times and about 3.0 times). We applied the lease-adjusted basis for Layer 1 and disclose it as a condition. The verdict is REJECTED on the Layer 2 score either way. On the reported basis the name would fail Layer 1 as well.

Layer 2 measure five. The return-on-capital measure is defined as follows, and the definition was made explicit during review: half-year operating profit after depreciation on the rent-as-cost view, excluding other income and exceptional items, doubled, divided by equity plus bank borrowings. On that measure the result is 7.9 percent, a FAIL. Adding other income would give 9.8 percent, but other income is not earned by running restaurants.

Not published or not fetchable.
