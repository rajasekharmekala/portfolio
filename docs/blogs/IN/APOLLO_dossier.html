Before you read

Apollo Micro Systems (APOLLO) is a Hyderabad firm that makes the electronics, and now whole weapons, inside India's missiles, torpedoes and mines, mostly for state defence agencies[1]. We reject it for now, because 35.56 percent of the promoters' shares are pledged to lenders, above our 25 percent limit[2]. The business is real, with sales up 78 percent in the half and ₹1,765 crore of orders, but the ₹414.90 price assumes 30 percent yearly profit growth for ten years[3]. Cash is the weak point: operating cash was negative in two of three years, and buying Premier Explosives could cost ₹2,536 crore, above the ₹1,519 crore net worth[4]. We would be wrong to reject it if the pledge falls below 25 percent, the mine order comes on time and profit per share outgrows the share count[5].

1. What the company does

Apollo Micro Systems Limited is a Hyderabad company that designs and builds the electronics inside India's weapons. Its founder and managing director, B. Karunakar Reddy, started it in 1985 and still runs it[1]. The company says it has taken part in more than 150 home-grown defence programmes and has put more than 700 technologies on board missiles, torpedoes, rockets and mines[1]. Think of a missile as a flying computer. Apollo makes the steering computers, power units, fuzes and sensors that decide where the weapon goes and when it explodes.

Almost all of its buyers are arms of the state. The Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) designs a weapon and picks makers for its parts. Defence public sector companies such as Bharat Dynamics (BDL), Bharat Electronics (BEL) and Hindustan Aeronautics (HAL) then build the finished system and buy parts from Apollo[6][5]. Apollo is paid only after it delivers and the customer has tested the goods, so cash arrives late[1]. Working capital is the money a business has tied up in stock and unpaid bills, and it is the central issue in this report.

Orders come in two kinds. A development order pays for a prototype, and a production order pays for a series. Management told analysts that production orders will anchor the business from FY29, and that positive operating cash flow in every quarter will start then[5]. Until then the mix of development work and production work decides both the margin and the cash.

The company is changing shape. It began as a supplier of sub-systems and now wants to make whole weapons, and its government licence lets it make and test missiles, torpedoes, mines and bombs[1]. It bought IDL Explosives in November 2025, a maker of bulk explosives for Coal India[1]. On 9 July 2026 it signed to buy 41.33 percent of Premier Explosives for about ₹1,550 crore in cash, and a mandatory offer for up to 26 percent more at ₹698 a share followed[7][4]. It also holds 34 percent of ShauryAstra, a new weapon-design company led by Dr V.K. Saraswat, a former chief of DRDO, and it is building a new plant that management says will be about 12 times the size of today's[5].

Who pays Apollo, and why orders arrive in lumps

The state is the customer, so the size of defence spending sets the ceiling. The Union Budget for 2026-27 gave the Ministry of Defence ₹7.85 lakh crore, which is 15.19 percent more than a year earlier, and set aside ₹1.39 lakh crore for buying from domestic industry[8]. That money is spent in large, uneven orders. One missile or mine programme can be worth more than a year of the company's sales, and the date it is signed depends on committees, trials and budgets, not on the company.

Three live examples show the pattern. The navy's multi-influence ground mine (MIGM) won its approval from the Defence Acquisition Council on 3 July 2026, trials are complete, and management expects a purchase order by February or March 2027 with a 20 percent advance[3][5]. The government plans to split the first 1,000 quick-reaction surface-to-air missiles (QRSAM) between BDL and private makers, and Apollo hopes to be one of them[5]. Orders for 2,000 Pinaka rockets are expected before December[5]. The consequence is easy to see in the order book. It stood at ₹1,704 crore on 8 August and ₹1,765 crore on 30 September, a rise of 3.6 percent in about seven weeks, yet management says it would reach ₹4,500 to ₹5,000 crore if MIGM lands[6][5].

What this means for an owner: revenue is likely to keep growing because work already signed is large, but the share price will react to a few order announcements, and a delay of one quarter is normal in this industry. The next chapter looks at why the business earns good margins on that work and why it ties up so much cash.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Order book: signed work that has not yet been delivered or billed.
  • Pledge: shares that promoters hand to lenders as security for a loan. If the share price falls, lenders can sell them.
  • Warrant: a right, sold today, to buy new shares at a fixed price within a set time.
  • Preferential issue: a sale of new shares or warrants to chosen investors, not to all shareholders.
  • Open offer: a rule that makes a buyer of control invite the other shareholders to sell at a set price.
  • Dilution is the fall in each existing share's slice of the profit when a company issues new shares.
  • MIGM: multi-influence ground mine, a naval mine that reacts to several kinds of signal from a ship.
  • QRSAM: quick-reaction surface-to-air missile system for the army.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong where the customer cannot easily replace it, and weak where growth must be paid for in cash. Both halves are visible in the filed numbers, and we weigh them in the same order below: the position in the market, the money tied up, who owns the company, and who competes with it.

What is strong: sole-source parts, high margins and a licence

Once a part has been qualified in a weapon, the maker keeps the job for the life of the programme. Apollo's own presentation lists six entry barriers, among them high switching costs, long approval cycles and the cost of test facilities[3]. For the new mine, management told analysts that the electronics and sensors Apollo supplies have no second source, and for QRSAM it is the only qualified maker of the navigation and onboard computer[5]. DRDO's policy is to build a second source in time, so this edge is strong but not permanent.

The money shows it. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges. The group's EBITDA before other income was 24.1 percent of sales in FY26, 25.8 percent in the first half of FY27 and 28.6 percent in the September quarter[3]. On the parent's own books the margin was 33.7 percent in the half year[3]. CARE Ratings notes that the company has kept this margin between 21 and 24 percent for four years while sales grew, and it attributes the gain to a better spread of fixed costs[1]. A high operating margin is the share of each rupee of sales left after the costs of running the business.

A government licence to make missiles, torpedoes, mines and bombs adds a second edge, because it lets Apollo move from selling parts to selling weapons[1]. That step raises the order size and the profit per order. It also raises the money needed to run each order, which is the weakness described next.

What is weak: cash, capacity spending and share issues

Defence work ties up cash for a long time. On Screener's measures the company held stock for 478 days and waited 194 days for customers to pay in FY26, so one rupee of sales turned into cash after about 442 days[9]. The company's own count of working capital days was 359 in FY26 and 353 in the first half of FY27, down from 626 in FY21[3]. That is real progress, but the business still needs about a year of sales as working capital.

The cash flow statement shows the cost. Consolidated operating cash flow was -₹78 crore in FY24, +₹10 crore in FY25 and -₹130 crore in FY26, against profit after tax of ₹31, ₹56 and ₹107 crore[3][9]. Capital expenditure is spending on plants, equipment and infrastructure, and it was about ₹221 crore in the last twelve months[10]. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying assets, and it was negative by about ₹138 crore over the same period[10]. The company pays for the gap with debt and new shares: borrowings rose from ₹208 crore in March 2024 to ₹663 crore in September 2026, and equity capital rose from 28 to 37 crore shares over the same period[9].

Share issues are a habit, not an event. The April-May 2025 preferential issue raised ₹742.25 crore, of which ₹454.77 crore went to working capital[11]. A new plan, approved by the board on 6 July 2026, would raise ₹3,322 crore at ₹416.60 a share, which is almost the current price[12][13]. Together with the existing 37.16 crore shares, the new equity and the warrants would lift the count by 21.5 percent to about 45.13 crore[12].

Who owns the company: a falling stake and a large pledge

The promoters held 63.92 percent of the company in 2018 and held 49.98 percent in September 2026[9]. That fall is the result of share sales to fund growth, and it is a trade-off many founders accept. The pledge is the more serious point. On 30 September 2026 the promoters had pledged 660.57 lakh shares, which is 35.56 percent of their holding[2]. At ₹414.90 those shares are worth about ₹2,741 crore, so a fall in the price could force lenders to sell if the loan terms require cover.

The pledge is not new, and it has not been small. It was 25.30 percent in September 2024, 47.97 percent in March 2025, 39.92 percent in March 2026, 30.51 percent in June 2026 and 35.57 percent in September 2026 (Trendlyne's rounding of the filed 35.56 percent)[14]. The last quarter added 5.06 percentage points just as the promoter family agreed to take 2.61 crore of the new warrants, which would cost about ₹1,087 crore at ₹416.60[14][15]. We do not know how the family will fund that, and the filings we could read do not say. The question matters because a pledge turns a promoter's debt into a risk for every outside shareholder.

Who competes, and what the cycle looks like

Apollo competes with other listed defence suppliers for investor money more than for the same orders, because each one holds its own approved parts on its own programmes. The nearest listed comparisons are Astra Microwave, Data Patterns and HBL Engineering for electronics, Mishra Dhatu Nigam for materials and Solar Industries and Premier Explosives for explosives. The table in chapter 3 compares size, price and returns for the names it lists; Data Patterns is not in it.

The cycle is set by the government's order calendar, which has been slower than companies hoped. An analyst on the Q2 call noted that large systems orders had been muted this year, and management replied that the QRSAM split between BDL and private makers is one reason for the delay[5]. For this reason we treat order timing, not demand, as the main risk to the sales numbers. The next chapter tests whether management has done what it said it would do.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Layer 1 asks two questions. The first is where the company stands on fixed tests such as debt, cash and ownership, which we call static levels. The second is which way each number is moving, which we call trajectory. A company can pass a level and still be heading the wrong way, or fail a level and be improving fast, so we show both.

Four money words recur. ROCE stands for return on capital employed, the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Net debt is borrowings minus cash. Price-to-earnings is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit. Our scoring file sets the floors: ROCE of 15 percent, debt-to-equity under 0.70, and a red line at 1.20.

Several price words also recur. The relative strength index (RSI) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast a price has moved, and the ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in rupees. A base is a sideways stretch the price builds before a move, and a pivot is the top of that stretch. Stage 2 is the Weinstein name for a confirmed uptrend, and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend. A breakout is a move above a pivot on heavy volume. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and the entry zone is the band where we would place one. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain from target divided by the loss to the stop.

Our FY28 EPS of ₹6.55 needs sales to grow 40 percent, a 25 percent margin and a 70 percent chance that MIGM is signed in time to add revenue in FY28. The weakest piece is the MIGM timing. Management first said the order would come in February 2026, then after the council's approval on 3 July 2026 said December or January, and on 26 October said February or March 2027[19][6][5]. Each statement moved the date by months. If the order arrives in March 2027 and production starts in the second half of FY28, MIGM would add far less than the ₹250 crore of FY28 revenue we assumed.

Evidence that would show us wrong: the navy's request for quotation, which management expects in November 2026, and a purchase order before 31 March 2027. We will know about the request within weeks, and about the order by the Q4 FY27 results in May 2027, based on the FY26 reporting date of 19 May 2026[5][20].

Weakest assumption in the value

The central value uses 66.69 times earnings, the median of three year-end multiples. A different but defensible choice moves the answer a long way: ₹328 at 50 times and ₹736 at the 112.4 median[28]. The multiple is a judgement about how much the market will pay for growth in Indian defence stocks in a year, and no filing can settle it. Peers show a range of 28 to 88 times earnings[32][30].

Evidence that would show us wrong: a sector re-rating or de-rating after the Q3 results season in February 2027, and the multiple that Premier and IDL earn once they are part of Apollo's profits. We will also watch the price-to-earnings ratio of the peers in chapter 3, because a move of ten points in the sector multiple moves our central value by about ₹65 on ₹6.55 of earnings. A reader should treat the central value as a range of about ₹330 to ₹530 and not as a point.

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF

The reverse DCF uses a 12 percent discount rate and a terminal value of 20 times year-ten earnings. Both are policy choices. A defence supplier with order visibility might deserve a lower rate, and a business that still needs ₹1 of working capital for each rupee of sales might deserve a higher one. We showed that a two-point change in the rate moves the answer by about two points of growth. The conclusion that the price needs about ₹1,900 crore of yearly profit in ten years is far less sensitive. Evidence that would show the method wrong: a change in how Apollo reports its cash flow, for example a large customer advance that turns operating cash strongly positive, would make the ledger's cash base more useful and would lower its implied growth. We will know that when the Q3 and Q4 FY27 cash flow statements are filed, about February and May 2027.

The red-team case: why our rejection could be wrong, and why it could be too kind

The case for being too harsh: the pledge has been between 25 and 48 percent for two years, and the stock has risen 131 percent from its 52-week low of ₹179.50 without a forced sale[14][27]. Lenders have not forced a sale, the new shares and warrants are priced at ₹416.60 and are paid in cash by 55 equity investors, including Tata Mutual Fund, and by 93 warrant investors, and the cash from the placement and the first warrant instalment would strengthen the balance sheet[12][15][38]. If the pledge falls below 25 percent in the December 2026 filing and the MIGM order lands, we would have rejected a name that could go on to rise further.

The case for being too kind: the first half of FY27 shows only ₹22 crore of operating cash against ₹71 crore of profit, and the Premier purchase may cost ₹2,536 crore for a business that earned ₹34 crore in the last twelve months with an operating margin of 7 percent[3][24]. Auditors have flagged negative net worth and cash losses at some subsidiaries[23]. The parent's effective tax rate was 51 percent in Q2, and management pointed to a move from a 25 to a 30 percent rate[5]. Management says positive operating cash in every quarter will not come until FY29, so FY27 and FY28 will need external money[5]. If the order book stalls near ₹1,800 crore, if the raise slips, and if the share price falls toward ₹250, the pledge would rise again, lenders could ask for cover, and the promoter family's warrants, which cost about ₹1,087 crore in total with a quarter due on allotment, would be harder to fund.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, which means no new money. The rule is the Layer 1 red line on promoter pledges: 35.56 percent of the promoters' shares are pledged against a limit of 25 percent[2]. The reward-to-risk is 0.35 at the ₹414.90 close and 0.90 at the top of our reference zone, against a floor of 2.5. The Layer 2 score is 3.0 of 5.0, which passes with no margin. Apollo is not a holding, since the transactions log has no line for it, so no sale or trim question arises.

A rejection does not say the business is poor. First-half FY27 sales are up 78 percent, the order book is a record, and the parent earns a 33.7 percent operating margin. It says that an owner is asked to accept a promoter pledge, a ₹2,536 crore purchase, a share count that may rise 21.5 percent and a cash flow that is only just positive, at a price that gives a probability-weighted value near today's price. Any one of these is manageable. Together they leave no margin for a delay in orders.
