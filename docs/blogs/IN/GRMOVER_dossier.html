Before you read

GRM Overseas mills basmati rice and sells most of it abroad under other firms' labels. It is also building a rice and food brand in India called 10X. This report rejects the stock: only 28 paise of each rupee of three-year profit came in as cash, and 63 percent of customer bills are more than six months overdue.[2][10] The company is solvent, the shares are down 53 percent to Rs 87.39 and the latest quarter was good, so the problem is cash quality and price.[1][3] The case can reopen after the half-year results of 13 November 2026 and the full-year cash flow in May 2027.

1. What the company does

GRM Overseas Limited is a basmati rice miller and exporter from Panipat in Haryana. The family firm began in 1974 as a partnership called Garg Rice and General Mills, became a public company in 1995 and listed on the Bombay exchange in 1996 and on the National Stock Exchange in 2022.[6] It runs three milling plants, two in Haryana and one at Gandhidham in Gujarat next to the Kandla and Mundra ports, with a combined capacity of 4,40,800 tonnes of rice a year and nine colour-sorting lines that remove broken and discoloured grains.[3] The group has three subsidiaries: GRM International Holdings in the United Kingdom, GRM Fine Foods in the United States, and GRM Foodkraft in India, in which the parent holds 91.48 percent.[3][5]

Two very different businesses sit inside the one company. Abroad, GRM sells basmati to retail chains and importers in more than 55 countries, and about 95 percent of that international revenue is private label, meaning the rice is packed under the buyer's own brand. Only about 5 percent is sold under GRM's own labels, Himalaya River and Tanoush, through the UK subsidiary.[3][2] At home, the 10X brand was launched in 2020 and now sells packaged basmati, atta (wheat flour), edible oil, besan (gram flour) and ready-to-cook items through 176 distributors and more than 1,03,000 small neighbourhood shops, backed by actor Salman Khan as brand ambassador.[3]

The reader will meet some money and market vocabulary again and again, so the next three paragraphs define it once. EBITDA, short for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, is profit before the finance and accounting charges. ROCE, or return on capital employed, is the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses. EPS means earnings per share, the company's profit divided across all shares. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying assets. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before any financing. Working capital is the money tied up in stock and in unpaid customer bills. Operating margin is the profit from running the business as a share of sales. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money.

Valuation words come next. The price-to-earnings ratio is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit. Net debt is borrowings minus cash. Dilution is the fall in each owner's slice of the company when new shares are issued. Guidance is a company's own published forecast. A consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who cover a stock, and GRM has no such coverage. The 20-day moving average, the 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the average closing prices over those windows, lines that a price tends to bounce off or stall beneath.

Price-chart words finish the list. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend on the Weinstein stage scale. A base is the sideways shelf a stock builds between the two, and its pivot is the top of that shelf. A breakout is a close above the pivot on strong volume. RSI, the relative strength index, is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been moving. ATR, the average true range, is the average daily price swing in rupees. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut. A limit order only fills at your price or better. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain from the entry to the target divided by the loss from the entry to the stop.

How the business turns paddy into profit

Basmati milling is a spread business. The company buys paddy at harvest, dries, ages and mills it, sorts the grain, packs it and sells it later, so most of its cost is the rice itself and its profit is the gap between what it pays and what it receives.[6] Aged basmati sells at a premium, which is why mills hold large stocks. At 31 March 2026 the group held Rs 435.1 crore of inventory, about 104 days of sales, and Rs 577.0 crore of customer receivables.[3][15] That is more than Rs 1,000 crore of working capital for a business that earns an operating margin near 5 percent, and it explains why the report spends so much time on cash.

Export credit terms stretch to 180 days, and the banks lend against the stock at a net interest cost of 2.5 to 5 percent a year after government subvention.[2] Cheap funding is part of the model. If customers pay late, however, the interest subsidy stops covering the delay, and the company funds the gap from its own pocket. Chapter 2B shows that this is exactly what the filings show for FY26.

Who owns the company and what changed in 2025 and 2026

The promoter group, led by managing director Atul Garg, held 63.06 percent at 30 September 2026, down from 72.43 percent in March 2025. Foreign funds held 6.35 percent, domestic institutions 2.91 percent, and 29,343 holders in all.[15] The fall in promoter stake came from three events. The company issued 90,70,000 convertible warrants at Rs 150 each in August 2024 to raise Rs 136.05 crore from promoter and non-promoter investors, and converted them during FY26. It then issued 13,81,40,000 bonus shares in a 2:1 ratio in February 2026, which tripled the share count to about 20.7 crore without changing anyone's percentage.[2] The promoter group still bought 2.5 lakh shares in the open market in June 2026, which lifted its stake by about half a point.[19] No promoter shares are pledged.[8]

For an investor, the warrant conversion is both good and bad. It added about Rs 136 crore of equity and pushed gearing from 0.86 to 0.61 times, which helped the Acuite credit upgrade in July 2026.[6] But the new shares came with a lock-in. The March 2026 filing shows 2.3154 crore locked-in shares, 11.1 percent of the company, and gives no expiry dates. When a lock-in ends, those shares can be sold.[8]

What happened to the share price in June 2026

On 5 June 2026 the stock closed at Rs 152.15. Four sessions later, on 11 June, it closed at Rs 87.30, a fall of 43 percent.[17] On 9 June alone, 5.13 lakh shares had changed hands on the Bombay exchange by mid-afternoon against a one-month daily average of 1.11 lakh.[22] On 10 June the exchange asked the company to explain the move, and the company replied on 11 June. The reply text was not available to us.[23] The stock hit its lower circuit of 9.4 percent on 11 June, with sell orders left unfilled.[24] The announcements feed for 9 to 12 June lists only the promoter group's share-purchase disclosures and the exchange's clarification request. It lists no results, rating action or business update in those days.[23]

Outside commentators point to the end of the lock-in on about 11 percent of the shares, valuation de-rating, foreign selling and thin institutional ownership.[25][26] We cannot prove any of these causes from filings, and one source is an unverified blog, so we treat them as plausible rather than established. What the filings do prove is the size of the supply that could have arrived, and that the price has not recovered: the 52-week low of Rs 77.00 was set on 1 October 2026, and the close on 28 October was Rs 87.39.[1][24]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when it earns more than its capital costs and turns profit into cash. GRM clears the first test narrowly and fails the second. The picture is easiest to read in three parts: what is genuinely good, what is weak, and what the accounts say about governance. A short comparison with the KRBL idea that first put GRM on our list closes the chapter.

What is genuinely good

Growth is real. Consolidated revenue rose 31 percent in FY26 to Rs 1,769 crore and another 30.5 percent in the June 2026 quarter, while profit after tax rose 24 percent in FY26 to Rs 76 crore.[6][3] The statutory Food segment lifted its profit 62.6 percent in Q1 FY27 to Rs 31.82 crore, a margin of 8.5 percent against 6.1 percent for FY25.[19][10] Operating profit before other income rose from Rs 24 crore to Rs 36 crore in the quarter.[15] Those are the numbers of a company whose core food business is getting more profitable as it adds branded volume.

The funding position is sound. Gearing is 0.61 times, interest cover is 5.58 times, debt service cover is 4.32 times, and the current ratio is 2.33 times.[6] Acuite upgraded the long-term rating to ACUITE A and the short-term rating to ACUITE A1 in July 2026, citing scale, a stronger capital structure and the warrant money.[6][35] The group has no pledged promoter shares, only Rs 2.9 crore of contingent liabilities and no key audit matters in its auditor's report.[8][2] For a lender, that is a safe borrower. For an owner, safety is necessary but is not the same as value.

What is weak

The first weakness is structural. About 95 percent of export revenue is private label, so GRM sells a commodity under someone else's name and has little power over price.[3] Its operating margin has slid from 8 percent in FY22 to 5 percent in FY26 as sales grew, and Acuite itself says the FY26 EBITDA margin fell from 7.30 percent to 6.58 percent because of brand-building spend.[15][6] Growth bought with lower margins needs a higher return on the extra capital, and the extra capital is not earning it.

The second weakness is capital use. Consolidated ROCE has stayed at 14 percent in each of FY24, FY25 and FY26 (17 percent in FY23 and 29 percent in FY22), against a floor of 15 percent in our rules, and the standalone company earns 11.3 percent.[15][16] The company's own presentation states 20.2 percent for FY26, but it does not publish the formula, so we cannot reproduce it. Our own calculation in Chapter 3 gives 14.0 percent including other income and 9.8 percent without it.[3] Other income, mostly interest and treasury gains, was Rs 36.7 crore in FY26, which is 36 percent of pre-tax profit.[3]

The third weakness is the one that decides the verdict: the cash does not follow the profit. Cash flow from operations was negative Rs 53.6 crore in FY26 even as profit reached Rs 76 crore, because inventory grew by Rs 121 crore and receivables by Rs 97 crore in the year.[2][3] Chapter 2B shows how old those receivables are.

What the accounts say about governance

The auditor, Mehra Goel and Co., gave an unmodified opinion with no key audit matters on the FY26 consolidated accounts, and another auditor reviewed one subsidiary.[2] Two features deserve attention anyway. The bank credit lines are guaranteed by the promoter directors, so the family stands behind the debt.[2] And the receivables note shows no credit-impaired balances at all, even though a quarter of the book is more than a year past due.[2] A company that expects to collect everything may be right, but the reader should know that the accounts hold no cushion for the case where it is wrong.

The June 2026 quarter was reviewed, not audited. The limited review report notes one subsidiary, not named, whose Rs 0.43 crore of revenue and Rs 0.24 crore loss were not reviewed by its own auditor, and an associate (the coffee brand) that cost Rs 0.36 crore.[5] Both amounts are small. They are worth a line because the plan is to buy more of such businesses with Rs 200 crore, and each new associate adds a set of accounts that the group auditor takes largely on trust.

Why the KRBL comparison does not rescue the idea

GRM entered our queue as a peer of KRBL, the large listed basmati company, with a note that it was a Gulf-exposed comparator likely to miss the 15 percent ROCE floor. That is confirmed: 14.0 percent consolidated and 11.3 percent standalone.[15][16] But the comparison also shows what GRM lacks. KRBL generated Rs 933 crore of operating cash in FY26 and trades at 10.6 times earnings. LT Foods generated Rs 910 crore and trades at 20.4 times. GRM generated negative Rs 54 crore and trades at 23.7 times.[30][31][15] The market pays a higher multiple for the smaller, less cash-generative company. That is the main valuation puzzle in this report. It may reflect a premium for the 10X brand story and for growth of 43 percent over the last twelve months, but a premium for growth is only fair if the growth turns into cash. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

An estimate is only as good as its weakest input. This chapter names the weakest assumption in each estimate, argues the strongest honest case against our own verdict, names the single claim on which the verdict turns and says when we will learn whether it holds.

Weakest assumption in the earnings estimate: the operating margin holds near 6 percent

Our FY27E and FY28E profit assume an operating margin of 6.0 and 5.9 percent. FY26 was 5 percent on Screener's measure and 6.58 percent on Acuite's EBITDA measure, and Q1 FY27 was 8 percent. The base case therefore sits between a weak full year and a strong quarter.[15][6] The assumption is weak for two reasons. The company says it will keep spending on brands, which lowers margin, and the unbranded domestic line that drove Q1 growth is a thin-margin trade. Each half-point of margin on FY28 revenue is worth Rs 8.7 crore of profit or about Rs 8 on the share value. If the margin were 5.0 percent, as in FY26, FY28 profit would be about Rs 78 crore and the base value about Rs 72. If it were 8 percent, as in Q1 FY27, profit would be about Rs 130 crore and the value about Rs 119. The estimate is therefore sensitive, and the base case is a middle path rather than a forecast we can prove.

Weakest assumption in the valuation: 19 times earnings is a fair multiple

The 19 times multiple is a judgement placed between peers. LT Foods earns a higher return on capital, generates real cash and trades at 20.4 times. KRBL and Chaman Lal Setia generate cash and trade near 10 times. GRM's own three-year median is 25.0 times, but that median is lifted by the run-up of 2025 and 2026, when the multiple reached 58.3 times on 15 May 2026 while earnings were flat.[31][30][32][17] A 19 times multiple is therefore defensible but not provable. At 14 times the base value would be Rs 64. At 25 times it would be Rs 113. The choice matters less than it seems for the verdict, because the cash red line fails first, but it matters a great deal for the entry zone: at 18 times no valid zone exists above the Rs 77.00 support, and at 25 times the ceiling for a 2.5 to 1 entry is Rs 85.96.

The red-team case: the strongest honest argument against rejecting GRM

A fair critic would say the cash test is mechanical and that FY26 was a year of deliberate stock building. Inventory rose by Rs 121 crore, much of it aged basmati that earns a premium, and the working capital was funded by Rs 136 crore of new equity, not by new debt. The rating agency, which sees the same books, upgraded the credit in July and expects Rs 91 to 101 crore of cash accruals in FY27 and FY28. The promoter family added shares near the low. The June quarter showed operating profit up about 50 percent. At 7.3 percent implied growth near Rs 77, the stock would be cheap if the bills are good.[3][6][19][15]

That case has force, and it is the reason we set a reopening path instead of a permanent exclusion. But it leans on three things we cannot verify. The first is that the Rs 144 crore of bills over a year old are collectible, which the annual report neither explains nor provisions. The second is who the late payers are. The report does not say, and a Gulf concentration would turn collection into a question about war and sanctions. The third is that FY27 cash will reverse FY26, which the half-year cash flow on 13 November will start to show. Our rule requires evidence of cash, and the evidence is not in yet.[2][27]

The bear case that remains if the name never reopens

If the bills are not collected, profit has been overstated for two years and the stock is worth the Rs 42 in our bear case or less. The accounts show no credit-impaired balance, the promoter directors guarantee the bank limits, and the 10X Ventures plan could pull Rs 200 crore of cash and shares into unproven brands. A further supply overhang cannot be ruled out, since 11.1 percent of the shares were locked in at March 2026 and we have no expiry dates.[2][8] The bear case is not a prediction, but it is the outcome our entry rules are built to avoid.
