Eco Recycling Ltd (Ecoreco) is a Mumbai e-waste recycler that has traded on BSE for years and on NSE since 17 August 2026.[11][13] Our verdict is REJECTED for now, with no position advised. Margins are very high and debt is near zero, but only 42 paise of each rupee of profit became cash over three years.[1][2][4] The price is ₹380.65, and our price file holds only 46 daily bars, so the 50-day and 200-day trend tests cannot be run.[30] We would be wrong if the half-year cash flow due in mid-November shows profit turning into cash and the price closes above ₹408.70[30].

1. What the company does

Think of Ecoreco as a company that is paid to take old electronics away and then paid again for what comes out of them. Brands that sell phones, laptops, servers and appliances in India must, by law, make sure a set share of the weight they sold is recycled. Most of them do not own a recycling plant, so they hire a registered recycler such as Ecoreco, which shreds and sorts the devices at its Mumbai-area plants and then hands the brand a certificate that proves the recycling happened.[4][22] Ecoreco says it was the first Indian e-waste company, started in 2005, has recycled more than 45,000 tonnes and serves over 500 clients.[4] Its installed capacity is 31,200 tonnes a year, of which 6,000 tonnes is a line for lithium-ion battery scrap.[1][4]

The company is tiny for what it earns. The 2025-26 annual report says it had 55 permanent employees, consolidated revenue of ₹48.18 crore and consolidated profit after tax of ₹22.90 crore.[1][5] The Chairman told shareholders that total income was about ₹53 crore, pre-tax profit about ₹31.5 crore and cash surplus about ₹13 crore, and that the company has no borrowings.[11] A business with 55 people and a 47 percent net margin (the share of revenue left as profit) is either a very good niche or a revenue line that depends heavily on a regulatory certificate. Chapter 2 tests which one it is.

Revenue comes from three lines. Sales of recovered material and refurbished equipment made up 73 percent of FY26 revenue, data-destruction services made up 5 percent, and a line called other operating revenue made up the remaining 22 percent.[1] The company does not say what is inside that last line. On the Q1 FY25 call management declined to split revenue between recycled material, refurbished goods, EPR fees and data destruction, saying a breakdown would reveal its pricing to competitors.[10] In the same call the Chairman said revenue from operations includes all four, so the EPR fee income is somewhere in these three lines, and our best inference is that most of it sits in other operating revenue. That is an inference, not a disclosure.

The arithmetic is simple. EPR certificates are priced per kilogram. The regulator's rule sets the lowest price at 30 percent of a penalty called environmental compensation and the highest price at 100 percent of it. For the IT and telecom category the penalty is ₹112 a kilogram and the lowest certificate price is ₹34 a kilogram.[22] If all ₹10.65 crore of FY26 other operating revenue were certificates for that category, it would equal roughly 3,100 tonnes at the floor price or roughly 950 tonnes at the ceiling.[1][32] This is a sizing illustration only. The company has not published its certificate volumes or prices.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPR (extended producer responsibility) is the legal duty of a brand to arrange recycling for part of the weight of the electronics it sells, normally by buying certificates from a registered recycler.
  • ITAD (IT asset disposition) means collecting, data-wiping, refurbishing or recycling business computers and servers, usually for banks and multinationals.
  • CPCB is the Central Pollution Control Board, the national regulator that registers recyclers and runs the EPR certificate portal.
  • Environmental compensation is the penalty set by CPCB for rule breaches, and it is the yardstick for the lowest and highest EPR certificate price.
  • Black mass is the powder left after shredding spent lithium-ion batteries; it holds the cobalt, nickel and manganese that refiners buy.
  • MTPA means metric tonnes per annum, the usual way to state a recycling plant's yearly capacity.
  • Permitted to trade is an NSE category that lets an already listed company trade on NSE without a new public issue.
  • debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' equity. A figure of 0.04 means 4 paise of borrowings for each rupee of equity.
  • Operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue. In this report it leaves out other income.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit before financing costs and accounting charges. The company's own version includes other income.
  • earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares in issue.
  • return on capital employed (ROCE) is operating profit as a share of the money tied up in the business. A high figure means each rupee is working hard.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself collects, before it buys plant or repays lenders.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is cash flow from operations minus spending on plant and equipment.
  • working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company owes its suppliers.
  • guidance is management's own forecast of what it expects to achieve.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain from entry to target divided by the loss from entry to stop. A ratio of 2.5 to 1 means ₹2.50 of possible gain for each ₹1 risked.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend on the four-step trend scale used in this report.
  • A base is a sideways stretch of trading that forms a floor. Its top is the pivot, and a breakout is a close above that top on strong volume.
  • A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • The 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 sessions. It is a slow line that the price tends to bounce off.
  • The relative strength index (RSI) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast price has moved, and the average true range (ATR) is the typical daily swing in rupees.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business starts with scarcity of permits and trust. Ecoreco holds CPCB and state pollution board registrations, an international R2v3 recycling certification, membership of the TERRA network of certified e-scrap and ITAD providers, and a data-destruction offer that includes a mobile shredding truck.[4] Those credentials matter to multinational clients because a brand that sends a server to a recycler is also trusting it with the data on the drives. Management said on the Q1 FY25 call that it insists producers hand material over at a nominal price and bear the other costs, which keeps its cost of acquiring waste low.[10] The numbers agree: materials, net of purchases and stock change, were ₹12.73 crore against ₹48.18 crore of FY26 revenue, so about 74 percent of each rupee of sales was left after material cost.[4][32]

The same credentials do not make the sector easy. The company itself lists as a threat that e-waste generators prefer to sell to the informal sector, which pays more because it carries no compliance cost.[1] Its own slides say about 78 percent of Indian e-waste is handled informally and the national collection rate is about 11 percent.[4] India had 386 registered e-waste recyclers by March 2026, so a formal licence is common, not rare.[26] The edge is therefore a client list and a brand, and a client list can leave. A moat that depends on a few multinational accounts and on a regulatory price floor deserves a lower multiple than one built on a patent or a plant that rivals cannot copy.

The margin is the main puzzle. Operating profit before other income was about 60 percent of revenue in FY26 and 71 percent in FY25, against 7 percent at Pondy Oxides, 10 percent at Gravita and 12 percent at Namo eWaste, the three listed recyclers we could verify.[14][27][28][29] Metal recyclers buy scrap at market prices and sell metal at market prices, so their margins are thin. Ecoreco's margin looks like a service business that is paid to take material and sells certificates for the privilege. That model is lucrative when certificate prices hold, and it is fragile when they fall. We cannot test the pricing, because the company withholds the EPR split.[10]

Scale is the other limit. Revenue of ₹48 crore and profit of ₹23 crore come from one business segment and one main site.[1][5] The company's own note says it operates in a single business segment, so there is no segment table to audit.[5] A single large client, a single regulatory change or a single plant outage could move profit by a large share. The company does not disclose client concentration, and we treat that as a gap rather than assume it is small.

Where the policy tailwind is real

The legal demand is real and rising. Recycling targets for producers are 60 percent of estimated waste generation for FY24 and FY25, 70 percent for FY26 and FY27, and 80 percent from FY28.[23] NITI Aayog estimates India's e-waste at 6.19 million tonnes in 2024, rising to 14 million tonnes by 2030.[25] The government has also approved a ₹1,500 crore incentive for recycling critical minerals, and Ecoreco says it is among the eligible entities.[11][26] A rising target means more certificates must be bought every year, which is the core of the bull case. The company's 19 October 2026 press release makes the same point but adds that the pace of commercial growth depends on regulation, investment and the economics of recovery.[12]

Where the policy risk is real

The certificate price band is under legal challenge. Large manufacturers have contested the 30-to-100 percent band before the Delhi High Court, a January 2026 interim order stayed enforcement against one petitioner, and no final judgment had been issued by June 2026.[23] If the floor is struck down or cut, the high margin in other operating revenue is the first thing to go. The company's ₹10.65 crore line already fell 34 percent in FY26, a year in which targets rose.[1] Rising targets and falling certificate revenue do not fit together neatly, and management has not explained it.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means comparing what management said it would do with what happened. We audited the last two years of calls and annual reports. The record is mixed. On the capital side management kept its word: it said the 18,000-tonne plant expansion, a ₹50 crore investment, would be funded entirely from internal cash, and the balance sheet shows no bank debt.[3][10] On the operating side the promises were bigger than the results.

In May 2024 the Chairman said he was hopeful of about 50 percent capacity utilisation by March 2025 and almost 100 percent by March 2026. He also said that at ₹40 a kilogram of profit on about 12,000 tonnes, profit could be about ₹48 crore to ₹50 crore.[9] Actual FY25 consolidated profit was ₹23.38 crore and FY26 profit was ₹22.90 crore, roughly half of that figure.[2][4] The company has also stopped giving tonnage, so utilisation cannot be checked. The Q1 FY25 call used a rule of thumb of 30 rupees of profit per kilogram to infer about 3,000 tonnes in the quarter, which is about 12,000 tonnes a year against a 25,200-tonne plant, or under half.[10] We cannot say what utilisation is now.

The CFO letter in the FY24 annual report promised a lean balance sheet in FY25 with a continued focus on reducing current assets.[3] Current assets instead rose from ₹30.43 crore in FY24 to ₹43.23 crore in FY25 and ₹71.72 crore in FY26, and trade receivables rose from ₹4.45 crore to ₹15.65 crore.[4] The Q1 FY25 call said CPCB was about to set higher EPR fees and that fee income should ramp up.[10] CPCB did publish a price floor, in final guidelines dated 25 August 2025, but the company's other operating revenue fell 34 percent in FY26.[1][22] The rule arrived, and the revenue did not follow.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue grew from ₹15 crore in FY22 to ₹48.18 crore in FY26, and profit rose from ₹13 crore to ₹22.90 crore, but the shape matters more than the end points.[1][14] The big step came in FY24 and FY25, when the operating margin jumped from about 22 percent to 59 and then 71 percent.[14] FY26 was flat: revenue rose 10 percent, operating margin fell to 60 percent and profit fell 2 percent.[1][4] The Chairman called FY26 encouraging, and by his own measure total income grew 14 percent to ₹52.81 crore.[4][11]

Other income is a bigger part of the profit story than it looks. It was ₹4.63 crore in FY26 against ₹2.34 crore in FY25, mostly gains on investments, and it swung from a loss of ₹0.95 crore in Q4 FY26 to a gain of ₹2.94 crore in Q1 FY27.[1][4][14] Company-defined EBITDA of ₹33.32 crore in FY26 includes that other income, so the cleaner operating figure is about ₹28.7 crore, which is the 60 percent margin shown in the table.[4][32] We use the cleaner figure for margin and note where a number includes other income.

The balance sheet is strong where it is easy to see and weaker where it is not. Borrowings are ₹4 crore against a net worth of ₹110.62 crore. Layer 3 turns a verdict into an entry price, a stop and a position size. Here the size is zero, because the verdict is REJECTED and the conviction tier is TIER_0. A tier is a rating of how much of the portfolio an idea may command. Tiers run from TIER_1, the largest, to TIER_4, and TIER_0 means no position. Each tier has a risk budget, which is the share of the portfolio we accept losing if the stop is hit. It is stated in basis points, where 100 basis points is 1 percent, and TIER_3 allows 50. Portfolio heat is the sum of those risk budgets across all open positions, and this name adds none. For a name that qualifies there are two action labels. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP means buy in steps as the price pulls back to verified support. WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT means do not buy now and wait for the price to fall to support.[31] The table at the end of this chapter is built by the report template. Its let-winners-run row says a held winner may grow to 22 percent of the portfolio before it is trimmed, with the stop trailed under the 21-day exponential average or the 50-day simple average. That row is not applicable here, because the stock is not held.

The support ladder puts the close 0.76 ATR above the only measured floor. That is the standard band, which would normally call for an ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP limit order. The ladder assumes a verified floor, and ours is a one-day low in a 46-bar record. So we give a re-test reference zone and not an order.[30][31] The top of the zone, ₹371, is the highest fill at which the 2.5 to 1 turnaround floor holds on our base target.
