Should a buyer put new money into the shares now, and if not, what would have to change first? The answer comes from a fixed process, not from a feeling about the company.

The process has three layers. Layer 1 is a set of hard gates. A gate is a yes-or-no test with a number attached. If any gate fails, the stock is rejected and the later layers are not run. That rule keeps two questions apart: whether a company is a good business, and whether its shares are a good purchase today.

Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five operating measures for the type of business. Layer 3 is the entry posture. It measures how far the price sits from its nearest support level, in units of ATR, which is the average true range: the typical daily price swing, averaged over the last fourteen sessions.

Several technical terms recur. A 50-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 50 sessions, and a 200-day moving average is the same measure over 200 sessions. A base is a sideways stretch in which a stock stops falling and builds a floor. A breakout is a move above the level that has capped the price. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is the operating profit a company earns on all the money tied up in it. Cash-to-profit (CFO/PAT) is the operating cash flow divided by reported profit after tax, over three years.

Some words in the gate tests carry a specific meaning. Asymmetry is the reward-to-loss ratio: how far the target sits above the price, divided by how far the stop sits below it. A measured move is the height of a base projected upward from the point where the base breaks out. A retest is a return to a level the price has already broken above, which then holds. The base pivot is the price level above which a base counts as broken out. Dilution is the fall in each existing holder's share of a company when new shares are issued.

Stage 2 is the markup phase of a stock's trend, in which the price sits above a rising 50-day average and that average sits above the 200-day average. Stage 4 is the reverse, a markdown in which the price falls below falling averages. The debt-to-equity ratio is borrowings divided by shareholders' equity. The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share, and it shows how many rupees investors pay for each rupee of profit. Operating margin (OPM) is the share of sales left after the day-to-day costs of running the business, before interest, tax and depreciation.

Working capital is the money a business has tied up in stock and in unpaid bills, net of the bills it owes its suppliers. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges. EPS is earnings per share: the company's profit divided by its number of shares.

2 What the company does

Eco Recycling is an e-waste company. E-waste is discarded electronics such as computers, phones, printers and lamps. The company describes itself as India's first and leading e-waste management company, a pioneer in the sector since 2005[1]. Its services cover reverse logistics, which means collecting goods from the user and moving them back into the supply chain; data destruction; IT asset disposition, the process of removing and resaling or recycling business equipment; recycling of e-waste; lamp recycling; precious metal recovery; and help for producers to meet their extended producer responsibility obligations[1].

Extended producer responsibility, usually called EPR, is the rule that makes manufacturers and importers pay for the recycling of the products they sell. Under the E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022, producers and importers must buy EPR certificates from registered recyclers, which creates a traceable chain from the producer's obligation to the recycler's processed tonnes[2]. Eco Recycling sells both the service and the processing capacity that such certificates need. That makes its revenue partly a function of how strictly the rules are enforced, which is a point the red team returns to in section 8.

Joint venture for IT asset disposition

The company has announced a 50-50 joint venture in India with Electronic Recyclers International, Inc. (ERI) of the United States[3]. ERI is described as one of the largest electronics recycling companies in the United States. The partner says the two will co-own ERI India, which will be based in Mumbai, and that the business is due to launch in India later this year[4]. The partner's announcement says the venture will combine ERI's IT asset disposition experience with Ecoreco's established local practices. 8 Fatal flaws and red team

A red-team review asks the opposite question from the one the gates ask. It assumes the verdict is wrong and looks for the best honest case for buying, and then asks whether the case survives. The flaws below are the points where the buy case is weakest.

Flaw one: profit is not yet collected

The business reports strong profit, but operating cash has been below profit in each of the last three years, and debtor days rose from 63 to 119 in FY26[1]. A company that sells more and waits longer to be paid can show rising profit for a long time while its cash falls behind. That is the pattern the cash test measures. The flaw is serious because it is the one thing the reported profit cannot show. The fix would be a reduction in debtor days, not a rise in margin.

Flaw two: the technical base is not verifiable

The share has fallen from its 52-week high, sits below its 20-day average, and has seen relative strength turn negative, at minus 7.48 percentage points over 28 sessions[1]. The ledger cannot give the averages that define a Stage 2 uptrend. Without them, the report cannot prove that the stock is in an uptrend, and the rules require proof rather than the absence of a breakdown. A buyer who acts now would be buying on hope that the base holds.

Flaw three: the valuation has no verified anchor

At 32.3 times earnings[1], the stock trades at roughly twice the multiple of the other waste company in the peer set. The only published target, 720 rupees, has no stated method. No verified multiple exists for the company, so no sensitivity can be built, and the valuation argument rests on one target, 720 rupees, that no source explains. A buyer would be paying for a future that is not yet in the cash flows.

Flaw four: dilution and the promoter warrants

The board has approved issuing 3,00,000 warrants to the promoter at 411 rupees each[19]. Dilution is the fall in each existing holder's share of the company when new shares are issued. The 3,00,000 warrants are about 1.6 percent of the 19.3 million shares outstanding[15], so if all were converted, existing holders' stake would fall by about that much. At that price the warrants are worth about 12.3 crore rupees, which is about 1.6 percent of the 776-crore market value[1]. The promoter's willingness to pay a premium above the current price is a positive signal, and the report notes it. The fetched sources do not show whether shareholders approved the issue, or whether the warrants have been paid for. Until that is known, the warrants are a small but unresolved item, and a reader should check the outcome in the next filing.

Flaw five: the revenue depends on a policy regime

The EPR certificate system ties demand for the company's recycling to how strictly producers must meet their obligations[2]. The Central Pollution Control Board sets floor prices for the certificates[16]. A change in those prices, or in enforcement, would change the company's revenue without any change in its operations. The fetched sources do not quantify that exposure, so the report flags it as a risk without a number.

Red team: the strongest case for buying

The strongest case for buying is that the company is a high-return, almost debt-free business that is growing faster than its share price suggests. Profit has risen from 18 crore in FY24 to 24 crore on the trailing figure[1], the June quarter profit was up 13.3 percent on a year earlier[8], the company has a joint venture that could widen its service range[4], and the stock is near a support level at 0.89 ATR on the market ledger. If the cash arrived, the case would be strong.

The red team does not accept that case yet, for three reasons. The cash has not arrived, and the rules do not pay for a forecast of cash. The technical base cannot be shown to be rising. And the target that would make the reward large enough is not anchored to any verifiable input. The case is a good thesis waiting on evidence, and the report's job is to say which evidence would turn it into an entry.

The bear case is more specific than the bull case, and the specific items are the three tests that fail. The bull case depends on events that have not yet happened. The verdict follows the evidence that exists today.
