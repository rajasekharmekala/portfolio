How the verdict was reached

The underwriting rulebook sorts every candidate through three layers. Layer 1 is a set of hard gates. Each gate is pass or fail, and one failure is enough to reject the stock at once. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five measures that matter for this business, and Layer 3 checks whether the price sits close enough to a support level to buy. Because Layer 1 fails here, Layers 2 and 3 are not run, and the reasons are set out in chapter 3.

The financial part of Layer 1 asks three questions of the reported accounts. Is return on capital employed (ROCE) at least 15 percent? ROCE means the profit a business earns on all the money put into it, so 15 percent is a floor below which the business is not earning its keep. Is debt-to-equity below 0.70? Debt-to-equity means borrowed money divided by shareholders' money, and above 0.70 the company depends too heavily on lenders. Is the three-year sum of cash flow from operations (CFO), the cash the business generates from its own work before any plant spending, at least 0.70 of three-year profit? The answer shows whether reported profit has turned into cash.

The technical part asks whether the price is in a clean uptrend. The ledger works this out from daily closing prices up to the session of 12 October 2026. A Stage 4 is the opposite of Stage 2: a stock in decline, below falling averages, which is why the rules block any stock in that state. The asymmetry floor then tests the reward against the risk. The target must be anchored either to forward earnings times a three-year median P/E multiple, or to a measured move, which is a technical target equal to the height of an earlier price range, added to the level the price rose above. The stop is the level at which the position is closed to limit the loss, and it is set from the average true range (ATR), the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions, so the stop sits a fixed number of normal swings below the price.

This verdict is REJECTED because two Layer 1 gates fail. The recorded trigger is asymmetry, at 1.30 to 1 against a floor of 3.0 for a core holding, and the momentum test in the scoring rules also fails, at 6.58 ATR against a limit of 1.2. Rejection archives the file. It is a statement about the price and the rules, not a judgement that the company is poorly run. The rules keep those two questions apart on purpose, because a good business can be a poor entry point.

Chapter 1. What the company does

Shivalik Bimetal makes components that sense and switch electric current. Its core product is a thermostatic bimetal, a strip of two metals bonded together that bends as it warms and so opens or closes a circuit, as in a heater or a motor protector. The company's own product page describes it as two or more layers of different alloys firmly bonded together.[1] Its second product is the shunt resistor, a precision low-resistance element that lets a meter measure current in an electric vehicle battery or a smart meter. The company describes itself as India's only fully integrated maker of these precision bimetals and low-ohmic shunt resistors, which means it does the metal bonding, strip rolling and assembly in house rather than buying in parts.[2] The stock trades on NSE as SBCL under ISIN INE386D01027 and is classed as Electrical Components and Equipment.[3] It was founded in 1984 and is headquartered in New Delhi, with factories at Chambhaghat and Kather in Solan and a new plant at Pune.[4][5]

The customer list is the important part for a beginner to understand. Shivalik does not sell to shoppers. It sells to the makers of switchgear, meters, cars, batteries and industrial controls, who then sell the finished product. The company reports more than 300 customers globally and about 1,000 employees.[4] Shunt resistors and bimetals are the two engines, and management now describes a move up the value chain, meaning more finished assemblies and fewer raw strips. The call on 7 August 2026 gives the strategy in management's own words, and the Pune plant is the main test of it.[6]

The Pune plant is the single largest new commitment. The company received a Consent to Operate for Phase I from the Maharashtra Pollution Control Board, valid until 30 June 2032.[4] Management said the plant could bring in a business worth INR 300 to 400 crore in about three years, and that the spending needed for it is in the range of INR 20 to 25 crore, because most of the heavy equipment already sits in the existing plant.[6] That capital figure is small, which means the growth case rests on the customer programmes landing, not on the plant cost. Management also said the first phase can serve only one model, so the revenue cannot arrive before the year-end.[6] A trade-press item says the launch had slipped from an April 2026 schedule while the consent was pending, and it puts the planned investment at about ₹20 crore, funded from internal accruals.[7] Business news reported the start of Phase-I production after the approval.[8]

Two governance items need watching. The chief financial officer, Rajeev Ranjan, resigned by letter dated 6 August 2026, with effect from 31 October 2026.[4] A filings tracker lists the same resignation as a management-change filing on 6 August 2026.[9] The company secretary and compliance officer, Aarti Sahni, is due to leave on 7 December 2026.[2] Neither change proves a problem, but a new finance head arriving during a plant ramp-up and a cash-conversion question is the kind of change that deserves a closer look at the next filing. The trading window closes from 1 October 2026 until 48 hours after the September quarter results.[2] A filings archive lists 154 filings from 1997 to 2026, which is where the resignation notices and the transcripts can be checked.[10]

What this means for you: this is a manufacturer of components for other manufacturers, and its profit depends on how many programmes it wins and how much of the metal value it keeps. The business is real and growing, but the reporting and ownership questions in chapter 6 matter as much as the growth.

The momentum tests are the rule for whether a stock can be bought at all. Test A and Test B are two routes to a pass, and a stock must pass one of them in full. Test A passes three of its four conditions and fails the stretch test A4. Test B passes four of its five and fails B3, the same closeness-to-the-low condition. Both routes fail for the same reason, which means the stock is not disqualified by its trend or its strength, but by its distance from the level where a buyer would normally enter. B2 is read as a positive excess return over 28 sessions, which is the rule's stated test. B4 and B5 measure different things over different windows, because the rule names no window. B4 uses the ledger's dry-up ratio over 63 sessions, which divides the quietest session's volume by the 63-session average; it is 0.16, so the quietest session was about a sixth of the average. B5 uses the ledger's expansion multiple over 20 sessions, which divides today's volume by the 20-session average; it is 2.17. The two tests answer different questions, and the report does not read a volume trend from them. Test B fails on B3 whichever way B4 and B5 are read, so the verdict does not move.

The two failing conditions can only be met together in a narrow set of cases. A4 needs the close at or below ₹781.7, which is the 63-session low plus 1.2 ATR. A1 needs the close above the 50-day average of ₹1,040.59. Both cannot hold while the 50-day average stays where it is. The momentum test therefore cannot pass without either a sharp fall in the price or a long sideways period that brings the average down to the price. The asymmetry floor must also be cleared before the stock can be bought.

Chapter 6. Fatal flaws and the red-team case

A red-team review asks what would make the bullish reading wrong. Each point here is taken from the sources, and each one can be checked. The strongest bear points are the valuation, the cash conversion in FY26, the analyst coverage, the ownership history and the management changes. None of them is a Layer 1 gate on its own, but together they explain why the file should not be reopened on the strength of the price alone.

Valuation. The price is 198.6 percent above the 52-week low and 61.6 percent above the 200-day average. The P/E of 59.6 and the book multiple of about 12.7 times mean that investors are paying for growth that has not yet shown up in the accounts.[2][18] The one analyst target found, ₹1,114, sits 1.2 percent above the close, which means the only forecast on record sees almost no further upside.[15] A stock priced this far above its one published target is exposed if the growth disappoints. Cash in FY26. The company's three-year cash test passes at 0.85, but FY26 alone was 0.57, and working-capital days rose to 128. Ownership.
