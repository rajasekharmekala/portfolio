1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Stock Cycles

Core Investor Question: How did a 160-year-old engine maker end up owning an electric-scooter business, and what did each turn cost?

Greaves Cotton dates itself to 1859. Its own “Built on Legacy” timeline starts with James Greaves, later joined by George Cotton, and runs through a 1939 joint venture with Ruston & Hornsby of the UK to make diesel engines in India [1][4]. The corporate identity on every filing, however, comes from incorporation in 1922 [1]. The working assets are engine design skill, plants around Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar (CSN, formerly Aurangabad) and a spares and service network. This chapter shows how they were assembled, what was bought and sold, and why group operating profit swung from ₹272 crore in FY19 to ₹25 crore in FY22 [60].

Control passed in 1947 to industrialist Lala Karam Chand Thapar [1]. Today the promoter is DBH Investment Capital India Private Limited (formerly Karun Carpets), with 55.78% at 31 March 2026 and no pledge of shares; Karan Thapar is Chairman [35][6]. The engine skill came in layers: a 1976 partnership with Lombardini Motori for a light diesel engine unit at CSN, a 1998 partnership with the Piaggio Group, BS-II compliant engines in 2005 and an R&D centre in 2006 [1][4]. BS stands for Bharat Stage, India's tailpipe emission standard, and every step up forces a redesign. Greaves calls itself the largest maker of single-cylinder diesel engines [44].

The timeline has two halves. Until about 2006 the company built engine capability slowly with two long-lived partners. Since 2018 it has made a fast run of deals and exits around that engine base, covering electric vehicles, vehicle finance and engineered components. The second half carries most of the risk and most of the explanation for the earnings record below [1][4].

The deals since 2018 share one logic: a stable engine maker buying growth outside the engine. Ampere Vehicles came first. The FY19 annual report records a 67% stake for ₹77 crore with an option on another 13% for ₹75.5 crore, and the FY20 report says Greaves completed 100% [13][12]. Ampere became Greaves Electric Mobility Limited (GEML) [41]. In July 2020 it took 74% of Bestway Agencies, an e-rickshaw maker [11]. GEML also holds MLR Auto, which builds L5 three-wheelers (the official class for full-size auto-rickshaws and small cargo carriers) at Toopran in Telangana, taken to 51% in FY24 and 67.41% in June 2025 for ₹55 crore [8][9][6]. In the other direction, Greaves quit construction-equipment manufacturing in mid-FY15 “due to non-viability of operations on account of its continuing losses” [17].

Excel Controlinkage was the one deal aimed at the core. Excel, based in Nagpur, makes push-pull cables and gear-shift levers. Its FY22 turnover was ₹166.5 crore with profit before tax of ₹36.7 crore, and about 40% of revenue was exported [38]. Greaves took 60% on 8 May 2023, 80% on 12 August 2025 and the last 20% for ₹113.47 crore on 13 August 2026 [8][6][2]. Excel's revenue slipped from ₹267.68 crore to ₹252.06 crore in FY26, then grew 14% in Q1 FY27 and 30% in Q2 FY27 [6][25][24].

Management says the portfolio is now tidy. In FY26 it exited finished farm equipment, hurt by Chinese imports, and pruned parts of retail [24][26]. Q2 FY27 still carried ₹60.1 crore of one-offs: an impairment of Bestway goodwill (₹17.6 crore) and the ELE brand (₹2.49 crore), and a faster write-down (₹27.30 crore) of a discontinued “Teja” three-wheeler brand and old two-wheeler tooling [1][2]. The CFO said there was “nothing more at this stage”, yet called such reviews a regular period-end exercise [62].

Some online claims did not survive checking, so we do not use them. Ranipet is described as a closed engine plant, but every annual report shows it as GEML's electric-vehicle plant [59][11][6]. “Anglian Omega” and “Bharat Bharati” appear in no filing or call from FY15 onward [6][17]. Greaves Retail is a division of the parent, not a company [6][52].

Leadership has run in three eras. Sunil Pahilajani was MD and CEO until 16 September 2016. Nagesh Basavanhalli was MD and CEO from 27 September 2016, was styled Executive Vice Chairman by 2022-23, became Non-Executive Vice Chairman on 12 May 2023 and stepped down on 30 April 2025 [15][12][9][6][7]. Dr Arup Basu, who became Deputy MD in May 2022, was MD from 12 August 2022 until he resigned on 14 April 2025, so the two overlapped as executive leaders during 2022-23 [9][6]. Parag Satpute has been MD & Group CEO since 14 April 2025 on a five-year term [6]. The CFO also changed (Manish Poddar replaced Akhila Balachandar, who resigned on 19 March 2026), as did the auditor (Price Waterhouse replaced Deloitte from FY26) [26][6][7]. FY26 is the new team's first full year, so its record is short.

Greaves.Next is that team's plan, unveiled on 6 November 2025 [28]. Its three moves are Accelerate the Core, Build New Muscle onto the Core, and Expand into New Horizons, applied to Energy, Mobility and Industrial Solutions plus two investees, GEML and the EV lender Greaves Finance (GFL) [28][4]. Targets are 16-20% organic revenue growth a year for four to five years, core EBITDA margins of 13-15% and ₹500-700 crore of investment [28][1][4]. EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a plain measure of operating profit. An older board vision of ₹15,000 crore of group revenue by 2030 sits above the plan [31][30]. From FY26's ₹3,437 crore that needs about 44.5% growth a year (our arithmetic); an analyst said “around 50%” [28]. Core revenue grew 16% in FY26 and 20% in H1 FY27, so the organic target is within reach of the record, but the 2030 figure needs acquisitions far larger than any deal on the timeline [4][1].

The quarter ended 30 September 2023 is the most important single event in the recent record. FAME-II was a central scheme paying a demand incentive on each electric vehicle sold, on condition that makers localised manufacturing under a Phased Manufacturing Programme. On 25 May 2023 the Ministry of Heavy Industries (MHI) told GEML it had not followed that programme and proposed to recover the ₹124.91 crore of subsidy paid, plus interest, and deregister GEML [8][18]. GEML denied the allegations but, to avoid a prolonged dispute, refunded ₹139.98 crore (subsidy plus ₹15.07 crore interest) on 27 October 2023 and provided in full for a ₹337.34 crore subsidy receivable. Together that is the ₹477.32 crore provision [8][18][19]. A ₹104.20 crore Pune land-sale gain offset part of it, and the quarter's profit after tax was ₹(374.59) crore [19].

The cash cost was the ₹139.98 crore refund; the receivable was income GEML had counted on and will never collect. Without eligibility GEML could not pass on the incentive: prices rose by ₹10,000-20,000 and electric two-wheeler volume fell from 1,08,710 in FY23 to 47,818 in FY24, down 56% [36]. Share recovered from about 3.1% in H1 FY25 to 4.8% in H1 FY27 [1]. Our judgement: this was a subsidy-compliance failure at the subsidiary, not an engine-business failure, since the parent's EBITDA margin was 13% in FY24 [8]. The filings we read do not say whether MHI formally restored full eligibility [33][54].

GEML has needed three equity infusions in four years: about ₹1,170.81 crore from Abdul Latif Jameel (ALJ) in June 2022 [9], ₹29.5 crore from minority holders in FY25 [6], and a ₹530 crore rights issue in July 2026 (Greaves ₹331.12 crore, ALJ's vehicle ₹198.87 crore), leaving holdings near 62.5% and 37.5% [2]. The IPO meant to fund growth was not pursued. GEML filed its draft prospectus on 23 December 2024 for a fresh issue of up to ₹1,000 crore plus sales of shares by Greaves and ALJ [37][36], and SEBI issued observations in May 2025 [6][68]. Karan Thapar explained that ALJ “did not want to be a promoter post a public listing” and so would have to dilute [29]. In February 2026 the then CFO still said “we will be raising approximately a primary of 1,000 crores” [27].

Then the plan changed. The Q2 FY27 results say GEML “decided not to proceed with its proposed IPO and did not avail the one-time extension of SEBI observations”, and ₹12.66 crore of IPO costs became an exceptional charge [2]. On 29 October GEML's MD, Vikas Singh, said the matter is “underway with the IPO committee” and “we are not stating a definite date” [24]. Our read: the 2024 filing has lapsed, any listing needs a fresh one, and an IPO is an option, not a plan. The CFO said the rights money covers the cash burn for 12-18 months [24].

The earnings cycle has three phases. In FY15-FY19 the engine business ran at 12-17% standalone EBITDA margins and group revenue stayed between ₹1,616 crore and ₹2,015 crore [60][8]. Margin and cash were the story, not growth. Operating cash flow stayed positive every year, from ₹114 crore to ₹280 crore, and the company paid out 59-74% of profit as dividends [60].

The second phase, FY20-FY22, was a squeeze from every side. BS-VI forced redesigns, COVID cut FY21 revenue 21%, and then came what the FY22 report called a “continuous increase in RMC due to exceptional commodity inflation”, where RMC is raw-material cost [12][11][10]. A chip shortage and weaker three-wheeler sales added to it. The parent's margin fell from 13% to 7% to 4%, its revenue from ₹1,988 crore in FY19 to ₹1,178 crore in FY22, and its ROCE (profit on capital employed) from 27% to 4% [8][6]. Group EBITDA margin hit 1.5% in FY22 and group operating cash flow turned negative for the first time in the series, at ₹(56) crore [60].

The third phase, FY23-FY26, is two stories at once. The parent recovered to 13% by FY24 and 14% by FY26, on revenue of ₹2,365 crore, and ROCE reached 21% [8][6]. The core businesses earned ₹383 crore of EBITDA at 14.4% in FY26 [4]. GEML's EBITDA margin improved only from about -30% to about -20% [26][4], so group EBITDA rose from ₹136 crore to ₹239 crore, a 7.0% margin [4]. In H1 FY27 the core margin slipped from 14.9% to 12.0% as commodity prices rose after developments in West Asia and customer price indexation lagged; the group margin was 6.0% [1][25]. Weak operating cash flow, about ₹(334) crore over FY22-FY26, is covered in Chapter 6 [60][2].

What the history means for an owner

Greaves is two businesses with different histories. The engine and genset core has earned 13-14% margins at parent level for three years and fell back in two squeezes, FY21-FY22 and H1 FY27. So the 13-15% band is a through-the-cycle target, not a floor. We think a return to it is the more likely path once OEM contracts pass on higher costs, which management places from Q4 FY27 [24], but pass-through lags by four to five months, so another dip is possible [62].

The subsidiary has never made an operating profit, lost its subsidy once and has needed new equity three times, with no breakeven date given [24]. Group earnings depend on how fast GEML's loss per unit shrinks, from ₹20,000 to ₹6,300 in a year [1]. The 2030 ambition is a hope; the 16-20% organic target is the figure to test.

Example 1, Energy Solutions: a 250 kVA genset for a factory or clinic

The buyer is a manufacturing plant, IT park, clinic, bank, petrol station or railway site that cannot stop when the grid trips; the product page lists these uses [46]. kVA measures electrical capacity, so 250 kVA is about 200 kW. Management says demand has moved “from addressing basic power deficits to prioritising uptime and operational continuity” [6]. The product is model GPWII-PIV-250C: 415 volts, three-phase, a 385-litre fuel tank, 3,110 kg dry weight, an enclosure rated for 50°C and compliance with the CPCB IV+ emission rules for gensets [46].

Engines for small and medium sets are made in-house; for the highest horsepower a strategic partner supplies the engine, and management says its data-centre play is “limited” [26][24]. Assembly is at Talegaon near Pune, with engines from the CSN plants [6]. No list price is published; buyers are sent to a dealer [46]. Sales go through “3S Composite Dealers” (sales, service and spares) and direct institutional orders, with a five-year extended warranty available [46][6].

The money continues after the sale. A turnkey option covers site preparation, installation and commissioning [43]. The largest single order to date, ₹35 crore in Q4 FY26, bundles supply, installation, commissioning and long-term maintenance [26]. Aftermarket grew 35% in FY26 against 20% for the segment [4].

Example 2, Mobility Solutions: the engine inside a three-wheeler or a French microcar

A three-wheeler maker needs a low-cost, durable engine that meets the BS-VI emission standard and costs little to run. A European microcar maker needs a Euro V+ diesel for its small quadricycles. Greaves does not name its Indian OEM customers; the one named is Ligier, the French microcar maker, for which Greaves is the sole engine supplier [1][62]. The company says it is the largest maker of single-cylinder diesel engines, with about 60% of diesel three-wheeler engines [44][6].

The products: the G435 A BS VI (435 cc, air-cooled, 5.6 kW) and G599 W BS VI (599 cc, water-cooled, 7 kW) for three-wheelers, and the G499 W Euro V+ (499 cc, 6.0 kW), billed as the world's first single-cylinder EU5+ L6eB diesel [47][48][49]. Single-cylinder means one piston, which keeps cost and weight low (about 59-64 kg). CNG engines, which run on compressed natural gas, are a small share [62]. Engines are built at the Chikalthana and Shendra units in CSN, and a dual-conveyor line was added in FY26 for capacity [6][26].

Delivery is to OEM assembly lines under multi-year contracts that index raw materials “but it comes with a lag” [24]. Price per engine is not disclosed; cost pass-through from Q4 FY27 is expected [24]. Spares reach owners through the Greaves retail network of 200+ distributors, 20,000+ mechanics and 7,000+ retailers [6]. Auto-engine revenue grew 48% in Q4 FY26 and 37% in Q2 FY27, though the CEO said the shift to CNG and electric has been “more gradual than we expected” [26][24][27].

Example 3, Mobility Solutions: Excel's push-pull cable and gear-shift lever

A push-pull cable is a steel wire inside a flexible sheath that carries a push or a pull from a driver's lever to a throttle, brake or gearbox. Excel's buyers are commercial-vehicle, construction-equipment, agricultural, marine and rail makers. Named wins include Stanley Black & Decker (a best-process-control award in January 2026), CAT (UK) and TAFE in Q1 FY27 and Tata Motors' international business in Q2 FY27 [3][1][42].

Excel makes cables, mechanical and electronic gear-shift and throttle levers, conduits and sensors in Nagpur, with a new conveyorised cable-assembly line commissioned in FY26 [6][26]. It is shifting from standalone parts to complete assemblies [6]. In FY22, before Greaves bought in, it earned ₹36.7 crore of profit before tax on ₹166.5 crore of turnover, with about 40% exported [38]. In FY26 its statutory segment result was ₹53.75 crore on ₹252.06 crore, 21.3% before central costs [6]. Billing terms and prices are not disclosed.

Example 4, Industrial Solutions: a certified fire-pump engine

A fire pump must reach rated pressure and flow at once. Global buyers want engines with FM and UL listings (independent safety certifications), which management says “very few companies in the world” hold [24]. Greaves sells to fire-pump makers and channel partners at home and abroad, to the armed forces directly, to marine and fishing-boat builders, and to agricultural makers of power tillers, mini tractors and pumps [26][25][1].

The range runs from the 3G11, 4G11 and 6G11 and D3V6 and D3V8 series at 30-750 HP, including a 750 HP fire engine launched in Q2 FY27, down to air-cooled 1-20 HP engines for tillers and sprayers [50][51][1]. Greaves cites over 20,000 fire engines deployed [50]. Exports of FM-UL engines started in Q1 FY27, and a global firefighting-equipment maker wrote a letter of appreciation in Q4 FY26 [24][26]. Per-unit prices are not disclosed. The annual report flags a volume decline at a major OEM, farm-price pressure and state orders linked to subsidies as risks to this segment [6].

Example 5, Investee: an Ampere electric scooter and an L5 three-wheeler

The buyer is a value-segment rider moving from a petrol scooter on running cost, or a fleet: a food-service major runs 20,000+ Ampere units, and e-commerce channels were about 13% of GEML retails [1]. The Nexus EX+ sells at ₹1,29,999 (₹1,35,999 with Intellipack connectivity) with a 5 kW motor, and the Magnus G-Max with Intellipack at ₹1,09,999 with a 142 km range on the Indian Driving Cycle. The whole range uses LFP batteries (lithium iron phosphate, a safer, nickel-free chemistry) [1][6]. Batteries carry a 60-month, 75,000 km warranty on current models [98].

GEML builds at Ranipet, with capacity of 4,80,000 units a year and about 40% utilisation in Q2 FY27, and sells through 418 dealers (320 a year earlier) [1]. The PM E-DRIVE scheme pays ₹2,500 per kWh, capped at ₹5,000 per vehicle, on e-2Ws up to ₹1.5 lakh ex-factory [57], about 3.8% of the Nexus EX+ price (our arithmetic). L5 vehicles are made by MLR Auto at Toopran (18,000 a year), with a financing tie-up offering up to 95% of the vehicle value [6]. L5 diesel share was 4.2% in Q2 FY27 [1].

Example 6, Investee: a loan from Greaves Finance

A driver or rider who cannot pay cash applies to GFL (ev.fin), which lends against electric two- and three-wheelers of any brand; approval takes “under 2.5 minutes” [6]. Managed assets were ₹630 crore at September 2026 (₹521 crore in March 2026) across 11 manufacturers [1][4]. GFL funds the book with debt, including ₹185 crore of 10.5% secured debentures in H1 FY27, and is rated IND A- (Stable) [2][6]. Its FY26 revenue was ₹39 crore and its own accounts show a profit of ₹1.57 crore against a ₹7.99 crore loss; the statutory segment shows a ₹4.74 crore loss, and the filings do not reconcile the two [4][6][2].

3. Distribution, Supply Chain & Unit Economics

Core Investor Question: How do Greaves products reach customers, and where does cash get stuck on the way?

Greaves has several routes to market, and each carries different cash terms. About 48% of group sales in FY26 went through dealers and distributors, down from 55%, and the network shrank from 1,755 to 1,255 dealers and distributors. The ten largest took 26% of dealer sales [6]. The rest went direct to original equipment manufacturers (OEMs, the companies that build the vehicle or machine carrying a Greaves part), to institutions and defence buyers, and to exports. The shrinkage looks deliberate: management exited two-wheeler parts, construction-equipment parts and some battery and digital platform businesses in FY26, and called the retail clean-up complete in Q1 FY27 [26][3].

OEM supply is where qualification bites. A vehicle maker cannot change an engine supplier mid-programme: the part must pass emission and durability tests and is then written into a multi-year contract. Dealer networks work differently, because Greaves sells to someone who resells. 4. Industry Structure, Competitive Moat & External Learning Hub

Core Investor Question: Who are Greaves' rivals, and does the company have a moat in any of its businesses?

Greaves competes in four unrelated industries, so it has four moat questions: gensets, three-wheeler and OEM engines, industrial engines, and electric two- and three-wheelers. The evidence is thinner than we would like, because the filings name few customers or competitors and publish no engine market shares [6]. Every B2B engine and genset peer lost margin to commodity inflation while revenue grew. Greaves’ core margin of 11.5% sits close to Kirloskar’s standalone 11.0%, so the compression is an industry cycle, not a Greaves failure [1][81]. The EV peer is different: Ather’s positive EBITDA is only its second such quarter, on 93,211 units against about 37,649 billed by GEML [80][1].

Gensets are the segment where scale and service decide. Mahindra Powerol was number one in FY25 with 23.8% of 1,51,634 diesel gensets, and the top five (Cummins India, Kirloskar, Caterpillar, Mahindra Powerol and Greaves) account for roughly 55–60% of organised revenue [79][90][88]. Greaves’ own share is about 4% [30][29]. The CPCB IV+ emission rule (a pollution standard for diesel gensets up to 800 kW, effective July 2023) added exhaust after-treatment and electronic injection, prices rose 5–18% and volumes ran at 80–85% of the earlier CPCB-II level [88][89][90][91]. That favours engineering depth. Greaves has a complete 160–750 kVA range, remote monitoring and a service network [1]. It lacks an in-house very-high-horsepower engine, which it buys from a partner, and its data-centre play is “limited” in the CEO’s word [62]. Our view is that buyers weigh service and brand above the engine, so this moat is modest and rests on dealer quality.

Three-wheeler diesel engines are the strongest moat and the weakest market. Greaves calls itself the largest maker of single-cylinder diesel engines, with about 60% of diesel three-wheeler engines on VAHAN data, and it is sole engine supplier to a French microcar maker [44][6][62]. Qualification and multi-year contracts protect that share. But India Ratings writes that “the 3W diesel engine industry is continuously shrinking amid rising environmental concerns, although GCL remains a market leader” [70]. These engines were ₹545 crore, or 15.8% of group revenue, in FY26 and about 42% of Mobility Solutions in H1 FY27 [3][1]. The L5 industry (auto-rickshaw-type three-wheelers) sold about 8 lakh units in FY26, with an electric share near 33% against 23% a year earlier [6]. Management concedes that the move to CNG and electric is “more gradual than we expected” [27]. The moat is real, and so is the erosion of the ground it defends.

Excel and Industrial Solutions are narrower. Excel defends itself with customisation and certification (IATF, ISO, CE) at OEMs; the filings name no competitors, so we cannot rank it [6]. Industrial Solutions has a harder moat in firefighting, where FM/UL listing, which “very few companies in the world” hold, gates global fire-pump OEMs [62][1]. In farm equipment there was no moat: Chinese imports made finished machines uncompetitive and Greaves exited [62].

The bull case, steelmanned

Greaves has real positions: about 60% of diesel three-wheeler engines, a sole-supplier relationship in Europe, a complete medium-horsepower genset range and a spares network that reaches 7,000 retailers [6][62][1]. Core revenue grew 24% in Q2 FY27, gensets 36% and auto engines 37%, and the margin dip matches peers while contracts are indexed to catch up from Q4 FY27 [1][62]. In EVs, loss per unit fell from ₹20,000 to ₹6,300 in a year on a plant at about 40% of its design, so the gain from spreading fixed cost is largely unspent [1]. The core holds ₹110 crore of net cash, the rating is AA-, no promoter shares are pledged, and ₹530 crore of fresh equity funds GEML for 12–18 months [62][69][67].

The bear case, steelmanned

The core product is exposed to a declining engine type, and India Ratings says so [70]. Core EBITDA margin fell 290 basis points in a year while revenue rose 20%, and management admits recovery has been “slower than we had anticipated” [1][62]. GEML has never been profitable, has raised equity three times in four years (₹1,171 crore in 2022, ₹29.5 crore from minority holders in FY25 and ₹530 crore in 2026), and faces incumbents that took 81% of new volume [9][2][84]. Write-offs recur: ₹477.32 crore in FY24, ₹39.34 crore in FY26 and ₹60.1 crore in Q2 FY27, even after a statement of “nothing more at this stage” [8][6][2][62]. Group operating cash flow was negative in FY22–FY25 and in H1 FY27 [6][2]. The ₹15,000 crore FY30 ambition needs roughly 44% annual growth (derived) against a 16–20% organic target [28][6].

Our independent judgment is that both cases are partly right because they describe different businesses. The core is a good, narrow-moat industrial franchise in modest-growth markets, and its margin dip looks cyclical: Kirloskar fell by a similar three points and Greaves’ contracts index with a lag [1][81][62]. We put the probability at roughly 60% that core EBITDA margin returns to at least 13%, the floor of management’s range, within about four quarters after pass-through completes, about 25% that it settles between 11% and 13%, and about 15% that it stays below 11% because metals keep rising or rivals discount. GEML has no moat today, a halved subsidy and strong incumbents. We think sustained positive EBITDA by the end of FY28 is below even, near 40%, because unit loss is falling fast yet volume must keep scaling while incumbents defend share. The core argument rests on pass-through, and the EV argument rests on cash.

Historical Failure-Mode & Base-Rate Twin: Hero Electric (India) (India, electric two-wheelers, 2007–2026)

Shared Vulnerability: One of India’s earliest electric scooter makers was an early FAME subsidy beneficiary with a low-speed portfolio and a large dealer network [92]. It was named among makers accused of breaking the Phased Manufacturing Programme (local-sourcing) rules, and the ministry sought about ₹133 crore back, which Hero disputed while claiming ₹556 crore of unpaid subsidy. Buyers moved to faster scooters from Ola, Ather and TVS, working capital tightened and new products came late. A government committee first found no violation, but an independent fraud investigation continued.

Historical Numbers & Base Rate: Insolvency began on 20 December 2024. The highest-voted rescue plan won 47.66% of creditor votes against the 66% required, the process expired on 13 February 2026, and the tribunal ordered liquidation in March 2026. Admitted claims included Bank of Baroda ₹55.36 crore, South Indian Bank ₹17.62 crore, IDFC First Bank ₹9.43 crore and an unsecured vendor ₹47.87 crore [92].

How GREAVESCOT Compares Today: GEML shares the exposure: a FAME refund of ₹139.98 crore, a low-speed Reo line, and a net worth of only ₹59.65 crore at 31 March 2026 before the 2026 rights issue [8][6]. It differs in having a parent that put in ₹331.12 crore and a rated balance sheet [2][72]. One failure is not a statistic, but the mechanism is clear, and Cummins’ electrolyzer write-down below shows the same pattern in a stronger company. Subsidy dependence without scale is where the risk concentrates, so share (4.8%) and unit loss (−₹6,300) matter more than any single quarter.

A second data point comes from a legacy engine maker. Cummins booked a $240 million non-cash charge in the quarter ended September 2025 and began a strategic review of its electrolyzer business after sales fell well short of a $400 million target set in 2020 [93]. A very good engine franchise took large write-downs when a new market lacked policy-backed demand, which is why our scorecard treats the EV moat as unproven.

India, CPCB IV+ emission transition and commodity spike, 2023–2026

Shared Mechanism: A new emission rule adds costly content (exhaust treatment, electronic injection), lifts unit prices and triggers pre-buying, then volumes settle below the old level while metal prices run ahead of contract repricing. Margins whipsaw even when revenue grows, because pricing is set quarterly and costs move daily.

Historical Numbers & Outcome: CPCB IV+ genset volumes ran at 80–85% of CPCB-II levels with price increases of 5–18% [89][90]. In Q2 FY27 Kirloskar's standalone EBITDA margin fell from 14.0% to 11.0% on revenue up 19% [81]; Cummins India revenue rose 4% while pre-tax profit fell 28.4% [83]. Greaves' core margin fell from 14.8% to 11.5% on revenue up 24% [1]. Greaves' group margin has swung 15% (FY17), 1.5% (FY22), 7.0% (FY26) [60][4].

Direct Implication for GREAVESCOT: Greaves' earnings are cyclical in margin more than in revenue, so a single year's EBITDA is a poor guide to the through-cycle level. The operating test is whether raw materials return from 67.8% of core revenue toward the 64% of a year earlier as OEM indexation catches up in Q4 FY27 [1][24]. If the pattern holds, the core margin recovers with no help from volume. If it does not, the 2022 precedent shows how far the margin can fall.

Genuine cash compounding versus accounting optics

Genuine: a standalone margin of 13–14% for three years, core net cash of ₹110 crore, doubled revenue, a GEML loss per scooter cut from about ₹20,000 to ₹6,300, and receivables older than three years that are almost fully provided [62][1][6].

Optics: a negative cash cycle resting on 92 payable days; owners' profit (₹107.14 crore in FY26) three times the group's because minorities absorb GEML losses; “business PBT” that excludes ₹60.1 crore of Q2 charges; treasury interest income that flatters the pre-tax line; and a lending-adjusted operating cash flow the company does not itself report [1][6][2].

Our judgment on earnings quality. The case that earnings are sound: the core produced real operating profit through a hard commodity cycle, carries little debt and reports receivables honestly. The case that they are not: over five years the group produced negative cumulative operating cash flow on ₹619 crore of EBITDA, booked about ₹418 crore of net exceptional charges from FY23 to H1 FY27, and shows ₹60 crore of fresh charges in the latest quarter [60][8][2]. Our weighting is that the core earns genuine cash profit, probably near its 11–15% EBITDA margin over a cycle, while consolidated numbers understate it because GEML's losses and the lender's loan book sit on top. Treat group earnings as low quality until GEML reaches positive EBITDA and operating cash flow turns positive on its own, which management dates only as “fairly soon” and “12 to 18 months” [24]. That is a judgment about the record, and the next chapters ask whether the business is good enough to wait for it.

How it was calculated:

We forecast ten years of free cash flow, add a terminal value, discount at 12.5% and solve for the growth that equates the total to enterprise value of ₹5,226 crore. The margin ramps in a straight line over five years. WACC, the weighted average cost of capital, is the return investors need from the whole business.
