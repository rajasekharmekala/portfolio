Before you read

Bharat Forge makes forged and machined steel and aluminium parts for trucks, cars, industry, defence and aircraft.[1][6] This report rejects the stock for now.[29] Return on capital employed (ROCE) is 12.6 percent against a 15 percent standard, the price is 85.9 times earnings against a three-year median of 67.0, and all three chart tests fail.[1][15][29] The business is sound, with moderate debt, no pledged promoter shares and a defence order book of Rs 11,196 crore.[2][16] The call would be wrong if the profit jump that analysts expect arrives on time, and the 5 November 2026 results are the first test[14][18].

1. What the company does

Bharat Forge is a Pune-based manufacturer that heats steel and aluminium bars and presses them into shape. This is called forging, and it produces parts that are stronger than cast or welded ones. The company then machines these parts to exact sizes. Customers buy crankshafts, front axle beams, steering knuckles, connecting rods and transmission parts for commercial vehicles (trucks and buses), passenger cars and heavy industry.[6] It is the flagship of the Kalyani Group, has 28 facilities in India and 8 in Europe and the United States, and has been making forgings for more than 60 years.[1][6]

Who pays the company? Mostly vehicle makers and engine makers. The standalone company exported Rs 4,011 crore of goods in FY26, and about 62 percent of that went to the Americas, mainly to North American truck and car makers.[4] In India the buyers are truck and passenger-car makers and, increasingly, the Ministry of Defence. The credit rating agency CARE noted that the United States contributed about 18 percent of consolidated revenue in FY25, which tells you how much the company's fortunes follow American truck demand.[10]

The company makes money by selling each part at a price that passes steel and aluminium costs through to the customer, then earning a margin on the forging and machining work. Margins differ a lot by place. In FY26 the Indian operations earned an operating margin of 24.4 percent. The overseas plants in Germany, Sweden, France, Italy and the United States earned 3.8 percent, and the small electric-mobility unit lost money.[4] That gap is the main reason consolidated profit looks weak compared with what the Indian business earns on its own.

Over the last six years the company has added businesses beyond auto parts. It builds artillery guns, armoured vehicles, small arms and now naval gas turbine generators through its subsidiary Kalyani Strategic Systems Limited (KSSL). It makes engine, structure and landing-gear parts for aircraft makers. It makes iron castings through JS Autocast and axle systems through K Drive Mobility, which it bought from American Axle in July 2025.[2][4][6] The company calls this plan Bharat Forge 2.0: more Indian manufacturing, more machining and complete systems instead of single parts.[6]

How the Indian business is built

The parent company, standalone Bharat Forge, earned Rs 8,396 crore of revenue and Rs 2,312 crore of operating profit in FY26, a margin of 27.5 percent, and revenue fell 5.1 percent because American truck orders were weak. [4] Beneath it sit four Indian subsidiaries. KSSL, the defence arm, had FY26 revenue of Rs 1,562 crore and a margin of 10.1 percent, up from 6.3 percent. In the June 2026 quarter its margin jumped to 17.2 percent on a richer product mix.[4][2] Management says the steady yearly margin for defence should settle at 22 to 23 percent, which would be a large step up from FY26.[3] Orders already in hand give the plan weight: the defence order book at 30 June 2026 was Rs 11,196 crore, about 6.4 times FY26 defence revenue of Rs 1,757 crore.[2][6]

The orders that sit behind the revenue

A large share of the Indian growth story depends on the Ministry of Defence contract for advanced towed artillery guns (ATAGS). On 26 March 2025 the Ministry signed contracts with Bharat Forge for the guns and with Tata Advanced Systems for the towing vehicles, at about Rs 6,900 crore in total.[22] Deliveries wait for a final approval in India. On the August 2026 call management said it expected a delay of a few weeks and that deliveries would start two to three months after the approval.[3] Until then, defence revenue was flat: it was Rs 1,772 crore in FY25 and Rs 1,757 crore in FY26. The June 2026 quarter was better, with KSSL revenue up 71 percent.[6][2]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Forging: shaping hot metal under heavy pressure so that the grain of the metal lines up with the part's shape, which makes it stronger than a cast part.
  • Order book: signed orders not yet delivered. For defence, the company reports the executable order book, which is the part it can deliver under current contracts.
  • Qualified institutional placement (QIP): a share sale to large professional investors such as mutual funds, at a price set by a regulator-linked formula. It raises cash but adds shares, so each existing share owns a smaller slice of the company.
  • Exceptional item: a one-off gain or loss, such as the cost of closing a German plant, that the company reports separately from normal operations.
  • Operating margin: operating profit as a share of revenue. The number most analysts use for this is EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) margin.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The franchise has real strengths. Bharat Forge is India's largest auto-component exporter and one of the world's largest forgers, and it has kept long relationships with global truck and engine makers.[10] It has deep skill in metals and in the heat treatment and machining that follow forging, which is why aircraft engine makers, power-generation engine makers and chip-equipment makers are now qualifying it as a supplier. These approvals take three to five years, according to management, which creates a barrier for new entrants.[3] Two credit raters give the company their second-highest long-term grade, AA+ with a stable outlook, and both raters reaffirmed it in the 2025-26 reporting year.[9][10] Net debt to equity is 0.45 on the company's own measure.[2] The Indian plants earn good margins: 27.5 percent in the parent company in FY26 and 24.4 percent across Indian operations.[4] On 1 October 2026 the Delhi High Court dismissed an appeal by Mr T K Jain against a May 2025 order that set aside an arbitral award in a dispute with the company, so that order stands.[13]

The weaknesses are just as clear. First, results follow the cycle of trucks and cars. Standalone revenue fell 5.1 percent in FY26 because American truck makers cut inventory, and exports to the Americas fell from Rs 3,317 crore to Rs 2,487 crore.[4] Second, overseas plants lose money or barely break even. Europe earned a 2.8 percent margin in the June 2026 quarter, and the United States lost money at the operating line after a press broke down and halted steel production for almost three months.[2][3] Third, the company is capital hungry and keeps tapping investors: it raised Rs 1,650 crore in December 2024 and Rs 2,000 crore in September 2026.[10][19] Fourth, working capital is heavy. The cash conversion cycle was 136 days in FY26 and inventory days were 195.[1][10] Domestic car demand is not the problem: Indian passenger vehicle sales rose 30.7 percent to 1.36 million units in July to September 2026.[21]

Defence: strong order book, slow conversion

Defence is the part of the story where the evidence is clearest and the delay is plainest. The executable order book was Rs 10,961 crore at March 2026 and Rs 11,196 crore at June 2026, so the pipeline is long.[4][2] Revenue, however, has not followed. In May 2025 management guided 15 to 20 percent growth for FY26, and actual defence revenue slipped from Rs 1,772 crore to Rs 1,757 crore.[8][6] In February 2026 it guided 30 to 40 percent growth for FY27. The first quarter helped: KSSL, the defence arm, grew revenue 71 percent to Rs 423 crore. But the big artillery deliveries have not started.[7][2][3] This matters for you because the share price already pays for defence delivery. If the first ATAGS deliveries slip into calendar 2027, the extra year of waiting is a cost that the price does not show.

Aerospace and the new sectors: early and credible

Aerospace revenue is about Rs 400 crore and management says it will double in two years.[3] On 5 October 2026 the company announced a long-term contract with Pratt and Whitney Canada for critical engine components, which supports the claim that approvals are turning into orders.[12] Aerospace was almost 26 percent of non-auto exports in the March 2026 quarter.[5] The starting point is small, though. Rs 400 crore is 2.4 percent of FY26 consolidated revenue of Rs 16,812 crore, so even a doubling adds roughly 2 points of revenue share. Semiconductor and data-centre parts are smaller still, and management could not give a data-centre revenue number.[3][1]

The weak corner: Europe, the United States and electric mobility

The overseas plants are the clearest drag. In the June 2026 quarter the German steel unit BF CDP triggered an exceptional charge of Rs 333.5 crore on overseas operations, and total exceptional items were Rs 358 crore.[2] The German provision itself was Rs 330.4 crore, with Rs 26.7 crore of related costs.[23] Management expects to wind up the unit by the end of calendar 2027 and shift part of its orders to India, and it says the cash outflow comes after 12 months.[3] In electric mobility the company took a Rs 450 crore impairment in the March 2026 quarter and said that adoption has followed a different path from what it first expected.[4][5] These steps are the right ones. They also confirm that earlier capital was spent on businesses that did not earn their cost.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A promise from management is useful only if it can be checked later. We compared what management said in May 2025, February 2026 and May 2026 with what the company filed afterwards. One promise was kept on time, two were met in part, two were missed, one was changed within a quarter, one shows early signs and one is still open. That record is mixed, and it tells you to give the next set of promises less weight until they show up in a quarterly result.

Capital allocation also needs a verdict. The company raised Rs 1,650 crore through a QIP in December 2024 and spent Rs 1,030 crore of it on repaying debt and Rs 549 crore on buying American Axle's Indian assets, which is a use that matches what was said.[10] In September 2026 it raised Rs 2,000 crore at Rs 1,890 a share, a 2.96 percent discount to the floor price, for capex of about Rs 1,800 crore that management said would start earning after 12 to 18 months.[19][2] Three months earlier the plan for capex was Rs 800 to Rs 850 crore. Management explained the rise by saying demand is coming faster than capacity.[5][3] That may prove right. It also means the funding need more than doubled in one quarter. The September 2026 sale added 2.2 percent to the share count, and the December 2024 sale lifted equity capital from Rs 93 crore to Rs 96 crore.[19][1]

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a weak joint. This chapter names the joint, says what evidence would show it has failed and says when we will know. The point is to be specific enough that the next quarterly result can prove us wrong.

Our FY27 figure of Rs 29.2 assumes the parent company's margin returns to about 27 percent and overseas losses shrink as the German unit winds down. If the 160 basis point cost hit of the June quarter proves to be permanent, the parent margin stays near 26 percent and parent-company EBITDA is about Rs 145 crore lower for the year, or roughly Rs 2 a share.[3] Management said the margin per tonne will come back, but also that reported margin will improve only gradually because price increases raise revenue and cost together.[3] The chairman told CNBC-TV18 on 10 August that margins should recover to 27 to 28 percent in the second quarter, which is a firmer claim than the call's gradual wording.[24] We will know on 5 November 2026 from the September quarter, and again on the December quarter in February 2027.[14]

The base value uses 59.6 times trailing earnings. That is the 25th percentile of the last three years, but the three years included a period when profit grew about 30 percent a year.
