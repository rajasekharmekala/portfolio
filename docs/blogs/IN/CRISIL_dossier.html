1. What the company does

Crisil was founded in 1987 as India's first credit rating agency. A credit rating is an opinion on how likely a borrower is to repay its debt in full and on time, and companies that borrow from banks or sell bonds pay an agency to publish one. Crisil is today a listed company of which S&P Global, the American ratings and data group, owns 66.64 percent through three of its entities. The stake has not moved in twelve quarters and none of it is pledged[17][16][23]. Its financial year is the calendar year, so the quarter ended 30 September 2026 is its third quarter of 2026. Some data feeds call that quarter "Q2 FY27", which is the April-to-March convention of most Indian companies and does not apply here[44][1]. The group employs more than 5,000 professionals and serves over 11,400 clients in 40 countries[7].

The company reports two segments. Ratings Services, 28 percent of 2025 turnover, covers bond and bank loan ratings in India and the Global Analytical Center, a team that does back-end analytical work for S&P Global Ratings[48][11]. Research, Analytics and Solutions, the other 72 percent, is itself three businesses. Crisil Coalition Greenwich sells benchmarking data to global banks, so a bank can see how its trading or lending revenue compares with peers. Crisil Integral IQ supplies risk, regulatory and credit-lending staff and tools to banks and asset managers. Crisil Intelligence publishes industry research, data and consulting in India[3][7]. In nine months of 2026 revenue was ₹3,228.2 crore, of which Ratings Services contributed ₹973.4 crore (30 percent) and Research, Analytics and Solutions ₹2,256.4 crore[1][3].

The two segments make money in different ways. In ratings, the issuer pays: a fee when the rating is first given, and a yearly fee for keeping watch on it afterwards. The accounts book part of the first fee over the following 11 months and the rest when the rating is awarded, so a good year for new ratings feeds the next year's income as well[10]. In research and analytics, banks pay annual subscriptions for benchmark data, hourly or monthly fees for staff on risk projects, and project fees for consulting. About 13 percent of 2025 revenue came from S&P group companies, mostly for the Global Analytical Center work, which is priced at cost plus a fixed margin set under transfer-pricing rules[10][7].

The profit mix is far more lopsided than the revenue mix. In the nine months, Ratings Services earned a segment profit of ₹453.3 crore on a 46.6 percent margin, while Research, Analytics and Solutions earned ₹453.4 crore on a 20.1 percent margin[3]. So the 30 percent of revenue that is ratings produces half of the segment profit. That matters for everything that follows: the ratings margin is high and steady, whereas the research margin depends on how many hours of paid staff time the company can sell and at what price. People are the main cost, at 53.3 percent of 2025 revenue[10].

How the money flows: ratings fees, subscriptions and billable time

Think of the ratings business as a toll road with two kinds of toll. The first is paid when a borrower needs a new rating, for example before a company sells bonds or takes a bank loan above the size at which banks ask for one. The second is paid every year on every active rating, so the larger the stock of ratings, the larger the yearly income that arrives whether or not the borrower issues anything new. Crisil reported more than 1,280 new bank loan ratings in 2025 and more than 7,200 active ratings outstanding on companies[11]. Management said on its April 2026 call that surveillance fees, the second toll, were the main driver of ratings growth that quarter, and that they follow the new-rating business of the two years before[7].

The research side has more moving parts. Benchmarking subscriptions are booked evenly over the contract period. Staff-time contracts are booked as hours are worked. Software and consulting are booked as work is completed[10]. Because each pattern has its own rhythm, quarters in this segment can move for timing reasons. In April 2026 the finance chief said that renewals closed earlier than usual had added about USD 4.5 million to the first quarter and would even out over the year, which is what the later quarters showed[7][3].

For you as an owner, the practical meaning is that annual figures describe this company better than any single quarter. Management says the same, and the calendar-year reporting cycle makes the fourth quarter the largest for the research segment: fourth-quarter 2025 research margin was 26.2 percent against 21.9 percent in the third quarter of that year[6][3]. The next subsection looks at who the customers are, because concentration decides how safe those fees are.

Who pays: Indian borrowers, global banks and S&P itself

Ratings customers are Indian companies, banks, non-bank lenders and infrastructure issuers. The agency has the largest share of fresh bond ratings in the country: of 725 new rated issuances in the year to March 2026, Crisil rated 339, or 46.8 percent, ahead of ICRA at 20.1 percent and India Ratings at 19.3 percent[35]. Research customers are mostly the world's largest banks and asset managers, and Indian banks and mutual funds for the local research products[7].

The related-party note in the audited accounts lists services sold to S&P group entities. Adding every "professional services rendered" line gives ₹475.5 crore for 2025, or 13.0 percent of revenue from operations, of which one entity, Standard & Poor's Financial Services LLC, accounts for ₹253.5 crore, or 6.9 percent[10]. This is a stable, cost-plus stream, but it means one-eighth of revenue depends on the parent's choices about where to place back-office work. Beyond the parent, management does not disclose any customer concentration figure, and the 11,400-plus client count suggests no single outside customer is large[7].

The weakest link in the customer picture is the discretionary spending of global banks. Coalition Greenwich and Integral IQ both sell services a bank can postpone, and Integral IQ's performance was described as muted in 2024 for exactly that reason[9]. Management argues that volatility makes banks want more insight, not less, and that it has diversified into regional banks, wealth management and private markets[7]. Chapter 2 weighs how much of that argument the numbers support.

A calendar-year company with a seasonal rhythm

Crisil's quarters follow a pattern that looks odd to anyone who expects steady growth. Profit after tax in the four quarters from September 2025 was ₹193 crore, ₹242 crore, ₹233 crore and ₹216 crore, then ₹205 crore in September 2026[23]. The fourth quarter has been the strongest in each of the last three years: net profit rose 25 percent from the third to the fourth quarter in 2025, from ₹193.1 crore to ₹241.5 crore[5][1].

This matters because the share price rose 3.68 percent in the week into the results[50], and many readers will compare the latest quarter with the one before it. Crisil's own advice is to judge the business over a year. We follow that advice in chapter 3, where the figures are annual, and use the quarterly series only to ask whether the trend is improving or fading. Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Credit rating: an opinion, written by an agency, on how likely a borrower is to repay on time. Higher ratings mean lower borrowing cost for the issuer.
  • Surveillance fee: the yearly fee an agency earns for keeping a rating up to date after it is first assigned.
  • Bank loan rating (BLR): a rating on a company's bank borrowings, required for larger loans under bank capital rules.
  • Global Analytical Center (GAC): Crisil's team that does analytical and operating work for S&P Global Ratings at a cost-plus margin.
  • Segment profit: the profit of one business before shared costs such as unallocated depreciation and other income.
  • Calendar-year reporting: Crisil's fiscal year ends on 31 December, so its third quarter ends on 30 September.
  • Related party: a company under common control, here the S&P Global group, whose dealings with Crisil are disclosed separately.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when customers keep paying it even though they could stop, and when rivals cannot easily copy what it does. By that test Crisil's ratings franchise is strong and its research franchise is good but more exposed. The ratings agency has a regulatory licence, a 46.8 percent share of fresh ratings, a brand that investors ask for by name and an annuity of yearly fees on more than 7,200 active ratings[35][11]. The research arm owns benchmark data that banks use to measure themselves, and Crisil won 26 independent analyst recognitions in 2025, 20 of them from one research house (Chartis)[5]. Its weakness is that it sells time and advice to clients whose budgets move with markets.

Ratings: a licence-protected annuity with a margin near 45 percent

The Securities and Exchange Board of India licenses rating agencies, and only eight were registered at March 2026, of which three are listed (CARE Ratings, ICRA and Crisil Ratings)[35]. Companies that issue listed bonds need a rating, and Indian banks ask larger borrowers for one before lending. That requirement creates the demand, and the licence limits the number of suppliers. Within it, Crisil won 339 of the 725 fresh ratings assigned in 2025-26, so almost every second new rating went to it[35]. Management attributes the share to investor preference for what it calls best-in-class assessments, a claim that is hard to test directly but is consistent with the share figure[1].

The numbers show how little the segment depends on any one quarter of bond issuance. Corporate bond issuance by value fell 6.1 percent in the first nine months of 2026 and the number of issuers fell 7.2 percent, yet Ratings Services revenue rose 23.6 percent and segment profit rose 26.9 percent in the same period[1][3]. The gap is explained by bank loan ratings (bank credit grew 19.1 percent by August 2026), by yearly surveillance fees earned on ratings assigned in earlier years, and by growing support work for S&P Global Ratings[1][7]. Management warned in April 2026 that if bond yields rose further the bond market would suffer, but that corporate borrowing has historically swung between bank loans and bonds, with no lasting shift[7].

What would damage the franchise is regulation that loosens the licence, or a rating failure that costs trust. SEBI tightened the rules in February 2026 for agencies that rate instruments overseen by other regulators, requiring separate grievance channels and disclosures. The cost to Crisil looks small, though the circular signals that the regulator is watching how agencies expand their scope[37]. The S&P connection also works as a reputational anchor, which is hard to measure.

Research, Analytics and Solutions: a benchmark franchise with a people-cost problem

This segment is larger by revenue (₹2,256.4 crore of ₹3,229.8 crore in nine months) and earns about half the margin of ratings[3][2]. The best part of it is Coalition Greenwich, whose data on how global investment banks earn revenue and spend capital is used by the banks themselves, so each new subscriber makes the benchmark more complete. The acquisition of McKinsey PriceMetrix for USD 38 million (about ₹335 crore) extends that model to wealth managers in the United States and Canada, with a database that covers USD 8 trillion of client assets[20][21].

The weaker part is the time-and-materials work in Integral IQ. Here the company is paid for people, not for data, and clients can cut budgets when markets are uncertain. Total employee cost was ₹1,943.3 crore in 2025, or 53.3 percent of revenue, against 54.1 percent in 2024, so there is no sign yet that artificial intelligence tools have lowered the cost of delivering a unit of work[10][14]. The segment's quarterly margin ranged from 17.2 percent to 26.2 percent across the last five quarters, which tells you the margin depends on utilisation and timing as much as on pricing[3][6][7].

Management's answer is that artificial intelligence makes proprietary data and regulatory credibility more valuable, because generic analysis becomes cheap while trusted analysis does not. On the April 2026 call it also said Crisil sells the same kind of work at the same margins to S&P, and that clients in regulated banking want help adopting AI responsibly[7]. That is plausible for the data products, but not yet proven for the staff-time work, which is why the research segment's margin is a Layer 2 test in chapter 2B.

What could erode the strength: pricing, AI and the parent's choices

Three forces could narrow the advantage. The first is pricing. Management said it prices "in a disciplined manner" but declined to share a five-year price trend, so we cannot separate volume from price in the ratings growth[7]. The second is artificial intelligence: a bank that can ask a language model to draft a credit report may buy fewer hours from an outsourced team. The company lists both the arrival of stronger AI models and slow client adoption as risks, and it treats AI mainly as an opportunity[7].

The third is the parent. The Global Analytical Center is paid on a transfer-pricing basis, and its volume depends on how much work S&P chooses to place in India. The same relationship that gives Crisil a steady 13 percent of revenue can also move work elsewhere, and the margin is not disclosed[10][7]. The positive side is that S&P's own Ratings division reported that its non-transaction revenue grew 4 percent in the third quarter of 2026 on annual fees and "Crisil", a sign that Crisil's contribution is still growing[36].

Taken together, the franchise is strong where it is licensed and annuity-like, and acceptable where it competes for discretionary bank budgets. The scorecard in chapter 2B converts those judgments into five tests with explicit thresholds, then checks whether management's past promises were kept.

What the share price pays for each rupee of profit

Valuation answers a simple question: how many years of today's profit does the market pay for the company? The first measure is the price-to-earnings ratio, the share price divided by the last twelve months of earnings per share. A second measure is the free cash flow yield. Free cash flow is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment, and the yield is that cash divided by the market value. 5. What could happen next

Chapter 4 asked what the chart says. This chapter asks what the price already assumes, how analysts are changing their numbers, where we differ from them, and what the stock could be worth in three cases. A reverse discounted cash flow (reverse DCF) works backwards: instead of forecasting cash flows to find a price, it takes today's price and solves for the yearly growth in cash flow, over ten years, that would justify it at a 12 percent required return and a terminal value of 20 times cash flow. If the growth the price demands is far above what the company has ever done, the price is expensive. If it is below what the company can plausibly do, the price is cheap.

Our variant perception

Our view differs from the Street's in one place and agrees in another. For 2026 we agree: our estimate of ₹125.2 is within 0.4 percent of the Street's ₹125.65. Both depend on the usual fourth-quarter lift of 25 percent or more over the third quarter. For 2027 we are cautious: ₹138 against ₹148.15. The Street needs 17.9 percent earnings growth, close to the pace of the nine months of 2026 (profit +24.7 percent) when the first quarter was boosted by timing and foreign exchange. We think the more recent evidence, a research margin of 17.2 percent and operating margin down from 31 percent to 28 percent in three quarters, deserves more weight[3][23].

Where we are more constructive is the multiple. The Street's average target of ₹5,142 divided by its own 2027 estimate is 34.7 times, below the 38.3 times at which the stock has traded for the last year. We assume the multiple holds at 38.3 times, the one-year median, rather than falling. The difference is a bet that a business with a 33 percent return on capital, no debt and a licensed ratings annuity keeps earning a premium to its Indian peers (27.4 times for CARE and 22.2 for ICRA) so long as profit grows at 10 percent or more. If growth stays below 10 percent, the premium is hard to defend, and the bear case in the table takes it down to 33 times.

Put simply, the setup is a reasonable business at a full price, where the Street may be about 7 percent too high on 2027 earnings and about right on the multiple. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions, and the honest question is which of them is weakest and when we would know. This chapter states the red-team bear case against our own view, one claim at a time, then names the single claim on which the verdict hinges.

The 2027 earnings estimate of ₹138: the research margin is the weak link

We assume the research segment earns a 21 percent margin in 2027 on revenue up 8 percent. The weakest part is the margin. It was 26.2 percent in the fourth quarter of 2025, 22.7 percent in the first quarter of 2026, about 20.4 percent in the second and 17.2 percent in the third, so the direction is down by about three points a quarter, even after allowing for seasonality[6][7][2][3]. If the third-quarter level is the new normal, the 2027 estimate falls to roughly ₹125 and the whole entry case changes. The best counter-argument is that the third quarter in 2025 had a larger foreign-exchange gain, and that depreciation from the PriceMetrix intangibles will stop growing once a full year is in the base[3][23].

We will know on fourth-quarter results in mid-February 2027, when the full-year research margin is published. Last year the fourth quarter ran 4.3 points above the third. If it does not rise by at least 3 points this year, the downward risk to our estimate becomes the central case. The date is not yet announced, and the October meeting was notified on 1 October, about three weeks ahead[49][38].

The multiple: 38.3 times is a premium that needs growth to defend

We hold the multiple at the one-year median of 38.3 times. The weakest point is that the median covers a year in which the multiple ranged from 33.8 to 48.1, and the range was wide because investors were unsure what growth to pay for[25]. The Reserve Bank raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50 percent and adopted a calibrated tightening stance[1]. Crisil's ratings head said on the April 2026 call that rate rises do not favour the bond market because yields harden further[7]. That a higher rate also compresses valuation multiples is our own inference, not a Crisil statement.

Evidence will come from three places: the results of ICRA and CARE Ratings over the next fortnight (their price-to-earnings multiples of 22.2 and 27.4 show what the market pays for pure ratings), the Reserve Bank's next policy meeting, and Crisil's own fourth-quarter profit growth. A multiple of 33 times on ₹138 gives ₹4,554, close to the top of our zone. At that value the gain-to-loss ratio at ₹4,545.00 is only 0.02, against 2.00 at 38.3 times. The zone top is therefore a bet on the multiple holding, and a pullback to the zone does not settle that question.

Artificial intelligence and the staff-time model

The red-team case is that Integral IQ, which sells staff time, is the type of business whose pricing generative AI can erode: a bank can ask a model to draft credit reports, so clients buy fewer hours or negotiate lower rates. Management says the work is regulated, domain-heavy and increasingly AI-assisted, and that AI opens doors in mid-tier banks[7]. The evidence so far is thin in both directions: employee cost was 53.3 percent of revenue in 2025 against 54.1 percent in 2024, and research margin fell in the third quarter, so no productivity gain has appeared in the cost ratios[10][14][3].

We will see the cost-ratio answer in the annual report for 2026, due between February and April 2027. If the employee cost ratio is above 54 percent despite AI tools, the bear case gains weight. If it falls below 52 percent with stable revenue, the bull case does.

Concentration on S&P and a handful of global banks

About 13 percent of 2025 revenue came from S&P group companies, priced at cost plus a margin that is not disclosed, and the top global banks drive Coalition Greenwich[10][7]. A shift in S&P's offshoring policy, or a downturn in global bank budgets, would hit both at once. The hinge for this risk is S&P's own commentary on Crisil: in the third quarter of 2026 S&P reported that its non-transaction revenue, which includes the Crisil contribution, grew 4 percent, and that the 2026 growth will face tougher comparisons in the fourth quarter[36].
