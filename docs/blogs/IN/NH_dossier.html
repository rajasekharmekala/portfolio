1. What the company does

Narayana Hrudayalaya (Narayana Health, ticker NH) is a hospital group founded in 2000 in Bengaluru by the heart surgeon Dr Devi Prasad Shetty. Its idea was to treat large numbers of patients at low prices by running every hospital as efficiently as possible. On 1 July 2026 the group had 55 healthcare facilities: 20 hospitals and heart centres in India with 5,461 operational beds, 20 clinics and dialysis centres, two hospitals in the Cayman Islands with 164 beds, and 13 facilities in the United Kingdom with 330 beds. About 20,950 people work there, of whom about 12,900 are clinical staff.[4]

Three kinds of customer pay the bills. In the Indian hospitals, 43 percent of operating revenue came from patients who walk in and pay themselves, 33 percent from insured patients (including corporate and public-sector cover), 18 percent from government schemes such as the Central Government Health Scheme, and 5 percent from international patients, in the June 2026 quarter.[4] Cardiac care is the largest specialty at 33 percent of India hospital revenue, followed by oncology (cancer care) at 16 percent and medicine and gastroenterology at 13 percent. About 73 percent of the Indian hospital revenue comes from hospitals that Narayana owns outright on freehold land.[4]

A hospital sells beds that are in use, so the key measure is ARPOB, which is average revenue per occupied bed, the money a used bed earns. ARPOB for the India hospitals was ₹19.2 million in the June 2026 quarter against ₹17.6 million a year earlier, a rise of about 9 percent.[4] The rise came from more complex work, such as robotic heart surgery (about 100 cases a month at the Bangalore heart hospital) and bone marrow transplants, and from a better mix of paying patients, not from price rises, which management says are in the low to mid single digits.[11]

The business now has three engines of very different quality. India hospitals are the proven engine. In the June 2026 quarter India earned ₹13,247 million of revenue, Cayman ₹5,514 million and the United Kingdom ₹8,257 million, so India was about 49 percent of the three-region total before inter-company eliminations.[5] The UK arm is Practice Plus Group, bought on 6 November 2025 for about £188.8 million, of which about 95 percent of revenue came from the state-funded National Health Service when it was acquired.[14][15][10] Cayman has a profitable hospital and a loss-making insurance company, and India has a loss-making clinic and insurance programme. How the money is made and spent

In the June 2026 quarter the group spent ₹5,143 million on medical consumables (19.2 percent of revenue), ₹3,270 million on fees to doctors, ₹7,248 million on other employees and ₹6,478 million on other running costs, which together make ₹22,139 million. Revenue of ₹26,836 million less those costs is ₹4,697 million. Adding ₹355 million of other income gives earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization of ₹5,052 million, or 18.8 percent of revenue. The 18.8 percent therefore includes other income.[4] That figure is called EBITDA. In India hospitals the biggest cost is people: doctors and nurses are about 28 percent of revenue, consumables 23 percent, other expenses 17 percent and rent or revenue share 2 percent.[4]

Depreciation (₹1,561 million) and finance costs (₹868 million) in the same quarter were each close to double the year-earlier amounts, which were ₹843 million and ₹452 million.[4] This matters for the investment case. The UK purchase was paid for mostly with borrowed money, so it adds interest and the cost of writing down newly bought assets before it adds much profit. Profit after tax for the quarter rose only 5.7 percent while EBITDA rose 40.0 percent.[5]

How the UK deal was paid for explains the debt chapter. The cost was about £190 million, of which about £40 million came as equity from the Cayman subsidiary's own cash and £150 million as a seven-year loan from HSBC and ICICI Bank in the UK, serviced from the UK business.[13][3] An analyst on the November 2025 call put the price at about 9.2 times the target's EBITDA, and management did not dispute the figure.[13]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ARPOB: average revenue per occupied bed. How much a bed in use earns for a period. It rises with complex surgery and falls with discounted work.
  • ALOS: average length of stay in days. A shorter stay frees the bed for the next patient, so it raises revenue per bed. It was 4.3 days in the June 2026 quarter, and management wants 3.9 to 4.0.[10]
  • NHS: the National Health Service, the state-funded health system of the United Kingdom. It pays most of the UK arm's bills at fixed prices.
  • CGHS: the Central Government Health Scheme, which pays hospitals fixed package rates for serving central government staff and pensioners.
  • Net debt is borrowings minus cash and investments. It is the figure the company quotes (0.42 times equity at 30 June 2026), and chapter 3 shows why the debt test uses gross borrowings instead. Lease liabilities are the rent promises on leased buildings, which carry interest like loans.[4]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The Indian hospital business is strong, and the evidence is in the margin. India hospital EBITDA was ₹3,229 million in the June 2026 quarter, a margin of 24.8 percent, against ₹2,309 million and 20.6 percent a year earlier.[4] For the full year to March 2026 the India hospital margin was 23.1 percent against 20.7 percent in FY25.[6] Management credits three things: more high-end procedures, a better mix of paying patients, and efficiency work that cut the length of stay and let each bed treat more people.[11][10] Importantly, the gain came without adding beds. An analyst put the gap since the last bed addition at seven to eight years, management agreed that it had added none, and the vice chairman said in May 2026 that Narayana is the only listed group that has not added a single bed since listing. Every extra rupee came from using the same beds better.[10][11]

That is also the weakness. A business that grows only by squeezing more out of fixed beds runs into a ceiling, and the ceiling is close. The new beds are late: the Kolkata (Rajarhat) hospital moved from a FY28 to a FY29 opening, the Central Bangalore lease also moved to FY29 and the South Bangalore hospital moved from FY29 to FY30 between the May 2026 and August 2026 presentations.[6][4] Meanwhile the whole industry is building. ICRA, a credit-rating agency, expects eleven listed hospital chains and two large unlisted ones to add about 14,500 beds in FY26 and FY27, at a cost of ₹30,000 to ₹32,000 crore, which is about 26 percent more beds than they had at the end of FY25.[17][18] Jefferies, a broker, expects Max Healthcare and Apollo each to add more than 1,000 beds over the next 12 to 18 months and expects ARPOB growth of mid-single digits at best in calendar 2026.[19]

The competitive position is therefore good in cardiac and transplant work in Bangalore and ordinary in the rest. Management said in February 2026 that many new hospitals are opening in the Sarjapur area and North Bangalore, and that competition from nearby hospitals in northern India had already cost some volume.[12] Narayana's own reasoning is that its low prices and efficiency keep it competitive. That reasoning is tested by the numbers in chapter 3, which show it earns a lower margin than the best-run peers (19 percent on trailing twelve months against 27 percent at Max Healthcare) while carrying far more debt.[1][24]

India hospitals: what is strong

Cardiac sciences are 33 percent of India hospital revenue, and the group says it does the largest volume of robotic cardiac surgery in the country, about 100 cases a month, far more than many competing hospitals do.[4][11][12] It also does a very large number of paediatric bone marrow transplants. These are procedures where patients choose the surgeon, not the building, which supports both price and volume.[11]

The second strength is cost. Management reports that India doctors and nurses cost 28 percent of revenue and consumables 23 percent, both flat or lower than a year ago while revenue per patient grew.[4] The group also owns most of its Indian hospital land (about 73 percent of revenue comes from owned hospitals), so it does not pay rising rent.[4]

Cash flow confirms the quality of the Indian core. Cash from operations at the group was ₹1,621 crore in FY26 against ₹986 crore in FY25, and 99 percent of operating profit (₹1,621 crore against ₹1,641 crore), even after the UK was added for five months. Screener's own cash-conversion line, which divides cash from operations before tax by operating profit, shows 108 percent.[1] That is the signature of a business that collects its bills.

Cayman: a good hospital and a loss-making insurer

The Cayman hospitals earned US$44.8 million in the June 2026 quarter, up 5.1 percent on a year earlier, a seasonally soft quarter.[4][10] The insurance arm, Cayman Integrated Healthcare, wrote US$15.4 million of revenue against US$5.3 million a year earlier but lost US$3.7 million at the EBITDA level.[4] Management said in February 2026 that the insurer's focus would move from growing the book to improving underwriting, and in August 2026 that all of the contracts up for renewal in July were renewed at higher prices.[12][10] The insurer's EBITDA loss fell from US$5.1 million in the March 2026 quarter to US$3.7 million in the June 2026 quarter.[6][4]

United Kingdom: a bought turnaround that has not yet turned

Practice Plus Group had £229 million of revenue in its last full year and an expected EBITDA of about £20 million before the deal, according to the trade publication Healthcare Today.[15] Under Narayana it earned £65.0 million of revenue and an EBITDA margin of 8.8 percent in the June 2026 quarter, against £67.6 million and 10.5 percent in the March 2026 quarter.[4] Management blamed a severe UK heatwave that cost about 70 days of operating capacity and told investors the worst should be behind it.[10] After financing the loan and writing down newly valued assets, the UK business made a small loss for the group: a profit after tax of minus £0.7 million in the March 2026 quarter and minus £1.9 million for the five months to March 2026.[6]

About 95 percent of the business's revenue came from the NHS when it was bought, against about 70 percent at the closest UK peer. The plan is to raise private-pay work over four to five years and to cut costs with Narayana's software.[10][13] On the November 2025 call the head of the international business said that, like for like, private patients pay 20 to 30 percent, and sometimes 35 percent, more than the NHS.[13] That plan is plausible and slow. It will not lift group returns in the next few quarters, and management itself says it is too early to measure the UK return on capital.[10]

Integrated care: clinics and insurance in India

Narayana is building small clinics and a health insurer to bring patients to its hospitals. The clinics see outpatient visits equal to about 30 percent of the hospitals' outpatient footfall, and they feed referrals for complex surgery.[10] They cost money. The clinic and insurance units together lost ₹674 million of EBITDA in FY26 and the insurer lost ₹201 million after tax in the June 2026 quarter alone, against ₹60 million a year earlier, with claims at 219 percent of earned premium.[6][4] Management's own words are that this is early, the book is small and a few large claims can move the result.[10]

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A hospital group is judged on four things. How much does each bed earn? How much of that turns into margin? How much new capacity can it open? Does the cash back the profit? We picked five KPIs for that reason. They are India revenue per occupied bed with length of stay, the India hospital margin, the group margin once the new ventures are included, delivery of new capacity against plan, and cash conversion with return on capital. ROCE, which is return on capital employed, is the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses.[4][6] The filings give discharges, ARPOB and length of stay, but no occupancy percentage. So we left occupancy out rather than estimate it.

The rule in the scoring file is simple. Each KPI scores one point for PASS, half a point for WATCH and nothing for FAIL. The company qualifies when the total is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 and no more than one KPI fails.[29] Narayana scores exactly 3.0 with one FAIL. It qualifies, but with no margin for error. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management is open with numbers and candid on calls, and it delivers on the core business. Its record on timing is weaker. We checked what it said about capital spending, project dates, new-venture losses and the debt ceiling. We compared each statement with the outcome one to two years later.[9][6][4][12]

Capital spending is where plans and outcomes differ most. In May 2025 the company planned ₹9,100 million for greenfield projects and acquisitions in FY25. It spent ₹5,145 million. It planned ₹1,350 million for brownfield expansion and spent ₹159 million.[9] For FY26 it planned ₹4,240 million and spent ₹1,089 million, a quarter of the plan. Separately, it paid ₹22,010 million for the UK business, which was not in the plan.[6][3] Management explains the higher cost per bed by construction prices that are 60 percent above those of five years ago. It blames some project delays on licensing at partner-run sites.[11][10]

Overall, management scores well where it controls the outcome: margins, cash and the debt ceiling. It scores poorly where it depends on others: approvals, partners and insurance claims. Two lessons follow for an investor. Discount any stated opening date by a year. Assume the loss-making ventures will take longer to turn than promised.[4][12]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue grew from ₹3,701 crore in FY22 to ₹7,896 crore in FY26. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could fail. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, the evidence that would show it wrong, and the date on which we will know. Then it states the strongest case against our own view, and picks the one claim on which the verdict hinges.[10][11]

Weakest assumption in the profit estimates

Our FY27 figure assumes that the March 2026 quarter's profit of ₹10.96 per share is a fair run-rate for the next three quarters. That quarter included Cayman and India at normal strength and the UK at a 10.5 percent margin. The June quarter shows the UK at 8.8 percent, the insurer's loss growing in India and costs from new centres still to come.[6][4][11] If the September quarter profit is below about ₹2,000 million (about ₹9.8 per share), our FY27 estimate is too high and the bear case gains weight. The evidence arrives with the September 2026 results on 30 October 2026.[1]

Weakest assumption in the value

The central value assumes that the stock deserves its three-year median P/E of 41.3 on average profit. That is the median of a period when the company had low debt and a 24 percent margin. Today it has much more debt and a lower margin, so a lower multiple is plausible. Peers trade at 52 to 57 times, which supports the multiple, but those peers carry less debt.[2][23][24][25][26] If the multiple settles at 35 times, the central value is ₹1,619 and the stock offers no reward at the close. The evidence is the market's response to each of the next two results. The size of this risk is large. Moving only the multiple from 41.3 to 30.1, with our central profit unchanged, gives ₹1,392, a fall of 15.5 percent from the close. That is why the target and stop in this report are reference scenarios, not executable recommendations.

Layer 3 is the timing and size step. It asks how far the price is from support and how much of the portfolio a position may take. The size here is zero for two reasons. The verdict is REJECTED, and no order exists. A conviction tier is a size bracket from TIER_1 (the largest) to TIER_4, and TIER_0 means no position.
