1. What the company does

Tata Technologies Limited is an engineering services company based in Pune. Carmakers, truck makers, aircraft firms and heavy-machinery makers pay it to design, test and build their products, and to run the software that holds a product's data together.[3][2] It had 12,895 people at 30 September 2026, and its shares trade on the NSE as TATATECH and on the BSE as 544028.[2][1] Its controlling shareholder is Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles Limited, which holds 53.34 percent, and Tata Capital holds another 1.84 percent, so the promoter group owns 55.17 percent.[17]

The customers fall into two groups. The first is the Tata Motors group, which the company calls its anchor customers: Jaguar Land Rover (JLR), Tata Motors Commercial Vehicles and Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles.[4] The chief financial officer told shareholders that about 54.4 percent of services revenue in FY26 came from them.[12] That share fell to 48.9 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[5] The second group is everyone else: European, American and Japanese carmakers, parts suppliers, Airbus and other aerospace firms, and heavy-machinery makers.[4][5] In plain terms, the biggest customer is also the owner. That is a comfort when work is plentiful and a risk when the owner's own projects slow down.

The company earns money in two ways. Services is the larger part: ₹1,356 crore of the ₹1,699 crore of revenue in the September 2026 quarter, or 80 percent. Engineers bill their time or deliver a fixed-price package, such as the design of a whole car.[3][30] Technology Solutions is the other 20 percent, ₹343 crore. It resells third-party software, builds enterprise systems and runs education and training projects for customers that include state governments.[3][1][15]

Four margin terms run through this report. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is the profit from selling the work before interest, tax and the wear on equipment are counted. The company reports it without other income. The gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of the people and subcontractors on a job, as a share of revenue. It was 27.9 percent in the September 2026 quarter: 30.1 percent in Services and 19.1 percent in Technology Solutions.[3] The operating margin is the share of revenue left after all running costs, and the EBITDA margin of 16.1 percent is the closest match the company reports. The net margin is the share of revenue left as profit after tax, which was 10.1 percent in the quarter against 12.5 percent a year earlier.[3]

Where the work is done and who runs it

The company works from 20 delivery centres in the US, Europe, India, China and South-East Asia.[15] Only 35 percent of Services revenue in the September 2026 quarter was delivered offshore, meaning from India or Romania, against 41 percent a year earlier.[3] The fall coincides with the purchase of a German firm and with full-vehicle programmes that need engineers near the customer. That is our reading, because the company gives no split of the change. Offshore work is cheaper to deliver, so a lower offshore share makes the margin target harder.

Warren Harris is chief executive and managing director. Uttam Gujrati became chief financial officer on 31 December 2025, replacing Savitha Balachandran.[13] The margin promise made in May 2026, which Chapter 2B tests, was therefore given under the current finance chief.[6]

Three newer building blocks

The first is BMW TechWorks India, a joint venture with BMW that began operating in the December 2024 quarter, when the company's share of profit was just ₹0.56 crore.[9] The company books its share of profit in one line because it does not control the venture. That share was ₹11.4 crore in the September 2026 quarter, up 115 percent on a year earlier, and with ₹8.3 crore of deferred income the venture added ₹19.7 crore.[4] The venture had passed 2,000 engineers by the June 2026 quarter.[5]

The second is ES-Tec Group of Germany, bought on 27 November 2025 for a total consideration of ₹684.06 crore, of which ₹406.72 crore was booked as goodwill.[15] ES-Tec does automotive driver-assistance and connected-car work, mainly for Volkswagen. Management says demand there is subdued, particularly from its largest German customer, and expects some added softness in the December quarter.[4] Goldman Sachs estimates ES-Tec at about 10 percent of revenue.[21]

The third is diversification beyond cars. In the June 2026 quarter aerospace revenue was about USD 10.2 million (up 38.1 percent on a year earlier) and industrial heavy machinery about USD 15 million, together about 18 percent of Services revenue of USD 136.6 million.[5] Aerospace revenue grew eight times over four fiscal years from a small start.[10] Aerospace programmes last 8 to 10 years, which would make revenue steadier, but the line is still small.[4]

Chart and valuation terms used in later chapters

Share-price charts are read through three averages and two gauges. A 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days. The 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average use 50 and 200 days, so they move more slowly and are the lines prices tend to bounce off. The RSI (relative strength index) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been moving, and a reading below 30 is called oversold. The ATR (average true range) is the usual size of one day's price swing, in rupees.

Our chart stages follow Stan Weinstein's scale. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend. Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend, with the price under a falling 50-day average that sits under a falling 200-day average. Stage 1 is the flat floor between a decline and a new advance. A base is that sideways stretch of price, the pivot is the top of it, and a breakout is a close above the pivot on heavy trading.

Three terms describe a plan to buy. An entry zone is the band of prices where we would buy. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, so it does not chase the market. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and we sell. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain we expect divided by the loss we risk: the target minus the entry price, divided by the entry price minus the stop.

Seven money terms appear in the numbers chapters. EPS (earnings per share) is the year's profit divided by the number of shares. ROCE (return on capital employed) is operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself collects, before spending on equipment and before paying owners. Capital expenditure is money spent on buildings, computers and other long-lived assets. Working capital is the cash tied up in unpaid customer bills and unfinished work, less what the company owes suppliers.

Four more terms judge the balance sheet and the forecasts. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Net debt is borrowings minus cash, and a negative figure means the company holds more cash than debt. The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share, the number of rupees paid for each rupee of yearly profit. Guidance is a company's own forecast for the coming period, and a consensus estimate is the average of the forecasts that outside analysts publish.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when customers keep coming back and rivals cannot easily copy what it does. Tata Technologies has one clear strength, which is that it can take responsibility for a whole vehicle. It has three clear weaknesses: it depends on one customer family, its profit margin has fallen, and its recent profit growth has trailed its sales growth. This chapter weighs each with evidence, and Chapter 2B turns the result into scores.

Strength: it builds whole vehicles

Most engineering-services firms sell a slice of the work, such as one electronic system or one test programme. Tata Technologies sells the whole vehicle. Its June 2026 investor deck lists 37 or more full-vehicle programmes, 250 or more vehicle programmes over 30 years and 15 or more battery-electric programmes.[10] In the September 2026 quarter it won a USD 138 million full-vehicle programme for a luxury European carmaker, spanning two vehicle platforms, and a North American commercial-vehicle maker chose it to lead a new vehicle platform.[2][4]

We checked the USD 138 million claim at its source. It is in the company's press release and investor deck of 14 October 2026 and in the earnings call.[2][3][4] The customer is unnamed because of a confidentiality agreement, and the chief executive says only that it works in the premium car market. It is a car programme, not an aerospace one, which corrects the label on the watchlist note that first listed it. The programme runs about two and a half years, begins to add revenue in the second half of FY27, and followed pre-work that the company did to show the customer it could meet an aggressive timetable.[4]

The size of the win is easy to overstate, so we did the sum. USD 138 million over 2.5 years is about USD 55 million a year (138 ÷ 2.5 = 55.2). The September 2026 quarter revenue of USD 177.9 million is a yearly rate of about USD 712 million (177.9 × 4 = 711.6).[3] The programme is therefore worth about 7.8 percent of the current revenue rate (55.2 ÷ 711.6). One broker note quotes the value as USD 128 million and says the deal can add 7 percent to FY28 growth.[21] The company's own filings say USD 138 million, and we use that figure.

Competition is a second strength. The chief executive said that outside India the company mostly meets established European and North American engineering-services firms and, increasingly, Chinese ones, and "seldom" meets Indian firms.[4] Credit raters see a durable franchise too. CARE Ratings reaffirmed its AA+ rating with a stable outlook in March 2026, citing brand recall and a three-decade position in engineering research and development.[15]

Weakness: one customer family

Concentration shows up in three places. First, the annual reports disclose revenue from major customers, meaning those above 10 percent of sales: ₹2,080.65 crore in FY24 (40.7 percent of revenue), ₹1,766.24 crore in FY25 (34.2 percent) and ₹1,817.65 crore in FY26 (33.0 percent).[14][13] The share has fallen, but the drop slowed sharply last year. Second, the related-party note shows that JLR alone paid for ₹1,337.36 crore of services in FY26, which is 24.3 percent of revenue. Tata Motors Commercial Vehicles paid ₹342.40 crore and the parent's passenger-vehicle arm ₹446.93 crore. The three lines add up to ₹2,126.69 crore, or 38.6 percent of the year's ₹5,505.57 crore.[13] Third, the anchor share of Services revenue was 54.4 percent in FY26 and 48.9 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[12][5]

These are three yardsticks for the same fact. About 40 percent of all revenue, and about half of Services revenue, comes from one family of companies that also owns 53.34 percent of the stock. The company did not give the anchor share for the September 2026 quarter in the call or the deck. The Q2 deck shows that customers paying more than USD 50 million a year fell from three to two, and the chief executive called the change at "one of those anchor customers" planned and tactical, to reverse in the second half.[3][4] Both the February and June 2026 investor decks say anchor sentiment softened after a cybersecurity event.[11][10] Project work outside the group is also uneven. CARE records that the Vietnamese carmaker VinFast was about 22 percent of revenue in FY23 and about 11 percent later, as its major EV development projects were completed.[16]

The anchor also gives a signal about the future. The board asked shareholders in May 2026 to approve JLR dealings of up to ₹1,750 crore in FY27, which is 31 percent above the ₹1,337.36 crore of FY26 (1,750 ÷ 1,337.36 = 1.31).[13] A cap is permission, not a forecast. It does show that the board expects the work to grow. The company also parks spare cash with its parent in short-term deposits, which stood at ₹1,785.50 crore on 31 March 2025, up from ₹222 crore a year earlier.[15] That ties the company's cash to the same group, although the borrower is a Tata group company. CARE lists a lower captive share as a route to a higher rating, which tells you the rater also sees it as the main weakness.[15]

Weakness: margins and profit conversion

The EBITDA margin, which is operating profit divided by revenue, was 18.3 percent in FY22 and 18.1 percent in FY25 (₹646 crore on ₹3,530 crore, and ₹934 crore on ₹5,168 crore). It fell to 15.5 percent in FY26 (₹853 crore on ₹5,506 crore).[1] Management says it chose to keep engineers on the payroll through a slow year instead of cutting capacity to protect margin.[6] In the September 2026 quarter the margin was 16.1 percent, up 40 basis points on a year earlier and flat on the June quarter, after absorbing a wage increase that the CEO and CFO both put at about 100 basis points.[3][4]

Profit after interest and depreciation moves less well than profit before them. The EBIT margin, which includes the BMW venture's profit, slipped from 14.4 to 14.1 percent over the year, partly because a new Pune office raised depreciation and lease interest, as management says.[3][4] Net income rose only 3.6 percent against revenue growth of 28.4 percent. Management says that without non-cash foreign-exchange items, profit before tax would have grown 16.4 percent and profit after tax 13 percent.[4] That adjusted figure is the company's own and we have not been able to check it, so Chapter 2B scores the reported figures.

The auto engineering cycle and the rupee

NASSCOM data in the company's June 2026 deck puts global engineering outsourcing at about USD 82 billion in 2024 and USD 135 billion by 2030, growth of 8.5 to 9.5 percent a year. The part open to Indian providers is USD 20 to 21 billion in 2024. By 2030 it reaches USD 31 to 32 billion in a "disrupted" case (7 to 8 percent a year), USD 38 to 39 billion in a baseline case (11 to 12 percent) and USD 46 to 47 billion in an accelerated case (14 to 15 percent).[10] The deck calls the last 12 to 18 months a pause in decisions, not in demand. The pause was costly for this company: revenue grew 1.0 percent in FY25 and 6.5 percent in FY26.[10][1]

Management's thesis for the cycle is that Chinese carmakers have set new standards for speed and cost, so Western carmakers are shrinking their own engineering teams and outsourcing more. It points to BMW's plan to cut German headcount while still expanding the Indian joint venture, and to Volkswagen's plan to cut 100,000 jobs by 2030.[4] The same trend hurts the German business the company just bought, where customers are restructuring. We cannot test the thesis from outside. The evidence for it is the run of large deal wins, five in the September quarter alone.[4]

The rupee matters to the reported numbers. Dividing the quarter's rupee revenue by its dollar revenue gives an implied rate of about ₹95.5 per US dollar in the September 2026 quarter (16,988 ÷ 177.9), against about ₹87.7 a year earlier (13,233 ÷ 150.9). In other words, a dollar cost about 8.9 percent more rupees.[3] It explains why rupee revenue grew 28.4 percent while dollar revenue grew 17.8 percent, or 22.2 percent at constant exchange rates. About 60 percent of FY25 revenue came from outside India, since India was ₹2,074.70 crore, or 40 percent of the total.[15] A weaker rupee lifts the rupee value of that foreign revenue while much of the delivery cost is in rupees, so it usually helps margin. The company publishes no sensitivity, so we put no number on the gain, and our central case assumes no further fall in the rupee.

What this means for an owner: the strength is real and checked, and it is the reason the business deserves a place on the watchlist. The weaknesses are the reason the price matters so much. A buyer is paying for growth that depends on one family of customers and on a margin that has not yet recovered, so Chapter 2B asks whether five specific measures support the story.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 1, in Chapter 3, asks whether a company is solvent and clean. Layer 2 asks a different question: does the business do the specific things that make money in its own industry? For an engineering-services firm those things are growth in the work it bills, the margin it keeps, how much it depends on its biggest customer, how fast customers pay, and whether growth reaches profit.

Each measure scores 1.0 for PASS, 0.5 for WATCH and 0.0 for FAIL. The five measures count equally, and the score is simply their sum. The company qualifies when the score is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 and no more than one measure fails. Two failures, or a score under 3.0, would reject the company however attractive the price. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A promise audit compares what management said one or two years ago with what the accounts later showed. We read the earnings-call transcripts from January 2025 to October 2026. This company says it does not give numeric revenue or earnings guidance, but its chief executive and finance chiefs do say what they expect, and those statements are what we test.[9]

The record is mixed in a specific way. Management has been right about direction and early on timing. In July 2025 it said double-digit growth for FY26 stayed its "North Star" and that the second half would beat the first; revenue grew 6.5 percent and the second half did beat the first.[7][13] It also promised to try to preserve margin through the year; the EBITDA margin fell from 18.1 percent to 15.5 percent.[7][1] Its new promises, made in May 2026, are for double-digit organic growth in FY27 and an exit margin above 18 percent.[6] Growth is already well ahead of the promise, and margin has not yet moved.

The pattern is useful: management's directional calls on diversification, deal wins and the BMW venture have been right, while its calls on the pace of growth and margin in a slow year were too optimistic. The two promises that matter most for the investment case, an exit margin above 18 percent and double-digit organic growth, are the ones still open or half done, so this table does not remove the need for the December-quarter margin to prove the point. The scorecard result is a qualified PASS_WITH_WATCH: 3.0 of 5.0 with one FAIL. Three things would change it. A margin of 17 percent or more would lift measure 2 to a PASS. Two failures, for example if the margin slipped under 15 percent while profit growth stayed weak, would reject the company on the operating test alone.

What this means for an owner: the operating story is good enough to keep the company under review but not strong enough to excuse a high price. The next chapter turns the same facts into five-year figures and checks the red lines that can reject a company outright.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions. This chapter names the weakest one under each estimate, says what evidence would show it wrong and says when we will know. It then gives the red-team case, which is the strongest honest argument that our own verdict is mistaken.

The central value of ₹664: the weakest link is the margin path

Our central value assumes the EBITDA margin climbs by quarter from 16.1 percent in September 2026 to 16.6 percent in December, 17.4 in March 2027, 17.8 in June and 18.0 in September 2027, an average of about 17.5 percent over the twelve months. The margin has been flat at 16.0 to 16.1 percent for three quarters, and the September quarter absorbed a wage increase of about 100 basis points.[3][4] Management says it will add margin in the second half through full-vehicle programmes, lower subcontractor cost and AI tools, but it also says Germany stays soft in the December quarter and the new Pune office raises depreciation.[4]

The evidence is the December-quarter margin. Our base path needs 16.6 percent on revenue of about ₹1,745 crore. A reading of 17 percent or more on about ₹1,780 crore would put the company on the bull path, which needs about 18.2 percent on average, and a reading near 16 percent would remove most of the upside. The company has not announced the date. Last year it reported the December quarter on 16 January 2026, so we expect mid-January 2027.[20]

The Reverse DCF of 15.75 percent: the weakest link is the starting cash flow

The ₹661 crore starting point is what the FY26 statements report, but it was helped by timing. A working-capital inflow of ₹132.88 crore and income tax paid that was ₹114.69 crore lower than the year's tax charge together added ₹247.57 crore to FY26 cash from operations.[13] On a stricter basis of ₹497 crore, the price needs 19.55 percent growth a year, not 15.75 percent. The company's offsetting items, other current assets of -₹649.37 crore and other current liabilities of +₹805.28 crore, are large and we could not see their make-up, so the cash-flow quality is a judgement and not a proof.

The evidence will be the half-year cash flow statement and the March 2027 annual figures. Listing rules require a half-year cash flow statement with the September results, but we did not obtain it in a form we could read, so we cannot yet say whether the first half repeated the FY26 pattern.

The probabilities and the P/E of 31.1: the weakest link is the multiple

We apply the peers' median trailing P/E of 31.1 to our own earnings. The stock now trades above that, at 40.4 on Screener's figures and 34.7 times consensus FY27 EPS, and its own P/E was 61 in March 2024 and 37.8 in March 2026, so a multiple above 31 is not new for it.[25][24][1] If the market keeps paying about 34.7 times, which is today's multiple on FY27 EPS, the base-case value would be about ₹741 (34.7 × 21.36), not ₹664. The reverse risk is just as large. Holding earnings at the base ₹21.36 and cutting the P/E to 26.3, the lowest peer, gives about ₹562, which is 14.5 percent below the price. We assume the premium shrinks because returns and margin trail the peers' (Chapter 3), but that is a judgement.

The evidence will be how the price and the multiple behave after the December-quarter results, and whether more analysts move from sell to hold. Today 9 of 18 analysts say sell, 5 say hold and 4 say buy, which supports our caution but is not proof.

Red-team challenge: the strongest case against rejecting the stock

The strongest honest bear case against our own verdict is that we are rejecting a good business at its floor. The chart is at support (0.41 ATR above the ₹645.10 low), volume is thin at 0.54 times its average, the RSI is 32.71 and the whole market is washed out, with only 17.9 percent of stocks above their 50-day average.[31] The company grew rupee revenue 28.4 percent, won a USD 138 million programme and four other large deals in one quarter, has AA+ credit and net cash, and has a controlling owner whose own programmes, such as JLR's push into North America and the Iveco deal at Tata Motors, management cites as sources of future work.[4][15] If the bull earnings arrive and the market pays 36 times, the stock could be near ₹850 in a year, a 30 percent gain for a 6.3 percent stop. Our answer is price, not quality. The central case already has the margin recovering and gives no gain, because the multiple of 34.7 times this year's EPS is paying for the recovery in advance. A gain of 30 percent needs both an average margin near 18.2 percent and a multiple that stays well above the peers, and we put one chance in four on that. The stop of ₹615.76 is 6.3 percent below the price, but the expected loss in the bear case is 29.5 percent, so the nominal stop does not control a gap on results.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, and no limit order is placed. The rule that decides it is the reward-to-risk floor. At the ₹657.25 close the central value of ₹664 gives (664 − 657.25) ÷ (657.25 − 615.76) = 0.16. At the top of the planned zone, ₹659.77, it gives (664 − 659.77) ÷ (659.77 − 615.76) = 0.10. The floor for a Core Alpha idea is 2.0 and for a Tactical idea 2.5.[32] The probability-weighted 12-month value of ₹661 returns 1.9 percent with the dividend, far below the 12 percent hurdle.

The stop and the zone follow the scoring rules. The Core stop for a Stage 1 base is the 63-day low minus one ATR: 645.10 − 29.34 = ₹615.76, which is 6.3 percent below the close and 6.7 percent below the top of the zone.[32][31] The entry zone runs from the support at ₹645.10 to half an ATR above it, ₹659.77. The chart is not the problem: at 0.41 ATR above support the stock is in the full-size band of our ladder, which allows full size below 0.6 ATR. Price is the problem, because the reward at that entry is ₹4 to ₹7 a share for a risk of ₹41 to ₹44.

The watchlist note labelled the idea TACTICAL_MOMENTUM, and we tested that label. A Tactical idea needs a Stage 2 uptrend and a reward-to-risk of 2.5. The stock is a Stage 1 base and fails the momentum gate. The Tactical stop would be ₹630.43 (645.10 − 0.5 × 29.34), which gives (664 − 657.25) ÷ (657.25 − 630.43) = 0.25, so the label changes nothing. We file it as CORE_ALPHA because the thesis is multi-year engineering demand, not a short momentum trade. Conviction tier is TIER_0, which means zero weight, because risking about ₹41 a share to make about ₹7 is not a bet we would size.
