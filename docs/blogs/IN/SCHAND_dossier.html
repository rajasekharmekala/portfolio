Before you read

S Chand and Company publishes school textbooks in India and earns nearly all of its profit in the three months to March, when schools buy for the new year[2][3]. The shares trade at about 6 times a year's profit and at 0.43 times book value, and the company holds more cash than debt[2][5][29]. We still say REJECTED, because the price chart is in a confirmed downtrend and our underwriting rule treats that as a disqualifier on its own[27][30]. The cheap price, the low-debt balance sheet with more cash than borrowings for most of the year and the guided 10 to 15 percent sales growth are real, but returns on capital stay below 10 percent and cash collection has slowed[1][3][5]. The verdict could start to change if the chart stops falling and the 5 November 2026 results show cash collection recovering, though more would be needed[17].

1. What the company does

S Chand and Company is one of the older school-book publishers in India. The group was founded in 1939 by Shyam Lal Gupta, and the listed company was incorporated in 1970[2]. Its books go to schools from the early years to Class 12. The strongest presence is in schools that follow the CBSE and ICSE syllabuses and the West Bengal board[14]. The school segment, called K-12 for kindergarten to Class 12, brings in about 80 percent of operating income[13].

The company does not sell to students directly. It sells to about 4,000 dealers, and those dealers account for roughly 90 percent of group sales[13]. A sales team of more than 700 people visits schools and teachers, and the school decides which books its students must buy[14]. About 3,000 authors write for the group, and the investor update counts more than 14,000 titles and school coverage above 45,000 schools[5][13].

Everything in this business follows the school calendar. New classes start in April, so schools order in January, February and March. In the March 2026 quarter the company booked ₹548 crore of revenue, against ₹799 crore for the full year[2]. The quarter's profit of ₹170 crore was more than twice the full year's profit of ₹73 crore, because the other three quarters lose money[2]. The company's own chart says the fourth quarter delivers 70 to 80 percent of annual revenue[3]. The losses in the other quarters are not a sign of trouble by themselves. They are how the calendar works.

The rhythm makes the cash cycle long. The company prints books from September to January, so stock builds up through the year. It sells on credit to dealers, so money owed to it peaks at the end of March. At 31 March 2026 customers owed the group ₹3,503 million, or about ₹350 crore, which the company puts at 160 days of sales[1][3]. A reader should hold on to this point, because most of the risk in this report sits in the gap between selling a book and collecting the money.

What is changing in the school syllabus

India's national curriculum body, NCERT, is replacing its textbooks to match the National Curriculum Framework. Management says the new books for Classes 1 to 8 are already out[4], and the revised Class 9 mathematics book went on sale on 21 September 2026[22]. CBSE released the 2026-27 curriculum for Classes 9 to 12 on 1 and 2 April 2026[23]. Each syllabus change forces schools to switch books, which gives private publishers a chance to win new adoptions. It also makes old stock harder to sell.

Management says it expects schools to adopt the new syllabus books fully in FY27 and FY28[3][6]. That is the main reason it guides to 10 to 15 percent sales growth this year. It also explains why the company carried more finished books at 31 March 2026, because it held both old and new syllabus titles[3].

Content licensing: selling textbook text to AI developers

AI developers pay to license text, images and video so they can train their models. S Chand had 2 such clients in its first year and 5 in FY26, and it wants 10 this year[4][6]. In FY26 about 70 percent of the revenue came from period licences running from 14 months to 5 years, and 30 percent from one-time licences[4]. The company booked ₹91 million in the June 2026 quarter and had ₹14 crore in hand by 11 August 2026[5][6].

Margins on this line depend on whose content is sold. The CFO said content from S Chand's own books earns about 90 percent margin. In FY26, however, 70 percent of the licensed content was sourced from third parties, against 20 percent a year earlier, so the margin fell[4]. Management also said pricing is 'very dynamic' and has seen a 'slight correction'[4].

The line is lumpy. By the end of nine months of FY26 the company had booked only ₹171 million of licensing revenue[32]. The full-year figure was ₹318 million, so about 46 percent of it arrived in the March quarter[3]. A reader should therefore treat the quarterly licensing figure as noise and watch the yearly total against the ₹400 million target.

Who owns the company

Promoters hold 46.99 percent as of September 2026, and none of it is pledged. The largest holders are Neerja Jhunjhnuwala at 22.68 percent and Himanshu Gupta, the managing director, at 16.99 percent[15]. Pledging means promoters have borrowed against their shares, which can force sales in a falling market, so a zero reading removes one common risk. Foreign investors hold 4.08 percent and mutual funds hold 0.29 percent through a single scheme[2][15]. The public holds 47.69 percent.

Institutional ownership is thin. Domestic institutions held 16.4 percent in March 2018 and held 1.26 percent in September 2026[2]. That matters for two reasons. The first is that few large buyers exist to support the price when it falls. The second is that the shares trade only about 30,000 to 40,000 a day on a normal day (the ledger shows 10,100 on 28 October, which is 0.3 times its 20-day average), so a stop-loss order may not fill at the price you set. What this means for an owner: the shareholder list gives comfort on governance but no help on liquidity, and chapter 6 returns to the liquidity point.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) — profit from running the business before financing costs, tax and the accounting write-down of assets. S Chand's own figure leaves out other income.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) — operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. It tells you how much profit each rupee of capital earns.
  • EPS (earnings per share) — the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • RSI (relative strength index) — a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been moving. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) — the typical daily price swing, in rupees. We use it to measure distance and to set stops.
  • FCF (free cash flow) — cash left after running the business and paying for plant, equipment and software.
  • cash flow from operations — the cash the business itself generates before spending on assets or financing.
  • working capital — the money tied up in day-to-day operations, mainly stock and money owed by customers, less what the company owes suppliers.
  • capital expenditure — spending on plants, machines and buildings.
  • gross margin — revenue minus the direct cost of the books sold, as a share of revenue. An operating margin is the same idea after all running costs and depreciation.
  • debt-to-equity — total borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Net debt is borrowings minus the cash held.
  • price-to-earnings — the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit.
  • guidance — the company's own published forecast for the coming period.
  • consensus estimate — the average of analysts' forecasts for a number.
  • buyback — the company buying its own shares back from the market, which shrinks the share count.
  • Stage 2 and Stage 4 — the Weinstein stage scale. Stage 2 is the confirmed rising phase. Stage 4 is the confirmed falling phase, where the price is below a falling 50-day average and a falling 200-day average.
  • base — a sideways stretch the price builds after a fall; a pivot is the top of that stretch, and a breakout is a move above it on rising volume.
  • entry zone — the band of prices where buying is planned, and a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better.
  • stop loss — the price at which the thesis is wrong and the position is cut.
  • asymmetry — the ratio of possible gain to possible loss from the entry price, also called reward-to-risk: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). Chapter 2 now turns from these terms to the business itself.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

S Chand has a real distribution advantage in school books, but that advantage has not produced growth or high returns for eight years. The revenue of ₹799 crore in FY26 is only a little above the ₹794 crore of FY18[2]. The rating agency ICRA makes the same point: operating income of ₹790 crore at the pre-Covid peak in FY18 was only expected to be passed in FY26[14]. So the strengths below protect the business, and the weaknesses explain why the shares are cheap.

Where the advantage is

The first advantage is reach. About 4,000 dealers carry roughly 90 percent of sales, and more than 700 sales staff keep up the link with schools and teachers[13][14]. A new publisher cannot copy that network quickly. The second is content: about 3,000 authors and thousands of titles that schools already know[13][5]. The third is in-house printing. The managing director said 80 to 85 percent of printing is done by the group itself, and a new plant near Delhi is being built to add capacity and cut costs over time[4][6].

The numbers back this up. Gross margin was 67.9 percent in FY26, even after a 6 percent GST increase on paper, and the group reported about 68 percent a year earlier[3][4]. The lenders agree. CARE reaffirmed its A rating with a stable outlook in August 2026, and ICRA upgraded its rating from A- to A in November 2025[13][14]. That means two credit analysts see a stable, cash-generating school-book business.

Where the advantage is thin

The company cannot raise prices much. The managing director said that the most it can take is 6 to 8 percent, because customers push back beyond that[4]. The CFO breaks steady-state growth into 3 to 4 percent more students, 4 to 5 percent more volume and 4 to 5 percent from price, which adds up to 10 to 12 percent a year at best[6]. Growth above that needs new products or acquisitions.

The industry is crowded. CARE calls it fragmented and competitive, with state boards, local publishers and subsidised NCERT and state books all in the market[13]. ICRA lists piracy, digital change and heavy working capital as risks, and it says any large sales return or write-off would hurt profit[14]. In May 2025 management itself said piracy of best-selling titles was a larger challenge that year[9].

Why returns on capital are low

ROCE was 9.97 percent and return on equity 7.20 percent in the latest Screener reading[2]. Two items on the balance sheet explain much of the gap. Goodwill of ₹3,359 million sits against total assets of ₹13,954 million, or about 24 percent, and the auditor tests it every year for impairment[1]. Cash, bank balances and current investments of about ₹1,887 million earn only interest[1].

Management has been asked repeatedly to write the goodwill down. The CFO said the only route is through the profit and loss account, which would produce a large reported loss, so it will wait for a large one-off gain, for example a sale of real estate or a stock-market listing of Smartivity, the toy-and-kit firm in which it holds a stake[6][9]. A write-down would raise reported returns without changing cash. We therefore show an adjusted ROCE in chapter 3 and do not rely on it for the gate.

What this means for an owner

An owner holds a reliable, cash-generating school-book franchise that grows at the pace of the school-age population plus a few points of price. That is not a compounder, and the ten-year record shows it. Sales grew 4 percent a year over ten years and average return on equity was 3 percent[2]. The case for owning it rests on the low price, the net cash and any buyback, not on growth.

That case can work. A company that earns about ₹68 crore a year and is valued at ₹458 crore pays back its price in under seven years of current profit, and it reported net cash of ₹118.2 crore at 30 June 2026 (₹104.8 crore at 31 March 2026) on the company's own definition, which leaves out lease liabilities[2][5][29]. But the case needs the cash to be real, and the next chapter tests that. Chapter 2B checks whether management's operating promises have been kept.

A reverse DCF works backwards. Instead of forecasting growth and asking what the shares are worth, it starts from today's price and asks what growth rate would justify that price. Two inputs shape the answer. The discount rate is the yearly return an investor needs, which the ledger sets at 12 percent for India. The base figure is TTM profit, meaning profit for the trailing twelve months, which is the last four reported quarters[28]. The method matters here because it shows how bleak the market's view is. It is also sensitive to the terminal multiple. Using the ledger's other inputs, our solve gives an implied profit decline of about 8.9 percent a year at 20 times, 6.2 percent at 12 times, 5.3 percent at 10 times and 4.3 percent at 8 times. The red-team bear case

The case against buying is a value trap. Revenue is flat on FY18 after eight years. ROCE has been under 10 percent in every year since FY21 (9.97 percent now), and the 10-year average return on equity is 3 percent[2]. Goodwill of ₹3,359 million and deferred tax assets of ₹720 million together equal about 39 percent of equity, and neither earns cash[1]. The ₹118.2 crore of net cash is the company's figure for 30 June 2026 and leaves out lease liabilities of about ₹48 crore. The March figure was ₹104.8 crore, and the company says it carries debt at the end of the December quarter, so the cash cushion is not constant through the year[7].

Governance and ownership give no support either. Promoters hold 47 percent and have not added to the stake, which slipped from 47.06 to 46.99 percent, and the share held by domestic institutions fell from 16.4 percent in March 2018 to 1.26 percent in September 2026[2].
