1. What the company does

V-Guard Industries was founded in 1977 by Kochouseph Chittilappilly to make voltage stabilizers, the boxes that protect a television or air conditioner from power surges [2]. It still holds about 40-45 percent of the organised stabilizer market [2]. Over four decades it added inverters and batteries, solar rooftop inverters, house wiring cables, pumps, switches, switchgear, fans, water heaters, air coolers and kitchen appliances. Most products carry the V-Guard name, while the kitchen goods that came with the Sunflame business keep the Sunflame and Superflame names [2][7]. In the year to March 2026 consolidated sales were ₹5,965.78 crore and profit after tax was ₹308.33 crore [5].

The company reports four segments. Electronics (stabilizers, inverters, batteries, solar inverters) earned ₹1,639.58 crore. Electricals (wires, pumps, switches, switchgear) earned ₹2,461.08 crore and is the largest at 41.2 percent of sales. Consumer Durables (fans, water heaters, air coolers, kitchen appliances) earned ₹1,615.88 crore. Sunflame, the kitchen-appliance subsidiary bought in 2022-23, earned ₹250.23 crore [5]. The segments behave differently. Electronics has the best margin, 17.9 percent in FY26. Electricals is the volume engine and depends on copper. Consumer Durables is the most weather-sensitive and had the thinnest margin, 1.6 percent [5].

For every ₹100 of sales in FY26 the company kept ₹36.30 after paying for materials and bought-in goods. That ₹36.30 is the gross margin, which is sales minus the direct cost of the goods sold. Staff cost took 9.0 percent of sales and all other costs, including advertising of 2.6 percent, took 18.5 percent. What was left, 8.8 percent, is the EBITDA margin, where EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization [7]. Depreciation of ₹107.89 crore and tax at 24.4 percent then brought it down to the ₹308.33 crore of profit [7]. Gross margin was 30.1 percent in FY23 and 33.6 percent in FY24, so the company has earned more on each rupee as it moved production in-house [9].

The company makes about 65 percent of what it sells in its own factories and buys the other 35 percent from outside makers [7][2]. It runs 16 manufacturing units, including plants in Coimbatore, Perundurai, Kashipur, Roorkee, Pantnagar, Haridwar, Kala Amb, Hyderabad, Sikkim and Vapi [7][2]. In FY26 it spent ₹179.85 crore on capital expenditure, which is money spent on plants, machines and moulds rather than on running costs [7]. Management now says that spending will average ₹150-170 crore a year for the next two years, against the ₹200-250 crore an analyst asked about [3]. Two projects are in progress: a ₹100 crore fan plant in Hyderabad that should start commercial production in FY27, and a ₹50 crore expansion of the Hyderabad battery plant that more than doubles its capacity [7].

Customers are households and small contractors reached through shops, not a few large buyers. The investor deck counts about 1,00,000 channel partners served by 35 branches [2]. Wires, the biggest single product, sell 90-95 percent through the retail trade [7]. We did not find a disclosed customer concentration in the filings we read, which fits a mass-market brand, but the channel is still a dependency: electricians and plumbers recommend wires and pumps, and a shopkeeper's margin decides which brand sits on the counter. South India gave 52.0 percent of FY26 sales and the rest of the country 48.0 percent; Kerala alone is about 15-16 percent [5][6].

Two forces make the numbers move from year to year. The first is weather. About 60 percent of the product range depends on summer, so a cool, wet summer hurts fans, coolers and stabilizers together [7]. FY26 showed it: sales grew only 7.0 percent, Consumer Durables fell 1.7 percent and its margin dropped from 4.2 to 1.6 percent [5]. The second is metal prices. Copper and aluminium go into wires, motors, fans and heaters, and the annual report says both reached record or near-record levels during the year [7]. In the June 2026 quarter management put price increases at about 14 points of the 23.5 percent sales growth, with volume adding only about 9 points [3].

Where V-Guard stands in each market

The company gives its own estimate of its share of the organised market in each product. It is about 40-45 percent in stabilizers, 14-16 percent in water heaters, 15-17 percent in solar water heaters, 9-12 percent in pumps, 8-9 percent in house wires, 5-7 percent in batteries and inverters, 5-7 percent in fans and about 5 percent in switchgear [2]. Management says it ranks in the top three in water heaters and the top four in domestic wires [3]. These are company estimates and we have not tested them against an outside source. They show a business that leads one small niche and is a mid-sized player in the big ones: house wires are a ₹21,000-25,000 crore market and fans a ₹12,000-14,000 crore market [2].

This matters for the story. Growth in the big categories has to come from taking share from Havells, Polycab, Crompton and others, or from the market growing 8-14 percent a year, which is the range the company quotes for most of its categories [2]. The company says it aims for 15 percent yearly sales growth, made of about 12 percent volume and 2-3 percent price in a normal year [3]. The next chapter asks how much of that is earned by the brand and how much depends on a good summer.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A strong business here means one that keeps its margins when costs rise, keeps its customers when prices go up and does not need borrowed money. V-Guard shows those traits in some places and not in others. The clearest strength is the balance sheet. Gross debt was ₹8.84 crore against net worth of ₹2,373.05 crore at March 2026, and the group held ₹231.16 crore more cash and investments than debt [7]. By June 2026 net cash had grown to ₹669.58 crore [2]. ICRA upgraded the long-term rating on ₹820 crore of bank facilities to AA+ with a stable outlook on 7 April 2026, and CRISIL reaffirmed its top short-term rating, A1+, on ₹150 crore of commercial paper in November 2025 [18][20].

The second strength is control of its own cost. The company has moved from buying finished goods to making them. For electronics, management said in October 2025 that in-house manufacturing was about 20 percent five years earlier and is now 50-60 percent [12]. Group gross margin rose from 30.1 percent in FY23 to 36.3 percent in FY25 and FY26 [9][7]. Management says most of that gain is done and the next steps will be small [12]. In the June 2026 quarter, with input costs jumping, gross margin held at 36.9 percent, the same as a year earlier [2]. That tells us price increases were passed on, which is the test of brand strength that matters most in a cost shock.

The third strength is reach. The company sells through about 1,00,000 channel partners and has 35 branches [2]. Non-South markets rose from 5 percent of sales in FY08 to about 48 percent in FY26 [2]. A retailer who already stocks V-Guard stabilizers or pumps has a reason to take its wires, switches and fans, which is how a stabilizer maker became a mass-market electrical brand.

Where the business is weak

The first weakness is weather. About 60 percent of the product range depends on summer, and the company cannot move the monsoon [7]. In FY26 a cool, wet first half cut sales growth to 7.0 percent, and the quarterly result swung from 3.6 percent growth in September 2025 to 23.5 percent in June 2026 [5][12][2]. Management said in July 2025 that it had hoped for 14-15 percent growth for the year and then expected 11-13 percent; the year ended at 7.0 percent [13][5]. A business whose year turns on rainfall has earnings that are hard to forecast, and the market usually pays a lower multiple for that.

The second weakness is that the strong June quarter was helped by price, not only by customers buying more. Management put the 23.5 percent growth at about 14 points of price and 9 points of volume, and said price rises of 5-18 percent varied by product [3]. In wires, volume growth was "very minimal" because customers delayed purchases after large price rises [3]. Price-led growth is real revenue, but it fades when metal prices stop rising, and it can reverse. Management itself notes that when metal prices fall, wire makers must write down inventory bought at higher cost [6].

The third weakness is competition and the cost of staying ahead. Wires need scale of about ₹1,000 crore of sales to make money, and new entrants, including lighting companies and a large new player, are moving in [6]. Management expects them to cost the incumbents only 1-2 points of growth a year for the first one or two years, but they are a real pressure on price [3]. An analyst on the July 2026 call said staff and other costs had almost doubled in five years, and management replied that the extended investment phase may last another two to three years [3]. The kitchen business is the fourth weak spot: Sunflame sales fell 1.6 percent in FY26 and segment margin in June 2026 was 4.0 percent against 4.4 percent a year earlier [5][2].

The fifth weakness is what V-Guard does not sell. Competitors in cables gain from government spending on power and infrastructure, which is business-to-business work. V-Guard sells to households and says its route to market is built for that [12]. In the June quarter Polycab's revenue grew 39 percent and profit 33 percent, against 23.5 and 76.4 percent at V-Guard [23][1]. V-Guard's profit growth is higher only because its profit a year earlier was depressed.

How the peers did in the same quarter

Peer results show how much of V-Guard's June quarter was industry-wide. Havells grew revenue 19.7 percent, but profit fell 15.3 percent because it doubled advertising spend to 4.4 percent of revenue from 2.6 percent [25]. Crompton grew consolidated revenue 11.8 percent and EBITDA 14.2 percent, with brushless fans up about 44 percent [24]. Polycab's revenue rose 39 percent and its profit 33 percent [23]. V-Guard's 23.5 percent revenue growth sits in the middle of that group. Its profit growth of 76.4 percent is higher than Havells' or Polycab's, partly because it spent less on advertising, 2.2 percent of revenue against 3.0 percent a year earlier [2][1].

The lesson for an owner is that V-Guard's margin gain in the June quarter has two parts: a real gain from price pass-through and in-house making, and a temporary gain from spending less on advertising than usual. Management said advertising would reach about 2.5 percent of sales for the full year [3]. We therefore treat the 10.5 percent EBITDA margin as a seasonal high and use 9.5 percent for the year in our estimates, a figure inside the 9-10 percent band that management says it can hold [3]. Chapter 2B tests that claim against the company's own record.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A company's earlier promises are the best evidence of how much to trust its next ones. We read the July 2025, October 2025, January 2026 and July 2026 call transcripts and the January 2025 and October 2024 calls, and set each target against what the filings report. The record is mixed in a pattern. Management delivers on things it controls, such as repaying debt, margins in a single segment and building factories. It misses on things that follow the weather, such as total sales growth and category growth [14][13][12][15].

The sharpest miss is sales growth. Management started FY26 aiming for 14-15 percent, cut the range to 11-13 percent in July 2025 and said in October 2025 that 15 percent "wouldn't be possible"; the year came in at 7.0 percent [13][12][5]. Capital spending went the other way. Management said in October 2025 that FY26 spending would be ₹120-130 crore and the cash flow statement shows ₹179.85 crore [12][7]. That is about 38 percent above the top of that range, though the annual report says the money went into a new research and innovation centre, plant capacity and moulds for new models [7].

3. The numbers, in plain words

Over three years V-Guard has grown steadily. Consolidated sales rose from ₹4,127.19 crore in FY23 to ₹5,965.78 crore in FY26, a compound rate of 13.1 percent a year, and profit after tax rose from ₹189.05 crore to ₹308.33 crore, or 17.7 percent a year [9][7]. Profit grew faster than sales because margins widened. The company moved more production in-house, and gross margin rose from 30.1 percent to 36.3 percent over the same years [9][7]. The last four quarters were stronger still: sales of ₹6,309.43 crore and profit of ₹364.73 crore [2][5][12].

The year in the middle of that run, FY26, was weak. Sales grew 7.0 percent and profit fell 1.7 percent to ₹308.33 crore because of a one-off ₹22.11 crore charge for new labour codes. Without it, profit would have grown 3.6 percent [5][7]. The first half was poor because of heavy rain and a cool summer. The second half recovered, with EBITDA margin of about 9.5 percent [6]. The quarter-by-quarter table shows the recovery: growth of 3.6, 10.6, 14.1 and 23.5 percent in four straight quarters, with EBITDA margin rising from 8.1 to 10.5 percent [12][10][4][1].

The balance sheet needs little comment because there is almost nothing on it to worry about. The company borrowed to buy Sunflame in FY23, and gross debt was ₹419.61 crore at March 2023. It repaid ₹273.95 crore of long-term loans in FY25 and had ₹8.84 crore of debt left at March 2026 [9][8][7]. Interest cover, which is operating profit divided by interest cost, was 34.0 times in FY26 [7]. Net worth grew from ₹1,406.96 crore at March 2022 to ₹2,509.90 crore at June 2026 [9][2]. Return on capital employed (ROCE) was 16.1 percent for FY26, down from 19.0 percent in FY25 as the one-off charge and weak summer cut profit [7]. ROCE stands for return on capital employed, the profit earned on the money tied up in the business. It was 17.8 percent over the twelve months to June 2026 [2].

Two cyclical features sit behind these numbers and are worth stating plainly. Cash flow moves with working capital, the money tied up in stock and customer bills less what the company owes suppliers. In the June 2026 quarter creditor days jumped to 91 from 71 because the company bought copper mainly from domestic suppliers who give credit rather than from importers who need cash in advance, and management called this a one-off [2][3]. Second, growth in the latest quarter was partly price. Of the 23.5 percent, about 14 points were price and 9 volume [3]. A reader should therefore set the 23.5 percent beside the 13 percent three-year growth rate, not extend it.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is weaker than the rest. This chapter names that assumption, says what evidence would show it is wrong and says when we will see that evidence. It then argues the case against our own conclusion, with numbers, and picks the one claim on which the whole view hinges. The nearest date is the September-quarter result on 29 October 2026, with the call at 3:30 pm India time on 30 October [26][27]. That result is after the 27 October close in our ledger, so nothing in this dossier reflects it.

A note on method. We hold ourselves to levels that can be measured. Where we set a threshold, for example a gross margin of 36.0 percent, it is our own rule and not a company target. We say so each time so that a reader can adjust it.

The weakest assumption behind the earnings estimate

Our FY28 profit per share of ₹11.1 assumes that an EBITDA margin near 9.6 percent survives once price rises stop adding to growth. The weak point is the pass-through of metal costs. Management says 75-80 percent of the cost rise has been passed on and 80-85 percent of the pricing actions are complete, and that some smaller categories still need price rises in the quarter to September [3]. If metal prices fall, rivals may cut prices faster than V-Guard can use up its higher-cost stock, and management itself says wire makers must then write down inventory [6].

Evidence that we are wrong would be a gross margin below 36.0 percent for the September quarter, or an EBITDA margin below 9.0 percent. The comparison is hard for gross margin, because September 2025 was 37.6 percent [12]. We will see both numbers on 29 October 2026. The better test is the December 2026 quarter, when the seasonal lift is gone; Q3 FY27 results are expected in late January 2027 (date not announced).

The weakest assumption behind the margin and the multiple

The margin assumes advertising returns to about 2.5 percent of sales without a loss of share [3]. Havells showed in June what happens when a rival fights for share: it doubled advertising and its profit fell 15.3 percent [25]. The multiple of 28 times assumes the sector's multiples hold while V-Guard's earnings catch up. The risk is a further sector de-rating: Havells, Crompton and TTK Prestige have each fallen 23-32 percent in a year [32][34][36]. We will know from the next peer results, due around late January 2027, and from the share price itself in the days after 29 October.

We hold the probabilities lightly. A reader who thought price rises were mostly permanent could move 10 points from the bear case to the bull case, which gives weights of 30, 50 and 20 percent and a weighted value of ₹309.60. A reader more worried about a reversal could move 10 points the other way, which gives 10, 50 and 40 percent and ₹267.20. We state our split of 20, 50 and 30 percent so that it can be changed.

The red-team case against our own conclusion

Our conclusion is that the business is sound, the central value is ₹310 and the stock is still not a buy. The strongest bear case against the business is this: sales rise only 8 percent in FY27 as price rises fade and volume is weak, the EBITDA margin falls back to the FY26 level of 8.8 percent, and the market pays 25 times. That gives FY27 profit per share of about ₹8.1 (sales ₹6,443 crore, EBITDA ₹567 crore) and a price near ₹203, which is 22.6 percent below ₹262.35 [5][7]. The weak spots that would lead to this case are real. About 14 points of the June growth were price. Wire volume growth was minimal. Payables were unusually high, so cash flow will not repeat [3][2].

The strongest case against our decision to reject is different. It says the stock is already oversold (RSI 21.39), sits within 0.23 ATR of support, trades 43 percent below its three-year median P/E and has sixteen analysts all above the price, so waiting for Test C means buying 10 percent higher [38][31]. We accept the cost. If the stock rebounds to its 20-day average of ₹288.81, the central value of ₹310 leaves only ₹21.19 of gain against ₹36.96 of risk to the exit price of ₹251.85, a reward-to-risk of 0.57 to 1 (our arithmetic). A bounce to the 20-day average would remove most of the asymmetry, so the rule costs us the first leg of a recovery and saves us from a failed one.

The decision is REJECTED for new money today. The rule is the technical routing rule in our scoring file. Only a Stage 4 breakdown can disqualify a stock whose business passes. That means a close below a falling 50-day average that is below a falling 200-day average, with the recovery test failing. V-Guard closed at ₹262.35 under ₹313.36 under ₹315.93. What would change it is a fresh ledger run in which Test C passes. That needs a close more than 1 percent above the 63-day low, which is above about ₹263.00 if the low stays at ₹260.40. It needs a close at or above the then-current 20-day average (₹288.81 on 27 October) with a positive two-week change or positive 28-day relative strength. It also needs an RSI of 32 or more.

A pass would move the verdict to PASSED_CONDITIONAL and WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, with a limit order as the way in. A limit order is an instruction to buy only at a stated price or lower. The entry zone is the price range in which we would place such an order. On 27 October the zone would be ₹256.00-262.00. We build it from ledger fields. The top is the ₹262.35 close rounded down to ₹262.00, so an order never chases the market. The bottom is the ₹260.40 support anchor minus half an ATR, which is ₹260.40 − 0.5 × ₹8.55 = ₹256.13, rounded down to ₹256.00; that keeps it above the exit. The stop would be ₹251.85. The central and bull targets would be ₹310 and ₹394. The ₹182 figure is the bear value, not a target. These levels do not carry over to a later date. We would rerun them on the new snapshot.

The stop loss is the price at which we would sell to limit a loss. For a Core stock the rule is the 63-day floor of ₹260.40 minus one ATR of ₹8.55. That gives ₹251.85. The stop sits only 3.9 percent below the zone top of ₹262.00. The stock fell 7.98 percent in the last week and 14.99 percent in the last month, so one bad day could gap through it [38].

Today the tier is zero and the weight is zero. After entry the let-winners-run rule applies. We would not trim just because the RSI passes 70. We would trail the stop under the rising 21-day or 50-day average while the trend and the fundamentals hold.
