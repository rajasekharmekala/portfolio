1. What the company does

Venus Pipes & Tubes turns stainless steel into pipes and tubes. It buys steel as coil, strip and billet, then shapes it into products that carry liquids, gases and steam through plants that cannot let the pipe rust or leak. The company says it makes five groups of product: high-precision and heat-exchanger tubes, hydraulic and instrumentation tubes, seamless pipes, welded pipes and box pipes[2]. A seamless pipe has no weld line. It is made by piercing a hot solid bar into a hollow shell and then drawing it to size. A welded pipe is made by rolling a flat strip into a round and welding the seam. Seamless pipe costs more and handles higher pressure. Welded pipe is cheaper and can be made in much larger diameters.

The factory is at Dhaneti, near Bhuj in Kutch, close to the Kandla and Mundra ports[5]. Installed capacity is 48,000 tonnes a year, split into 27,600 tonnes of welded pipe and 20,400 tonnes of seamless pipe[6][5]. Capacity has grown about seven-fold since FY19. The company serves more than 70 Fortune 500 customers and exports to more than 25 countries[2]. Its buyers are chemical, pharma, power, oil and gas and engineering firms, and now data-centre builders who need pipes for cooling loops[4]. Most of the money comes from selling pipe by weight, so the business lives on the spread between the steel it buys and the pipe it sells.

The mix in the June 2026 quarter was 55% seamless, 39% welded and 6% other products[4]. Domestic sales were 71% of revenue and exports were 29%, down from 37% a year earlier[5]. Higher steel prices lift reported revenue because the company passes them on, and management said this helped the quarter[4]. That matters for how you read growth: part of the 16% sales rise was price, not tonnes. Management put volume growth at more than 7% on a blended basis[4].

The business needs a lot of working capital. Management puts net working capital at about 120 days of sales[6], and inventory alone was 121 days of revenue in FY26[5]. Stock is held for long export shipping times and for customer inspection approvals. Cash is therefore tied up for four months, and that is why the company borrows heavily to grow and why its free cash flow (FCF) has been negative every year since FY22 (see chapter 3).

Three new layers on the old business

Management wants to move from selling pipe to selling a piping package. Step one is backward integration: a piercing plant that makes the hollow shell for seamless pipe. It started commercial output on 24 May 2023[16] and a matching extension of mother hollow capacity was added on 26 May 2026[15]. The company now says it is fully backward integrated for 20,400 tonnes a year[6]. Step two is fittings, the elbows, tees and flanges that join pipes. The fittings plant began commercial work on 26 May 2026[15]. Step three is pipe spooling, which means cutting, welding and testing pipe and fittings in a factory into ready-to-bolt sections.

The spooling plant is planned at about ₹70 crore of capital spending and rests on a letter of intent for ₹185 crore from one data-centre customer[5]. Management expects it to start by the end of the December 2026 quarter, to give about 5% of FY27 sales and 10% to 15% of FY28 sales[4]. A letter of intent is not a firm order, and the single-customer risk is real. Management also expects fittings to be 5% to 7% of FY27 sales[4].

Two claims in the watchlist idea that the filings do not support

The watchlist idea that sent us to this stock said the company raised ₹372 crore by a QIP to double seamless capacity, and that a piercing mill was about to start and lift margins by 250 to 300 basis points. We checked both against the company's own papers. The raise is real in size but it is not a QIP. It is a preferential issue of 22,27,544 shares at ₹1,670 to 18 named non-promoter investors, approved by the board on 16 September 2026 and by shareholders on 8 October 2026[11][28]. Of the money, ₹344 crore is for repaying borrowings and the rest for general corporate purposes[11]. It does not fund new seamless capacity. We found no QIP in three searches and in the filings we read.

The piercing mill is not a coming event. It began commercial production on 24 May 2023[16] and was extended on 26 May 2026[15]. We found no management statement that puts a 250 to 300 basis point margin gain on it in the transcripts from November 2024 to August 2026. What management does say is that EBITDA margin should be below 17% in FY27 and should reach about 18% in two years[4]. We treat the seed as a lead that was partly stale and partly wrong, and we price the stock on the filings.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Seamless pipe: pipe made without a weld line by piercing a hot bar into a hollow shell. Higher cost, higher pressure rating.
  • Welded pipe: pipe made by rolling flat strip and welding the seam. Lower cost, wider range of sizes.
  • Mother hollow: the rough hollow shell, made by a piercing mill, that is later drawn into seamless pipe.
  • Preferential issue: new shares sold to chosen investors at a fixed price, not to the public. It is different from a QIP, which is sold to qualified institutions under a separate rule.
  • Spooling: factory assembly of pipe and fittings into ready-to-install sections.
  • EBITDA margin: operating profit before depreciation and interest, as a share of sales.
  • Tonne per annum (tpa): yearly capacity in metric tonnes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest part of the business is that it has grown fast without losing its footing. Revenue went from ₹386.9 crore in FY22 to ₹1,166.8 crore in FY26, a 31.8% yearly rate over four years[5]. Management says the domestic market share in stainless pipes rose from 3.7% in FY20 to 6.2% in FY24[9], and the ICICI Securities note puts its seamless share near 10% in FY26[24]. Exports rose from ₹40 crore in FY22 to ₹400 crore in FY26[5]. Those are real gains in a market with many small, unorganised makers. The company also holds approvals that take years to win, such as ADNOC in the Gulf[7], and Crisil moved its outlook on the A rating from Stable to Positive in June 2026, with rated bank limits of ₹600 crore[12].

The weak part is that scale has not yet turned into profit. EBITDA margin fell from 18.2% in FY24 to 17.5% in FY25, then to 16.3% in FY26, and was 16.1% in the June 2026 quarter[5]. Profit after tax has stayed between ₹24.8 crore and ₹26.4 crore a quarter for five quarters, while revenue rose from ₹276 crore to ₹321 crore a quarter[5][13][6]. An analyst put this to management on the May 2026 call. Management agreed that profit growth "should come" as the big capital spending is finished[6]. We note that interest cost rose from ₹9.8 crore in FY23 to ₹40.8 crore in FY26[5], so the extra operating profit has gone to the lenders.

Moat is the right word to test here. A moat means something that lets a company keep its profit when rivals arrive. Venus has approvals, a port-side plant, an integrated line from hollow shell to fitting, and long customer relations. It does not have pricing power: its pipes are priced off steel, and management itself said competition is "there" but without undercutting[4]. Ratnamani Metals, a larger rival, already runs a pipe-spool business[4]. We call the moat narrow. It supports steady share gains. It does not support a high return on capital, and returns have been falling: the audited return on capital employed was 18.43% in FY26 against 21.74% in FY25[10].

What this means for an owner: this is a good operator in a hard-to-differentiate trade, so the stock rewards you only if margins rise or debt falls. The preferential issue speaks to the second point. The ₹344 crore of debt repayment can cut interest cost, which is the biggest drag between operating profit and net profit. Chapter 2B tests whether the operating numbers support that story.

Demand, trade policy and where the volume comes from

Demand has two legs. At home, power, oil and gas, chemicals and engineering firms are spending again. Domestic sales rose 31% in the June 2026 quarter[5], and in May 2026 management said it was the lowest bidder on tenders from BHEL worth more than ₹50 crore[6]. A third-party estimate puts the Indian stainless seamless pipe market at about $310 million in 2024, growing 8.8% a year to $360.6 million by 2029[27]. Venus grew seamless sales 15% in the June quarter, so it is growing faster than that market, which means it is taking share. That estimate is a market-research figure, not a filing, and we use it only for direction.

Exports are the exposed leg. From 1 July 2026 the European Union cut duty-free steel import quotas by about 47% and doubled the out-of-quota duty to 50%[25]. The quota is 18.3 million tonnes a year across 30 product groups[26]. Management said the European quota had been cut about 25% and that the export dip was due to geopolitics, not the quota[4]. Exports fell 9% in the June 2026 quarter and 22% in the March 2026 quarter, from ₹112.5 crore to ₹87.8 crore[5][6]. The company also sells to the United States, where a 50% steel tariff applied from June 2025 to all countries, which management said did not hit India alone[7]. Export orders are still more than 40% of the ₹600 crore core order book[4], so the dip may be timing, but we do not yet have proof.

Financing quality: three equity raises in five years

Growth has been paid for partly with new shares. The company raised money from its 2022 listing (₹56.9 crore of stock issued in FY22 and ₹150.5 crore in FY23), from share warrants (₹35.1 crore in FY25 and ₹36.3 crore in FY26) and now from the ₹372 crore preferential issue[18][11]. The promoters will fall from 48.41% to 43.71% after the issue and none of them is buying[11]. New shares are 10.75% of the old count (our calculation: 22,27,544 over 2,07,16,110 shares)[11][10]. The buyers are 18 investors that include Ashoka WhiteOak funds, Tata funds and Kotak Mahindra Life[11], which is a vote of confidence from professional money. It is also a sign that internal cash did not cover the spending: free cash flow was negative in each of the last five years[18].

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter lays out five years of revenue, profit, cash and debt, and then runs the Layer 1 gates. Layer 1 has two jobs. It checks the levels: is return on capital high enough, is debt low enough, does profit turn into cash? And it checks the direction: are those numbers getting better or worse? A stock can pass on levels and fail on direction, and the trend is often what the price reacts to.

Revenue has more than tripled since FY22 and profit has more than tripled, at 31.8% and 34.0% a year over four years (the company's own slide)[5]. Most of that came in FY23 and FY24, when sales rose 43% and 45%. Growth has since slowed to 19% in FY25 and 22% in FY26. Profit growth slowed more: 8% in FY25 and 10% in FY26 against 94% in FY24. That gap between sales growth and profit growth is the central fact of this chapter. It comes from lower margin, higher depreciation as new plant starts, and interest on the debt that paid for the plant.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A report that only argues for its own answer is not finished. This chapter plays the other side. First the red-team case in plain words: the stock rose 44% in about two and a half weeks and then lost 18.7% from the high in about four weeks. The first leg of that rise, a gain of about 19% (₹1,631.00 on 4 September to ₹1,938.10 on 16 September, our calculation), came before the 16 September funding announcement, and the sources we read do not establish its cause. Only the 17 and 18 September jumps, on about ten times normal volume, followed the news, and that burst suggests some buyers were trading the event. The institutions that bought at ₹1,670 are 14% in profit and can sell after the lock-in ends. A bear who reads the filings would say that the company has raised equity three times and still shows negative free cash flow, that profit has gone nowhere for five quarters, and that the market now pays 38 times earnings for a business whose margin has fallen for two years.

Weakest assumption behind the FY27 estimate: the margin and the interest saving arrive on time

Our FY27 profit needs about ₹34 crore a quarter from the September quarter onwards. That needs sales near ₹352 crore, a 16.5% margin, and interest cost falling to about ₹3.5 crore a quarter by March. The weakest link is the repayment. The issue needs allotment, and the notice allows 15 days from approval[11]. Lenders may charge prepayment fees, and the notice includes them in the ₹344 crore[11]. Working capital will pull in new borrowing, because inventory is 121 days of revenue[5]. The evidence that would prove the assumption wrong is a December 2026 quarter finance cost above ₹9 crore (our model has ₹7 crore) or a September quarter margin under 16%. The margin will be known when the September quarter is filed, expected in early to mid November 2026. StockAnalysis estimates 6 November[20].

Weakest assumption behind the FY28 and twelve-month estimate: 20% growth at a 17.5% margin

Management says the business can double by FY29 or FY30 and that margins can rise 3 to 4 points over time, with a conservative floor at 18%[4]. Our model gives a 17.5% margin on 20% growth. The weakest part is the mix shift: fittings are 5% to 7% of FY27 sales and spooling 5%, but both are new products that need customer approval, and management said approvals "can take a few months"[4]. If the approvals slip, the margin does not rise and the extra plant sits idle. The spooling plant leans on one customer's ₹185 crore letter of intent, which is not an order[5]. On the August 2026 call management said the spooling plant would start by the end of the December 2026 quarter. We will know when that quarter is reported in February 2027.

Weakest assumption behind the base target: the multiple returns to 32.5

The 32.5 median comes from a window that includes the period when profit was growing faster. The median is calculated by Screener from price over trailing profit and is not a filing[3]. A stock with falling margin and flat quarterly profit can stay below its median for a long time, and the P/E fell to 19.2 in March 2026[3]. If the multiple settles at 28 the base target falls under the price. The lock-in matters too. The notice says the new shares are subject to the lock-in the ICDR rules require, and pre-issue holdings of the allottees are locked under regulation 167(6)[11]. We state no lock-in length, because the notice refers to the regulation and does not print the period. Whenever the lock-in ends, supply could rise.

Weakest assumption behind the stop and asymmetry: the stock does not gap

The stop of ₹1,810.62 is 5.2% below the price, or 0.83 of a daily ATR. The stock fell 6.7% in one session on 13 October[22]. In a fast fall the exit price could be lower than the stop, so the real risk is larger than the planned risk and the true reward-to-risk is worse than 2.79. That reinforces the rejection.
