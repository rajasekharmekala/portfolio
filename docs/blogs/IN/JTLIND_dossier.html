Before you read

JTL Industries makes steel tubes and pipes used in buildings, solar farms, water lines and factories. This report rejects the stock for now, because over the last three years its operating cash flow was negative by about ₹335 crore while it reported about ₹315 crore of profit, and that breaks one of our hard rules.[1][2] The profit story itself is strong, with the March 2026 and June 2026 quarters the best on record.[4][10] The rejection could be lifted only if the half-year results due on 2 November 2026 show positive operating cash flow together with profit at least 50 percent above the same half last year, and even then the stock would move to a conditional watch, not to a buy.[1] The case would also fail if the stock closes below ₹69.00, because the price would then show that buyers no longer pay for the growth.

1. What the company does

Think of JTL as a converter. It buys rolled steel coil from large steel mills, cuts and shapes it into round, square and rectangular tubes, and sells the tubes to dealers, builders, engineers and exporters. The customer pays for the tube, and JTL keeps the gap between the tube price and the coil price, after its own costs. In the year to March 2026 the company sold 395,900 tonnes of tubes and pipes, which brought in ₹2,136 crore of sales.[2][6]

Most of the money comes from India. Dealers account for roughly half to three-fifths of sales, exports were about 10 percent in FY26 but only 5 percent in the June 2026 quarter, and government projects are now under 5 percent of sales, down from over 25 percent earlier.[3][6] Management cut the government share on purpose, because state buyers pay slowly and dealers pay in about a week.[3]

JTL runs four tube plants: Derabassi and Mandi Gobindgarh in Punjab, Mangaon in Maharashtra, and Raipur in Chhattisgarh. Together they can make about 936,000 tonnes a year, and management says the number will roughly double by the 2027 to 2028 financial years.[8][3] The Mangaon plant is where the growth is being built. It makes a newer product called direct forming technology (DFT) pipe, which uses a roll-free process to shape heavy structural tubes up to 350 by 350 millimetres.[4]

The group also owns JTL Defence, formerly RCI Industries and Technologies, a copper and brass alloy maker in Himachal Pradesh. JTL took it over through a bankruptcy court order dated 9 October 2025, and it is a small part of sales so far.[2][8] In February 2026 JTL agreed to buy 47.97 percent of Powersol Metalcraft, which is now an associate.[21]

Who pays, and how fast

Dealers buy on short credit, about seven to eight days, while government and some institutional buyers pay in months.[3] Customer concentration is low: the five largest customers are about 22 percent of purchases, according to the credit rating agency.[7] The weak point is the other side of the ledger, because customers owe JTL more each year. Debtor days, which measure how many days of sales are unpaid, rose from 34 days in FY24 to 71 days in FY26.[1]

For you as an owner this matters most: a tube maker can grow sales quickly, yet the profit only counts when customers pay. Section 3 tests that with cash flow, and section 2B tests it with operating indicators.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is the profit from running the factories before financing costs and accounting charges.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is the yearly profit earned on each rupee of money the business uses.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided across all of its shares.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow, the cash left after running the business and buying plant and equipment.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been moving; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in rupees, used to measure how far the price sits from a support level.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates, before spending on plants or paying for financing.
  • working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less the credit suppliers give.
  • debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' money; below 1.0 means the owners fund more of the business than lenders do.
  • net debt is borrowings minus the cash held.
  • operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales.
  • net margin is final profit as a share of sales.
  • price-to-earnings is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit; a P/E of 20 means 20 rupees for every rupee earned.
  • guidance is management's own published forecast for a coming period.
  • consensus estimate is the average of the forecasts published by the analysts who follow the stock.
  • capital expenditure is cash spent on plants and equipment that will last for years.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days; the 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average use 50 and 200 days, and each acts as a slowly moving trend line.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein scale, Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • base is a sideways price platform; the pivot is the top of that platform, and a breakout is a move above the pivot on strong trading volume.
  • entry zone is the price band where buying is planned; a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better.
  • stop loss is the price where the idea is proven wrong and the position is cut.
  • asymmetry — upside reward relative to downside risk — and reward-to-risk mean the same thing here: the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop.
  • measured move is a target found by adding the height of a price base to its breakout level.
  • dilution is the rise in share count when a company issues new shares, which shrinks each existing share's slice.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

JTL has a modest competitive position. It is a scaled, family-run converter in a fragmented, cyclical industry, and the credit rating agency says so directly: the industry is competitive and fragmented, and margins swing with steel coil prices.[7] Nothing in the filings shows pricing power that lasts through a cycle. What the company does show is a record of growing volume and a margin that has recently recovered.

The evidence for strength is mixed in a useful way. On the plus side, the group has a three-decade operating history, more than 1,000 dealers and retailers, and more than 1,000 product variants.[7] It has also bought its way into steel making, through Nabha Steel in April 2024 and a coil line at Raipur, which gives it some control of its own raw material.[8] The evidence against is that returns on capital have fallen every year since FY22. The company's own slide shows return on capital employed (ROCE) of 30.1 percent in FY23, 19.0 percent in FY24, 9.2 percent in FY25 and 8.6 percent in FY26.[4]

Why returns fell even as profit rose

Equity capital rose from ₹774 crore in March 2024 to ₹1,486 crore in March 2026, and the company raised about ₹380 crore of new equity in FY25 alone through share warrants and a share sale.[1][2] Fixed assets grew from ₹111 crore to ₹501 crore, with another ₹159 crore still under construction.[1] Profit stayed near ₹100 crore, so each rupee of capital earned less. Management says the plants are built and returns will recover, and it points to Mangaon running at only 42 percent of capacity in the June 2026 quarter.[3]

That is a fair argument, and it is also the part we cannot yet verify. A factory that is built is not a factory that is full, and until volume reaches the new capacity, the return on the new money stays low.

Where the competition stands

The sector leader, APL Apollo Tubes, shows what a strong version of this business looks like. It earns return on capital employed of 31.8 percent, reports operating margin near 8 percent, and generated ₹2,103 crore of operating cash flow in FY26.[16] JTL now earns a similar operating margin of 8 percent, but its capital returns are one third as high and its cash flow is negative.[1] The margin gains look real. Why JTL's cash flow lags APL Apollo's is not explained in the filings, and the working-capital detail in section 3 is the closest evidence we have.

The market for the product is healthy. APL Apollo's September 2026 quarter sales rose to ₹7,002 crore from ₹5,531 crore a year earlier, and benchmark steel coil prices have risen for three straight months to about ₹64,000 to ₹64,700 per tonne.[16][19] Rising coil prices help a converter that holds stock bought earlier, but they also tie up more cash in stock and unpaid bills.

Governance and disclosure flags

Four items weaken the quality of the numbers. First, the Enforcement Directorate searched JTL's premises on 16 April 2025, and the audit report for FY26 notes that it seized ₹35.00 lakh in cash from the company.[7][2] Second, a government tax investigation unit issued an order to the company on 2 March 2026, though the order's content is not public in the pages we could read.[21] Third, the auditor drew attention to unconfirmed balances: some receivables, payables and advances are stated as per the books, without confirmation from the other party.[2] Fourth, JTL lends to a company influenced by its key managers, Jagan Industries Private Limited, with ₹84.35 crore outstanding at March 2026 and ₹7.17 crore of interest income booked from it in FY26.[2]

None of these proves wrongdoing, and the credit rating agency removed its rating watch in April 2026 because no findings had been issued.[7] They do mean that the cash flow shortfall in section 3 cannot be explained away by trusting the accounts blindly. We treat them as reasons for a wider safety margin.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A tube converter lives on three things: the spread it earns per tonne, how full its plants are, and how quickly it turns steel into cash. The scorecard has five operating indicators, drawn from the book standard's capital goods and manufacturing list. One of them, product mix, was added because management has made it the centre of its story, and it takes the place of order book to billings, the listed indicator that does not apply here. Each indicator scores 1.0 for PASS, 0.5 for WATCH and 0 for FAIL, and a total of at least 3.0 out of 5.0 with no more than one FAIL is needed to qualify.

Order book to billings does not apply as a score, because JTL sells mainly from stock to dealers on a 7 to 10 day delivery. Management cites about 1 lakh tonnes of rolling orders, which at ₹60,882 per tonne is about ₹609 crore, or about 0.26 times trailing twelve-month sales of ₹2,314 crore. The only dated order book is exports of about ₹75 crore, about 0.03 times those sales.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

The best test of management is what it said 12 months ago against what happened. We checked the guidance given on the July 2025 and May 2026 earnings calls against the filed numbers and the 1 October 2026 business update.[13][12][15] The company delivered on margin per tonne, and it missed or slipped on volume, capital spending and capacity dates. Cash flow is still open.

The clearest miss is volume. In July 2025 the company guided for 5 lakh (500,000) tonnes in FY26 and delivered 395,900 tonnes, which is 79 percent of the target.[13][6] The most important open item is cash. In May 2026 management said it expected positive operating cash flow in FY27, after two to three negative years.[12] That promise is still open and is the single fact that decides this report.

The number

Three-year operating cash flow divided by profit is −1.06. The company took in about ₹335 crore less than it spent on operations, while it recorded ₹315 crore of profit.

How it was calculated

We added operating cash flow for FY24 (−₹22 crore), FY25 (−₹246 crore) and FY26 (−₹67 crore), and divided by the sum of consolidated net profit for the same years (₹113 crore, ₹99 crore and ₹103 crore).[1] The FY25 and FY26 cash figures also match the audited consolidated cash flow statement: −₹245.69 crore and −₹67.37 crore.[2]

The inputs

Operating cash flow from the Screener cash flow table and the annual report; net profit from the Screener profit and loss table.[1][2] The FY24 figure comes from Screener only, because we did not retrieve the FY24 annual report.

The range

If the FY24 figure were zero, the ratio would still be −0.99. Even a strong FY27 of +₹150 crore would leave the rolling three-year sum for FY25 to FY27 at −₹163 crore, so the plain red line would still read negative next year. The exemption is the only near-term cure.

If the number is off

If the real ratio were above 0.25, the red line would pass and the name would route to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. Any error of the size we can imagine cannot lift −1.06 to 0.25, so small input errors cannot change the verdict.

The number

Return on capital employed on the March 2026 balance sheet is about 9.6 percent, and the June 2026 quarter annualised is about 12 percent.

How it was calculated

The 9.61 percent is the Screener figure.[1] The 12 percent is our own: June 2026 quarter profit before tax of ₹48 crore plus interest of ₹5 crore is ₹53 crore, times four is ₹212 crore, divided by equity plus borrowings of about ₹1,730 crore at March 2026.[1]

The inputs

Screener quarterly results and balance sheet. The company's own 8.6 percent FY26 figure uses a different capital definition.[4]

The range

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could fail. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show the assumption is wrong, and says when that evidence will arrive. It then argues the other side as hard as it can, as a red team would.

Weakest assumption behind the earnings estimate: volume grows 25 percent a year at ₹4,750 to ₹5,000 per tonne

The assumption fails if volume lags capacity or if coil prices fall and leave the company with expensive stock. A record first half does not by itself show that the second half will speed up, or that the extra sales will turn into cash. The first-half FY27 volume of 220,806 tonnes needs a second half of about 274,000 tonnes to reach our 495,000, and 294,000 tonnes to reach management's guide, which is 33 percent more than the first half.[15][3] The company missed its FY26 volume guide by 21 percent, and the September quarter was 13.7 percent below June.[6][15] We will know in two steps: the Q2 FY27 results on 2 November 2026, which show margin per tonne, and the Q3 business update at the start of January 2027, which shows volume.[1]

Weakest assumption behind the target: the market will pay 18 times earnings

The 18 times comes from one broker and from peers that trade between 14.8 and 17.4 times.[8][17][18] A multiple depends on whether the market believes the earnings are real. Hi-Tech Pipes has the same cash flow problem as JTL, trades at 17.4 times and sits about 44 percent below its 52-week high of ₹116.[17] If JTL's multiple resembles Pennar's 14.8, the central value falls to ₹66.60. The evidence will be the share price reaction to the 2 November cash flow statement.

Red-team bear case

The strongest honest bear case is that JTL's growth has been paid for by outside money, not by its own cash. Over three years it spent ₹335 crore more than operations brought in, raised about ₹380 crore of equity in FY25, tripled borrowings in FY26, and let supplier credit rise ₹121 crore in one year.[1][2] That supplier credit may be a one-off, since payable days rose from five to 29. If it reverses, FY27 operating cash flow will be negative again, even with higher profit.

Add to that the ₹292 crore of advances to suppliers and ₹185 crore of advances to other parties on the March 2026 balance sheet, the ₹84 crore loan to a company influenced by key managers, the auditor's note on unconfirmed balances, and an Enforcement Directorate search that has not been publicly concluded.[2][7] Each could have an innocent explanation, and together they make a lower multiple fair until cash is verified. The company's own slide shows operating cash flow to EBITDA of 1.1 times, which appears to be measured before working-capital changes: the audited statement shows ₹162 crore of operating profit before those changes, close to 1.1 times the ₹154 crore of EBITDA, and ₹67 crore of outflow after them.[4][2]

The bull answer is that a company building a plant tripling its capacity should consume working capital, that the plant is now built, and that margin per tonne already shows operating leverage. That answer is plausible. It is also the kind of answer that is only worth acting on after it shows up in the cash flow statement.
