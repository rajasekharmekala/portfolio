1. What the company does

Anand Rathi Wealth is a wealth manager for rich Indian families. It started in 2002 as a mutual fund distributor, launched its private wealth business in 2007, and began selling structured products in 2012[7]. It listed on the exchanges in 2021. A wealth manager is a firm that helps a family decide where to put its savings and then looks after those holdings for years. At 30 September 2026 the firm looked after ₹1,08,377 crore of client assets, called assets under management (AUM), up 18.4% in a year[3].

The clients are the customers, but the product makers pay most of the bill. When a family buys a mutual fund through the firm, the fund house pays the firm a yearly commission, called a trail commission, for as long as the money stays invested. Management says this earns about 1.09% a year on its model mutual fund portfolio after GST[8]. When a family buys a structured product, the issuer pays a commission that the firm books on day one; management puts the yield on its matured products at 1.17% a year on market value[8]. A structured product here is a bond whose payout is tied to a stock index, so the client gets a fixed target return if the index behaves within a set range.

Three groups of products make up the AUM. Mutual funds are ₹61,159 crore, or 56.4%. Structured products are ₹27,896 crore, or 25.7%. Other products, which the presentation does not break down, are ₹19,322 crore, or 17.8%[3]. Private wealth, the main business, holds ₹1,05,847 crore of that, or 97.7%. A smaller digital wealth unit serves families with ₹10 lakh to ₹5 crore and holds ₹2,531 crore[2][9].

People are the machine. The firm has 431 relationship managers, called RMs, who serve 14,309 client families, about 33 families each, and each RM looks after ₹246 crore on average[2]. Management says it builds RMs from within because it takes nearly five years for an RM to become successful[7]. A family usually starts with ₹1 crore to ₹5 crore and adds money as trust grows; in the September quarter 62% of net new money came from existing families and 38% from new ones[10].

How a structured product earns money, and who stands behind it

Structured products are issued by a non-bank lender, and most of the firm's issues come from its own group. In the June quarter, ₹1,875 crore of ₹2,187 crore of primary issuance went to group companies, or about 86%[11]. In the September quarter primary issuance was ₹2,073 crore and secondary trading was about ₹750 crore[12]. The firm says it only distributes these products and gives no guarantee on the group lender's borrowings[8]. That matters because the client holds a bond of the group lender, so the client carries the lender's credit risk, and the firm's name and fees rest on that lender staying sound.

The firm also owns a small stake in that lender, Anand Rathi Global Finance. At 31 March 2026 it held 75,60,000 shares valued at ₹217.1 crore and perpetual debentures of ₹46.6 crore, both unquoted[13]. Together that is ₹263.7 crore, about 26% of the ₹1,011.9 crore of shareholders' equity at that date[9][6]. Chapter 2 and Chapter 6 return to this link, because it is the main way the firm's fortunes and its clients' fortunes can move together.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Assets under management (AUM): the total value of client money the firm looks after. Fees follow it, so it is the main size measure.
  • Net inflow: new client money coming in minus money going out, before any gain or loss in market prices. It shows whether the firm is winning money, not just riding the market.
  • Trail commission: a yearly fee a fund house pays a distributor for as long as the client stays invested.
  • Adjusted profit: the company's own profit measure that leaves out fair value gains on investments, share-option expenses and their tax effect. Reported profit includes them.
  • ESOP (employee stock option plan): a plan that lets chosen staff buy shares later at a fixed low price. The firm records its estimated value as a salary cost over the waiting period, although no cash leaves.
  • Fair value gain: a profit booked because an unquoted holding was re-valued upward. It is not cash until the holding is sold.
  • Reverse DCF: a method that starts from today's price and works out the yearly growth the price assumes for ten years.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The firm's strength is trust that has been kept for a long time. Only 0.26% of client assets left in the first half of FY27, against 0.20% a year earlier, and attrition for the full year FY26 was 0.54%, as management stated[14][15]. Some 83% of client assets belong to families who have been with the firm for more than three years[14]. Staff stay too: management counted one regret departure among relationship managers in the first half[10]. Low turnover matters because a client usually follows his or her RM, so each lost RM is a risk to the assets he or she serves.

The firm's own record is the sales pitch. It reports that its strategy, a model mix of mutual funds and structured products run since June 2013, has returned 14.81% a year against 10.49% for the Nifty 50 with a beta of 0.59, an excess return of 5.97% a year after adjusting for risk[14]. Beta is a measure of how much a portfolio moves when the index moves; 0.59 means it has moved about 0.6% for each 1% move in the Nifty. These figures are the company's own and are not independently audited in the documents we read, so we treat them as a claim and not as proof.

The firm is winning share. Its share of equity mutual fund net inflows was 2.11% in the June quarter, against a 1.54% share of industry mutual fund assets, and its asset share has risen from 1.01% in March 2019 to 1.54% in June 2026[16]. The market is growing too: Indian mutual fund assets rose from ₹23.8 lakh crore in March 2019 to ₹87.1 lakh crore in August 2026, according to the company's slide[7]. A business that grows with a rising market and also gains share can compound for years, provided its RMs keep adding clients.

There are four real weaknesses. First, it depends on two executives: all 12,40,000 options of the 2025 plan went to the CEO and the Joint CEO, at an exercise price equal to the ₹5 face value[4][17]. Second, the group link described in Chapter 1 ties client products and the firm's own balance sheet to one lender[13][11]. Third, the people who own the company have been selling: the promoter share fell from 47.29% in March 2025 to 40.95% in September 2026, and the documents we read do not say why[18]. Fourth, the fee rate on assets is drifting down, from 1.44% of average AUM in FY25 to 1.38% in the first half of FY27 on our calculation[1][9][6].

Customers, concentration and the business cycle

No single client is large, but wealth is concentrated. Clients with ₹50 crore or more hold 31.0% of assets, up from 15.7% in 2021[14]. On the staff side, the slide groups the 431 RMs into three sizes of AUM and the largest group, 158 RMs, manages 80% of assets[2]. So a loss of a few senior RMs or a few very large families would matter more than the headline attrition rate suggests.

The business moves with markets, but less than the index. Management says that if the Nifty falls 5%, its total assets fall about 1.5%[19]. In the September quarter the Nifty fell 8.1% and adjusted profit still rose 22.2%[20]. A fall in the value of client assets cuts trail commissions at once, because they are a fixed share of market value. If all fees followed assets, a 10% fall in AUM would be worth about ₹149 crore of yearly revenue at the present fee rate on our calculation, although in practice only the trail part reacts at once[6]. Structured-product commissions depend on new issues, and these can slow if clients lose confidence. Management says 1,190 products in a row delivered the promised return until May or June 2026, and that some recent products paid 6% to 7% instead of the 14% to 15% targeted[12].

Rules matter as well. SEBI introduced a new expense-ratio framework for mutual funds from 1 April 2026[21]. Trade press reports that average distributable expense ratios fell by about 3 basis points for mid- and small-cap schemes, over 2 for flexi-cap and over 6 for large-cap in the first quarter of FY27[22]. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent. Management says the effect on its 1.09% yield is not significant[23], but the fee drift in our yield calculation is consistent with a slow squeeze.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A wealth manager is not judged on factory output. It is judged on four questions. Do clients keep adding money? Does the fee rate hold? Can each RM serve more families? Is the reported profit the profit the firm really earns? We chose five measures for those questions. Each is scored PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0) under the rule in the scoring file.

Here is the reason for each KPI. Net inflows show demand for the service. Revenue yield on assets shows pricing power. RM productivity and capacity show how far the model can scale. Client retention shows trust. Earnings quality shows whether the headline growth can be trusted. It is the gap between adjusted and reported profit[6].

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management gives guidance, which is its own published forecast, for revenue, profit and AUM at the start of each year. It reports progress every quarter. The record on profit is good. For FY25 it first guided ₹280 crore, raised that to ₹295 crore, and delivered ₹301 crore[24]. For FY26 it guided ₹375 crore and delivered ₹386 crore on its adjusted basis[25]. After nine months it had already delivered 76% of the revenue guide and 78% of the profit guide[26]. Revenue also beat in both years. The AUM guide is the exception. FY25 AUM came in at ₹77,103 crore against a revised ₹80,000 crore, and FY26 AUM at ₹93,037 crore against ₹1,00,000 crore[25][24].

Management explains the AUM miss as market timing: the year-end Nifty was 22,331, and AUM crossed ₹1 lakh crore only in April[15]. That is fair, because AUM is read on one day. It still means that the number most exposed to markets has been missed twice in a row, so we give it less weight than the profit guide.

Management also says it has grown profit more than 20% a year in every quarter since listing, and the September quarter was the 20th[19]. On the adjusted basis that is true: growth was 22.2% in Q2 FY27 with a mean of 31.1% over 18 quarters[20]. The caveat is the definition. The adjusted basis began to leave out ESOP expenses once they appeared in Q4 FY26, and on reported profit the September quarter was down 10.6%[3].

The number

We estimate the ESOP expense before tax at about ₹40 crore for FY26 (₹39.3 crore was booked), ₹168 crore in FY27, ₹98 crore in FY28, ₹60 crore in FY29 and ₹23 crore in FY30, a total of about ₹388 crore (the rounded years add to ₹389 crore). That leaves about ₹349 crore for FY27 to FY30. Of FY27's ₹168 crore, ₹92 crore was booked in the first half, so about ₹76 crore is left for the second half.

How it was calculated

We multiplied the options in each of the four vesting tranches by the fair value per option and spread each tranche's cost evenly over the years until it vests, then mapped the calendar to financial years.

The inputs

The plan granted 12,40,000 options at an exercise price of ₹5 on 12 January 2026, valued at ₹3,130 each, vesting in tranches of 248,000, 248,000, 372,000 and 372,000 options on 12 January 2027, 2028, 2029 and 2030[4]. Our schedule gives ₹39.9 crore for the 79 days to 31 March 2026 against ₹39.3 crore reported[15], and about ₹46 crore for a quarter in the first year against ₹46.2 crore reported for Q2 FY27[1][3][6]. The Q1 figure of ₹46.0 crore is derived from the half-year and Q2 slides, so it is not an independent check; only Q4 FY26 and Q2 FY27 count. The two direct checks match closely.

The range

If the company spread the cost evenly over four years instead, the charge would be about ₹24 crore a quarter, which does not match the ₹46 crore booked, so we rule that out. If options were not adjusted for the 1:1 bonus, the share count in the plan would be half of the 24.8 lakh we assume. The cost in rupees would not change, but the dilution, which is the rise in share count that shrinks each share's slice, would.

If the number is off

If the schedule is right, reported profit gets an easier comparison from Q4 FY27 and FY28 reported profit should rise faster than adjusted profit. The total cost is about 1.0% of today's market value, and the 24.8 lakh shares after the bonus equal about 1.5% of the share count, so even a large error would not change the quality of the business, only how much of adjusted profit belongs to existing owners[6].
