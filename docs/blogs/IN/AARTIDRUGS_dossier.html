1. What the company does

Aarti Drugs was set up in 1984 and is part of the Aarti group of chemical companies. It makes the active ingredient, called an API (active pharmaceutical ingredient), that does the work inside a pill. A drug company buys the API in bulk, mixes it with fillers and presses it into tablets. Aarti Drugs sells more than 50 such molecules to drug makers in over 100 countries, and the company says it is the world's largest maker of several older ones, including nimesulide, ketoconazole and the fluoroquinolone family of antibiotics.[8] These are company statements, not independent market-share data.[1]

The scale is large but spread thin. The company runs 14 manufacturing facilities with a monthly capacity of 6,876 tonnes and about 2,200 employees.[8] In FY26 (the year to March 2026) consolidated revenue was ₹2,565.3 crore. Of that, APIs were ₹1,979.4 crore, finished tablets and capsules (formulations) ₹331.2 crore, specialty chemicals ₹178.3 crore and intermediates and others ₹76.4 crore.[13] So about 77 of every 100 rupees of sales still come from APIs.[7]

Who pays? Drug makers in India pay for about 62% of sales and exporters for about 38%.[7] The customer list is wide. The largest domestic client is about 4% of domestic sales and the top ten are about 25%. The largest export client is about 7% of export sales and the top ten are about 27%.[7] Rating agency ICRA puts the top ten customers at 16.3% of API revenue in the first half of FY26.[19] No single buyer can break the company. The risk sits in the products instead. Customers also pay slowly and the company holds stock for about three months, so working capital, the cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills minus what is owed to suppliers, is large (receivable days 118 in FY26).[1] ICRA says five molecules (ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin, metronidazole, metformin and ketoconazole) made 46.3% of group revenue in that period.[19]

How does it make money? The company buys basic chemicals, much of it imported from China, runs them through several reaction steps, and sells the result by the kilogram.[20] The spread between selling price and chemical cost is the gross margin, meaning revenue minus the cost of goods as a share of revenue. It was 37.0% in FY26, up from 33.8% in FY24.[7] After wages, power and other running costs, EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) was ₹311.6 crore, a 12.1% margin.[7] That is the operating margin in everyday terms: the share of each rupee of sales left after the costs of running the business. Because most of these molecules are old and sold by many makers, price moves hit the top line hard. ICRA notes revenue fell 5.6% in FY25, mainly because API selling prices fell and export demand was weak.[19]

The last four years were a building phase. The company spent about ₹600 crore on a capex programme built around two new plants and says it is complete.[8] One plant at Sayakha in Gujarat makes methylamines, the key input for metformin (a diabetes drug), so that Aarti no longer buys it from outsiders. The other, at Tarapur in Maharashtra, makes salicylic acid, a chemical used in skin-care and flavour products that India mostly imports from China.[12] Making your own inputs is called backward integration: the company moves back up its own supply chain. The aim is a cheaper, steadier input cost. The two plants are the reason this report treats Aarti Drugs as a turnaround story rather than a steady compounder.

Who runs it and how the group fits together

Prakash Patil is Chairman, Managing Director and CEO, Harshit Savla is Joint Managing Director, and Adhish Patil is Chief Operating Officer and Chief Financial Officer.[6] Rashesh Gogri, a founder-promoter, sits on the board as a Managing Director and is also Vice Chairman of Aarti Industries and Chairman of Aarti Pharmalabs, two sister companies.[7] Promoters hold 54.07% of the shares as of September 2026, down from 57.13% in March 2024.[1] About 2.51% of the promoters' own shares are pledged as loan security, a small amount that first appeared in March 2026.[3]

The formulation arm, Pinnacle Life Science, is wholly owned. It holds a USFDA-approved cancer-drug (oncology) plant and a UK-approved tablet plant, but oncology was still pre-revenue in May 2026 and the August 2026 call reported no oncology sales.[6][8] Management says it has put about ₹200 crore into oncology development over 24 months.[8]

What this means for an owner

An owner of this stock owns a commodity-linked maker with decent customer spread, a ten-year sales growth of 8% a year that has slowed to 4% over five years, and a recent habit of spending ahead of earnings. The part you can judge today is whether the new plants lift margins and returns. That is the question the rest of this report tests.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest point is integration and scale in old, well-understood molecules. The company makes most of its own intermediates for the antibiotic and anti-fungal lines, has reaction vessels with more than 1,300 kilolitres of combined capacity, and has plants approved by the USFDA, the European authorities, WHO-GMP and others.[8] Crisil says in-house intermediates give partial insulation from raw-material swings.[20] Both ICRA and Crisil rate the company AA- with a stable outlook.[19][20]

The weakest point is that growth and returns have gone backwards. Revenue was ₹2,716.1 crore in FY23 and ₹2,565.3 crore in FY26, a three-year change of about -2% a year.[7][1] The company's own return on capital employed fell from 20.5% in FY22 to 11.5% in FY26.[8] ICRA's definition shows the same slide, from 16.4% in FY24 to 13.8% in the first half of FY26.[19] The reason is simple: capital grew faster than profit. Equity rose from ₹1,036.3 crore to ₹1,549.2 crore while operating profit barely moved.[7]

China is the main outside force, and it cuts both ways. Crisil says most raw material is imported from China, which exposes the group to supply and price shocks.[20] Management's view is that competing with China "was never an issue" for its product line.[6] The salicylic acid plant is a counter-example. After Aarti started producing, Chinese sellers cut prices sharply to drive out the new competition, and the plant produced only 67 tonnes in the June 2026 quarter against a breakeven of about 600 to 800 tonnes a month.[6][12] The company has applied for an anti-dumping duty, but the government asked for another year of injury evidence, so relief is probably a year away.[6]

Pricing power depends on the cycle. In the June 2026 quarter, selling prices rose 16% to 17% year on year while volumes rose only 3.5%, because the West Asia war pushed up crude-linked raw material costs and API prices followed.[6] Metformin prices were about 20% above their pre-war level.[6] Management itself says prices have eased since March and April, and warns that very high antibiotic prices cut domestic demand.[6] That is a favourable tailwind that this report treats as temporary until the September quarter proves otherwise.

On regulation the record is acceptable but unfinished. The Tarapur API plant (E-22) was inspected by the USFDA in September 2024, which issued a Form 483 listing seven observations, including weak investigation of batch deviations and an ineffective internal audit.[21] The agency later sent its EIR, the establishment inspection report, closing the inspection with a "voluntary action indicated" classification, so the plant can export products such as ciprofloxacin, celecoxib and zolpidem to the US.[22] Yet management confirmed in August 2026 that US sales "haven't started yet" and that metformin needs a new plant and a fresh inspection, at least 12 months away.[6]

Governance and accounting signals

The auditor's report on the FY26 accounts has no qualification or adverse remark, and the auditor reported no fraud.[15] Two items deserve a note. First, the auditors flag disputed tax demands as a key audit matter: ₹58.27 crore of indirect tax demands and ₹92.43 crore of income tax demands, together about ₹150.7 crore or roughly 10% of equity.[15] Second, Screener's automated checks note that the company may be capitalising interest cost; the FY26 notes show ₹6.59 crore capitalised (₹11.77 crore a year earlier) on the standalone books, which is small next to ₹33.7 crore of finance cost.[17][1] Neither is a red line, but both are items for the next annual report.

A pollution-board closure order hit one chlorosulphonation process at Tarapur after a hydrogen-chloride gas leak on 8 September 2025. Management said there was no injury and no material impact because of stock on hand.[11] It is a reminder that chemical plants carry safety and permit risk.

What the evidence does not show

We found no independent data on global market share, no filed plant-by-plant utilisation for the older API plants, and no published product-level margins. The claims of being the world's largest maker of several molecules come from the company. Nor is there a disclosed order book, because API sales are made to order and shipped quickly. A reader should treat the moat as moderate and cost-based, not as pricing power.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management talks freely on its quarterly calls, and the promises can be checked. In July 2025 the company gave guidance, which is its own published forecast: volumes would grow about 15% a year in FY26 and FY27, with prices turning from negative to flat in the second half.[12] By November 2025 it had cut FY26 to "single digit" growth, and the year ended with revenue up 7.5%.[11][7] That is a miss on the top line, though the company was open about it on the call.

The salicylic acid plant is the clearest failure. In July 2025 the plant was said to be ramping to 800 tonnes a month "very soon" and to contribute from the December 2025 quarter.[12] In November 2025 the near-term goal was 300 tonnes a month, rising to 500 in the March 2026 quarter.[11] In May 2026 management shut the plant because it was losing money on each tonne, and in the June 2026 quarter it made 67 tonnes.[9][6] The Sayakha plant did better. It started commercial production on 4 September 2025, as promised, though utilisation of about 40% in March 2026 fell short of the 45% to 50% goal.[11][9]

On money matters the record is better. Capex for FY26 was guided at ₹150 to ₹200 crore and the actual investing outflow was ₹173.4 crore.[12][7] Borrowings fell from ₹612.2 crore to ₹574.9 crore, and debt-to-equity (borrowings divided by shareholders' money) of 0.37 is below management's own 0.4 to 0.7 target band.[7][11] The margin goal is the one to watch. In July 2025 a 15% to 16% EBITDA margin was called "fairly achievable if everything goes right", and in May 2026 management guided FY27 to 13.5% to 14%, saying it would have been 14% to 14.5% without the war.[12][9]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of statements tell a plain story. Revenue was ₹2,488.6 crore in FY22 and ₹2,565.3 crore in FY26, so the business has not grown in half a decade. Profit after tax fell from ₹205.0 crore to ₹194.9 crore.[7] What changed is the cost of the capital behind it: equity grew from ₹1,036.3 crore to ₹1,549.2 crore, and fixed assets and capital work in progress together rose by about ₹520 crore.[7] That is the arithmetic behind the falling return on capital.

Read FY26 with care. The reported profit of ₹194.9 crore (up 16%) includes a tax refund of ₹16.38 crore, and the tax line was only ₹16.0 crore on a pre-tax profit of ₹210.9 crore.[7] Pre-tax profit was flat (₹211.8 crore a year earlier). Strip the refund out and FY26 profit is about ₹178.5 crore, a gain of about 6%. Management says the normal tax rate from here is about 25%.[11] The tables that follow use the filed numbers, and the estimates use 25%.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT under the technical routing rule. Aarti Drugs passes the safety lines and qualifies as a turnaround. It is in Stage 3, not Stage 4. The 27 October close of ₹402.25 is 2.25 ATR above the nearest support of ₹368.85. Anything beyond 1.6 ATR is the NO_BUY band, so a market order is not allowed. The rule keeps such names on the list with concrete limit triggers, and we do the same.[31]

How the buying rules work, in plain words. We measure how far the price sits above its nearest support, in ATR units. Up to 0.6 ATR is the FULL_SIZE band, where a full-size order is allowed. Up to 1.2 ATR is the STANDARD band, where a normal order is allowed. Up to 1.6 ATR allows only a small starter order. Beyond 1.6 ATR is the NO_BUY band: no purchase at the market price. IMMEDIATE_BUY means buy now, and it needs the FULL_SIZE band. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP means buy in steps on a pullback into the STANDARD band. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so 35 basis points is 0.35%. TIER_4 is the smallest conviction tier, with a position cap of 1% to 2% of the portfolio.

The entry zone is ₹372.00 to ₹386.50. The top, ₹386.50, sits just under the 200-day average of ₹388.44. It is 1.19 ATR above the swing low, which is the STANDARD band of the ladder (up to 1.2 ATR).
