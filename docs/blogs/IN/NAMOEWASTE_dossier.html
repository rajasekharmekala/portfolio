1. What the company does

Namo eWaste Management Limited collects, dismantles and recycles electronic waste and, since 2025, spent lithium-ion batteries. It was incorporated on 13 January 2014, runs its oldest plant in Faridabad, Haryana, and listed on the NSE SME board (called NSE Emerge) on 11 September 2024 at an issue price of ₹85.[3][14][25] Akshay Jain, the founder, is managing director, but he now holds only 4.81 percent of the shares directly. His mother holds 38.89 percent and his father, who became chairman in March 2026, holds 25.28 percent, so the promoter family owns 69.05 percent in total.[1][6][18]

The business has four revenue lines. The largest is recycling: the plants shred and sort old laptops, phones, air conditioners and similar goods, and the company sells the copper, aluminium, iron, plastic and circuit-board fractions to buyers. The second is refurbishment, which repairs working devices for resale. The third is the sale of certificates under extended producer responsibility, usually shortened to EPR, which is a legal duty for brands and importers to see that a set share of what they sell is recycled by a registered recycler. The fourth, new in 2025, is battery recycling, which produces a black powder called black mass that holds lithium, nickel, cobalt and manganese.[2][12][16]

The statutory accounts split FY26 revenue of ₹194.59 crore into ₹170.65 crore of product sales (88 percent), ₹22.74 crore of services (12 percent) and ₹1.20 crore of exports (under 1 percent).[4] Management does not publish a full split. From its call we can build one: refurbishment ₹35 crore, EPR ₹25 crore and batteries ₹18 crore, which leaves roughly ₹117 crore for plain recycling.[12] That last figure is our subtraction, not a company number. The EPR figure also sits above the ₹22.74 crore of services in the statutory split, so either some certificate income is booked in product sales or the call figure is rounded; we cannot reconcile the two. It implies recycling grew about 8 percent while the other three lines grew faster, which is worth remembering when management talks about 40 to 50 percent growth.

The company buys most of its material directly from businesses. Management says 80 to 85 percent of procurement is business-to-business under 12 to 18 month contracts, and only 10 to 15 percent comes from scrap aggregators.[12] By category, about 30 percent of e-waste volume is IT equipment, about 15 percent is telecom and mobile devices and the rest, at least half of the total, is consumer goods such as air conditioners and washing machines, which pay least.[12] The accounts also show that 88 percent of revenue is booked as product sales and that ₹156.60 crore was spent on purchases of stock-in-trade, so a large part of the business resembles buying and selling processed scrap rather than charging a fee.[3][4]

Four things to know about the exchange this share trades on. The SME board is a separate segment of the NSE for small companies, with lighter governance rules: the company's own filing says the main corporate-governance chapters of the listing rules do not apply to it.[19] Trading is thin: the 60-day median is 31,600 shares a day, about ₹0.9 crore. Price moves are capped by a daily band; the stock rose 20.0 percent in one session on 22 September 2026, which suggests a 20 percent band (our inference).[25] Migration to the main board needs three years of listing, so the earliest date is 11 September 2027.[33][34]

How a kilogram of e-waste turns into money

Think of a plant as a purchasing department with a shredder attached. It pays for old goods, strips them into fractions, and sells each fraction. The profit is the gap between what it pays and what the fractions fetch, less the cost of shipping, labour and power. Management puts the gross margin on e-waste at 15 to 18 percent and on batteries at 20 to 25 percent.[12] Our own subtraction from the accounts gives a gross profit of ₹44.4 crore on ₹194.6 crore of revenue, or 22.8 percent, because the accounts include higher-margin refurbishment and EPR income.[3][4]

The EPR certificate is a second income stream stacked on the first. A brand with a legal recycling target buys certificates from a registered recycler, and the recycler books the fee as service income. Since 2024 the pollution regulator has set a floor price on these certificates, ₹22 per kilogram for consumer electronics and ₹34 for IT and telecom equipment, and Namo says it refuses business below the floor.[12][26] This matters because the EPR line of about ₹25 crore is roughly the same size as the whole year's EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) of ₹23.1 crore (₹22.56 crore if the ₹0.54 crore of other income is left out). We do not know the cost attached to that fee, so it does not mean profit would vanish without it, but it does show how much of the profit pool rests on one regulatory number.[12][15]

Capacity: what is built, what is only announced

Management quotes 82,000 tonnes a year of capacity. The investor presentation breaks it down: Palwal 32,000, Nashik e-waste 9,300, Faridabad 5,200 and the Nashik battery plant 12,400 tonnes, which sums to 58,900 tonnes operating, plus the Hyderabad plant of 25,000 tonnes that had not started when the May 2026 call was held. Those pieces add to 83,900 tonnes, 1,900 more than the headline, and the company does not explain the gap.[16][17] Management says the Hyderabad site slipped about two months because of heavy rain and should run from July 2026, but no start-up announcement appears in the NSE feed we read up to 28 October 2026.[12][23] The company website quotes a different figure, over 1 lakh tonnes, so the reader should expect capacity numbers to differ by document.[24]

The CPCB (Central Pollution Control Board) registration number and authorised capacity of each plant are not stated in any filing we read, and the CPCB portal returned no readable text when we fetched it. We therefore cannot verify the authorised tonnage per plant. The annual report says only that the company is an authorised recycler, and on the November 2025 call management said it had permission for single-shift working only.[2][13] Utilisation was about 60 percent of effective e-waste capacity in FY26 and about 10 percent at the battery plant.[12]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • E-waste is discarded electrical and electronic equipment, from phones and laptops to air conditioners.
  • EPR (extended producer responsibility) is the rule that makes brands and importers pay for recycling a set share of what they sell; the proof is a certificate bought from a registered recycler.
  • Black mass is the shredded powder from spent lithium-ion batteries that holds lithium, nickel, cobalt and manganese.
  • Hydrometallurgy is a chemical process that dissolves black mass and separates the metals; it is the next, still unbuilt, step for Namo.
  • MTPA means metric tonnes per annum, the yearly processing capacity of a plant.
  • SME board (NSE Emerge) is the NSE segment for small companies, with lighter rules and thinner trading than the main board.
  • Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid bills; the cash conversion cycle counts the days it takes to turn it back into cash.
  • CFO/PAT is cumulative cash flow from operations divided by profit after tax over the same years; it tests whether profit turns into cash.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A recycler competes on three things: the right to process (registration and plant permits), access to material, and the price at which it sells what it recovers. On the first, Namo is formally authorised, holds ISO 9001, 14001, 27001 and 45001 certificates and won the Material Recycling Association of India's Recycler of the Year award in FY26.[2][16] On the second, its edge is direct contracts with businesses: it serves more than 300 clients across 105 recycling categories and says it has recycled over 86 million kilograms, including 3.8 crore mobile phones and about 6 lakh laptops.[1][16] Those are company statements; the accounts do not show how concentrated sales are today.

Concentration is the weak spot. The offer document, the last place the company disclosed it, showed the ten largest customers at 47.49 percent of FY24 revenue, down from 68.77 percent in FY23, and the ten largest suppliers at 78.80 percent of FY24 purchases.[11] In November 2025 management said one top multinational gives it 50 to 60 percent of its EPR business and that this business was routed through Vardhman Sales Agency, a firm in which a director of the company is a partner.[6][13] In FY26 the company booked ₹14.09 crore of pro charges from Vardhman Sales Agency and ₹7.01 crore of sales to Vardhman Recycling LLP; with smaller related-party sales the total was ₹21.2 crore, 10.9 percent of revenue, and the pro charges alone were 62 percent of the ₹22.74 crore services line.[4][6] We read that as a real dependency on one client and one related channel.

On the third, unit economics, the evidence is mixed but leans positive. The operating margin (EBITDA excluding other income, divided by revenue) rose from 9.0 percent in FY25 to 11.6 percent in FY26 while sales grew 30 percent. Freight and cartage fell from ₹6.64 crore to ₹4.51 crore even as volume rose, which supports management's claim that opening plants closer to the material cuts shipping costs.[3][4] Against that, employee cost doubled to ₹8.69 crore as the plants staffed up, and the gross margin on plain e-waste is only 15 to 18 percent by management's own account.[4][12]

The moat is narrow. The chemistry and machines are standard, and the managing director said on the May 2026 call that the process is the same across recyclers and the difference is know-how, for which patents will be filed after the pilot plant runs.[12] Larger unlisted rivals named on the call, BatX, Lohum and Attero, are already ahead in battery refining.[12] The regulatory moat is real but contested: producers including Samsung, LG, Havells, Daikin and Voltas have challenged the certificate floor in the Delhi High Court, and as of mid-2026 there was no final judgment.[26][29]

What the rules give the company, and what they could take away

The E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022 raised the recycling target for most producers to 70 percent of past sales for FY2025-26 and FY2026-27, and it rises to 80 percent from FY2027-28.[27][28] That makes demand for certified recycling grow by law. The 2024 amendment lets the regulator set a price band for certificates, with a floor of 30 percent of the environmental compensation payable for missing the target.[26][30] Namo's average realised price of ₹28 per kilogram sits above the consumer floor of ₹22 and below the IT floor of ₹34.[12][26] One source reads the floors as ranging from ₹8 to ₹22, another as ₹10 to ₹41, so the reader should treat the category floors as approximate until the regulator's schedule is read directly.[26][32]

If the court strikes the floor, the market price could fall toward earlier auction levels. One reverse auction saw bids as low as ₹5.90 per kilogram.[26][31] Our illustration, not a forecast: if Namo's ₹25 crore of EPR income were re-priced from ₹28 to ₹5.90 per kilogram on the same volume, it would fall to about ₹5.3 crore, a loss of about ₹19.7 crore of income against FY26 profit before tax of ₹20.0 crore.[3][12] The costs of collection do not fall with the certificate price, so the effect on profit could be large. That is why the court case is the main swing factor in this report.

Metal prices and the battery bet

Namo sells recovered metal, so its revenue moves with copper, aluminium and, for batteries, lithium, nickel and cobalt prices. The annual report names metal-price swings as a risk.[2] Management says black mass is sold at over 90 percent payables on the metal content, which ties its price to metal indexes rather than to a fixed rate. The company still holds ₹39.81 crore of inventory, about 97 days of cost of sales, so a fall in metal prices would hit the stock in hand before it hit new purchases.[4][12]

The battery plant is the growth story: management guided FY27 revenue of about ₹400 crore, half from batteries, and a later move into hydrometallurgy costing ₹50 to ₹60 crore, to be funded with debt after a pilot plant starts in December 2026 or January 2027.[12] Utilisation was about 10 percent in FY26 (1,200 tonnes), and management itself says sourcing, not capacity, is the limit.[12][13] This is the largest single swing in our scenarios.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 asks whether the business is running well on the measures that matter for this kind of company, not for companies in general. For a recycler that handles physical goods bought in advance, five measures decide the outcome: how full the plants are, what price the regulatory certificates fetch, how wide the margin is after freight, how fast stock and bills turn back into cash, and how secure the supply of material is. Each is scored 1.0 for PASS, 0.5 for WATCH and 0.0 for FAIL. The file passes the layer if the total is at least 3.0 of 5.0 and at most one KPI fails.[41]

The five were chosen because each one is where a recycler can fail while headline profit still looks fine. A plant that is half empty carries fixed costs. A certificate price set by a court is not set by the company. A margin that is eaten by freight does not scale. A stock that grows faster than sales hides cash. Supply that rests on one client or one related party can disappear with a contract.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has been unusually specific on calls, which makes it possible to check. The pattern over the last 18 months is that direction has been right and timing has not. The company grew every year, kept its bank debt close to zero and used the full IPO money as stated: ₹11.20 crore for the Nashik battery unit and ₹22.00 crore for working capital, with the remainder spent on general purposes and issue costs.[1] But growth, capacity dates, the battery share and the related-party promise were all missed or only partly kept.

The largest miss is a statement about related parties. In November 2025 an investor asked about transactions with Vardhman. The managing director said the business would be migrated to Namo and that the related-party transaction would be completely gone the following year.[13] On 18 August 2026 the board recommended shareholders approve related-party transactions with Vardhman Sales Agency for FY2026-27.[21] That does not prove a breach, since the migration may be gradual, but it is the opposite of what was said.

Another gap is the headline growth rate. In May 2025 management said it had been growing 45 to 50 percent a year and expected the pace to continue, and in May 2026 it targeted a 45 to 50 percent compound rate for three years.[14][15] FY26 revenue grew 29.9 percent. An analyst on the May 2026 call pointed out the shortfall against a guide of ₹220 to ₹230 crore; management said 35 days of Palwal production were lost while machines were moved, costing about ₹25 crore of revenue.[12] The explanation is plausible, but it is an explanation of a miss.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 reads the accounts for five years. FY22 and FY23 are standalone figures from the offer document, and FY24 to FY26 are consolidated, meaning the parent company and its 99.99 percent owned subsidiary Techeco Waste Management LLP are added together after removing trades between them.[9][11] The jump from standalone to consolidated happens at FY24, so growth for that year mixes more activity with a wider reporting scope, and we flag it rather than adjust it.

The picture is of a small company that has grown fast and become more profitable. Revenue rose from ₹44.78 crore in FY22 to ₹194.59 crore in FY26, a compound rate of 44 percent over four years, and profit after tax rose from ₹1.81 crore to ₹14.35 crore.[3][11] The operating margin roughly doubled, from 5.8 percent to 11.6 percent. The balance sheet is nearly debt-free after the ₹51.2 crore IPO in September 2024, which is why return on equity, at 14.9 percent, looks modest: shareholders' money has grown faster than profit.[4][5][25]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on assumptions that can be wrong. This chapter takes each estimate, names its weakest assumption, says what evidence would show it is wrong and when we will know. It then sets out the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, called the red-team bear case, and names the single claim that the whole conclusion depends on, the hinge.

Profit estimate: the weak point is volume at the battery plant

Our base case roughly doubles battery revenue, from about ₹18 crore to about ₹32 crore. That is utilisation of the Nashik plant near 20 percent, in a year when management itself says raw-material sourcing is the limit and FY26 utilisation was about 10 percent.[12][13] The bull case needs far more. Management's FY27 guide puts about ₹200 crore of its ₹400 crore in batteries, against ₹18 crore delivered. On the FY26 ratio of ₹12 crore of own recycling at 10 percent use, ₹200 crore would need more than the existing plant can do, which is why we treat it as unlikely. Evidence we are wrong: a battery line below ₹15 crore in the half to September 2026, or half-year revenue below about ₹115 crore. The half-year statement is expected after the 30 September close. The filing date had not been announced on 28 October 2026.

Certificate price: the weak point is the court

The base case assumes the floor of ₹22 and ₹34 per kilogram holds. The Delhi High Court has not decided the challenge by Samsung, LG, Havells, Daikin, Voltas and others, and in January 2026 it stayed one requirement for one petitioner.[26][29] Evidence we are wrong: a judgment quashing the price band, or a regulator revision of the compensation rates. No hearing date was found in the sources we read. A lower floor would hit the ₹25 crore EPR line first, as the illustration in chapter 2 showed. We keep this risk as its own 10 percent row in chapter 5 and not inside the bear row.

Cash conversion: the weak point is stock

The base case assumes operating cash flow improves with scale. Inventory of ₹39.81 crore is 97 days of cost of sales and rose 19 percent in FY26, partly because management buys ahead for the battery plant and last-quarter corporate discards.[4][14] Evidence we are wrong: a half-year cash flow statement with operating cash flow below ₹3 crore, or inventory above ₹45 crore. The half-year statement and the FY27 audited statements in May 2027 are the tests.

Related party and governance: the weak point is the Vardhman channel

A director's firm supplied ₹14.09 crore of the ₹22.74 crore of service income in FY26, and the board has asked shareholders to approve the same in FY27, against the November 2025 statement that it would end.[6][13][21] Other signals: three finance chiefs in about two years (the latest appointed 18 August 2026, with the previous one resigning effective 30 or 31 May 2026, nine to ten days after the FY26 accounts were signed), a Faridabad audit firm, one independent director replaced in March 2026, stock options granted at a ₹50 discount to market, and internal inconsistencies in the annual report such as a subsidiary profit shown as +₹2.06 crore in one place and −₹1.92 crore in another.[1][6][8][9][22][23] Evidence we are wrong: a signed-off wind-down of the Vardhman channel. Next test: the half-year related-party disclosure.

Exit: the weak point is liquidity

A position that cannot be sold cannot be protected by a stop. The median day trades about ₹0.93 crore, the free float is about ₹233 crore, foreign funds hold 0.00 percent and domestic funds 1.86 percent, and the stock gained 47.6 percent in about two weeks, during which the NSE asked the company for a clarification.[18][20][25][35] A rise driven by thin trade can reverse quickly. The ₹283 stop is 14.3 percent below the close and could be gapped through on a 20 percent band day. Evidence we are wrong: median daily value above ₹3 crore for a quarter, or inclusion in a main-board index after migration.

Multiple: the weak point is the 30 times

Our base multiple is 30 times, near the top of the peer range of 20.9 to 32.1 times, and it is applied to FY28 profit about a year ahead of the price date, while peer multiples are trailing. Both choices favour the stock. At 35 times the base would be ₹348, and at 25 times ₹249. 7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED. That means no purchase at any price in this cycle. The rule behind it is Layer 1 hard red line 3. Three-year operating cash flow over profit must be at least 0.25, or 0.40 for mature businesses without an order book. We apply 0.40 to Namo. The company has traded since 2014 and has no order book, so it is mature in the sense of the rule, even though it listed only 25 months ago. Its ratio is −0.02 on the latest audited basis and −0.38 to −0.45 on the other bases, so the verdict is the same at 0.25.[3][10][41]

We do not apply the relaxation for fast-growing manufacturers. The company has no order book. It books 88 percent of revenue as product sales of processed scrap. It calls itself a recycler. The verdict changes only in two ways. The first is a three-year ratio that reaches the floor. The second is a reviewer who reads the exemption in, which would move the file to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. A half-year result cannot do either alone. It is the first evidence to watch.

Here is what the ratio needs. Take the window FY25 to FY27. Profit would be ₹8.46 crore, ₹14.35 crore and our base ₹17.9 crore for FY27, a total of ₹40.7 crore. At 0.40 the three years need ₹16.3 crore of operating cash flow. FY25 and FY26 together banked minus ₹3.79 crore. So FY27 alone needs about ₹20.1 crore, which is more than the whole year's profit. At 0.25 the need is about ₹14.0 crore. The test gets easier a year later, when the weak FY25 drops out. For the window FY26 to FY28 the two later years need about ₹16.8 crore combined, against about ₹40.7 crore of expected profit, a conversion of 41 percent.
