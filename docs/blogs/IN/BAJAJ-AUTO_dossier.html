The question is practical: should a buyer act now, wait for a pullback, or reject the stock on this evidence? The answer comes from rules fixed in advance, so the verdict does not depend on how strong the brand feels on a given day. The company is Bajaj Auto Ltd, which makes motorcycles, scooters and three-wheelers and trades on the National Stock Exchange (NSE).

The analysis runs in three layers, and each one is a gate. Layer 1 is a set of yes-or-no floors covering the business, its cash and the price chart. If any floor fails, the stock is rejected and the later layers are not run. Layer 2 is a scorecard of operating measures, each marked healthy, watch or failing. Layer 3 is timing: how far the price sits above a verified level of support. Here Layer 1 fails, so Layer 2 and Layer 3 are reported for information but not scored.

The price terms come first. RSI, the relative strength index, is a 14-day gauge of how hard a stock has moved up or down. A reading below 30 is called oversold. It describes the speed of a fall and says nothing about when the fall will end. ATR, the average true range, measures a typical daily move, and it is the yardstick used to measure distance from support.

The chart terms follow. Stage 1 is the basing phase, when a stock that has fallen moves sideways. Stage 2 means the markup phase, when the price sits above rising averages. Stage 4 means the breakdown phase, when the price falls through a falling long-term average. A base is the sideways range a stock forms after a fall. Its top is the pivot, the level a move has to clear. A breakout is a close above that pivot on heavy volume.

A few trading terms come next. An entry zone is the band of prices where a buyer would start buying, and the rules set it from support. Asymmetry is the ratio of the reward to the risk, calculated as (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). A measured move is a target built by adding the height of a base to its breakout point.

The financial terms are the last group. ROCE, return on capital employed, shows how much profit a company earns on the money tied up in it. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a measure of operating cash profit before financing and accounting charges. EPS, earnings per share, is profit divided by the number of shares. The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) divides the share price by EPS.

Debt-to-equity (D/E) is borrowing divided by the shareholders' stake. Operating margin (OPM) is the share of sales left after operating costs. Free cash flow is the cash left after a business pays for the capital spending it needs. A buyback is the company buying its own shares back from shareholders, which reduces the cash it keeps.

Cash conversion closes the list. Cash from operations (CFO) divided by net profit after tax (PAT) over three years shows whether reported profit turns into cash. A ratio near 1.0 means it does. The dossier shows both a consolidated view, which includes subsidiaries, and a standalone view, which covers the parent alone. Where the two disagree, the text says which one the rule uses. 4.3 Momentum gate

The momentum gate is a separate test the scoring file requires every candidate to pass, Test A or Test B, before any allocation. It is computed in code from the frozen price snapshot, so it contains no judgment. Test A asks whether a stock is already in an uptrend that still leads the market. Test B asks whether a stock is turning up out of a base, with volume and relative strength improving. A stock that fails both is not allocated, however good its business.

Test A fails on its first condition. The structure condition requires the price above the 50-day average and the 50-day average above the 200-day average. The price is ₹9,778, the 50-day average is ₹11,431 and the 200-day average is ₹10,223, so the price is below the 50-day average. The other Test A conditions pass. The 200-day average is not falling, the 63-day relative strength is positive at 3.24 points, and the price is 0.56 ATR above its support, well inside the 1.2 ATR limit. Those passes do not rescue Test A, because the structure condition is the one the test is built around.

Test B fails on two conditions. The 28-day relative strength is negative, at minus 11.05 points, against a required rising reading. The 20-day volume is 0.86 times its average against a required 1.0 times. Three other Test B conditions pass. The 50-day average is up 1.78 percent over 21 sessions, against a limit of a fall of no more than 0.5 percent. The price is within 1.0 ATR of the base pivot. The 63-day volume dry-up ratio is 0.27, against a limit of 0.9. Those passes show the stock has gone quiet, which is typical of a basing stock. They do not show buyers have returned, which is what Test B needs.

The gate's result is NO_MOMENTUM. The scoring file marks the thresholds as provisional, so the result is a rule outcome to respect rather than a precise measurement. The direction is clear, though. Both tests fail on the things that matter most for timing: trend structure and the return of volume. The gate does not claim the stock will fall further. It says the evidence for an entry is not there yet.

4.4 Asymmetry and target anchoring

Asymmetry is the last gate, and it answers whether the reward justifies the risk. The formula is (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). The scoring file sets a floor of 3.0 for the core bucket and 3.5 for the tactical bucket. The target must be anchored to something that can be checked: either a technical measured move from the base, or a forward EPS estimate multiplied by a three-year median P/E. A broker's consensus target does not qualify under the rule, because the reader cannot check how it was built.

This dossier could not verify either anchor. The ledger records the base low but not the top of the base, so a measured move from the base height cannot be computed. A forward EPS estimate could not be verified in a form the sources publish, and the three-year median P/E is not published in any fetched source. Both searches returned no usable figure. Without a target, the ratio cannot be computed, and the rule records the outcome as FAIL_ASYMMETRY. Chapter 6 sets out the inputs and the stop levels that would be needed.

The gate does not say the stock is worth less than its price. It says the case for upside has not been written in a checkable form, so the reward side of a trade cannot be measured. A reader who has a view on the company's earnings can form it, but the rule does not accept it as a target, and the verdict does not rest on it.

5 Layer 2 KPI scorecard

Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five measures that drive a particular business, each marked healthy, watch or failing. For an automobile maker, these are the measures a manager would watch: volume growth and market share, the margin, the export mix, the progress of the electric range, and how the company deploys its cash. The scorecard shows whether the operating engine is running well.

Layer 2 is not scored here because Layer 1 fails. The rule runs the scorecard only for a candidate that has passed the hard floors, and a traffic-light result for a rejected stock would look like an endorsement the rule does not give. 5.1 Why the scorecard is not scored

The three-layer system gives each layer a separate job. Layer 1 decides whether the business and its chart are good enough to consider. Layer 2 decides whether the operating engine is healthy enough to justify the capital. Layer 3 decides whether the price is close enough to support to act. If Layer 2 were scored on a rejected name, a reader might take a green row as a reason to buy, even though the price and cash gates have already said no.

The readings below remain useful for a reader who wants to track the company. They show the operating facts a future scorecard would use and where those facts stand today. Each reading comes from a fetched source, and each is dated.

6 Layer 3 entry posture

Layer 3 is the timing gate. It asks how far the price sits above the nearest verified support, measured in units of average true range, and it maps that distance onto a ladder of entry bands. A stock right at support can be bought with a stop just beneath it. A stock far above support is extended, and a buyer who chases it pays for a move that may already be spent. Layer 3 is not evaluated here, because Layer 1 failed. The figures below show where the price stands and what the rule would need.

6.1 Where the price sits against support

The nearest support in the ledger is the 63-day base low and the 20-day swing low, both at ₹9,637. The close of ₹9,778 is ₹141 above that level. With the ATR at ₹252.22, the distance is 0.56 ATR. On the scoring file's ladder, a distance under 1.0 ATR is the standard band and under 0.5 ATR is the full-size band, so the stock is in the standard band on distance alone. Distance is only the first condition. The band tells you the price is near a level, not that the level is strong. A level earns that status after the price has bounced from it and held on later tests, and the ledger does not record such a test.

The price also sits below the averages that define the trend. It is 10.4 percent below the 20-day average, 14.5 percent below the 50-day average, and 4.4 percent below the 200-day average, which is itself rising. Those gaps show a recovery attempt inside a longer decline, not a confirmed uptrend. The stock is 21.6 percent below its 52-week high of ₹12,470 and 15.2 percent above its 52-week low of ₹8,491.5. Those readings are context, and none is a support level on its own.

8 Fatal flaws and red team

A red-team review asks for the strongest honest case against the verdict. Here the verdict is a rejection, so the review asks whether the rejection is too harsh and whether the company has a flaw that would stop it ever passing. Four flaws matter. Each is set out below with its evidence and its answer.

8.1 The cash basis might be the wrong test

The first flaw concerns the rule itself. A vehicle maker with a captive finance arm reports cash flow that includes the arm's lending. The consolidated cash test therefore penalises the group for the growth of a finance book, which is a legitimate business. The strongest version of this argument is that the standalone test measures the manufacturing engine, and the finance arm should be judged separately. The evidence supports part of that. Standalone CFO has run at between 0.89 and 1.00 times standalone profit in each of the last three years, and the three-year standalone ratio is 0.93. Consolidated borrowing sits almost entirely outside the parent, which fits the finance-arm explanation.

The answer is that the rule was written to be strict, and a strict rule should not be relaxed for one company after the fact. A reader who chooses the standalone view should say so openly. The dossier also records a gap in its own evidence: the asset quality of the finance book, such as non-performing loans or credit costs, is not in the research record. The standalone reading therefore cannot be judged on the risk side of the finance book. Even on the standalone view the stock fails the technical floors, so the verdict stands.

8.2 Market share is slipping at home

The second flaw matters most for the long-run case. Domestic wholesale volume fell 12 percent in September, and the company's share of Vahan registrations fell in the first half of FY27. The bear case is that the company is growing through exports and the electric line while losing the core commuter market to rivals. If that continues, the margin that funds the business will come under pressure over time. FADA data for June and July shows the same direction, with Bajaj's share falling by 6.37 and 4.16 percent respectively.

The answer is that the company's first-half wholesale volume is still up 24 percent, and the home market is growing, so the share loss is relative rather than a fall in sales. The Chetak capacity plan and the electric share of about 30 percent of domestic business give a second engine. Vahan registrations for September, up 13.6 percent, point the other way. The bear case is credible but not proven, and the results on 27 October are the test.

8.3 Exports carry currency and geographic risk

The third flaw is concentration. Exports were about 51 percent of units in the June quarter, and the company's growth in that quarter was led by exports. A slowdown in one export market, a currency move against the rupee, or a logistics disruption could cut volume quickly. The Sahi article names Latin America and the Middle East as export destinations exposed to geopolitical and logistical friction, Sahi (as of 21 Jul 2026).

The answer is that the export franchise is broad, and the June quarter showed the upside, with exports up 54 percent. The same markets that drove the quarter could reverse. A reader should treat the export lead as a source of volatility as well as growth.

8.4 Oversold is not the same as a bottom

The fourth flaw is the temptation the RSI creates. At 23.2, the RSI says the stock has fallen unusually fast, and many investors read that as a sign of a bounce. The rule does not work that way. The ledger shows the stock fell 17.7 percent in a month and 12.8 percent in two weeks, and the market was weak. A stock can stay oversold for weeks while a base forms, or it can keep falling. The RSI measures the speed of the move, not the direction of the next one.

The answer is the chart rule. A bounce is not a breakout, and the rule waits for the breakout. The stock could bounce tomorrow and still fail the core test if it does not clear its 50-day average on rising volume. A buyer who acts on the RSI alone is buying a hope rather than a structure, and the rule exists to prevent that trade.
