What the company does

Think of TBO as a wholesale market for travel. A small travel agent in Dubai, Manila or Madrid has customers who want a hotel in Bali or a flight to Delhi. The agent cannot call thousands of hotels and airlines one by one. So the agent logs into the TBO website, searches the stock that TBO has gathered from suppliers, books it, and pays TBO. TBO keeps a slice of the price and passes the rest to the supplier. The company says it serves about 50,000 travel agents, lists more than 10 lakh hotels and reaches about 150 countries.[3]

A short history

The company began in 2006 as Tek Travels Private Limited and started in India with air tickets.[19][20] It grew into a global platform and, over time, moved its weight from flights towards hotels, because hotels earn a higher share of each sale. The chain of events that matters to an investor is short.[21]

  • 2006: founding as Tek Travels by Ankush Nijhawan and Gaurav Bhatnagar.[19]
  • May 2024: public listing. The prospectus is dated 13 May 2024 and the company raised ₹400 crore of new money, which it later reported as fully spent on technology and subsidiaries.[18][3]
  • 2025: purchase of Jumbonline, a Spanish hotel supplier, at a cost of about ₹227 crore.[3]
  • September 2025: agreement to buy Classic Vacations LLC of the United States from The Najafi Companies for up to USD 125 million (about ₹1,100 crore).[9][10] The deal closed and Classic became part of the group from 1 October 2025.[3]
  • July 2026: first-quarter results for the year to March 2027, which the company itself called its toughest quarter in two years.[6]

How TBO earns money

TBO's revenue is a share of what agents spend, called gross transaction value (GTV). The share it keeps is the take rate. On airline tickets the net commission is small, about 2 to 3%, while hotel mark-ups run higher, about 7 to 8%, according to the credit-rating agency.[3] That is why the hotel share of GTV matters. It rose from 59% in FY25 to 64% in FY26.[2] Revenue divided by GTV rose from 5.64% to 7.27% in the same period, partly because Classic Vacations sells higher-priced, higher-margin trips.[2]

The Classic Vacations deal in plain words

Classic Vacations is a United States wholesaler of luxury trips. It sells to premium travel advisors, not to the public. TBO paid USD 110.36 million at closing, about ₹1,050 crore, using a USD 70 million (₹630 crore) term loan plus its own cash.[3] Classic earned operating profit of about USD 11.2 million on revenue of USD 111 million in calendar 2024.[3] The strategic idea is to sell high-ticket travel to advisors who care more about service than about price. The management letter says full integration should finish in FY2027.[2]

Why the business is strong or weak

TBO's strength is breadth. It joins thousands of suppliers to tens of thousands of agents on one platform, and every extra supplier makes the platform more useful to agents and the reverse. That is a network effect, and it is hard for a new entrant to copy. The weakness is that the business sits in the middle of a value chain and earns thin margins on a very large flow of money. Small changes in take rate, currency or payment timing move profit a lot.

Industry and competition

The global business-to-business travel market has been sized by commercial research houses at about USD 35.9 billion in 2026 with a forecast growth rate of 17.5% a year to 2035.[27] A separate wholesale distribution study puts the 2025 market at USD 80.35 billion, up from USD 52.18 billion in 2022.[28] The two studies define the market differently and differ by more than twofold, so neither should be read as exact. A third source sizes the narrower hotel bed-bank segment at USD 4.8 billion in 2025, growing 6.3% a year.[29] These are paid-research summaries read from public snippets and not audited data.

The named rivals are global: Hotelbeds, WebBeds, Expedia Partner Solutions, Booking Holdings, and the airline-ticket systems Amadeus, Sabre and Travelport.[30] In India the listed consumer-facing players are MakeMyTrip, ixigo, EaseMyTrip, Thomas Cook India and IRCTC, but these sell mainly to the public, not to agents. TBO's own advantage is a strong position in air and hotel for agents outside India, with 54% of GTV from abroad.[3] Market share data for TBO against the global bed-banks is N/A. No retrieved source gives a reliable share, and guessing would mislead.

Credit view and ownership

CARE Ratings holds TBO's bank facilities at A-, outlook Stable, as reaffirmed on 15 December 2025 after the Classic closing, and it removed an earlier rating watch.[3] The agency listed three negative triggers: a take rate below 3.50%, gearing above 0.75 times, and a bad outcome in the FEMA case.[3] The agency's April 2025 note also praised experienced promoters and the healthy capital structure.[4] The promoter group holds 44.40%, unchanged for nine quarters, and reports no pledged shares.[1][16][17] The largest promoter names are LAP Travel Private Limited at 21.60%, Gaurav Bhatnagar at 17.33%, Manish Dhingra at 4.87% and Ankush Nijhawan at 0.60%.[16] An independent pledge check through the exchange filing itself was not retrieved, so the no-pledge claim rests on two secondary reports and is marked as such.[15]

Case note on ownership flows. Foreign institutions have cut their stake from 42.79% in June 2024 to 28.93% in June 2026. Domestic institutions raised theirs from 6.61% to 21.09% in the same time.[1] Money has moved from one type of holder to another and the total free float has not shrunk, but the shift tells us that overseas buyers have been the sellers on strength. A reader should not treat the buying by domestic funds as proof of cheapness.

Working capital and the receivables question

Screener shows debtor days of 537, 867, 853 and 724 for FY23 to FY26.[1] Those numbers look alarming, but they are inflated because debtor days divide receivables by revenue, and TBO's revenue is net of what it pays suppliers while its receivables are gross. The annual report does not give a clean bad-debt figure in the pages read, so credit losses on receivables are N/A here. A large book that grows faster than sales is still the most important thing to watch.

Shareholders

The promoter group holds 44.40%, unchanged. Foreign institutions hold 28.93%, down from 42.79%, domestic institutions hold 21.09%, up from 6.61%, and the public holds 4.02%, spread across 37,774 holders.[1] The report's related-party note shows that TBO's own subsidiary in Dubai, Tek Travels DMCC, did ₹1,414 million of sales and ₹3,632 million of purchases with the parent in FY26.[2] These are disclosed and arms-length by the company's statement, but they are worth knowing.

Management and capital allocation

The board has a non-executive independent chairman, Ravindra Dhariwal, and the founders run the company as joint managing directors. The chief technology officer, Akshat Verma, is a whole-time director, and Vikas Jain is the chief financial officer.[2] Capital allocation since listing has gone mainly into acquisitions: about ₹227 crore for Jumbonline and about ₹1,050 crore for Classic.[3] The Classic term loan is secured on the shares of the United States subsidiaries and by a parent guarantee of up to USD 77 million.[2] The company pays no dividend. A promoter group that holds 44% with no pledge, an experienced board and an A- credit rating are positives. A pattern of buying growth with debt, in a year when cash from operations was negative, is the counterweight.

Regulatory exposure

An Enforcement Directorate complaint dated 13 September 2023 led to a show-cause notice dated 19 September 2023. It alleges that TBO allowed foreign agents to pay in rupees through third parties, in breach of FEMA to the extent of ₹493.70 million. The company itself identified total contraventions of ₹712.25 million.[2][11][13] The maximum penalty under the law is three times the amount involved, which would be about ₹214 crore on ₹712.25 million. The statutory auditor has added an emphasis-of-matter paragraph, which draws attention to the matter without qualifying the opinion.[2] Contingent liabilities in the annual report totalled ₹452.16 million at March 2026 against ₹199.47 million a year before.[2] The worst case of about ₹214 crore is roughly 73% of FY26 profit before tax of ₹293 crore (214 / 293). That is material to one year of earnings, although the company held ₹1,591.8 crore of cash and liquid investments at March 2026, and the penalty actually imposed could be far lower. The regulator is also not sympathetic: the RBI declining post-facto approval in April and September 2025 shows the regulator has not been sympathetic.[12]

Test B also fails. The 50-day average is rising and relative strength is turning up, and volume dried up during the base, so three conditions pass. But the stock is 4.42 ATR from the base pivot against a limit of 1.0, and today's volume is below average where the test wants a rise. The verdict from the gate is NO_MOMENTUM. A name that fails both tests is not allocated, however good its business.[34]

How it was calculated

Each case multiplies an estimate of profit per share for FY27 by an assumed multiple. Base: ₹320 crore of profit divided by 10.87 crore shares is ₹29.4 per share. At 55 times earnings that is ₹1,620. Bull: ₹380 crore gives ₹35.0, and at 60 times that is ₹2,100. Bear: ₹270 crore gives ₹24.8, and at 40 times that is ₹992. Weights of 50%, 20% and 30% give 0.5 × 1,620 + 0.2 × 2,100 + 0.3 × 992 = 810 + 420 + 298 = ₹1,528.

Estimate: trade asymmetry if the stock breaks out

The number

The reward-to-risk ratio is about 4.96 to 1, from a stop of ₹1,569.90 and a technical target of ₹2,144.90. This is measured from the ledger close of ₹1,666.40 and would clear the tactical floor of 3.5 on paper. It is not a usable entry, because the stock fails the Layer 1 posture gate at that price, a breakout entry above ₹1,772.50 scores only 1.84, below the floor, and only a retest entry at or below about ₹1,697.70 qualifies (4.74 at ₹1,670).

How it was calculated

The stop is the lowest of the 20-day swing low (₹1,600.00), the 20-day average (₹1,678.89) and the 50-day average (₹1,669.98), less half an ATR of ₹60.21, which gives 1,600.00 − 30.11 = ₹1,569.89, or ₹1,569.90 rounded. Risk from the ledger close is 1,666.40 − 1,569.90 = ₹96.50. The target uses a measured move: the 52-week high of ₹1,772.50 plus the height of the base, 1,772.50 − 1,400.10 = ₹372.40, gives ₹2,144.90. Reward is 2,144.90 − 1,666.40 = ₹478.50. Then 478.50 / 96.50 = 4.96.

The inputs

The inputs are the ledger's swing low, moving averages, ATR, 52-week high and 63-day base low.[31] The stop rule for the tactical bucket and the asymmetry floor of 3.5 come from the repository scoring configuration.[32] The ratio is computed from today's close, but a real entry would be at the breakout above ₹1,772.50, where risk is larger: 1,772.50 − 1,569.90 = ₹202.60 and reward is 2,144.90 − 1,772.50 = ₹372.40, a ratio of 1.84. That is below the floor.

The range

At the ledger close of ₹1,666.40 the ratio is 4.96, but that price is not buyable under the gate. At a breakout buy above ₹1,772.50 it falls to 1.84, so it fails the 3.5 floor. Solving the ratio for entry price, only entries at or below about ₹1,697.70 meet 3.5 (at ₹1,670 the ratio is 4.74; at ₹1,700 it is 3.42). A breakout entry above ₹1,772.50 never qualifies on this target.

If the number is off

A target built from a measured move is not a forecast. If the base is judged to start at ₹1,600 instead of ₹1,400, the target falls to about ₹1,945 and the ratio drops to about 2.9, below the floor. The estimate therefore depends on the base definition.

Why these estimates might be wrong

A fair test of any report is to argue against it. This chapter builds the strongest honest case against the conclusion, which is that the stock should be rejected for now, and against the business case behind the numbers. It also names the one claim on which everything hangs.

Fatal-flaw checks

The list below tests whether any single weakness could break the investment case outright. Each item is a way the business or the share price could fail. None of them changes the verdict today, because the stock is already rejected on price posture. The cash conversion and FEMA items would, if they worsened, turn a price-only rejection into a rejection of the business as well.

  • Cash conversion. Profit of ₹244 crore against operating cash of minus ₹21 crore in FY26 may be a one-off from the acquisition year, or it may be the start of a pattern. Debtor days of 724 are extreme on any basis.[1] If receivables stay high while payables fall, the float that funds the business could reverse.
  • FEMA case. The cap is three times the amount involved, about ₹214 crore on ₹712.25 million (3 × 712.25 = 2,136.75 million). That is about 73% of FY26 profit before tax of ₹293 crore, so the worst case is material, though it is a cap and not a forecast, and the final penalty is N/A until adjudication. The regulator has already declined post-facto approval twice.[12]
  • Take-rate quality. The rise from 5.64% to 7.27% comes largely from buying Classic, not from better pricing. Organically the take rate is slightly lower after price cuts.[6]
  • Geographic risk. Management called Q1 the toughest quarter in two years because of the Middle East war, and the Middle East regional growth figure is N/A in the recorded sources.[6] If the conflict persists, the gap between reported and constant-currency growth (about 11 points) could narrow and reveal slower underlying growth.
  • Valuation. At 67 times earnings and 11.7 times book value (book value ₹143 per share), the stock offers little cushion. Foreign holders have cut their stake by about 14 points in two years.[1]
  • Integration. Classic carried an operating profit of about USD 11 million on USD 111 million of revenue. The group is paying a high multiple for a business whose integration is still in progress.[3]
  • Basis gap. The standalone parent earns 6.85% return on equity while the group earns 17.4%. The group figure leans on subsidiaries, mostly overseas, which are harder for an outsider to audit.[2]
The hinge claim. The whole case rests on one claim: that TBO's receivables are collectible and the FY26 cash shortfall is timing, not loss. If that holds, the business is high-quality and the rejection is only about price. If it does not, the rejection is about the business too. The evidence read supports timing: payables also grew, the cash balance is rising, and an A- rating was reaffirmed in December 2025.[3] It is not proof, and a full FY27 cash-flow statement is the test.

Where this report may be too harsh

A reader could argue that the rejection is purely technical and that a stock outperforming the index by 17.7% over 63 days should not be rejected for being 3.58 rupees under a moving average. That is a fair point. The rule exists because a close that cannot be defended with a tight stop forces a wide stop and a poor reward-to-risk, and the distance to real support here is large. The market downtrend adds to it.
