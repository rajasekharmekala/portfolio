1. What the company does

This chapter says who pays Airtel, for what, and how those payments become profit. It ends with the plain meanings of the terms used later.

Bharti Airtel Limited trades on the NSE as BHARTIARTL and on the BSE as 532454. It sells mobile phone service, home broadband, television, business connectivity and mobile money. At 30 June 2026 it had 681 million customers in 15 countries: 492 million in India and 189 million in Africa[2]. It also controls Indus Towers (consolidated since 19 November 2024), which rents out mobile towers, so the group is paid by phone users, by households, by companies and by other phone operators who share its towers.

Most of the money comes from India mobile and from Africa. In the June 2026 quarter, India mobile service earned ₹29,929 crore of segment revenue, Africa ₹17,566 crore, Airtel Business ₹5,665 crore, tower rental through Indus ₹8,525 crore and Homes ₹2,288 crore, before ₹6,304 crore of sales between the group's own units was removed[1]. Group revenue was ₹58,539 crore, up 18.4 percent on the year[2].

How India mobile earns money

India mobile customers pay monthly for calls and data. About 92 percent are prepaid, which means they top up in advance, and 30 million are postpaid, which means they receive a monthly bill[3]. The key number is average revenue per user, written ARPU, the monthly revenue per customer. It was ₹264 in the June 2026 quarter, against ₹250 a year earlier and ₹209 in March 2024[3][9]. A rise in ARPU adds revenue and almost all of it reaches profit, because the network is already built, so ARPU is the first number to watch.

Management says it will not wait for a headline tariff rise. The India chief executive told the August 2026 call that there is 'substantial headroom' in the current customer base through upgrades to unlimited plans and postpaid[4]. The Executive Vice Chairman said in May 2026 that 'the price architecture in this country is broken', because unlimited data is capped at about ₹340 to ₹350 a month while entry packs start at ₹199[7].

Africa, Homes, Business and towers

Africa sells mobile voice, data and mobile money in 14 countries. Airtel Africa plc is listed in London and Lagos, and Airtel raised its share of it from 62.62 percent to 78.93 percent in the June 2026 quarter by issuing 146,761,335 new Airtel shares at ₹1,923 each to Indian Continental Investment Limited, a promoter-group company, in exchange for its Airtel Africa shares[1]. Homes sells fibre and fixed wireless broadband to 14.7 million households. Airtel Business sells connectivity, cloud and security to companies. Indus Towers earns rent from phone operators for tower space[3].

The group is owned by Bharti Telecom Limited (39.51 percent in June 2026), which is itself held by the Bharti family company and Singtel, and by other promoter-group companies; together the promoter group held 50.07 percent[18][20].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • A crore is ten million rupees. ₹1,000 crore is ₹10,000 million, and one lakh crore is one trillion rupees.
  • ARPU, average revenue per user, is monthly revenue per mobile customer. Constant currency means growth restated at last year's exchange rates, so currency moves drop out.
  • Leases are long-term rent contracts, mostly for towers and fibre, that accounting rules show as debt. Deferred payment liabilities are instalments owed to the government for spectrum (radio licences) and for past licence-fee disputes known as AGR dues.
  • Non-controlling interests, NCI, are the parts of subsidiaries owned by outsiders.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

This chapter asks what protects the business and what threatens it, with the evidence on pricing, Africa, spending needs and governance.

The case for the business is steady, growing cash from a service that customers pay for every month. Revenue rose from ₹1,39,145 crore in FY23 to ₹2,10,973 crore in FY26, a rate of 14.9 percent a year on the audited revenue line[11][5]. The company calls its ARPU 'industry leading'[2]. The case against it is capital intensity, large government instalments, a currency-driven part of its growth and a chart that has not yet turned.

Pricing: the lever that has not been pulled

ARPU has climbed from ₹193 in March 2023 to ₹264 in June 2026, a rise of 37 percent in about three years, with most of the rise between March 2024 (₹209) and December 2024 (₹245)[11][9][3]. The latest quarter had one extra day, which lifts the figure by about one percent[4]. In the quarter to March 2026 ARPU slipped to ₹257 from ₹259, so the path is not a straight line[3].

The investment case needs further ARPU growth without a general price rise. Management said on 5 August 2026 that 'the longer-term pricing architecture of the industry still needs to be repaired'[4]. We found no announced rise, though an analyst on the November 2025 call expected one within three quarters, so we do not count on it[17]. A general price rise would be a bonus that our central case leaves out.

Africa: strong growth, flattered by currency

Airtel Africa reported revenue growth of 31.0 percent in US dollars for the June 2026 quarter, of which 21.1 points were real growth at constant exchange rates[22]. The difference is currency. The Nigerian naira strengthened from 1,585 to 1,367 per dollar between the two June quarters, and the company says macroeconomic tailwinds supported currency appreciation[22]. The company's sensitivity to a one percent dollar move is $63 million to $65 million of revenue and $31 million to $33 million of EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), with no period stated in the extract we read[22].

Airtel reports Africa in rupees, and a dollar cost 11 percent more rupees between the two quarters, an average of ₹85.42 in June 2025 against ₹94.80 in June 2026[3]. So Africa's rupee revenue rose 45 percent while its dollar revenue rose 31 percent and its real growth was 21 percent. Group growth of 18.4 percent therefore includes a currency boost that would reverse if the rupee recovered or the naira slid. Our poor case in Chapter 5 is only a proxy for this: it assumes slower growth and a thinner margin, and it does not model the currency move or turn it into parent earnings per share.

Capital needs, government instalments and governance

Capital spending is large: ₹47,522 crore in FY26 and ₹13,386 crore in the June 2026 quarter, about 22.5 and 22.9 percent of revenue[3]. The group also owes the government spectrum and AGR instalments: ₹8,612 crore was paid on 31 March 2026, and ₹7,934 crore falls due within a year (₹2,073 crore for spectrum and ₹5,861 crore for AGR); AGR instalments then rise to ₹6,330 crore in the next year and ₹22,195 crore over the three years after[5]. The annual report lists ₹19,184 crore of contingent liabilities, of which ₹9,767 crore are DoT and other regulatory demands that the company contests[5]. Governance evidence is cleaner: the auditor's opinion on the June 2026 results is unmodified, Crisil rates Airtel 'AAA/Stable', and the promoter-group shares are declared unpledged[1][20][18].
