Pennar Industries (NSE: PENIND) makes buildings, tubes and machine parts for other manufacturers. The verdict is REJECTED, so the stock goes to the archive. It fails Layer 1, the hard-gate test that comes first, on both its financial and momentum tests. The balance sheet fails because debt-to-equity means total borrowings divided by shareholders' money, and the figure is 1.00 against a limit below 0.70. The return test passes narrowly: return on capital employed (ROCE) measures profit earned on all the capital, and 15.2 percent clears the 15.0 floor.

How the verdict was reached

The underwriting rulebook sorts every stock through three layers. Layer 1 is a set of hard gates, and each gate is pass or fail. If any gate fails, the stock is rejected at once. The later layers are then not run. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five measures that matter for the business. Layer 3 checks whether the price is close enough to a support level to buy.

The financial part of Layer 1 asks three questions of the reported numbers. Is the return on capital at least 15 percent? Is debt below 0.70 times shareholders' money? Does the company turn its profit into cash? Each question has a fixed threshold, so the answer does not depend on how the company is described in the news. Chapter 3 works through the numbers.

The technical part asks whether the price is in a clean uptrend and whether the stock is beating its benchmark index. The ledger calculates these results from daily closing prices, up to the session date of 9 October 2026. Prices and averages are never taken from web pages. Chapter 4 sets out the technical results.

The verdict here is REJECTED because the financial part and the technical part of Layer 1 both fail. Rejection means the stock is archived, not that the company is a bad business. The rulebook keeps the two questions apart on purpose. A sound company can still be a poor entry point, and a weak balance sheet can make a sound company a poor holding.

What the company does

Pennar makes parts and buildings for other companies, not for shoppers. Its best-known product is the pre-engineered building (PEB), a steel frame cut and welded in a factory, shipped in pieces and bolted together on site. That method is quicker and cheaper than building with brick, so it suits warehouses, factories and data centres. The company's website describes "eight manufacturing facilities" in a pan-India presence [1], while the rating agency counts 14 facilities, 11 in India and three overseas [2]. The two counts do not match and neither source explains the gap, so this report shows both.

The group describes itself as a multi-location, multi-product engineering company [3]. The rating report lists eight business divisions: PEB, precision tubes, process heating, hydraulics, engineering services, body-in-white (the car body parts made for vehicle makers), steel and railways [2]. Precision tubes are accurately sized steel pipes. Process heating covers boilers and heat-recovery equipment for factories. Hydraulics covers the pressure-driven cylinders and pumps used inside machines. In the June 2026 call, management said engineering services grew well in the quarter and that its US sales team was closing more work [4].

Revenue mix shows where the money comes from. CARE says PEB revenue reached ₹1,570 crore in FY26 [2], against total operating income of ₹3,633.72 crore [2]. Working: 1,570 ÷ 3,633.72 = 0.43, so PEB is about 43 percent of the business. The rest comes from the other divisions, and management says its US PEB and engineering services businesses are growing faster than the group average [4]. The sales figure in the Screener table is ₹3,620 crore [3], a few crores lower, because the two sources define income slightly differently.

In FY26 the company also bought assets from Telko Enterprises, Inc. and Telko Properties, LLC in the United States, for about US$14 million, roughly ₹130 crore [2]. CARE links that deal, a guarantee given for a joint venture and higher short-term borrowing to the rise in debt, and that debt is the central problem of chapter 3. CARE also says the promoters have more than four decades of experience in engineered steel products [2]. The company is listed on both the NSE and the BSE [3]. The next chapter asks whether the business is getting stronger or only getting bigger.

Why the business is strong or weak

Growth is the first strength. Sales reached ₹3,620 crore in the year to March 2026, and the three-year compound growth rate is 8 percent [3]. A compound growth rate is the steady yearly rate that would take the starting figure to the finishing figure, so 8 percent means sales grew by about 8 percent a year on average. Profit grew faster. Net profit rose from ₹98 crore in March 2024 to ₹139 crore in March 2026, and Screener's three-year compound rate for profit is 21 percent [3]. That rate is Screener's own calculation over a three-year window, so it is not a rate between the two March figures quoted in this sentence. Profit growing faster than sales means the company is keeping more of each rupee it earns, which is the core of management's margin plan.

The weak spots are execution and returns. Management said its Tech Pennar division was the weak spot, because the order book was thin and execution slips cost it customers [4]. On returns, management said a return on capital employed of 20 percent and a return on equity of 12 percent are "lower than what we would traditionally want to see" [4] [9]. The sources do not explain the basis difference between the two, so it is treated as an unreconciled source conflict, and the gate result does not depend on it. Two cash-related terms explain much of the debt story. working capital means the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, net of what the company owes its suppliers. Management says it is keeping working capital tight [4]. It also says working capital borrowing was used at 85 to 90 percent for the 12 months to August 2026 [2], which leaves little room to spare.

capital expenditure means money spent on new plants and equipment rather than on running costs. CARE says the company's ratings are constrained by debt-funded capital expenditure [2], and management says its capacity spending has already been deployed [4]. The spending is real, but it was paid for with borrowed money, which is why the leverage test matters more than the order book for the verdict.

The number

The company would need about ₹348 crore less borrowing, or about ₹497 crore more net worth, to bring debt-to-equity down to 0.70 on the narrow measure.

How it was calculated

The borrowing ceiling is 0.70 × ₹1,163 crore = ₹814 crore, and 1,162 − 814 = 348. The net worth needed is 1,162 ÷ 0.70 = ₹1,660 crore, and 1,660 − 1,163 = 497. Each step holds the other figure fixed.

The inputs

Borrowings of ₹1,162 crore, equity capital of ₹67 crore and reserves of ₹1,096 crore, all for March 2026 [3]. The 0.70 limit comes from the underwriting rulebook.

The range

On CARE's wider debt of ₹1,667 crore [2], the cut needed would be about ₹853 crore (1,667 − 814). Management said the promoters have committed about ₹50 crore of new capital, of which ₹20 crore is already in [4]. That covers only part of the gap. Management also expects debt-to-equity to get close to 0.7 by the end of the year [4], which is a target, not a reported figure. It is not known from the sources whether that ₹20 crore is already in the March 2026 reserves. If it is not, net worth would be about ₹1,183 crore and the ratio about 0.98 (1,162 ÷ 1,183), which is still a fail.

If the number is off

The estimate moves with the balance sheet, not with the share price. If profit is kept in the business, reserves rise and the gap narrows. If acquisitions or working capital borrowing rise, the gap widens. The estimate should be redone with each new balance sheet.

Layer 1 disposition: the hard gates and the verdict

Layer 1 is the first of three underwriting layers. It is a set of hard gates that must all be passed, and each gate is pass or fail. If any gate fails, the stock is rejected and the two later layers, the business scorecard (Layer 2) and the entry-timing check (Layer 3), are not run. asymmetry means how much a stock could gain compared with how much it could lose from the entry price. The rulebook requires a verified target before the asymmetry test can pass, and that test is covered below.

The financial part of Layer 1 has three tests, set out in chapter 3. Return on capital employed passes at 15.2 against a floor of 15.0. Debt-to-equity fails at 1.00 against a limit below 0.70. The three-year cash flow ratio passes at 1.92. A failed test in any part is enough to reject the stock. The technical part was also evaluated from the ledger, even though the financial part had already failed, because the ledger already holds the data. Its Stage 2 label passes, but the 200-day average is falling, and both momentum tests fail, so the technical gate fails with the verdict NO_MOMENTUM.

The asymmetry test was not run. The financial part failed, so under the rule applied here the stock is rejected at that point and the target was not verified. Layer 2 and Layer 3 were not run either, as the rulebook requires after a Layer 1 failure. This is a recorded gap, not a pass: the stock was not scored on its target or its business scorecard. The archive verdict is a gate outcome, not a valuation call.

The adversarial rules add one more point. The underwriting skill lists high valuation, low returns, promoter pledges and heavy debt as items that must lead to rejection. This company shows two of those four, heavy debt and a pledge of 8.64 percent of promoter holding. The pledge alone would not decide the verdict, but it reinforces the rejection.

Final Action Verdict: REJECTED, archive. The failing rules are the leverage limit (debt-to-equity below 0.70 on the reported balance sheet) and the momentum gate (no pass on Test A or Test B). The level that would reopen the file is a filed balance sheet with borrowings at or below about ₹814 crore, together with a later ledger pass on Test A or Test B.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Every figure in this dossier is built from reported numbers, and several could move. The first source of error is the definition of debt. The Screener figure of ₹1,162 crore counts borrowings only [3]. The CARE figure of ₹1,667 crore also counts acceptances and corporate guarantees [2]. The verdict holds on either definition, but the gap to the 0.70 limit changes from about ₹348 crore to about ₹853 crore, so the wrong definition would understate or overstate the repair needed.

The second source of error is the promoters' capital promise. Management said the promoters had committed about ₹50 crore, of which about ₹20 crore was already in place [4]. If the balance does not arrive, the leverage ratio stays high. Management also said acquisitions could push the ratio above its comfort level [4]. A stated target of 0.7 is a management aim. The reported balance sheet is what counts.

dilution means the spreading of a company's profit across more shares when new shares are issued. The 550,000 shares allotted to the promoter on warrant conversion, which began trading on 29 September 2026, are a small example [19]. The promoter share count rose from 53,532,500 to 54,082,500 as a result [12]. The amount is small, but it shows how a promoter capital raise can add shares to the family's holding rather than to other owners' shares.

Some source text is less reliable than it looks. The company's call transcript prints "USD 100 billion" for the US backlog in one place and "USD100 million" in another, and the company's figure is USD 100 million [4] [9]. The same call prints "0.7%" where management means 0.7 times, and it calls 0.7 the stated target [20], which a later summary repeats [17]. Several sites are AI-generated summaries, so we used those only where the primary text matched.

Two smaller definition gaps also matter. Screener's sales of ₹3,620 crore and CARE's total operating income of ₹3,633.72 crore differ slightly because the sources define income differently [3] [2]. The price in this report is the ledger close of 9 October. Any later quote for the stock is not used in the tests, so a small move since then would only shift the distance measures by a fraction of an ATR.

The return gate is the thinnest number in the file. It clears its floor by 0.2 points, so a restatement of about that size would flip it. The leverage failure does not depend on that gate, which is why the verdict is more secure than the return test alone.
