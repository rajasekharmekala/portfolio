1. What the company does

In plain words: Pennar shapes steel into buildings, tubes, boiler parts and car frames for builders, industrial firms and carmakers. This chapter says what it sells and where its cash gets tied up.

Pennar Industries Limited is an engineering company based in Hyderabad. It trades on the NSE under the symbol PENIND and on the BSE as 513228. The watchlist file carries it as PENNARIND, and this book uses that label for the portfolio record[4][9]. It was founded in 1975 as a steel strip maker. Today it sells steel-based engineered products through eight divisions: pre-engineered buildings and structural units, precision tubes, process heating (boilers), hydraulics, engineering services, body-in-white car frames, steel, and railways[4][9]. CARE counts 14 plants, 11 in India and three abroad[9].

The company reports in two segments. The first is diversified engineering: tubes, steel strip, hydraulics, rail parts, boilers and car frames. The second is customised building solutions, which means pre-engineered steel buildings (PEB) for factories, warehouses and data centres in India and the United States, with engineering services attached[4]. CARE puts PEB revenue at ₹1,570 crore in FY26, up 17.3 percent[9]. The US arm grew by buying the assets of Telko Enterprises for about US$14 million, roughly ₹130 crore, during the year[9]. International revenue was 24.1 percent of FY26 sales[4]. A crore is ten million rupees.

Customers are builders, industrial firms, railway coach makers and carmakers. The Q1 FY27 earnings release names Saint-Gobain, Toyota Boshoku, Schaeffler, Tube Investments, MEIL and many US contractors[3]. The company says it took orders worth ₹944 crore in the three months before 12 August 2026[3]. On the August call management gave the order backlog as ₹1,008 crore for PEB India, more than US$100 million for PEB US and ₹150.75 crore for boilers[5]. About 25 percent of revenue comes from what management calls legacy businesses: steel strip, railways and tubing. It puts no new capital into them[5].

Steel is the main raw material. CARE says raw material is about 60 to 65 percent of the cost of sales and that steel strip is a large part of it[9]. The company mostly makes to order, so steel price swings reach profit unless contracts pass them on. The managing director said most of the latest steel rise was passed through, after a one or two month lag that cut PEB margins in the June quarter[5]. Customers advance 15 to 20 percent on orders, against 40 to 50 percent at some rivals, and the company uses non-cash letters of credit more than they do, management said in June 2025[8]. The company also holds 45 percent of ZAP91 Solar India, a solar module venture with Zetwerk. It has given a ₹110 crore corporate guarantee for that venture[1][5]. How the company earns its money and where the cash gets tied up

A building order starts with design, then steel is bought, cut and shipped, and the customer pays in stages. The company pays for steel mostly through bank letters of credit. A letter of credit, or LC, is a bank promise to pay the supplier on a set date. In the balance sheet these appear as trade payables called acceptances. They stood at ₹394.80 crore at 31 March 2026 against ₹421.71 crore a year earlier[1]. The finance cost note shows ₹19.00 crore of bill discounting charges in FY26, which is where the cost of this credit shows up[1].

In June 2025 the managing director said the firm makes heavy use of non-cash LCs, and that this 'comes into our total debt, it comes into our interest cost'[8]. That sentence is the company's own description of how it treats acceptances, and Chapter 3 uses it. The filing also shows a ₹264.45 crore supplier finance programme inside trade payables, at 6 to 7 percent interest[1]. It does not say whether this overlaps the acceptances line, so we show both readings.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

In plain words: the business is large and widening its margin, but it carries a lot of debt and customers pay more slowly. This chapter gives the evidence on both sides.

The case for the business is scale, spread and a steady margin climb. Sales have grown from ₹2,266 crore in FY22 to ₹3,620 crore in FY26, and profit after tax from ₹42 crore to ₹139 crore[19][1]. It serves six end markets. CARE rates its bank loans CARE A with a stable outlook and says the product range, long record and a healthy order book support the rating[9]. The case against it is the balance sheet. In FY26 borrowings rose 47 percent, receivables rose 26 percent against sales growth of 12 percent, and interest took 38.7 percent of EBITDA before other income[1]. Chapter 2B scores these points. This chapter gives the evidence.

Where the strength is

The margin trend is real. EBITDA, which is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, was ₹401.32 crore in FY26 on the company's own definition, including other income, which is 11.09 percent of revenue from operations. The same figure for FY25 was ₹347.44 crore, 10.77 percent[1][4]. It was 12.27 percent in June 2026[4]. CARE expects its own margin measure to rise gradually as PEB grows[9].

Backlog is high. PEB India stands at ₹1,008 crore, which management calls the highest it has carried, and the US arm has crossed US$100 million of backlog, from about US$70 million in the first quarter[5].The US arm earns a short operating cycle: CARE says receivables there are realised in about 15 days[9].

Promoters are adding equity. In June 2026 the company allotted 30,00,000 convertible warrants at ₹168 each to Pennar Holdings, a promoter company, for ₹50.40 crore. It paid ₹12.60 crore up front and ₹6.93 crore when 5,50,000 warrants were exercised in July; 24,50,000 were outstanding at 30 September[4][5][15].

Where the weakness is

Debt is the weak joint. Gross borrowings were ₹1,141.52 crore at 31 March 2026 against ₹775.12 crore a year earlier. CARE says the rise came from the Telko purchase, the ₹110 crore guarantee for the solar venture and more working capital borrowing, and it calls the debt-funded capex a rating constraint[1][9]. CARE's overall gearing, which counts acceptances, was 1.35 times at March 2026 against 1.24 times a year earlier and 1.32 times in March 2024[9][10][11]. Working capital use of bank limits was 85 to 90 percent in the 12 months to August 2026[9]. The standalone borrowings note lists a covenant that net debt must not exceed 1.0 times net worth. The standalone figure was 0.89 at March 2026, which leaves about ₹105 crore of room on that test[1]. The note does not name which lender holds this covenant.

Receivables and working capital are the second weak joint. Net receivables were ₹731.46 crore against ₹580.38 crore a year before. That is 73.8 days of sales against 65.7 days, and the company's own working capital days went from 76 in June 2025 and February 2026 to 82 at March 2026[1][6][7][8]. On the May 2026 call the managing director said some projects were stuck awaiting handover and that collection was expected in six to eight weeks[6]. The August call gave no receivable figure to test that statement[5]. Advances from customers fell 34.7 percent to ₹81.74 crore[1].

Returns are thin for the risk. Return on equity was 12 percent. Management's ROCE (return on capital employed) of about 20 percent uses its own formula, against 15.2 percent on Screener and 13.6 percent on our closing basis[1][6][19]. The sell side is thin: one analyst is on record with a ₹260 target[20][21].
