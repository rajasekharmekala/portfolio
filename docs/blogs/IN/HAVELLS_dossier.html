1. What the company does

Havells India sells electrical goods under six brands, including Havells, Crabtree, Standard, Reo and Lloyd. It runs 17 factories, makes about 90 percent of what it sells in-house, and sells 24 product families through about 20,700 direct dealers and about 2.72 lakh retail outlets in more than 3,000 towns[9][10]. Think of it as the electrical aisle of an Indian hardware shop, owned by one company: the wire inside the wall, the switch on it, the breaker behind it, the fan above it and the air conditioner in the bedroom. Most sales are to households and small contractors (management puts the split at roughly 70 to 75 percent consumer and 25 to 30 percent business), and the rest goes to utilities, builders and industry[5].

Cables are the largest business, at 38.6 percent of fiscal 2026 revenue[2]. House wires are made of copper, and power cables are mostly aluminium, so the selling price follows metal prices[3]. Raw materials are about 68 to 70 percent of revenue, most orders carry variable prices, and the company holds two to two and a half months of stock whose cost is already locked in[10]. That has a simple consequence. When metal prices jump, revenue jumps with them, but profit per rupee of sales can fall if prices to customers lag behind. The company also has to tie up more cash in stock, and that tied-up cash is called working capital: cash held in stock and customer bills, less the credit suppliers give. Management says it raised prices by 10 to 25 percent over the last twelve months, and by more than 30 percent in cables and wires[3].

Lloyd is the problem child. It sells air conditioners, refrigerators, washing machines and televisions. Air conditioners are close to 75 percent of its sales, so the first quarter (the hot season) is huge and the second quarter is small: Lloyd sold ₹1,460 crore in April to June 2026 and ₹615 crore in July to September[1][3]. In fiscal 2026 Lloyd revenue fell about 23 percent to ₹3,948 crore and the segment lost ₹203 crore before interest and tax[10][2]. The company carries ₹310.47 crore of goodwill and ₹1,029.00 crore of brand and other long-life intangible assets for Lloyd, and its auditor flagged the test of whether those values still hold as a key audit matter[9].

Two newer lines matter for the story. Renewables, a segment created in April 2026, sells solar panels, solar pumps and electric-vehicle chargers and had sales of ₹329 crore in the September quarter, up 147.8 percent[1]. Havells put ₹600 crore into Goldi Solar, which makes much of the panels it sells, and management calls the plan a brand-and-channel business rather than trading[3][2]. Meanwhile the company is spending heavily on new plant: ₹1,400 crore of capital expenditure (cash spent on plant and equipment) is planned for fiscal 2027, of which about ₹800 crore goes to cables and ₹200 crore to a new research centre[1][18]. The promoters own 59.35 percent of the shares and none of those shares are pledged[14].

How Havells earns a profit: contribution margin and seasons

The company reports a number called contribution margin for each segment. It is sales minus materials, factory running costs, direct selling costs and depreciation, shown as a share of sales[1]. Think of it as what is left of each rupee of sales to pay for brand building, head office and profit. A segment with a high contribution margin can afford to spend on advertising. A segment with a low one cannot. Group operating profit is reported as EBITDA, short for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, and it is the number that analysts follow most closely.

Seasons matter as much as metals. The first quarter carries the air-conditioner season, the fourth quarter carries year-end dealer incentives and cooling-product stocking, and the second quarter is the quietest for Lloyd[3][5]. A single quarter therefore tells you little about the year, and this report leans on the half-year and the trailing twelve months wherever it can.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • contribution margin — sales less materials, factory variable costs, direct selling costs and depreciation, as a share of sales; the company's own measure of what each segment earns before shared costs.
  • segment result — a segment's profit before interest, tax and shared head-office costs (also called segment EBIT).
  • FMEG — fast-moving electrical goods, the trade name for fans, lights, switches, wires and small appliances that people buy often and in small amounts.
  • pull-led distribution — a sales method in which consumers draw products from dealers, instead of the company pushing stock into the channel with discounts.
  • unrealised fair-value gain — a paper gain from raising the recorded value of an investment; no cash has come in.
  • trailing twelve months — the latest four reported quarters added together; here, October 2025 to September 2026.
  • ₹ crore — a crore is ten million rupees, so ₹1,000 crore is ₹10,000 million.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The main strength is reach. Havells sells through about 20,700 direct dealers and 2.72 lakh outlets, and management calls the brand and the distribution network the biggest asset that never shows up on the balance sheet[10][3]. CARE Ratings, a credit rating agency, expects the company to keep its leadership in cables, consumer durables, switchgear and lighting. It rates its bank debt at its top level, CARE AAA with a stable outlook, and reaffirmed that on 25 September 2026[10][11]. A separate corporate governance grading, from a CARE group company, is CG 1 (CG One)[12].

The returns support the claim. Return on capital employed, or ROCE, has stayed between 22 and 30 percent in each of the last twelve years, and it was 25 percent in fiscal 2026[13]. Return on equity has averaged 19 percent over ten years[13]. The company's own, slightly different, measure of capital returns has slipped from 25.1 percent in March 2026 to 23.5 percent in September 2026 as new plants are built[4][1]. Management expects it to recover to 25 percent or more within a couple of years, but gave no date[3].

Switchgear is the proof of pricing power. Its contribution margin has been 37 to 38 percent for years, it was 38.1 percent in the September quarter and its segment profit margin was 25.4 percent[1]. Management says the normal range is 38 to 40 percent and that volumes grew close to double digits, so the quarter's 31.3 percent revenue jump is part price and part volume[3][21]. This is the business in which a strong brand and an electrician's habits protect the price.

The weak spots are just as clear. Cables are the biggest segment but management admits it is not the margin leader there and that the underground-cable business is commoditised[3]. The September quarter's cable contribution margin fell to 13.9 percent from 17.7 percent, because aluminium prices fell during the quarter, which management says weighed on margins against a strong year-earlier quarter[1][3]. New rivals are arriving: Crompton has entered house wires, and the Aditya Birla Group's UltraVolt says it will be the second-largest wire maker by capacity at launch and aims to be among the top two in five years, backed by ₹1,800 crore[3][19]. Management itself concedes that irrational behaviour by new entrants could cost a little contribution margin for a while[3].

Lloyd is the second weak spot. The segment lost ₹203 crore in fiscal 2026 after earning ₹131 crore the year before, and lost another ₹155 crore in the first half of fiscal 2027[2][1]. An analyst put the capital deployed in Lloyd at about ₹4,000 crore on the April 2026 call, and management did not dispute the figure[5]. Asked for a date when Lloyd turns profitable, the chairman said it was difficult to give any timeline[3]. That makes Lloyd the one part of the company where you are paying for hope rather than results.

Finally, the headline growth is partly price. Sales rose 34.6 percent in the quarter, but price rises of 10 to 25 percent over twelve months, and more than 30 percent in cables and wires, did much of the work, while cables and wires volumes grew close to double digits[1][3]. Over three years sales have compounded at 10 percent a year, and profit at 16 percent, a figure lifted by the fiscal 2026 fair-value gain[13]. Sales beat the ₹5,937 crore poll expectation and profit matched the poll, yet the share fell about 6 percent on 23 October to a new 52-week low, because the margin and Lloyd mattered more than the sales[20][17]. The 9.7 percent profit growth compares the consolidated quarterly profits of ₹349.22 crore and ₹318.28 crore; the ledger's data vendor shows 9.6 percent on its own basis[2][33]. A reader who sees 35 percent growth and assumes it will repeat has misread the quarter.

Governance, accounting and balance sheet checks

The balance sheet is clean. Borrowings were ₹239 crore at September 2026 and CARE says the only debt is lease liabilities, with gearing of 0.03 times and ₹2,364 crore of free cash and liquidity at March 2026[13][10]. Claims against the company not acknowledged as debt are ₹7.96 crore and disputed taxes are ₹30.39 crore, which is small next to profit of about ₹1,700 crore[9]. The auditor, Price Waterhouse, gave an unmodified opinion on the September 2026 limited review[2].

Two governance points deserve a note. The impairment test on Lloyd's assets, worth ₹2,641.92 crore in all once other non-financial assets are added, is the auditor's only key audit matter[9]. And on 27 October 2026 the company told the exchanges that Mr Samir Kirit Anjaria had resigned as President; management has described its leadership changes as a normal three-to-four-year rotation[13][3]. One departure is not a pattern, and it is flagged here only because it arrived on the same day as the price hit a new low.

Momentum and turnaround inflection gate

Our process runs three momentum tests on every name. Test A asks whether the stock is in a strong uptrend now. Test B asks whether it is leaving a flat base and starting one. Test C asks whether a stock that has already fallen has stopped falling and is turning up from a floor. A stock that fails all three is not bought at the market price, however good its business.

The exact limits come from the portfolio scoring file, and they decide every Pass or Fail below[34]. Test A needs the price above a rising 50-day average that is above a 200-day average that is not falling over 63 sessions, a three-month return that beats the Nifty 50, and a close within 1.6 ATR of support. Test B needs the 50-day average to have fallen no more than 0.8 percent over 21 sessions, a four-week return that beats the Nifty 50, a close within 1.5 ATR of the 63-day low, a 63-day volume dry-up ratio of 0.92 or less, and session volume of at least 0.95 times the 20-day average. Test C needs a higher low within a 2.0 percent tolerance, a close at or above the 20-day average with positive four-week strength or a two-week gain, a close within 2.0 ATR of support, and an RSI of at least 32.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

An estimate is only as good as its weakest input. This chapter takes each estimate in turn, names its weakest input, says what evidence would show it wrong and says when we will see that evidence. It then gives the strongest case against our own verdict, which is the red-team view, and names the one claim on which the whole case hinges.

Weakest input of the FY27 estimate: the second-half margin

Our FY27 profit rests on a second-half EBITDA margin of 9.3 percent, against 8.0 percent in the first half. The lift has three supports. Advertising spending, which doubled to ₹286 crore in the first quarter, is meant to return to 2.7 to 2.8 percent of sales for the year[1][18]. Price rises of 5 to 7 percent are planned for the third quarter[3]. And Lloyd's stronger season is said to start in the third quarter and carry into the fourth, with cost increases passed on[3].

The risk is that each support fails a little. Management says it will pass on costs only as far as competition allows, and it has said that it also wants to hold market share[5]. Each point of margin in the second half is worth about ₹1.74 a share, or about ₹66 of share value at 38 times. We will know at the third-quarter result, likely in the second half of January 2027.

Weakest input of the FY28 estimate: cables and wires under new competition

The cable and wire business is 38.6 percent of sales and its contribution margin has already dropped from 17.7 to 13.9 percent in a year[1]. Crompton has entered wires, and UltraVolt plans to be the second-largest wire maker by capacity at launch and a top-two player within five years[3][19]. Management says new entrants usually take share from unorganised makers, but it concedes that irrational pricing could cost some margin[3].

At the current run rate cable sales are about ₹10,900 crore a year, twice the first half's ₹5,455 crore. The metal that hurt the September quarter is still moving against the company: the London Metal Exchange cash price of aluminium fell 6.1 percent between 25 September and 12 October, and its October average is 6.8 percent below September, while copper is flat[31][32]. Each 100 basis points of contribution margin is therefore about ₹109 crore of pre-tax profit, ₹1.29 a share, or about ₹49 of share value at 38 times. A 300-point fall would take about ₹147 off the value, which is 15 percent of today's price. We will see it in each quarterly cable margin from the third quarter onward.

Weakest input of the value: the multiple

The 38 times we apply is the current multiple on reported profit. It is not a floor. Nomura, which still holds a price target of ₹1,232, has cut its multiple to 35 times, the low end of the stock's 35 to 45 times band, and cites rising competition and a rising cost of capital[17]. Motilal Oswal uses 40 times but on lower profit, and arrives at ₹1,060[27]. The five peers in chapter 3 trade between 22 and 37 times[22][23][24][25][26].

If the stock settled at 32 times, which sits above Crompton's 25.9 and V-Guard's 30.2 and below the cable peers' 35 to 37, our FY28 profit would be worth ₹954, which is 5 percent below today's price[25][26][22][23]. Each turn of multiple is worth about ₹30 a share. The evidence will arrive over the next two quarters as the market judges whether margins have bottomed.

The red-team case: why the verdict could be too kind

The strongest case against buying is that the growth is lower in quality than it looks. Sales rose 34.6 percent but profit only 9.7 percent, and half-year profit is down 4 percent[2]. A large part of sales growth is metal prices. First-half cash from operations was negative at minus ₹162 crore. Plant spending of ₹1,400 crore a year plus a dividend of about ₹627 crore is more than the ₹1,572 crore of operating cash earned in FY26, so the company is drawing down a bank balance that fell from ₹2,351 crore to ₹1,190 crore in six months[1][13]. CARE has named a cash balance below ₹500 crore on a sustained basis, and a return on capital employed below 20 percent, as triggers for a rating downgrade[10]. Neither is close, but the direction of both is down.

Lloyd is the second risk. The auditor's only key audit matter is the carrying value of ₹310.47 crore of goodwill and ₹1,029.00 crore of indefinite-life intangible assets, a total of ₹1,339 crore, which is about ₹21 a share, or 14 percent of book value[9][13]. Management passed the March 2026 test using a discounted cash flow model that depends on growth and margin assumptions that Lloyd has not yet delivered[9]. An impairment would not use cash, but it would cut reported profit by about ₹21 a share in the year it was booked, and it would end the story that Lloyd is only in an investment phase.

Third, the foreign shareholders are leaving. Their holding fell from 24.83 percent in March 2024 to 14.47 percent in September 2026, while mutual funds rose from 3.57 percent to 9.46 percent[13][14]. Part of the domestic buying is index funds: an HDFC index fund alone holds 4.95 percent[14]. Index funds buy a stock because of its weight and not its price, so if those flows slow, the buyer of last resort is missing.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, for now. Our rules set a hard red line for a close below a falling 50-day average that sits below a falling 200-day average, when the recovery test (Test C) also fails. Havells meets every one of those conditions on 27 October 2026[33][34]. On distance from support alone, the name would qualify: it is 0.29 average daily swings above support, inside the 0.6 band that our ladder treats as full size[34]. The red line overrides that. So we do not size a position and we do not place an order. This is a verdict on timing and structure, not on the company. The balance sheet, pledge and cash tests pass. The operating scorecard passes at its floor. The reward-to-risk ratio, also called asymmetry, divides the gain to the target by the loss to the stop, and it is 2.98 at the close.

If the red line lifts, we would place a limit order, which fills only at our price or better and does not chase. The entry zone would be ₹975.00 to ₹1,010.00. The stop loss is ₹966.79, which is the 63-day low of ₹999.20 less one average daily swing of ₹32.41. The central target is ₹1,133, the bull target is ₹1,504 and the bear value is ₹768. At the top of the entry zone the ratio of gain to loss is 2.85, and at the close it is 2.98. Both are above our core floor of 2.0.

We would not buy simply because the ratio passes. The stop is only 4 percent below the close. The stock fell 8.5 percent last week, so a normal wobble could trip it. Against the bear value of ₹768 the ratio is 0.52. The probability-weighted gain of 8.8 percent is below our 12 percent return requirement. A passing ratio on a stop this close is a rule satisfied, not a margin of safety.

Entry timing follows a support ladder that measures how far the price sits above its nearest support, in ATR units, where the ATR is the typical daily swing. Within 0.6 ATR is the FULL_SIZE band, up to 1.2 ATR is STANDARD, up to 1.6 ATR is LIMIT_ORDER_REDUCE, which means a starter limit order only, and beyond 1.6 ATR is NO_BUY, which means no purchase at the market price[34]. Havells sits at 0.29 ATR, so the ladder alone says FULL_SIZE. The red line comes first, which is why the answer is still no.

Position size follows risk, not conviction alone. The risk-unit rule gives each conviction tier a risk budget, which is the share of the whole portfolio we accept losing if the stop is hit: 1.00 percent for TIER_1, 0.75 for TIER_2, 0.50 for TIER_3, 0.35 for TIER_4 and zero for TIER_0. The weight is the risk budget divided by the stop distance, and it cannot exceed the tier cap, which is 7 to 10 percent for TIER_1, 4 to 6.5 for TIER_2, 2 to 3.5 for TIER_3, 1 to 2 for TIER_4 and zero for TIER_0[34]. Today the tier is TIER_0, so the weight is zero. That is not a rounding result. It is the rule that a name with a hard red line gets no money. If the name later clears the red line, we would start it as a TIER_3 position with a 3.5 percent cap. The stop distance of 4.3 percent would otherwise allow a weight of 11.7 percent, so the cap binds.

If the stock then rose, the let-winners-run rule would apply. It means we do not sell a rising stock just because the RSI passes 70 or because its weight passes 15 percent. We would trail the stop up to the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average, and we would allow the weight to grow to a ceiling of 22 percent. The aim is to stop a good holding from being cut short by a mechanical rule while the trend and the earnings are intact.
