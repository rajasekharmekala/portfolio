Premier Explosives makes explosives and rocket fuel at six Indian factories, and defence and space buyers took 81 percent of its sales in the year to March 2026[2]. We say REJECTED: the ₹1,393 crore order book has not yet become profit, as the firm kept only 10.0 percent of sales as operating profit against a promised 15 to 20 percent[3][2][6]. Apollo Micro Systems agreed in July 2026 to buy the founders' stake, its ₹705.65 public offer closed on 26 October, and the shares fell 7.0 percent to ₹588.10 the next day[14][13]. 1. What the company does

Premier Explosives Limited is a Secunderabad company, founded in 1980, that makes "high-energy materials". In plain terms, it makes things that burn or blow up on command. Miners and builders use its detonators and explosives to break rock. The armed forces use its rocket propellants, flares, chaff and warhead fillings. The space agency uses its large solid-fuel rocket parts[2][3]. The company runs six factories in Telangana, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra, and employs more than 850 people, about 100 of them engineers or scientists[3].

Defence and space are now the main customers. In the year to March 2026 the company sold goods and services worth ₹3,883.4 million (₹388.3 crore). Defence and space provided ₹3,154 million of that, or 81 percent, and commercial explosives provided ₹729 million[2]. In the quarter to June 2026 the split moved to 77 percent defence and space and 23 percent commercial[3]. The company supplies solid propellant for the Akash, MRSAM, LRSAM and Astra missiles and says it has delivered more than 2,500 booster grains for Akash[3]. It also runs two government-owned propellant plants under contract, at the space agency's Sriharikota site and at a defence laboratory plant in Jagdalpur[3]. The space agency contract brings in about ₹18 crore a year and has about 2.5 years left[5].

Money comes in lumps, because defence orders are won by tender and delivered in batches. Several big orders depend on materials the customer supplies free of charge, on inspections before dispatch, and on export licences from the government[5][8]. A sale is recorded only when those steps finish, so a quarter can look weak even when the order book is full. On 14 August 2026 management put the order book at ₹1,393 crore, of which ₹1,309 crore is defence, ₹42 crore is explosives and ₹42 crore is services[4]. The company states that this is 3.59 times its sales for the year to March 2026[3].

The commercial side is a different business. Bulk explosives, which are loaded into drilled holes at mines, earn "very thin" and sometimes negative margins, according to the managing director, who said the company had already withdrawn from Coal India contracts and still supplies Singareni Collieries[4]. He also said more than 40 players compete in this field[4]. The company expects about ₹80 crore of revenue from the bulk business in the year to March 2027[4]. Annual-report text also says sales in the year to March 2026 fell partly because of a ban on making electric detonators[2].

Ownership is changing. Until recently the founding family, through the AKS Family Trust, held 41.33 percent of the shares[2][1]. On 9 July 2026 Apollo Micro Systems signed a deal to buy that stake for about ₹1,550 crore in cash, and then had to offer to buy up to 26 percent more from the public[14]. Apollo says the deal should close in the third quarter of the financial year (October to December 2026), subject to approvals[3][14]. If every public share were offered, Apollo could end with 67.33 percent[16]. A newspaper put the whole deal, including the public offer, at ₹2,300 to 2,500 crore before it was signed[19].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • relative strength index (RSI) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast a share price has moved lately; the full name is relative strength index, readings under 30 are called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR stands for average true range, the average size of a stock's daily price swing in rupees; it is used to measure how far the price sits from a level.
  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a profit measure that ignores borrowing costs, tax and the wearing out of equipment.
  • ROCE stands for return on capital employed, the profit earned each year on the money tied up in the business.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share, which is the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • free cash flow is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment.
  • FCF is the short form of free cash flow, as defined in the line above.
  • operating margin is the profit left from each rupee of sales after the costs of running the business, before interest and tax.
  • debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' money; below 0.7 is a comfortable level.
  • net debt is borrowings minus the cash a company holds.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share; it tells you how many years of current profit you pay for up front.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates, before spending on equipment or paying financiers.
  • working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company owes its suppliers.
  • capital expenditure is spending on plants, machines and buildings.
  • guidance is a forecast that the company itself publishes for a coming period.
  • consensus estimate is the average of the profit forecasts that brokerage analysts publish.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 trading days; the 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the same idea over 50 and 200 days, and the price often pauses or turns near them.
  • Stage 2 is the Weinstein stage of a confirmed uptrend, and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend; Stage 1 is a flat floor after a decline.
  • A base is a flat stretch of trading, often several months long, that a price builds before its next big move; its pivot is the top of that stretch.
  • A breakout is a move above the pivot on strong trading volume, and a retest is the pullback that comes back to check the pivot from above.
  • A stop loss is the price at which we agree the thesis is wrong and we sell; a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better.
  • A reverse DCF works backwards from today's share price to the yearly profit growth that the price needs; the discount rate is the yearly return an investor demands, which is used to shrink future cash to today's value.
  • The entry zone is the band of prices where we would be willing to buy, and asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The company's best argument is scarcity. It describes itself as the first private company in India to make solid propellant for missile programmes and as the only qualified Indian maker of countermeasures such as flares and chaff[3]. Its propellant supplies 100 percent of the requirement for MRSAM, LRSAM and Astra, and it makes pyrogen igniters for all stages of the Agni missile[3]. Making these products needs licences, qualified plants and years of testing with the defence research organisation, so a new competitor cannot copy them quickly. That barrier is real and it is the reason the order book has grown from ₹750 crore at March 2025 to ₹1,569 crore at March 2026 and ₹1,393 crore at June 2026[24]. ICRA put it at about ₹899 crore in June 2024[9], and a September 2025 broker note at ₹1,297 crore[34].

The same facts show the weakness. The rating agency ICRA, in its July 2025 review, listed what holds the business back: work that ties up a lot of money, lumpy defence sales, sensitivity to raw material prices and exchange rates, high dependence on a few customers, and a heavily regulated industry that needs licences for each product[8]. Government customers also supply key materials free, so a late delivery from them delays Premier's own sales[5]. ICRA withdrew its ratings on 13 November 2025 at the company's request, after the company moved to another rating agency[11]. The result is that scarcity has not turned into pricing power on the income statement. The operating margin was 22 percent in the year to March 2024, 14 percent in 2025, 10 percent in 2026, and 5.7 percent in the quarter to June 2026[1][3].

Safety and reliability are part of competitive position in explosives, and here the record is mixed. A fire on 4 January 2025 at Peddakandukur killed one person[12][10]. On 29 April 2025 an explosion in a propellant mixing building at Katepally killed three workers and led the state pollution board to order the plant shut[32][8]. ICRA says the board revoked the order on 2 June 2025 and allowed three months of operation[8], while news reports from 17 September 2025 describe the board lifting the closure order and clearing production to resume[32][33]. The sources differ, so we cannot give one restart date. ICRA estimated that the accident would cost ₹25 to 30 crore of sales in the year to March 2026 because the company could not make large-diameter rocket motors[8]. The company says the flares plant that was hit has been recommissioned and is producing[5]. The wider Katepally expansion was still unfinished in May 2026 and could run into the third quarter of 2026-27, and in August water trials of the new RDX and HMX plant were planned for September[5][4].

Compared with the larger explosives maker Solar Industries, Premier is small. Solar reported ₹3,668 crore of sales in the quarter to June 2026 alone, a defence business up 123 percent, and an order book of ₹21,350 crore[37]. Premier's sales for the last twelve months are ₹349 crore[1], so Solar's one quarter is about ten times as large. The point is not that Premier must match Solar. The point is that Solar earns a 27 percent operating margin on that scale, and Premier earns 7 percent[41][1].

Demand is not the problem. India's defence exports reached a record ₹38,424 crore in the year to March 2026, up 62.66 percent[35], and the defence budget for 2026-27 is ₹7.85 lakh crore, with ₹2.19 lakh crore set for capital spending[36]. Our reading is that the business has a real position in a growing market but has not yet shown that it can deliver on time and at a good margin. The next chapter looks at five measures that test exactly that.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A single set of ratios cannot judge a defence supplier whose sales depend on orders, inspections and licences. We therefore picked five measures that drive this particular business: order cover, delivery against its own forecasts, operating margin, cash conversion, and plant and licence reliability. Each measure scores 1.0 for PASS, 0.5 for WATCH and 0 for FAIL. The rule is that a company qualifies only if it scores at least 3.0 out of 5.0 and has at most one FAIL. The scorecard matters because it asks whether the order book is turning into profit, which is the question a defence supplier must answer.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management speaks with confidence, so we checked what it said against what happened. The October 2025 air force order for chaff and flares, worth ₹429.56 crore, is a test case[27]. Only ₹21 crore of it was executed in the quarter to June 2026[4]. In August 2025 the managing director said that the target of ₹600 crore of sales for the year to March 2026 still stood[7]. By November 2025 he said the range was ₹500 to 600 crore, with an operating margin of 15 to 20 percent[6]. In May 2026 he set a target of ₹600 to 700 crore for 2026-27[5][29][30]. The company then reported ₹388.3 crore and a 10.0 percent margin[2]. That is a miss of 22 percent against the lower end of the revised range.

The second check is about timing. The ICRA report of July 2025 said that the company had been charged liquidated damages, which are penalties for late delivery, on three chaff and flare orders worth about ₹533 crore, because it could not secure raw materials and parts on time[8]. In May 2026 management said the chaff work was done, that the flares would be finished in the first quarter of 2026-27, and that it was still trying to recover part of the penalties from the defence ministry[5]. An analyst on the August call recalled a ₹30 crore reversal as part of that plan[4]. In August 2026 the CFO said about ₹75 crore of flares was still to be delivered, that it would take another four to five months, and that the penalty reversal was still "in process"[4].

On the positive side, management promised to cut debt and did. Borrowings fell from ₹83 crore at March 2023 to ₹61 crore, ₹41 crore and ₹32 crore in the next three years[1]. Finance costs in the standalone accounts fell from ₹111.0 million to ₹36.5 million[2]. The balance sheet is the strongest part of this company, and it is the reason we do not see a solvency risk.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of results tell two different stories, depending on whether you look at sales or at profit. Sales grew from ₹199 crore in the year to March 2022 to ₹388 crore in the year to March 2026, a gain of 95 percent, but they peaked at ₹417 crore in 2025 and have since fallen[1]. Over the last twelve months, which run to June 2026, sales are ₹349 crore, down 27 percent[1]. The three-year growth rate is a healthy 24 percent a year, worked out as (₹388 crore divided by ₹202 crore) raised to the power one-third, minus one[1].

Reported profit looks much better than operating profit. Net profit rose to ₹46 crore in 2026 from ₹29 crore, yet operating profit fell from ₹58 crore to ₹40 crore[1]. The gap is other income. Screener shows it jumping from ₹2 crore to ₹36 crore. The annual report's own note shows ₹42.1 crore (₹4,214.49 lakh), and the two sources group some lines differently, so we use the annual report note for the one-time items[2]. In that note, ₹34.2 crore (₹3,417.80 lakh) is a purchase discount from a settlement with a supplier, for materials bought and used in the previous year, and ₹5.0 crore is a reversed provision[2]. Neither will repeat in the normal course of business.

Cash flow tells a third story. Cash from operations was ₹79 crore in 2024 and ₹118 crore in 2025, then minus ₹1 crore in 2026[1]. The 2025 figure was helped by amounts owed to suppliers growing by ₹85.7 crore, and 2026 gave most of that back with a ₹74.6 crore reduction[2]. Over three years the company generated ₹196 crore of operating cash against ₹103 crore of profit, a ratio of 1.90, so the long-run cash conversion is sound even though the latest year is not[1].

Debt is the cleanest line. Borrowings fell every year, from ₹83 crore at March 2023 to ₹32 crore at March 2026[1]. Dividing ₹32 crore by shareholders' funds of ₹289 crore (equity capital ₹11 crore plus reserves ₹278 crore) gives a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.11, which matches the 0.11 in our market-data ledger. The cost of that debt fell as well, so interest is no longer a worry. The weaker spot is the money tied up in stock and customer bills, which was 168 days of inventory and 50 days of unpaid sales at March 2026[1].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter is the red team. It takes each of our estimates and attacks its weakest assumption, because a rejection is only worth having if we can say what would prove it wrong. We also state the one claim on which the whole verdict hangs. The bear case against the stock is already in Chapter 5, so the attack here is on our own central case.

Our sales estimate may be too low

We assumed ₹430 crore for 2026-27, 72 percent of management's ₹600 crore. The case against us is that the quarter to June 2026 was held back by export licences and shipping delays, not by lack of demand, and that the order book is ₹1,393 crore[4]. Management expects ₹150 to 200 crore of exports in the quarter to September[4]. If that happens, half-year sales would be near ₹250 crore and our ₹430 crore would look low. We will know when the half-year results arrive, expected in mid-November, since the company held its last half-year call on 14 November[6].

If we are wrong here, the effect is large. Each extra ₹100 crore of sales at a 10 percent margin adds about ₹7.4 crore of profit after tax, or about ₹1.38 a share. Ten extra points of margin on ₹430 crore would add more than ₹4 a share. We have therefore kept the rejection tied to price as well as to the forecast.

Our margin estimate may be too low

We used 10 percent for 2026-27 and 12 percent for 2027-28. Management says the 5.7 percent in the quarter to June was caused by raw materials and by fixed costs spread over low sales, and that it will recover once contracts at old prices are completed and new ones are priced higher[4]. The margin was 22 percent in 2024 and 14 percent in 2025, so a return to the mid-teens is not impossible[1]. Two quarters at about ₹130 to 145 crore of sales and a margin just above 12 percent would only confirm our central case. To move toward the analysts' ₹14.20 and a purchase that clears the floor, the company would need about ₹170 crore a quarter at a margin of 16 percent or more (Chapter 7 shows the arithmetic).

The risk in our view is that we treat the first quarter as typical. It was the second-weakest of the last nine quarters by margin, and only the quarter to March 2026 was lower[1]. If the next quarter is better, the stock may rise faster than our model allows, and we would then reassess.

Apollo's ownership may lift the multiple

We held the multiple at 49. A new owner with a defence electronics order book of ₹1,704 crore and a plan to raise ₹3,322 crore could bring orders, funding and better pricing[22][21]. Peers trade at 78 to 113 times trailing profit[41][42][43][44]. Apollo's management guides for 40 to 45 percent revenue growth[23]. The counter-argument is that Apollo has 30.5 to 35.6 percent of its promoter shares pledged, that its purchase of the founders' stake is not yet complete, and that the source we read could not confirm that the ₹3,322 crore has been received[42][15][20]. Management said the benefits would become clearer in December and in the next quarter[4].

The multiple is the most uncertain input, because it measures mood as well as value. We chose a multiple that a buyer with inside knowledge agreed to pay, which is a better anchor than a peer average of companies with far higher margins. Because that purchase is not yet complete, the anchor is a signed price, not a cash exit.

One-time gains could still arrive

The company is still pursuing a refund of about ₹30 crore of penalties, and an insurance claim of ₹6.1 crore is waiting for settlement[4][2]. At a 26 percent tax rate the penalty refund alone would add about ₹4.13 to one year's earnings per share (₹30 crore x 0.74 / 5.376 crore shares). That would make a single year look better than our estimate without changing the business. The next two quarterly reports will show whether either arrives.
