1. What the company does

Think of KMEW as a marine contractor that owns its own fleet. Port authorities and river-transport agencies in India hire it to scoop sand and silt out of shipping channels, a job called dredging, so that large vessels can sail in without grounding. A dredger is a floating machine that sucks or digs the seabed and dumps the spoil elsewhere. KMEW buys second-hand dredgers, repairs and refits them in its own workshops, and puts them to work on contracts it has won through tenders. The company was set up in 2015, listed on the small-company platform of the BSE in 2021, and moved to the main board in 2024.[19][22]

Dredging brought in ₹193.50 crore of the company's ₹273.50 crore of segment sales in FY26 (the year to March 2026), before ₹17.22 crore of sales between group companies are removed. That segment also includes the second business: renting out port ancillary craft such as tugs, pilot boats, mooring boats, speed patrol boats and survey vessels, usually on multi-year charters to government ports. In Q1 FY27 management put dredging and ancillary at 83 percent of revenue, with shipbuilding growing from nothing in FY25 to ₹53.26 crore in FY26.[6][22]

The third business is new. KMEW bought a shipyard company (now called Knowledge Shipyard) in August 2025 and is building a larger yard at Saphale, near Mumbai, to build tugs, ferries and work boats for itself and for third parties. Management expects the first vessels to launch from the yard before March 2027, with up to 14 vessels a year at Phase 1 and 18 a year when all phases finish.[7][13]

Who pays the company? Almost entirely Indian government bodies: the Dredging Corporation of India (DCI, which sub-contracts work), the Inland Waterways Authority of India, and major ports. CARE Ratings says the two green tug contracts, worth about ₹650 crore over 15 years, make up 47 percent of the order book, and that the top five orders make up about 65 percent of it. Customers are credit-safe but few, and they pay slowly, which matters in chapter 3.[19]

The money comes from three places. Dredging jobs are won by tender and paid on completion or by stages, so revenue arrives in lumps. Charter contracts pay a fixed daily rate for years, so revenue is steady. Shipbuilding earns a margin on vessels sold plus a government subsidy that management says is 10 to 15 percent of vessel cost and arrives after the vessel is built.[12][13][16]

What changed in the last four quarters

Revenue was ₹201 crore in FY25 and ₹256 crore in FY26, and the last twelve months to June 2026 reached ₹323 crore.[22][23] The June 2026 quarter alone brought ₹115.41 crore, compared with ₹48 crore a year earlier. Two projects drove it: a ₹50 crore rock-dredging job at the Jawaharlal Nehru Port Authority (JNPA) and a maintenance-dredging job at Pondicherry port. Management says about ₹60 crore of revenue slipped from the March quarter into June.[15] Without that slip, the March quarter would have been closer to ₹128 crore and the June quarter closer to ₹55 crore. Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Dredging: digging or sucking sand and silt from the bottom of a channel, port or river to keep it deep enough for ships.
  • Capital dredging versus maintenance dredging: capital dredging makes a channel deeper or wider for the first time. Maintenance dredging removes silt that has built up since.
  • Charter and hire: renting a vessel, with crew, to a customer at a daily rate, usually for years.
  • Tonnage tax: a shipping-company tax regime in India where profit from running qualifying vessels is taxed on the ship's size, not on its actual profit, so the tax bill is very small.
  • Order book: signed contracts the company has not yet delivered. Divided by yearly sales, it shows how many years of work are already booked.
  • Preferential issue: selling new shares or warrants directly to chosen investors at an agreed price. It raises cash but spreads profit over more shares.
  • Share split: cutting each share into several smaller ones so the price per share falls. The company is no bigger and each holder owns the same share of it.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A good business is one that earns a high return, is hard to copy and has customers who keep paying. KMEW looks strong on the first test and unproven on the other two. Its EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), a measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges, was 38 percent of sales in FY26 (₹96.80 crore on ₹256.28 crore) and about 39 percent in FY25, so the margin has held for two years. For a fleet-owning contractor that is high, because most of its costs are fuel, crew and repairs that it controls.[19][20]

Why can KMEW earn that? Management gives two reasons. It buys older vessels cheaply, repairs them in house, and keeps them at work 270 to 300 days a year for dredgers and a full year for tugs. And it bids selectively for jobs where it expects 35 to 40 percent EBITDA margins.[12][17] CARE Ratings agrees that in-house repair and high utilisation limit costs, and that DCI, the state-owned incumbent, runs older and larger ships that overlap little with KMEW's shallow-draft fleet.[18][19]

The weaker half of the case is the moat. Management says dredging faces 'very little to no competition', but the same call includes a shareholder who argues that private ports can buy their own dredgers, and that nothing in the technology blocks entry.[14] Dredging is won by tender, and CARE lists 'tender-driven nature of contracts' and 'cyclicality' as risks that can squeeze margins.[19] DCI is investing in larger dredgers with new government money, and the market is attracting new entrants. KMEW's edge today is execution and asset availability, not a patent or a licence.[17][18]

Customers are strong credit risks but pay slowly. Trade receivables were ₹76.75 crore at March 2026, about 109 days of sales, and CARE puts the average collection period near 104 days. Slow payment ties up working capital, which is the cash a company needs to fund bills its customers have not yet paid.[3][19] The audit report adds an emphasis of matter about a ₹39.01 crore claim against one client: ₹14.13 crore was received in FY25, ₹8.60 crore more in FY26, and ₹24.89 crore was still outstanding after more than a year with no provision.[5] Management said in August 2026 that it had received all the money, but that is a statement on a call, not an audited number.[12]

Finally, the business is a bet on capital. The company wants to spend about ₹1,000 crore on new assets over roughly 18 months, including a shipyard, tugs and a large dredger, from a business whose fixed assets, after depreciation, were ₹211 crore at March 2026.[13][22] If those assets earn what the old ones did, the company is excellent. If not, depreciation and interest arrive before the revenue does. That is why strength today and the price today are different questions.[20]

Who the company competes with

DCI is the only listed pure-play dredger. Its shares trade at 60.8 times earnings, the price-to-earnings ratio, which is the share price divided by profit per share, with a return on capital employed of 4.28 percent and an operating margin (operating profit as a share of sales) near 20 percent, so the incumbent earns far less on its capital than KMEW does.[28] Shipbuilders such as Cochin Shipyard and Garden Reach Shipbuilders build far larger ships for the navy and exporters. They are comparables for the new shipbuilding line, not rivals for dredging contracts.[27][29] The closest listed marine-services peer is SEAMEC, which owns diving-support ships and trades at 15.8 times earnings.[26]

The comparison matters for how to read KMEW's 64 times earnings: it is more than SEAMEC (15.8), more than Cochin Shipyard (44.4) and Garden Reach (28.3), and a little above DCI (60.8), whose earnings have only just come back from losses.[26][27][28][29] The market is paying a premium for growth, not for a proven moat.

5. What could happen next

Every share price is a bet on future profit. A reverse discounted cash flow (reverse DCF) works backwards from today's price and asks how fast profit must grow every year for ten years to justify it. We use the portfolio's fixed settings: a 12 percent discount rate (what a rupee next year is worth today) and a terminal value of 20 times profit in year ten.[32][33]

The ledger solves 22.12 percent a year, starting from trailing profit of ₹130.95 crore (market value ₹8,395 crore divided by a trailing price-to-earnings of 64.11). Our own solves land at 20.6 to 21.5 percent a year depending on whether we start from the twelve-month profit or from our FY27 estimate. The ledger would normally start from FCF (free cash flow, which is the cash left after capital spending), but KMEW has no published figure, so it starts from profit. At 22 percent a year, profit would rise from ₹131 crore now to about ₹960 crore in year ten, against 18 percent a year in the last three years.[22][31][34]

That pace is not impossible for a company with a ₹1,000 crore target for FY29, but it demands years of compounding on top of a plan that has not yet started to earn. In other words, the price assumes the plan works. It does not leave room for a late shipyard, a slow dredging year or a tax change.[13][20]
