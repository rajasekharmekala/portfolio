1. What the company does

Stylam Industries Limited, listed on the NSE as STYLAMIND and on the BSE as 526951, was set up on 28 October 1991 and is based in Chandigarh.[2][8] It makes decorative laminates, which are layers of paper and resin pressed into hard sheets that cover furniture, kitchen units, doors and walls. Its range also includes compact laminates, solid acrylic surfaces, exterior wall cladding sold as Fascia, and restroom cubicles.[2] The group reports one segment, laminates and allied products.[1] In FY26 it earned revenue of ₹1,129.29 crore, up 10.2 percent from ₹1,025.09 crore.[1]

The business is an export business first. Exports were ₹830.77 crore, or 73.6 percent of FY26 sales, and home sales were ₹298.52 crore, or 26.4 percent.[1] CARE Ratings, a rating agency, put the export share at about two-thirds and notes that about 60 percent of raw materials are imported, which acts as a partial hedge against the rupee.[7] No customer supplied 10 percent or more of revenue in FY26, according to the annual report's segment note.[1] The annual report lists 257 export dealers in six regions and names none of them.[1]

Plants, capacity and the new third plant

Production sits at two sites in Panchkula, Haryana, called Works I and Works II, the second at Manak Tabra.[2] On 22 September 2026 the company said it had started commercial production at a new plant next to the Manak Tabra site, called Manak Tabra-2. The filing puts existing capacity at about 8.60 crore square metres a year, measured at 1 millimetre thickness, running at about 90 percent, and the new plant adds about 5.25 crore, a rise of about 61 percent, for about ₹300 crore funded from internal accruals.[13] The investor presentation gives a different utilisation, 74 percent for the laminates division, possibly a different measure (not confirmed); we do not blend the two.[2] The company employs about 1,242 people.[2] The plant started three weeks after the latest date management gave and a year after the first.

Who owns and runs the company

Control changed in 2026. Aica Kogyo, a Japanese maker of high-pressure laminates, agreed on 26 December 2025 to buy at least 40 percent of Stylam.[8][10] It paid ₹2,250 a share, ₹1,525.3 crore in total for 6,779,224 shares, and completed on 17 June 2026, after which Aica's nominees form a majority of the board.[9][11] Only 466,116 of the 4,406,496 shares offered in the open offer, the bid Aica had to make to all other shareholders, were tendered.[11] The founding family now holds 16.86 percent in two names, with Aica at 40 percent, which is why the exchange filings show the combined promoter group at 56.86 percent.[16] Management says the managing director, Jagdish Gupta, and his son Manit Gupta still run the company and that Aica is a strategic partner with no daily role.[3][4] What this means for you: the shareholder register changed, the family dispute behind the sale shaped the delays (Chapter 2), and an analyst asked on the May 2026 call about a put option that would let Aica buy about 12 percent more, and the terms are not in the filings we read, so we cannot size that overhang.[4]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business is that it is the most profitable large laminate maker in the peer group, sells mostly to export dealers with no single customer above 10 percent, and has almost no debt.[1][14] The case against is that its home market has not grown for about four years, its main plant launches have run a year late, and the control change came from a family dispute. Chapter 2B scores these points. This chapter explains the evidence.

India demand: a growing market that Stylam has not shared in

IDBI Capital, a broker, sized the Indian laminate market at about ₹9,000 crore, ₹6,500 crore at home and ₹2,500 crore of exports, with growth of 8 to 10 percent a year to FY33 and about 57 percent of it held by organised makers.[28] The same report puts industry EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a measure of operating profit) margins at 13 to 17 percent.[28] Stylam's home sales were ₹298.52 crore in FY26 against ₹294.24 crore a year earlier, which is +1.5 percent in a market growing 8 to 10 percent.[1] An investor on the July 2026 call said the home business had been stuck near ₹300 crore for four years, and management did not dispute it.[3] Management's explanation is that the domestic market was neglected for three to five years because of the family dispute, and that it is now rebuilding the sales team, adding distributors in cities where it had no presence, and opening warehouses. It says results will show from the December quarter.[3] The company also said it had stopped the losses in the home market.[3] We treat the revival as a promise until two quarters of home sales growth appear.

Exports: the engine, and its exposure

Exports grew 13.7 percent in FY26 to ₹830.77 crore.[1] IDBI estimates that organised makers hold about 75 percent of laminate exports, which fits a company with 257 export dealers.[28][1] The June 2026 quarter set a record for export sheets at 2.07 million, but home sheets were 1.10 million, so exports were 65 percent of volume.[2] Europe is half of the regional export figure, so a European slowdown would hit hardest. We found no source on European import duties for Indian laminates, so that risk is stated and not sized. Forward currency sales were small at 31 March 2026, USD 60 lakh and EUR 60 lakh, so most export income is unhedged. With two-thirds of sales in foreign currency, CARE says forex swings are a vulnerability.[1][7]

Capacity expansion: a year late, but done

The third plant is the main growth lever. Management said in May 2025 it would start by September 2025 at a cost of about ₹260 crore (the transcript wording is garbled and we read it as excluding GST), in January 2026 that it would start by March 2026 at about ₹320 crore including GST, in May 2026 that it would start between the end of June and mid-July, and in July 2026 that it would start by about 1 September.[6][5][4][3] It started on 22 September 2026.[13] The reasons given changed: a Supreme Court observation that required an environment clearance from Delhi, heavy rain in Chandigarh, and, in July, the family dispute, which a whole-time director said made the family itself slow the project.[5][3] The capital spent was real. The annual report shows work in progress of ₹221.45 crore at 31 March 2026 and ₹116.48 crore of capital commitments not yet paid.[1] Management says the new plant needs only about 150 workers and aims for 25 to 30 percent utilisation in the first year and 80 percent in year two, worth ₹250 to ₹300 crore of revenue in FY27 and ₹600 to ₹700 crore the year after.[4][3] That ramp matters more than anything else here, because the share price already assumes it (Chapter 5).

Margin trend and raw materials

The EBITDA margin was 16 percent in FY22 and FY23, 20 percent in FY24, 18 percent in FY25 and 20 percent in FY26, and the last four quarters, from September 2025, were 19, 21, 20 and 21 percent.[14] Management said the June 2026 margin of about 21 percent was not an inventory gain but came from efficiency and higher use of the existing plant.[3] It also said it ordered phenol three to four months ahead and holds five to six months of melamine, so the West Asia conflict has not yet fully reached its costs, and it has raised prices by 3 to 5 percent, with two rounds at home.[4] CARE expects a margin of about 17 to 19 percent over the medium term, below the 20 percent management guides to.[7] A margin that stays at 20 percent while input costs are high is the strongest claim in the story, and it is the one we test in Chapters 5 and 6.

Working capital: cash tied up in stock and customers

Stylam's working capital — cash tied up in stock and customer bills, less supplier credit — is large because it sells more than 1,200 designs, which forces it to hold a lot of stock.[7] CARE measured its operating cycle at 128 days in FY25, with 82 days of stock and 64 days of collection.[7] Screener's cash conversion cycle, a different measure, was 150 days in FY26 against 163 and 147 in the two years before.[14] In FY26 stock rose by ₹33.2 crore to ₹218.44 crore, receivables rose by ₹4.5 crore to ₹208.38 crore, and trade payables rose by ₹35.6 crore, so a rise in supplier credit paid for the stock build.[1] Without the extra ₹35.6 crore of supplier credit, FY26 cash from operations of ₹177.41 crore would have been about ₹141.8 crore. That is still healthy, but the headline owes something to timing.

Customers, dealers and governance

At home the company has seven regional distribution centres.[2] Abroad it works through the 257 dealers noted in Chapter 1. CARE said the board has five independent directors, and the managing director was paid ₹3.07 crore in FY26.[7][1] The family dispute was settled in writing on 26 December 2025, covering all claims and legal proceedings over 4.55 percent of the shares, 771,400 shares once registered in the name of the late Rattan Devi.[12] Rated by CARE at A+ with a watch for developing implications, the rating was last reviewed on 9 March 2026 and awaits the result of the control change.[7] What this means for you: governance is cleaner on paper than a year ago, but a family fight and a change of control inside 12 months are why we treat the name as deferred.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks whether the company breaks a hard red line and which archetype it fits. First, three terms. A Stage 4 share is in the falling downtrend phase. A base is a flat period when a share trades sideways and builds a floor. The debt-to-equity ratio means borrowings divided by shareholders' equity. The scoring file's India red lines are: "Debt-to-Equity >= 1.20x (non-financial; excluding standard trade LC acceptances where interest coverage >= 3.0x) or interest coverage < 2.0x"; "Promoter pledge >= 20% of promoter holding or rising pledge into a falling stock"; "3-Year Cumulative CFO / PAT < 0.25 (or < 0.40 for mature non-order-book businesses) without working-capital/order-book turnaround evidence"; and "Stage 4 breakdown (Close < falling SMA50 < falling SMA200) with no base formation (Test C not passing)". A laminate maker is a mature, non-order-book business, so we apply the stricter 0.40 limb of the cash line and use no relief clause.

The archetype standard for a COMPOUNDER is "ROCE (or adjusted ROCE ex-goodwill / ROE for financials) >= 15% (prefers >18%), D/E < 0.70, 3Y CFO/PAT >= 0.70". The manufacturing relief, a 0.50 cash bar for businesses growing 20 percent a year, is not available at 5.8 percent growth and is not needed. The tactical archetypes need a breakout, a close above the top of a trading range, or a rising chart, which Stylam lacks. Next, the Core floor. asymmetry is the ratio of possible gain to possible loss. The Core bucket's floor is 2.0, measured as "(target_base - entry_ref) / (entry_ref - stop_loss)".
