What the company does

Stylam makes decorative laminates. A laminate is a thin, hard-wearing sheet, usually built from layers of paper soaked in resin and pressed under heat, which is then bonded to plywood or particle board to make kitchen counters, wardrobe doors, office furniture and wall panels. The company sells under the STYLAM brand and is described on its Screener profile as the largest laminate manufacturer in India, with a product range that includes high-gloss, matte and textured surfaces, compact laminates for cladding and solid surfaces made from acrylic [1]. Its investor page states that it has a strong presence in over 80 countries [9]. A laminate maker of this kind earns its return on the scale of its plants and on the cost per sheet, so the manufacturing footprint is the business, and that is why the capacity additions and the utilisation of those plants matter more than any single quarter.

The business has two halves that behave differently. Exports are the larger half. In FY26 the company's export sales were INR 831 crore and domestic sales were INR 298 crore, which means exports were about three-quarters of the year's revenue of INR 1,129 crore [6]. The export book is spread across Europe, Asia-Pacific and the Middle East, with the United States at 10 to 15 percent of laminate revenue and the Middle East at a similar share, according to the Q1 FY27 call summary [5]. Exporting brings two kinds of exposure that a domestic maker does not carry. Shipping costs and container availability can move a quarter's revenue, and import duties can change the price a foreign buyer will pay. The company reports that US duty was re-set to 10 percent with effect from 24 July 2026, after a planned move to zero, and that orders have continued at that rate [5].

The domestic half has been stuck for several years. Management says the domestic run rate has been stagnant at about INR 300 crore for four years, and that domestic is roughly a quarter of revenue [5]. In the June 2026 quarter, domestic sales were INR 79 crore against exports of INR 247 crore [6]. The company is now rebuilding the domestic sales organisation: new sales teams, new distributors, warehouses in states where it was previously thin, and a marketing push to restore brand trust. Management expects a gradual volume ramp from the second quarter of FY27 and a significant improvement from the third [5]. For a reader, this is the main operating question. An exporter with a flat domestic business can still compound, but only if the export and new-plant growth is large enough to hide the flat part. The Q1 numbers show that the company has not yet reached that position.

Plants and the third plant

The third plant is the centre of the growth case. The company's exchange filing says that it commenced commercial production at the Manak Tabra-2 plant from September 22, 2026, with a capex of Rs.300 crore and a capacity of 5.25 crore square metres [1]. On the July call, management had guided to commercial production by September 1, 2026, possibly August 15 to 20, and had said that a dry run was already under way [5]. The filing date is three weeks later than the call's September 1 date, and the earlier history includes repeated slippage since November 2025, which the call attributed to family matters, environmental clearances and heavy rain in Chandigarh [5]. The plant is described as a greenfield facility next to the existing works at Manak Tabra in Haryana [6].

Guidance is a company's own forecast of its coming results. Management's guidance for the plant is conservative but specific. It expects INR 250 to 300 crore of revenue from the new plant in FY27, based on first-year capacity utilisation of 25 to 30 percent or more, with ramp-up month by month because customers do not place annual orders in this industry [5]. The next major capital expenditure announcement, which is the company's plan for new spending on plant and equipment, has been deferred to the second quarter of FY27 and is expected to target laminate-adjacent products such as plywood, MDF and particle board for the domestic market [5]. These are plain facts about the plan. The reader should keep them in mind for the monitoring chapter, because each one comes with a date that can be checked.

Ownership, the Aica transaction and the board

The ownership picture changed in the last twelve months. The Japanese building-materials group Aica Kogyo bought its way into the company in two steps under a share purchase agreement signed in December 2025. The second closing, on 17 June 2026, transferred 1,716,240 shares, which took Aica's total to 6,779,224 shares and to 40 percent of the total voting capital [7]. The total equity capital is 16,948,060 shares of Rs. 5 each, which is the reference figure for every per-share and market-value figure in this dossier [7]. The transaction was valued at 15,253 million rupees, which is about INR 1,525 crore [8]. The stake was bought directly from the promoter group, and the filing classifies Aica as part of that group [7]. The deal also triggered a mandatory open offer to public shareholders [28].

The practical meaning for a shareholder is about control and about what the partnership will deliver. The Tokyo wire report says that the transaction gives the Japanese firm majority control of the board [8]. Aica attended its first board meeting on 22 July 2026 [5]. Management says that Aica will not be involved in daily operations. The expected benefits are technology transfer for Japanese high-pressure laminates and possible sourcing of acrylic solid surface through Aica's global distribution, and management said clarity on both would come in three to four months from the July call [5]. On the Q1 call, management gave a three-to-four-month window for clarity on the technology transfer and acrylic orders, but no firm date for either. Our reading is that the partnership is real as a shareholding change, but its operating value has not yet been shown in any reported number.

What would change this view is simple to state. If Aica's technology transfer and acrylic sourcing begin to show in the order book or in the export line, the partnership moves from a shareholding fact to an earnings fact. If neither appears within the three-to-four-month window, the stake remains a financial event, and the market is right to price it that way.

Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when it earns a high return on the money it has put in, when it funds its growth from its own cash flow, and when its customers and its cost position are hard to copy. Stylam scores well on the first two tests and is harder to judge on the third. Its return on capital has been high for years. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is the operating profit a business earns on the capital tied up in it. The consolidated ROCE was 34 percent in FY24, 27 percent in FY25 and 27 percent in FY26 [1]. The headline figure on the same page is 26.6 percent [1]. The standalone table on the Trendlyne page shows 24.94 percent for FY26, and a live quote page shows 24.99 percent [2] [12]. The aggregators differ by a few points because they use different capital bases. Every version is far above the 15 percent floor.

The balance sheet is conservative. Borrowings were nil in FY24, INR 36 crore in FY25 and INR 29 crore in FY26, against reserves of INR 798 crore and equity share capital of INR 8 crore [1]. The Screener profile flags the company as almost debt free [1], and management describes it as net debt-free [6]. Net debt is borrowings less cash, so net debt-free means that cash covers the borrowings. That matters for a laminate business, because its plants are heavy and its prices move with raw materials. A business with little debt can absorb a bad quarter without a forced sale of assets. The company's investing outflows, however, have been large: INR 126 crore in FY25 and INR 156 crore in FY26, mostly on the third plant [1].

Cash generation has improved. Cash from operations was INR 109 crore in FY24, INR 107 crore in FY25 and INR 177 crore in FY26 [1]. The standalone table, which the Trendlyne page reports, shows the same pattern at INR 112.9 crore, INR 107.5 crore and INR 177.1 crore [2]. The FY26 operating cash flow of INR 177 crore covered the year's investing outflow of INR 156 crore, the largest of the three years, so the plant build was paid for from the business's own cash in that year. That is a meaningful change, and it is one reason the cash gate passes.

The margin and its meaning

Operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales, before interest, tax and depreciation. It shows how much each rupee of sales leaves after the direct costs of making and selling the product. Stylam's operating margin was 20 percent in FY24, 18 percent in FY25 and 20 percent in FY26 [1]. Those readings are high for a manufacturer of this kind, and they show that the company has kept its price discipline through a period of raw-material swings. In the June 2026 quarter the margin rose to 21.09 percent, a gain of 2.38 percentage points on the year before, and management said the gain came from efficiency and higher utilisation, not from an inventory windfall [4] [5]. Management guides to a 19 to 20 percent margin once the new plant ramps up. That guide is below the current print, which the company describes as conservative.

Where the moat is, and where it is thin

The company's advantage lies in scale in one product category, in its export distribution, and in its manufacturing cost. It is the largest laminate maker in India by its own description [1], and it sells to more than 80 countries [9]. Those are real assets. Scale in a plant business lowers the cost per sheet, and a distribution network that spans many countries is slow to build. The thin part is the domestic market. A laminate buyer in India can switch brands quickly, and the company's domestic sales have not grown in four years despite price increases, which suggests that brand and channel are not yet the moat that the export numbers imply. The restructuring is an admission that this part of the franchise needs rebuilding.

The quality of the earnings is good in one sense and questionable in another. The FY26 profit of INR 150 crore came with a cash flow of INR 177 crore, which is a healthy sign [1]. But the cash conversion cycle is 150 days and debtor days are 67, which means the company waits a long time for its cash after it ships [1]. The gap is working capital, which is the cash tied up in stock and customer credit, net of supplier credit. For an exporter, long receivables are normal, but they tie up working capital and they raise the risk of a bad debt if an overseas customer fails. The reader should watch the working capital figures in each annual report.

Here is what this means for an owner. The business has the returns and the balance sheet that the framework asks for. Its weak points are a flat domestic market, long collection periods and a growth engine that depends on a single plant ramp. Those points are not disqualifying on their own. They are the reason the company needs the price to be in a healthy trend before it can be bought, and the price is not in that position.

Growth: what the sales and profit lines say

Growth is the change in a line from one year to the next, shown as a percentage. Sales grew 12.1 percent in FY25 and 10.2 percent in FY26, which is a steady pace for a maker that is exporting into a slow European market and a flat domestic one. Over three years the compound annual growth in sales is 6 percent, according to the Screener figure, which is modest for a company with a plant expansion under way [1]. That three-year rate is lower than the two recent years because sales fell in FY24 before growing again, so the window starts at a higher level. Profit behaved differently. Profit grew faster than sales over three years, but the operating margin did not widen: it was 20, 18 and 20 percent across the same years. Return on equity and return on capital

Return on equity (ROE) is profit after tax divided by shareholders' equity, the return earned on the owners' money. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is operating profit before tax divided by the capital the business uses, which includes borrowings. ROCE is the better gate because it does not depend on how much the company has borrowed. The consolidated ROE averaged 22 percent over three years and was 20 percent in the last year, and the headline ROE on Screener is 19.9 percent [1]. The ROCE reading is the one the Layer 1 gate uses, and at 26.6 percent it clears the 15 percent floor and the 18 percent preference for Core names.

Borrowings against equity, and what it means here

Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by owners' equity. A ratio of 0.70 means the company has seventy rupees of debt for every hundred rupees of equity. Here, borrowings of INR 29 crore against equity of INR 806 crore (equity share capital of INR 8 crore plus reserves of INR 798 crore) give a ratio of 0.04 [1]. We computed the ratio from the two published lines rather than taking a ratio from a page, because the aggregators do not publish a single consistent debt-to-equity figure for this company. The standalone pages show the same order of magnitude [2]. The debt gate passes by a wide margin. It would take a rise in borrowings of about INR 535 crore to reach the ceiling on the current equity capital, which the company has no sign of planning.

Working capital and receivables

Screener shows debtor days of 67 in FY26 and a cash conversion cycle of 150 days [1]. The cycle is long, and it is one reason the company's cash flow lags its profit in some years. For this gate it does not matter, because the cumulative cash ratio is 0.98. For a reader who plans to hold the stock, it is a line to watch, because a deterioration in collections would hit the cash ratio first.

Why the stage reads as Stage 3

The framework calls the stage from the moving averages and the price position. A Stage 2 markup needs the close above a rising 50-day average and above the 200-day average. Here the close is above the 200-day average, which is rising in the ledger record, but it is below the 50-day average, and the 50-day average has been falling since at least ten sessions ago. The 50-day average stood at 3,439.14 ten sessions ago and at 3,423.42 twenty-one sessions ago, and it is now 3,414.96 (ledger, 13 Oct 2026). The stock is therefore in the phase that follows a top: higher prices were reached, the trend has flattened, and the price is now trading back toward the averages. That is the Stage 3 reading, and the ledger labels it as such. No death cross has formed, because the 50-day average is still above the 200-day average, which is why the framework does not treat this as a Stage 4 breakdown. Stage 4 is a markdown, a decline in which the averages fall below the price.

The distinction matters for the verdict. A Stage 3 stock is not a failed business, and it may re-enter Stage 2 if the price recovers its averages. But the Core rule is clear: Stage 1 with a breakout or Stage 2. A breakout is a rise above the level where the stock had been trading sideways. Stage 3 is neither. The gate is therefore a failure now, and it would become a pass only after the price confirms a new trend.

The drawdown: what happened after the results

The stock's drawdown has a clear sequence and clear causes. It reached an all-time high of INR 4,150 before the June quarter results, and it closed at INR 3,659 before the results were released on 22 July 2026 [4]. The market's first reaction was a fall of 7.3 percent, followed by a further 6.7 percent by the third day and 8.0 percent by the fifth [4]. The stock then held near INR 3,384 at the end of July, which left it 18.5 percent below the high at that point [4]. By 13 October the close was INR 3,269.8, 21.21 percent below the 52-week high in the ledger.

The market was not reacting to the profit number. Profit rose 70 percent, and the margin beat management's own guide. It was reacting to what the call said about the next two quarters. Sales grew 15.4 percent against a full-year target of 20 to 25 percent, which left the second half needing a much faster pace [4]. The plant start had been delayed several times. The domestic market had been flat for four years, and the restructuring plan was generic in its description. The Aica partnership had no timetable for the operating benefits. On top of that, the US duty was re-set to 10 percent, and raw-material prices, with phenol near USD 1,400 a tonne and melamine at USD 1,000 to 1,100 a tonne, were described as elevated and slow to normalise [5]. The stock has since drifted lower, not because the profit has fallen, but because the price is now pricing a slower path to the growth it promised.

What would reverse this is the same list in reverse. A plant that starts on the new date, a domestic line that turns in the third quarter, and an Aica deliverable that shows up in an order or a filing. Until one of those appears, the price is best read as a stock waiting for evidence.

Gate 1: return on capital passes

Return on capital employed is the test of whether the business earns more on its capital than the capital costs. A 15 percent floor is generous for a manufacturer, and the company clears it with a margin of more than 11 points on the headline figure [1]. The three years of data show a return that fell from 34 percent to 27 percent and has held there, which is a high plateau rather than a peak that is about to collapse. The lower standalone reading of 24.94 percent for FY26 still clears the floor [2]. The gate passes on every source we read. The reader should note that the new plant will add capital, so the ROCE will fall if the plant earns below the company's current return. That is a risk to monitor, not a failure today.

Gate 2: debt-to-equity passes

Debt-to-equity compares what the company owes with what its owners have put in. A business that borrows heavily to build plants has less room in a bad year, because interest must be paid whatever the sales. Stylam's borrowings were INR 29 crore at March 2026, down from INR 36 crore a year earlier, and its equity was INR 806 crore [1]. The ratio of 0.04 is far inside the 0.70 ceiling. The Screener profile calls the company almost debt free [1], and the company describes itself as net debt-free [6]. The gate passes without any adjustment. The company is not a financial company, so the financial-institution exclusion does not apply.

Gate 3: cash conversion passes

Cash conversion asks whether reported profit turns into cash. The three-year cumulative ratio is 0.98: INR 393 crore of operating cash against INR 400 crore of profit [1]. The year-by-year ratios are 0.85, 0.88 and 1.18. FY24 is the weakest year at 0.85, and the three-year window smooths it. Because the gate is cumulative, one strong year cannot carry a failing record, but here no failure exists. The gate passes, and the cross-check on the standalone table, with cash from operations of INR 177.1 crore in FY26, agrees [2].

Gate 4: technical posture fails

The technical gate asks whether the stock is in a phase that a Core name can be bought in. The rule is Stage 1 with a breakout from a base, or Stage 2 with a markup. The ledger puts Stylam in Stage 3, the topping and pullback phase. The close is 3,269.8, below the 50-day average of 3,414.96, and the 50-day average is falling, having been 3,439.14 ten sessions earlier (ledger, 13 Oct 2026). The stock does not meet the markup condition, because a Stage 2 name has a rising 50-day average that sits below the price. The framework therefore fails the gate and records FAIL_TECHNICAL_STAGE3. Gate 5: asymmetry fails

The asymmetry gate has two parts. The first is the ratio. For a Core name the floor is 3.0, which means the reward to the target must be at least three times the risk to the stop. The second is the anchor. The target must come from forward earnings multiplied by a three-year median P/E, or from a measured move on a base. A measured move is a price target set by repeating the distance of an earlier price move. An arbitrary target is not allowed. The stop is set by rule: below the lower of the swing low and the 50-day or 200-day average, less one ATR, which gives 2,651.12 on the ledger figures.

The target fails the anchor. The forward input exists only as a consensus. EPS is earnings per share, and the consensus forward EPS is INR 111.68 for the current year, labelled FY27 by the source. The consensus average price target is INR 3,699 across six analysts [16]. The three-year median P/E was not found. The aggregators give a five-year average P/E of 23.69 times, a five-year range of 16.19 to 31.88 times and a current P/E of 32.82 times [18], but none publishes a three-year median, so we could not construct the anchor the rule requires. The carried-forward target of INR 4,350 from the shortlist is not anchored in any source we could cite, so it cannot be used. On that unverified target, the ratio would be 1.75, which is well under the floor. The asymmetry chapter shows the arithmetic and the sensitivity.

The asymmetry estimate, line by line

Asymmetry is the ratio of the reward to the risk. The reward is the distance from today's price to the target, and the risk is the distance from today's price to the stop. A ratio of 3.0 means the target is three times as far away as the stop. A trade breaks even if it is right one time in four, and it makes money if it is right more often. The framework's floor for a Core name is 3.0, and the rule forbids targets that are not anchored to a number the reader can check. The table below walks through the calculation. Each line uses the line above it, so a reader can redo it.

The inputs come from the ledger and from the scoring configuration. The stop follows the Core rule, which places the stop below the lowest of the swing low and the 50-day and 200-day averages, less one ATR. The lowest of those three values is the 200-day average of 2,772.78, which sets the stop at 2,651.12. That stop is 618.68 rupees below the close, which is 18.9 percent of the price. The risk is therefore wide, because the 200-day average is far below the current price. A wide stop makes the ratio harder to reach, not easier, and that is the first reason the asymmetry gate is difficult for this stock.

The number

The number under test is the asymmetry ratio: the base target minus the price, divided by the price minus the stop. The Core Alpha floor is 3.0.

Fatal flaws: the case against the stock

A red-team section asks the strongest honest question against the candidate. The question here is not whether the business is good, because it is. It is whether a reasonable buyer could be wrong to add the stock to the Core list at this price, in this trend, on this evidence. The answer comes from five specific weaknesses. Each one can be checked against the sources in the notes, and each names the level or event that would reduce it.

The trend has broken, and the gate fails for that reason

The most direct flaw is that the stock is in Stage 3 and the 50-day average is falling. A Core name must be in a markup or a base breakout. A stock below a falling 50-day average has not started a new upward leg, however attractive its returns look. The 50-day average stood at 3,439.14 ten sessions ago and is 3,414.96 now, and the close is below it (ledger, 13 Oct 2026). The breakdown from the July peak is also 21.21 percent on the ledger's close basis. The flaw is confirmed by the ledger, and it is the reason the candidate is rejected. The level that would weaken the flaw is a close above the falling 50-day average, followed by the average turning up.

The valuation is above its own range

At a P/E of 32.7 on Screener's snapshot, and 32.82 on the TickJournal page, the stock already trades above the top of its five-year range of 16.19 to 31.88 times earnings. The five-year high is the highest annual reading, so the stock stands above its own record, not just near it. Its five-year average is 23.69 times [1] [18]. The forward multiple on consensus FY27 EPS of 111.68 is about 29 times at the ledger close, which is high for a manufacturer whose volumes are flat in the domestic market [16]. A stock priced above its own multiple range has less room for a re-rating and more room for a fall if the earnings do not arrive. The level that would weaken this flaw is a fall in the multiple toward the five-year average, or an earnings upgrade large enough to bring the multiple down while the price holds.

Growth is below the company's own target

Sales grew 15.4 percent in the June quarter against a full-year target of 20 to 25 percent [4]. To reach the low end of the target, the company would need the remaining three quarters to grow faster than the first, and that growth has to come from the new plant and the domestic turnaround together. The domestic run rate has been about INR 300 crore for four years [5], and the June quarter's domestic sales of INR 79 crore show that the restructuring has not yet lifted the line [6]. A reader who believes in the target has to believe in a sharp acceleration. The level that would weaken this flaw is a second quarter with sales growth of 20 percent or more.

The plant has slipped more than once

The third plant is half of the growth case. The company guided to commercial production by 1 September 2026 on the July call, and its exchange filing gives 22 September 2026 as the date commercial production began [5] [1]. The start had already moved from November 2025 to August 2026 and then to 1 September [5]. Every delay pushes FY27 revenue from the plant toward the end of the year, and the guided INR 250 to 300 crore depends on the first-year ramp of 25 to 30 percent utilisation. A plant that starts late and ramps slowly would hit both the revenue guide and the margin guide. The level that would weaken this flaw is a utilisation reading in the 25 to 30 percent range in the first full quarter of operation.

Raw-material costs and the US tariff

Two external costs sit against the margin. The company reports that phenol is near USD 1,400 a tonne and melamine between USD 1,000 and 1,100 a tonne, and it says these prices are elevated because of the West Asia conflict. Management also says that normalisation could take six to nine months even if the conflict ends [5]. The US duty is 10 percent after the July re-set, and the US is 10 to 15 percent of laminate revenue [5]. A margin of 21 percent in a quarter with high input costs is good, but the company has said it will not raise prices further unless raw materials worsen, which means the margin depends on input prices staying where they are. The level that would weaken this flaw is a fall in melamine and phenol prices, which would ease the cost base, or a US duty relief.

Control has moved, and the partnership is not yet visible

The Aica transaction gives the Japanese partner 40 percent of the company and, on the Tokyo wire's reading, majority control of the board [8]. For a minority shareholder that is a change of governance. The partner's benefits, technology transfer and acrylic sourcing, have no published date beyond management's three-to-four-month window from the July call [5]. A partner with control and no delivered benefit creates two risks: the strategy could move away from the company's current model, and the promised benefits could slip. The level that would weaken this flaw is a published technology-transfer agreement, or a first acrylic order that shows in the export line.

The data has gaps the reader should know about

Three gaps should be stated. The audited FY26 annual report could not be read in this run, so the cash figures rest on a compiled table. The promoter pledge, which the framework asks for, was not published in any source we could reach, so the pledge position is unknown. The next results date has not been announced, so the timing of the Q2 read is unknown. What would change this view:

  • If the audited FY26 cash flow statement shows cash from operations below INR 177 crore, the cash figures in the table would have to be restated, and the cumulative ratio would move.
  • If a pledge disclosure showed a material pledge by the promoter group, the ownership risk would rise sharply, given that the promoter holding has increased this year.

The red-team conclusion

The bear case does not say the business is failing. It says that the price has already moved to a level that assumes the growth and the multiple will arrive together, and that the trend has not confirmed either. Each of the five flaws is checkable, and each has a level that would reduce it. The verdict follows from the one flaw that the framework treats as a gate, the technical trend, together with the asymmetry gap that the consensus numbers confirm. On the evidence available, the bear case is stronger on timing than on the business.

Glossary

ATR, average true range, is the typical daily price swing over fourteen sessions. CFO is cash from operations. CFO to PAT is the cash conversion ratio. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by equity. OPM is operating profit margin. PAT is profit after tax. ROCE is return on capital employed, and ROE is return on equity. Stage 1 is a base, Stage 2 a markup, Stage 3 a topping or pullback and Stage 4 a markdown, in the Weinstein sense used by the framework.
