Before you read

Hitachi Energy India makes the transformers, switchgear and high-voltage direct current links that carry power across India's grid. It has almost no debt and a record order backlog. At Rs 28,395 the shares cost about 110 times trailing profit (106 on Screener's basis), so the price already assumes years of very fast growth. Our verdict is wait for support: we would buy only on a pullback to between Rs 25,420 and Rs 26,700, where the reward is about 2.5 times the risk. The call is wrong if analysts cut the profit estimate for the year to March 2028 by about four percent, if the price closes below Rs 24,318.58, or if the large high-voltage orders slip.

1. What the company does

Hitachi Energy India Limited was created in 2019 when the power-grid business of ABB India was demerged into a separate company. It was known as ABB Power Products and Systems India until it took its present name in November 2021.[1][8] Hitachi Energy Ltd of Switzerland owns 71.31% of the shares, and Hitachi Ltd of Japan owns all of Hitachi Energy Ltd.[27][13] The company makes transformers, which are the largest business, plus high-voltage switchgear, grid automation systems, power quality equipment and the converter stations that carry HVDC power. HVDC means high-voltage direct current, a way of sending bulk electricity over very long distances with lower losses than ordinary alternating-current lines.[8][37] It runs 19 manufacturing units in eight places, and the group employs about 7,500 people in India across two entities, of which the listed company reports 2,777.[37][6]

The customers are mostly large buyers who cannot switch off the power. Utilities and transmission companies order substations and transformers. Renewable developers order the equipment that connects wind and solar parks to the grid. Steel, cement and chemical plants order their own switchyards. Data centres are a new and fast-growing buyer. So is battery storage: in Q1 FY27 the company won its first BESS (battery energy storage system) order, for 165 MW and 330 MWh.[9] Railways and metros buy traction transformers.[9][10][19] About one rupee in four of sales comes from outside India: exports were Rs 2,134 crore of Rs 8,148 crore of FY26 revenue, or 26.2%.[2] Management says exports run at about 25% of revenue.[16][17]

The company earns money in three ways. It sells products such as transformers and switchgear. It sells projects, which are turnkey substations and HVDC links built over several years, with revenue booked as the work progresses. And it sells services, such as retrofits, bay extensions and control-system upgrades for equipment already in the field.[19][21] Customers pay large advances. Money received for work not yet delivered stood at Rs 2,835 crore at March 2026, up from Rs 1,750 crore a year earlier.[3] Raw material prices such as copper and steel matter, but about 70% of contracts carry price-variation clauses that pass most cost changes to the buyer.[23] One cost is unusual: royalties and group fees to the parent take about 8% to 9% of sales, according to a third-party summary of management commentary.[24]

The order backlog is the best single guide to the next two years. It stood at Rs 32,222.1 crore on 30 June 2026, a record, against trailing twelve-month sales of Rs 9,162 crore.[9][36][1] That is about 3.5 years of sales. The mix matters, though. About Rs 20,000 crore of the backlog is HVDC work, and management said in February 2026 that the rest, the core product orders, was about Rs 10,000 crore to Rs 11,000 crore.[25][20] HVDC projects are large, slow and lumpy, and they gave only about Rs 1,100 crore, or 15%, of FY26 revenue.[18] So the backlog is large, but two-thirds of it depends on a few big projects being built on time.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Transformer: a machine that raises or lowers electric voltage so power can travel far and then be used safely.
  • Switchgear: switches and breakers that connect, protect and isolate parts of the grid.
  • HVDC: high-voltage direct current, the long-distance technology used for the biggest transmission links. LCC and VSC are its two designs, the older line-commutated converter and the newer voltage-source converter.
  • Order backlog: signed orders not yet delivered. It is the company's visible future revenue.
  • Operational EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization): the profit measure management reports. It strips out some accounting items, so it is not identical to the profit in the statutory accounts.
  • Royalty: a fee paid to the parent for the right to use its technology.
  • GVA: gigavolt-ampere, a measure of transformer capacity. One GVA is a thousand million volt-amperes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong because it sells hard-to-copy equipment into a market where demand is rising faster than supply. Brokers note that India's power plan needs a large grid build. The Central Electricity Authority estimates Rs 7.93 lakh crore of transmission investment to connect more than 900 gigawatts of non-fossil power by 2035.[14] One brokerage expects renewable capacity to rise from 283.5 GW in March 2026 to 596.3 GW by FY32, and transformer capacity to nearly double, backing about Rs 9.2 trillion of transmission spending.[30] Longer lines and more grid-stabilising equipment follow from that build, and that is what this company sells.

The strongest piece of the moat is HVDC. A 360 ONE Capital note, summarised by Exencial Research, says Hitachi Energy holds a 49% share of India's HVDC projects against 28% for Siemens Energy and 23% for GE Vernova, and is present in 9 of India's 18 HVDC links.[31] The same note argues that only two firms can build the largest line-commutated links, Hitachi and GE Vernova T&D. That is a real barrier, but it is not a lock: GE Vernova was the lowest bidder on the Barmer to South Kalamb link, and 360 ONE puts the odds of either firm winning the next such order at about 50:50.[31] We treat the moat as strong but contestable.

Financial strength adds to it. Crisil rates the company AAA with a stable outlook and supports the rating with the parent's backing and a strong market position in the power grid segment.[13] Borrowings were Rs 86 crore against equity of about Rs 5,176 crore at March 2026, and cash and investments were Rs 4,689 crore.[1][3] The company can fund its planned Rs 4,000 crore of capacity additions from cash and profits without borrowing.[13][10] Capacity is the other part of the moat: the new Karjan transformer factory is meant to add 30 to 40 GVA, which management says roughly doubles current transformer capacity.[18]

There are four weaknesses. First, the margin is lower than that of peers. Royalty and group fees cost about 8% to 9% of sales, and the operating margin (the share of sales left after running costs) of 15.3% compares with 25.5% at GE Vernova T&D.[24][6][38] Second, competition is intense. Crisil names competition in capital goods and bidding pressure as a weakness.[13] In July 2026 the shares fell nearly 8% in a day after reports that four Chinese equipment firms were given a two-year exemption to bid for government power projects.[29] Management said it welcomes a level playing field and expects no material effect, and says Chinese entrants matter mostly in transformers and gas-insulated switchgear.[23][16] Third, orders are lumpy. FY26 orders were Rs 18,456.5 crore, only 1.6% above FY25, even though the backlog rose 53.5%.[22][10] Fourth, the buyers are mostly public-sector and project-driven, so a delay in a large project can move one quarter's results.[13]

What the evidence says about the moat

Put together, the evidence supports a strong position in high-voltage products and HVDC, a clean balance sheet and a visible backlog. It does not support a claim of unchallenged pricing power. The gross margin (the share of sales left after direct costs) was about 40% in Q1 FY27, and a Jefferies analyst noted on the call that it was over 350 basis points lower than a year earlier, which management attributed to product mix.[16][23] A margin that holds near 40% while competition rises would confirm pricing power. A slide would warn that the moat is narrower than the HVDC share suggests.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management gave little numeric guidance, which means its own published forecast. On the Q4 FY26 call it said it does not give forward-looking numbers, and the only stated target for FY27 is a double-digit operating margin, with no number attached.[18][24] So the audit looks at the promises that can be checked: margins, capital spending, HVDC award timing and factory dates.

On margins the company delivered early. It guided to a double-digit operating margin and said on the Q2 FY26 call that it had reached it ahead of its own target.[21] It then reported 15.2%, 15.6%, 16.4% and 16.0% in the four quarters since, and the Q4 FY26 operational EBITDA was Rs 452.4 crore.[12][11][10][9][26] On capital spending it fell short. The Rs 2,000 crore programme announced in October 2024 was meant to run over five years.[15] The plan was about Rs 750 crore for FY26 on the Q2 call and Rs 700 crore or more on the Q3 call, but only Rs 155 crore had been spent after nine months and the year closed at about Rs 509 crore.[21][19][4] On HVDC timing it slipped. In November 2025 management expected two HVDC tenders to be settled in FY26. As of August 2026 a greenfield project was still being bid, with an award expected within six months, and a rival had been the lowest bidder on another large link.[21][16] The Mumbai HVDC link was delivered: management said in February 2026 that commissioning was two to three weeks away, and the Q4 release reports it commissioned.[19][10]

3. The numbers, in plain words

EPS (earnings per share) is net profit divided by the number of shares. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a weakest assumption. This chapter names it, says what would show it is wrong, and says when we will know. It then gives the strongest bear case against our own conclusion and picks the one claim the whole call depends on.

The 65 times multiple is the weakest assumption

A multiple is a mood, and 65 times earnings two years out is generous for an equipment maker. The stock's two-year median trailing multiple is 160 times, but only because profit was tiny.[28] As profit catches up, the multiple must fall unless growth stays above 40%. Four of the five peers trade at 51 to 71 times forward earnings.[38][39][40][41] The evidence that would show we are too generous is a sector sell-off in which the peer group falls to 50 times. We will see it in the results of GE Vernova T&D on 30 October 2026 and of Hitachi Energy India in the first half of November.

The date for Hitachi's own results is not announced. Trackers estimate 6 November, and the legal deadline is 14 November 2026.[34][35][6]

The FY28 profit forecast depends on HVDC timing and margin

Consensus FY28 profit needs revenue to rise 44% in FY27 and 39% in FY28 with a margin near 16%.[7] The risks are specific. The gross margin fell year on year in Q1 FY27 despite a record quarter.[16] Royalties take 8% to 9% of sales.[24] The Karjan factory is not due until the last quarter of calendar 2028, so capacity for large transformers is tight until then. And a greenfield HVDC award is still pending and a rival leads on another link. The evidence that would show a problem is order intake below sales for two quarters in a row, or an operating margin below 15%. We will see the first signal in the Q2 FY27 results.[21][16]

The red-team bear case

The strongest case against owning the stock is that it is a good company at a price that needs everything to go right. A bear would say: the backlog is two-thirds HVDC, and a rival was the lowest bidder on one large HVDC link; FY26 orders grew 1.6%; four Chinese firms can now bid for public tenders; foreign holders have raised their stake from 4.96% to 11.96% since March 2025 and can leave quickly; and a Rs 4,200 crore equity-raising approval is on file.[25][29][22][31] If profit reaches only Rs 1,780 crore in FY28 and the multiple falls to 45 times, the price is about Rs 18,000, a 37% fall from here. That scenario needs no collapse, only slower orders and a market that stops paying for growth.

The best answer to that bear case is the net cash of Rs 4,603 crore, the AAA rating and the fact that earnings estimates are still rising. They limit the damage if the business slows, but they do not protect the price from a lower multiple.
