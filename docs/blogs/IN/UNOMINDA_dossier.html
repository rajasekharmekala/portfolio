Start here

This is a research file on one company, written for a reader who is new to equity analysis. It is the output of a screening process that decides whether a company is worth buying with real money. The process has three layers. Layer 1 holds the hard tests, and a company that fails any one of them is rejected outright. Layer 2 scores the operating health of the business. Layer 3 decides how to time an entry. Only a company that passes Layer 1 reaches the other two layers.

The verdict here is REJECTED. The short reason is that the share is not in an uptrend, the relative-strength test has turned negative, and the price target that the rules allow implies less upside than the process requires for the risk taken. The business itself passes its quality tests, which is why the file is worth reading even though the answer is no. The distinction between a good business and a good purchase is the central idea of this document.

Every figure carries a footnote number. The numbers in square brackets point to the reference list at the end, where you will find the page, the date it was retrieved, and the as-of date of the figure. Prices, moving averages, relative strength index (RSI), average true range (ATR), stage, volume and the momentum test come from the run's ledger, which is the authoritative price record for this process and carries an as-of date of 12 October 2026. Financial figures come from the company's consolidated filings as reproduced on the aggregator and data pages listed in the references, and are in Indian rupees, with crore meaning ten million.

Read the sections in order. The verdict is explained first, the business and the numbers come next, and the three layers follow. If you read only one table, read the Layer 1 gate table, because it lists each hard test, the value measured, and whether it passed.

A useful way to picture the process is a building inspection. Layer 1 is the structural survey: a building with a cracked foundation fails regardless of how attractive its lobby is. Layer 2 is the operating review, which checks whether the building is well run once it passes the survey. Layer 3 is the timing of the purchase, which asks whether the price is sensible for a building that has already passed both. This company's financial structure is sound, which is the equivalent of a good foundation. Its price trend and its reward-for-risk do not pass, which is the equivalent of a survey that finds a problem in the timing and the price rather than in the walls. The inspection therefore stops at the first failed survey item, and the rest of the file explains why that is the right rule and how it was applied.

The file is built so that a reader can disagree with a step and see exactly where the disagreement starts. If you think the price target is too low, the asymmetry table shows the input and the arithmetic. If you think the momentum result is too strict, the momentum table lists each of the nine sub-tests with its measured value. Nothing in the verdict depends on a number that is not shown. Where a figure could not be verified, the file says so, instead of filling the gap with an estimate. The sources that could not be reached, and the conflicts between them, are listed in the run notes near the end.

Two conventions matter throughout. First, the process distinguishes a fact from a hypothesis. A watchlist entry, which in this case says the name is a high-conviction auto-ancillary compounder with an entry zone and a stop, is treated as an unverified idea until the data confirms it. Second, the process prefers an explicit "not available" to a guessed number. When a value is not published or not verifiable, the file writes N/A and gives the reason. That rule is why the customer-concentration figure and the plant count are marked as not found rather than estimated.

Terms used in this dossier

Each term below appears in the verdict or in the tables. The definitions are plain and the examples use the company's own figures where possible, so that the terms are tied to something concrete rather than left abstract.

Base and pivot. A base is a sideways holding pattern that a stock forms after a fall, and the pivot is the price at the top of that base. A close above the pivot on heavy volume is a breakout, which is the event the Core rule waits for.

Glossary: where each term is used

The terms are listed in the order a reader meets them, and each one is defined once, where it first matters. The tables later in the file can be read without returning here, because the definitions below carry the meaning of each figure.

ROCE (return on capital employed). ROCE measures how much operating profit the business earns on every rupee of money that is tied up in it, whether that money came from shareholders or from lenders. Think of it as the yield on the whole factory-and-working-capital base. The process requires at least 15 percent and prefers more than 18 percent for a Core Alpha name. Uno Minda's consolidated ROCE was 20 percent for the year to March 2026 [1], so it clears both lines.

Debt to equity. This is the amount of borrowed money divided by the shareholders' own capital. A ratio of 0.40 means that for every rupee of owners' capital the company has 40 paise of borrowings. The process requires less than 0.70. Uno Minda's ratio, calculated from its March 2026 borrowings and equity, is 0.40 [1][11].

CFO to PAT (cash conversion). CFO is cash from operations, the money the business actually collected after paying its running costs. PAT is profit after tax, the accounting profit. Comparing the two over three years tests whether reported profit turns into cash. A ratio of 0.70 or more is the floor. Uno Minda's three-year ratio is 1.17, which means it has produced more cash than the profit it reported [1]. Depreciation, which is a charge that lowers profit but not cash, and a release of working capital are the likely reasons.

SMA (simple moving average). An SMA is the average closing price over a set number of trading days. The 50-day average shows the medium-term trend and the 200-day average shows the long-term trend. Price above both, with the averages rising, is the signature of an uptrend. Price below them, with the averages falling, is the opposite. The ledger shows the SMA50 at 1,219.98 and the SMA200 at 1,162.13.

RSI (relative strength index). RSI is a 0 to 100 gauge of recent buying against selling over 14 sessions. Readings below 30 are often called oversold, and readings above 70 overbought. A reading of 36.49 means the stock has been weak but is not in the extreme zone. It does not, by itself, predict a bounce.

ATR (average true range). ATR is the typical size of a daily price move, averaged over 14 sessions. It is used as a yardstick, so that a distance can be stated as a multiple of normal movement. Uno Minda's ATR is 34.09 rupees, so a move of one ATR is roughly three percent of the price.

Stage (Weinstein stage). Stage 1 is a basing phase, where a stock moves sideways after a fall. Stage 2 is a markup phase, where the trend is up and the averages rise. Stage 3 is a topping phase and Stage 4 is a breakdown. The Core Alpha rules accept a Stage 1 base only once it breaks out, or a Stage 2 markup. A Stage 1 label alone does not pass.

Relative strength (RS). RS compares the stock's return with the benchmark index over a set window. Positive excess return means the stock beat the market. The ledger gives 3.47 percent over 63 sessions and negative 6.32 percent over 28 sessions, which means the stock led the market over the longer window but has lost ground over the last month.

Momentum gate. This is a code-run screen with two alternative tests, called Test A (already in an uptrend) and Test B (entering an uptrend). A candidate must pass one of them. Failing both means no allocation, however good the business. The result for this company is NO_MOMENTUM.

Support distance. This is the gap between the current price and the nearest defined support level, stated in ATR units. A small gap means the price is near support. Here the gap to the 20-day swing low of 1,085 is 0.57 ATR.

Asymmetry (reward-to-risk ratio). Reward-to-risk is the upside to a target compared with the downside to a stop-loss. For a Core Alpha name the ratio must be at least 3.0, meaning the reward must be three times the risk. The target must be anchored to a verifiable method: either forward earnings per share multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, or a measured move from a documented technical base. An arbitrary target is not accepted.

Forward EPS and P/E. EPS, or earnings per share, is profit per share. Forward EPS is the expected figure for a coming year, here the consensus estimate for the year to March 2027. P/E (price to earnings) is the share price divided by EPS, and it shows how many years of current earnings the price represents. A three-year median P/E smooths out one unusually high or low year.

Promoter, FII and DII. The promoter is the founding group that controls the company. FII means foreign institutional investors and DII means domestic institutional investors, such as mutual funds and insurers. Their holdings are reported every quarter and show who is buying or selling. A pledge is shares the promoter has offered as security for a loan. A high pledge is a warning sign, because a forced sale can push the price down.

Layer 1, Layer 2, Layer 3. Layer 1 is the set of pass-or-fail hard gates. Layer 2 is a traffic-light scorecard of the business's operating health. Layer 3 is the entry-timing posture, which maps the distance to support onto a ladder of buy bands. The three layers run in sequence, and failure at Layer 1 stops the analysis. The next section explains how these layers were applied in this case.

How the verdict was reached

The verdict is the output of a fixed sequence, and the sequence is the reason the answer can be trusted even when it is unwelcome. The process starts with the price record, because every technical test depends on it. The run's ledger is built from the latest completed market session, which for this file is 12 October 2026, and it supplies the closing price, the moving averages, the RSI, the ATR, the stage label, the volume multiple, the 52-week range and the relative-strength figures. The ledger is treated as authoritative for all of these. This document does not re-derive them from a different feed, and where an outside page shows a different number, the difference is recorded in the run notes rather than silently replaced.

Next, the momentum gate is run in code against the same price record. It has two alternative tests. Test A asks whether the stock is already in an uptrend that is not yet stretched. Test B asks whether the stock is entering an uptrend from a base, with volume confirming the move. A candidate passes if it meets either test. The gate is written so that a good business cannot override it. The reason is simple: a company that is fundamentally sound can still lose money for a shareholder who buys while its price is trending down, and the momentum test is there to stop that purchase.

Then the financial tests are applied to the company's consolidated results. The process takes the return on capital employed, the debt to equity ratio and the three-year cash conversion ratio from the company's reported figures. The figures used here come from the Screener consolidated page, which reproduces the company's filed statements, and are cross-checked against two other pages where those pages report the same line. Where a cross-check disagrees, the disagreement is disclosed.

After the financial tests comes the asymmetry test. The rule asks for a target price that is anchored to a verifiable method. The file tries the only permitted anchors that the data can support: forward consensus EPS multiplied by the three-year median P/E from a fetched page, and a technical measured move. A stop-loss is set using the Core Alpha placement rule, which places the stop below the lower of the recent swing low and the moving averages, minus one ATR. The reward-to-risk ratio is then computed from the target and the stop.

Only a company that passes all of these Layer 1 tests reaches Layer 2, which scores three to five operating indicators, and Layer 3, which maps the entry posture. This company does not reach either layer. The rules are explicit that a Layer 1 failure rejects the name, and that Layers 2 and 3 are not scored for a rejected name. The file therefore does not give a Layer 2 score or an entry posture, even though the data for some of those indicators exists. Scoring them would invite a reader to treat a rejected name as a candidate, which is the exact error the process is designed to prevent.

Finally, the verdict is stated with its rule and its trigger. The rule is that any Layer 1 failure gives REJECTED. The triggers that would change the answer are set out in the section "What would change the verdict", and each one is a measurable level, not a judgement about the company's prospects. That framing matters. The question was never whether Uno Minda will be a good business over the next decade, which the financial record suggests it may be. The question is whether the present price, trend and target justify committing capital now, and on the rules set, they do not.

Case note: the business in one sentence

Uno Minda is a multi-product parts maker whose revenue rises with the number of vehicles built and whose profit depends on how well it passes raw-material cost increases to carmakers. The next question is whether the planned EV plants earn their cost of capital, and the answer will show up in the return on capital employed over the next two to three years, not in the first quarter after a plant opens. A reader can test that claim each quarter by watching whether the return holds near 20 percent while the new capacity is added. Two numeric rules follow. If raw-material costs stay above about 63 percent of revenue for the next two quarters, the profit case weakens. If they fall back toward 59 percent, the margin story recovers.

Why the business is strong or weak

A strong business earns a high return on the money it uses, turns its profit into cash, and grows without needing ever more debt. A weak business does the opposite. Uno Minda shows the first kind of behaviour on most measures and some weakness on others, and the reader should keep both halves in view. The return on capital is the clearest strength. The company's ROCE was 20 percent for the year to March 2026, 19 percent in the year before and 20 percent in the year before that [1]. A return near 20 percent, sustained for three years, is a sign that the company's factories and working capital earn more than their cost. Its return on equity was 19 percent for the same year [1], which tells the same story from the owners' side.

Growth has been strong over a longer period. Consolidated sales grew at a three-year compound rate of 20 percent, and profit grew at 23 percent [1]. Over ten years, the same two measures compound at about 23 percent and 28 percent, which the third-party summary also reports [29]. Growth at that pace means the company has been winning new programmes faster than it has lost old ones, which is the central skill in a parts business. Over ten years the share price has compounded at about 34 percent a year, which is the market's own record of that growth [1].

Cash conversion is the third strength. The company generated 1,720 crore of cash from operations in the year to March 2026, against profit of 1,284 crore on the consolidated basis that Screener reports [1]. Over three years, the cash it produced is 1.17 times the profit it reported. Two things could explain that gap in a healthy business: depreciation, which is a non-cash charge that lowers profit but not cash, and a release of working capital, meaning that customers paid faster or inventory fell. The company's working-capital days fell from 18 in FY25 to 12 in FY26 [1], which suggests the second effect played a part. Depreciation is large, at 704 crore in FY26 [23], so it is also a material contributor. The point for the reader is that the cash is real, which is what the cash test is designed to confirm.

The weaknesses sit in margins and in cash spent on investment. The operating profit margin has barely moved. It was 11 percent in each of the last three years on the consolidated basis [1], and the ETMoney table shows 11.43 percent for FY26 [23]. A margin that does not rise while sales grow fast suggests the company is passing cost pressure through to its customers rather than keeping it. That is common in parts supply, and it limits how much profit each new rupee of sales brings. In the June 2026 quarter the cost of raw materials and components rose to 3,539.66 crore from 2,663.89 crore a year earlier, which is about 63.7 percent of revenue against about 59.3 percent a year earlier [14]. That is a gap of roughly 4.4 percentage points, and it is the most likely reason the quarter's profit was flat despite a 24 percent rise in revenue.

The second weakness is the cash spent on the future. Free cash flow was negative 572 crore in FY25 and positive 204 crore in FY26, against investing cash outflows of 1,479 crore in FY25 and 1,651 crore in FY26, which are mostly capital spending [1]. Heavy investment is not a fault in itself. It becomes a weakness when the new plants do not earn the return the company expects, and at this stage the file cannot yet show that, because the plants are not yet producing. The company's own guidance acknowledges start-up costs [22],.

Ownership gives a mixed picture. The promoter group holds 68.36 percent of the shares, a level that has been broadly stable [1]. A low pledge is a real strength, because it removes one route to a forced sale. The shareholding data record the pledged share of the promoter holding at 0.0 percent for the June 2026 quarter [21], and a second data page reports the same promoter figure from the NSE filing [25]. The company's own investor pages also list shareholding patterns by quarter, with the April to June 2026 quarter the latest shown [30]. Foreign institutional holding, however, has fallen from 10.09 percent at March 2025 to 8.15 percent at June 2026, while domestic institutions have raised their stake to 17.52 percent [1]. Domestic buying has absorbed foreign selling so far. A reader should watch whether that continues, because foreign selling often coincides with a weaker share price.

Layer 2 scorecard

Layer 2 was not evaluated. The rule is that when any Layer 1 gate fails, the Action Verdict is REJECTED and Layer 2 and Layer 3 are not scored. This company fails three Layer 1 gates, namely the technical posture, the momentum gate and the asymmetry floor, so Layer 2 is not scored and no operating scorecard is shown for it. The Layer 2 section is not left blank to hide a result. It is marked not evaluated, with the rule named, so that a reader knows the scorecard exists in the process but was deliberately not applied.

Layer 2 is a traffic-light check on three to five measures that drive the particular business. For a parts maker, the measures usually include capacity utilisation, the order book in relation to sales, working-capital days, the spread of customers, and whether margins are expanding as volume rises. Each measure is marked pass, watch or fail, and a candidate needs a minimum number of passes to qualify. The purpose is to check whether a company that has passed the financial floor is also operating well, in the sense of having enough orders and enough capacity to deliver them. A business can be financially sound on past figures and still have a weakening order book, and Layer 2 is designed to catch that.

The reason the scorecard is not applied here is procedural, and it is worth explaining why the procedure matters. A scorecard can be made to look favourable for almost any company if the measures are chosen after the fact. Running the scorecard on a company that has already failed the hard gates would invite a reader to treat the rejected name as a candidate that is "nearly there" on operations. The process prevents that by stopping at the gate. The file therefore does not say which Layer 2 measures would pass or fail. It also does not give a score, because a score for a rejected name would look like an endorsement.

Some of the inputs are available in the record and would be the starting point if the name were re-underwritten later. The company reports working-capital days of 12 for FY26, down from 18 in FY25 [1]. The customer-share and plant-count measures, which a Layer 2 review would need, are not found. A future review would therefore have to start with a source for those two figures, and this file does not fill them in.

The value of recording the Layer 2 position in this way is that the reader can see what the process would look at next, without being misled into thinking the operating health has been graded. If the name is later re-underwritten after the price gates pass, the scorecard would be applied from the start, using the same measures and the same thresholds, and the result would be recorded as its own finding. Nothing from this rejected file should be reused as a score.

For the reader, the lesson of this part is that a good business can still be a poor purchase, and that the operating scorecard exists to test the business, not the price. The reader should not conclude from the absence of a score that the business has failed. It has not been tested at this layer, and that is a different statement. Layer 3 entry posture

Layer 3 was not evaluated. The entry-posture gate maps the distance from a verified support level onto a ladder of buy bands, and it decides whether a buyer should act now, wait for a pullback or stay away. It is applied only after Layer 1 and Layer 2 pass. This company fails Layer 1, so the entry posture is not assigned and no buy band is named. The rule is the same as for Layer 2: a Layer 1 failure rejects the name and stops the analysis.

The ladder is a simple idea. The closer the price is to a well-defined support level, measured in ATR units, the more confident a buyer can be that the downside is limited by a nearby stop. The further the price sits above support, the more the buyer is chasing. The configuration defines four bands, and the posture is one of several outcomes that the ladder can produce, from buying at full size to refusing to buy at all. The purpose is to protect the buyer from paying up for a move that has already happened, which is one of the commonest ways for a good idea to lose money.

The ledger does supply the input that the ladder would use. The support distance for this stock is 0.57 ATR, measured from the 20-day swing low of 1,085 rupees. On its face that is close to support. But the same number appears in the momentum table as a pass on the not-extended test, and its meaning depends on the other readings. A price that is near support in a falling trend is a different situation from a price that is near support after a breakout. Here the trend is not confirmed, and the support is the lowest point of a base, not a level that has held through a rising trend. For that reason the file does not turn the 0.57 ATR figure into a buy band. Doing so would imply an entry posture that the Layer 1 result does not support.

The reader should also see the practical effect of the stop, which connects to the asymmetry section. The entry zone on the watchlist, between 1,190 and 1,225 rupees, is above the current price. A buyer who waited for the stock to reach that zone would be paying more, and would face a larger risk, if the stop stayed at the level the rules set for the current price. The Layer 3 posture therefore cannot be considered in isolation from the Layer 1 asymmetry result, because the same stop sets both.

The honest statement is this. The support data would be needed for an entry decision, and it is present, but the entry decision is not made, because the company is rejected before the entry question arises. If the name is re-underwritten after the trend and the reward tests pass, the ladder would be applied at that time to whatever price and support level existed then, and the posture recorded at that point. The posture is not a forecast of where the stock will go. It is a rule for when to act, if the other gates have already said yes. Fatal flaws and the red-team case

A red-team case is the strongest honest argument against the conclusion. Its job is to ask whether the verdict could be wrong, and where the verdict is most fragile. The process uses this step to test its own reasoning before a rejection is published, so that a rejection is not based on a weak point that a reader could easily dismiss. The case below sets out the flaws in the company's position first, then argues the opposite side, then names the single claim on which the verdict hinges.

The first flaw is the trend. The stock is below its 50-day and 200-day averages, and its long average is falling. A stock in that condition has, historically, been more likely to keep falling or go sideways than to turn up without a clear catalyst. That is a general pattern, not a law, and it is the reason the momentum gate exists. The file does not claim the trend will continue. It claims the rules do not allow a buyer to bet that it will turn yet.

The second flaw is the reward. The only permitted target gives a ratio of 2.11 against a floor of 3.0. Even on the most generous reading of the anchor, the reward would have to rise by about 48 rupees, or about 4.3 percent of the price, before the gate passed. That sounds small, and the reader may ask whether the gate is being applied too literally. The answer is that the gate was set in advance, and changing it after a result is seen would undo the discipline it provides.

The third flaw is the relative-strength turn. Over the last 28 sessions the stock has lagged the benchmark by about 6.3 percentage points. Over 63 sessions it still leads by 3.5 points. The mixed picture means the stock was strong and has weakened recently. A reader who sees the longer window as more important will feel the stock is fine. A reader who sees the shorter window as the signal will see a fall in progress. The process weights the recent reading, because a stock that has just lost its leadership is a different risk from one that has kept it.

The fourth flaw is the margin and cost squeeze. Raw-material costs rose faster than revenue in the latest quarter, and profit was flat. If that continues, the forward earnings estimate, which the entire target rests on, could be cut. A cut to forward EPS would lower the anchored target and widen the gap to the 3.0 floor. This is the most important risk to the anchor, and it is the first place a bear case would press.

The fifth flaw is the capital programme. The company plans to spend 1,750 crore in FY27, with start-up costs at the new plants that management says are in its guidance but will still weigh on profit [22]. Heavy spending ahead of returns is a classic source of disappointment. If the new plants take longer to reach full volume, the return on capital could fall from its present 20 percent, and the quality case would weaken. The file cannot yet measure this, because the plants are not yet in the numbers.

The sixth flaw is data disagreement. The company's first-quarter profit appears as 267 crore, 295.83 crore and 316 crore depending on the source and the basis [15][14][1]. The 52-week high appears as 1,382 and as 1,355. These conflicts do not change the verdict, because the rejection rests on the trend and the reward, which the ledger and the consensus both support. But they lower the reader's confidence in the precise quarterly figures, and the file says so.

Now the other side. The bull case is that this is a high-return, growing, cash-generative business, with low promoter pledges and a debt level that leaves room to fund its plants. It has a long record of compounding profit near 28 percent a year over a decade [29]. Its shares fell about 20 percent from the high, which the bull would describe as a correction in a quality name rather than a break in the trend. Analysts' consensus targets average about 1,321 rupees, which is about 20 percent above the close and sits well above the anchored target [10]. The forward P/E falls from about 45 times to about 35 times on FY28 estimates, which a bull would argue shows the market expecting earnings to grow into the price [8]. On this view, the process is too strict and will miss a good compounder that is merely between phases.

The honest answer to the bull case is that the points are real but do not satisfy the rules. The consensus target is not an accepted anchor, so it cannot clear the gate, however well it reads. The fall from the high is a fact, and so is the lack of volume behind any recovery. A correction in a quality name can be a buying chance, but the rules require the chance to be confirmed by the trend and by the volume, and neither is present. The bull case, in other words, is a reason to watch the name closely. It is not a reason to allocate.

The hinge claim is the momentum result. The verdict would survive a change in the anchor P/E, because the momentum gate fails independently. Even if the three-year median P/E were 60 times, which would lift the anchored target to about 1,470 rupees and the ratio to above 6, the technical posture and the momentum gate would still reject the name. So the single claim that decides the verdict is not the valuation. It is the statement that the stock has not yet started an uptrend on the evidence of its price and volume. A reader who disputes the verdict should dispute that claim, and should bring the price and volume evidence to support the dispute.

The general rule is that a breakout requires a close above the base's upper boundary on confirming volume, and that is the level the next review would use.

The reward trigger is the one that a valuation-minded reader would most naturally look at. It requires either a higher three-year median P/E, which is a slow-moving figure, or a higher forward EPS, which depends on the next few quarters. At the current forward EPS, a P/E of about 51.5 times would be needed, which is only about four percent above the three-year median. That is a narrow gap, and it means the reward gate could be passed by a modest re-rating, even without an improvement in earnings. The reader should not expect that to happen by chance. A re-rating needs the market to decide that the company deserves the premium, and the price trigger would show that decision.

The earnings trigger works in the opposite direction. The forward EPS estimate is a consensus figure, and it can be revised. A rise in the estimate would reduce the P/E needed to reach the target, which would help the reward test. A fall would make it harder.
