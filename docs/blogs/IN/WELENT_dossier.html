1. What the company does

Welspun Enterprises Limited (WEL, NSE: WELENT, BSE: 532553) is the infrastructure arm of the Welspun Group. It was earlier called Welspun Projects Limited, and the group entered infrastructure in 2010 by buying MSK Projects (India) Limited.[16] Today it bids for, builds and sometimes operates water treatment plants, sewage tunnels and roads for Indian authorities. Its promoters hold 56.13 percent, and the June 2026 filing declares that none of those shares is pledged to lenders.[19] Pledged means handed to a lender as security, so a falling price could force a sale.

The customers are almost all government bodies, and that shapes everything about the business. The Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation (BMC) is paying for the 418 million-litres-a-day Dharavi wastewater plant, the 2,000 MLD Bhandup water plant and the 910 MLD Panjrapur water plant.[7][8] The National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) is the payer on the Aunta-Simaria bridge road, and WEL also builds the Sattanathapuram-Nagapattinam and Varanasi-Aurangabad roads. The Maharashtra State Infrastructure Development Corporation (MSIDC) awarded the 53-kilometre Pune-Shirur elevated road, and the Uttar Pradesh water mission and Delhi's municipal council are smaller payers.[8][23] These bodies pay when milestones are certified, not when work is done, so cash arrives later than revenue.

WEL makes money in five ways. First, it earns the profit on construction contracts, called engineering, procurement and construction (EPC). It keeps the design, bidding and project control, and hands most of the physical work to subcontractors.[16][18] Second, on hybrid annuity model (HAM) roads it builds the road and then receives fixed annual payments from NHAI. Accounting treats those payments as a financial asset that earns interest, and that interest (₹159.32 crore in FY26) is reported inside revenue.[1] Third, on build-operate-transfer (BOT) roads such as Pune-Shirur it collects tolls for 25 years after four years of construction, with a target equity return above 18 percent.[8] Fourth, it earns fees for operating and maintaining plants, about ₹5,400 crore of the order book.[9] Fifth, it sells mature road assets: six roads went to Actis in 2022 at roughly ₹9,000 crore of enterprise value, and Aunta-Simaria is now being sold for about ₹1,000 crore.[7][6]

A 60.09 percent subsidiary, Welspun Michigan Engineers Limited (WMEL), does the specialist work: tunnels, micro-tunnels, sewer relining and pumping stations. It had revenue of ₹874 crore and operating profit, measured as EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), of ₹185 crore in FY26, and on the August 2026 call its tunnelling machines were described as fully occupied.[8][7][4] A joint venture with the Adani Group, Adani Welspun Exploration Limited (AWEL), holds interests in oil and gas blocks. WEL owns 35 percent and has put in about ₹500 crore.[18][8] Its three adjacent offshore blocks hold about 1.1 trillion cubic feet of gas in place, and management expects income only from FY29.[4]

The order book is the stock of signed work still to be billed. It was ₹18,729 crore on 30 June 2026: 55 percent water, 30 percent transport, 11 percent tunnelling and 4 percent other, and by type 42 percent EPC, 29 percent BOT and 29 percent operations and maintenance.[4] In the June quarter revenue split 41 percent water, 35 percent transport and 24 percent tunnelling.[7] That is about 5.2 times the ₹3,615 crore of revenue for the year to March 2026, which is a long runway if the work is billed on time.[4][1]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • HAM (hybrid annuity model): a road contract where the government pays part of the cost during construction and the rest as fixed yearly payments afterwards.
  • BOT (build-operate-transfer): a road contract where the builder collects tolls for a fixed period, then hands the road back.
  • EPC: a pure construction contract with no long-term ownership of the asset.
  • Order book: signed work not yet billed. Divided by yearly revenue it shows how many years of work are secured.
  • Financial closure and appointed date: the day lenders confirm funding for a road, and the day the authority says construction may begin and the clock starts.
  • Contract assets: work done but not yet billed to the customer, which is a use of cash until the bill is paid.
  • MLD: million litres per day, the capacity of a water plant.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

WEL's strength is how it organises work, not what it owns. It owns almost no heavy equipment, so it can bid for many projects without tying up capital. Credit analysts at ICRA point to its experience of over four decades, its low borrowing and the financing terms that come from being part of the Welspun Group.[18] In September 2026 Crisil raised its long-term rating from AA-/Positive to AA/Stable, citing a larger order book and a shift to higher-margin projects.[16] Crisil calculates that the order book rose from 4.1 times to about 5.7 times a year's revenue between March 2025 and March 2026.[16] No source publishes WEL's share of any market, so we make no claim about market share.

The profit evidence is good but needs careful reading. Management reported an EBITDA margin of 22.8 percent for FY26 against a guided range of 18 to 20 percent.[8][4] That figure is measured on total income, which includes other income and the ₹159.32 crore of annuity interest on road assets.[1] Crisil puts the same year's margin at 16.1 percent, and the gap is mostly that annuity interest.[16] Both numbers are honest, but they answer different questions. The first tells you what the whole group earned, and the second tells you what the construction work itself earned. We use both in Chapter 2B.

The business also has clear weak points. Crisil lists high dependence on subcontractors, working-capital-heavy operations and order book concentration, with Pune-Shirur alone about 44 percent of the order book once operations contracts are excluded.[16] ICRA adds that the water work is concentrated in Maharashtra and comes mainly from one payer, the BMC.[18] Outside forces also hit the business: a stoppage of debris transport in Mumbai for several weeks, labour leaving sites around elections, and supply problems linked to geopolitics all slowed the June quarter.[7] Management calls these events outside its control. That may be so, but a contractor with a few giant clients carries those risks every year.

A separate risk is tax. In late September 2026 two state GST authorities sent show-cause notices to WEL: one in Uttar Pradesh proposing tax of about ₹71.24 crore for FY23, and one in Haryana for about ₹15.37 crore.[21][22] A show-cause notice asks a company to explain itself before any demand is final. The company says the demands are without merit. Disputed claims against the group already stood at ₹164.02 crore at March 2026, so the new notices would lift the total by more than half if they were upheld.[1]

Where the group profit really comes from

WMEL is the engine room. It contributed about 25 percent of consolidated revenue in FY26 but about 32 percent of consolidated EBITDA, because specialist tunnel and sewer work earns a higher margin than general contracting.[16] Its own FY26 margin was about 21 percent, and it targets growth above 25 percent a year for three years.[8] In the latest quarter its revenue fell 14 percent to ₹179 crore and its margin slipped to 21.3 percent, partly because orders were released late, but it has since added about ₹367 crore of orders, including ₹66 crore from New Delhi on 7 October.[4][23] The parent holds only 60.09 percent, so about 40 percent of that profit belongs to minority owners.

An analyst on the August call put water segment margins at 20 to 25 percent. Management agreed they are high and attributed them to technology and special relationships on selected projects, not to the average water contract.[7] That answer is plausible but not proven by any filing, so we treat the high margin as management's claim.

What the road peers show

Three listed road contractors have price-to-earnings ratios of 6 to 10 (the share price divided by profit per share), against 28.4 for WEL, and two of them also show negative three-year cash conversion.[31][32][34][27] The market is not pricing WEL as a road company. It is pricing the water and tunnelling mix, the lighter balance sheet and the practice of selling mature road assets. That premium is the main thing the new investor pays for, and Chapter 5 tests whether the earnings justify it.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management gives specific forecasts on most quarterly calls, which makes the track record easy to check. The picture is mixed. FY25 was beaten on revenue and profit. FY26 profit margins were beaten, but FY26 revenue was cut by about 10 percent from the original guide, three months after management said it was still on track.[12][10][9][8]

The largest miss is asset sales. In May 2025 management said it would sell two road assets in FY26. By May 2026 the balance 51 percent of the Mukarba Chowk-Panipat road was still on the books, and the Aunta-Simaria sale was signed only on 29 July 2026 and was due to close by 30 September.[12][8][6] No completion notice appeared in the exchange announcement list up to 15 October, so the sale has slipped past its own date. The agreement allows for later dates by mutual consent, so this is a delay, not a failure.[25][6]

Management also moved the FY27 guide. In February and May 2026 it said growth would be 15 to 20 percent. On the August call it said the figure would be closer to 15 percent than 20 percent, after a June quarter that fell 8 percent.[8][7] Crisil's own forecast is 10 to 15 percent for each of the next two years, which is below the company's range.[16] We treat management's range as its aim and the Crisil range as the cautious case.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter lays out five years of audited numbers and then applies the Layer 1 gates. Welspun Enterprises reports two sets of accounts. The consolidated accounts add up the parent, its subsidiaries such as WMEL, and the road companies it controls. The standalone accounts cover the parent alone and treat the road companies as investments. We use the consolidated accounts as the main basis, because they describe the whole group that you part-own.[1]

Two quirks distort the five-year picture. In FY23 profit jumped to ₹726 crore because of the sale of six roads to Actis, which came through other income, so FY23 is not a clean starting year for growth rates.[27][7] Separately, from September 2025 the company moved the interest it earns on HAM annuity receivables from other income into revenue, and restated FY25 to match, which lifts FY25 revenue from ₹3,584 crore as first reported to ₹3,695 crore.[14][1][2] Profit is not affected, but revenue and the operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, read higher than before.

The cash flow statement is the main reason for caution. Consolidated operating cash flow was minus ₹344 crore in FY24, minus ₹273 crore in FY25 and plus ₹112 crore in FY26.[2][1] The cause is receivables: the line for the rise in trade and other receivables took away ₹814 crore in FY25 and ₹584 crore in FY26.[1] Part of this is construction of HAM roads, which the accounts book as a growing receivable from the highway authority, funded by project loans in the financing section. Another part is water work that has been done but not yet billed.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could be wrong. This chapter takes each estimate in turn. It names the weakest assumption, says what evidence would show the error, and gives the date on which we will know. It then sets out the case against our own conclusion, as a sceptic would make it. It ends with the single claim on which everything hangs.

Weakest assumption behind the cash conversion estimate

The estimate of 0.46 to 0.71 assumes that the net rise in the road receivable is money spent on road assets, not money lost to slow collection. The annual report supports this. It says the movement in service concession receivables reflects revenue and interest accrued, less annuities received.[1] It does not split out the water work. There, contract assets of ₹1,318 crore sit on the balance sheet, and Crisil expects working-capital days to stay at 250 to 270.[1][16] Evidence that would show us wrong is a first-half FY27 cash flow statement that stays negative while the road receivable grows more slowly. We will see that statement with the September 2026 results, which are due by 14 November 2026.

Weakest assumption behind the core profit estimate

The ₹412 crore of core profit assumes that the ₹48.86 crore write-off of the Kutch oil block was a one-off. The oil joint venture still holds three offshore blocks, and the development plan for one of them is waiting for government approval. Management said on 5 August that approval should come in four to six weeks.[9][7] We found no announcement of approval in the exchange list up to 15 October.[25] Evidence that would show us wrong is a further write-off of the oil and gas stake, which stands at ₹266.21 crore on the books. We will see it in the September results.[1]

Weakest assumption behind the earnings estimate

The base case assumes 15 percent growth in FY27 and FY28. The quarterly record argues against it. Revenue fell in four of the last five quarters against a year earlier. The June 2026 quarter fell 8 percent, so the remaining three quarters must grow about 22 percent to reach a 15 percent year.[27][4] The cushion is the order book of ₹18,729 crore. Management also says all statutory approvals are in place except the Pune-Shirur appointed date.[7] Evidence that would show us wrong is September-quarter revenue below the ₹784 crore of a year earlier.[27]

Weakest assumption behind the target multiple

The base value assumes that the market will pay 20 times earnings, the three-year median. That is a fair assumption for a business like this. But the stock traded between 17 and 23 times from February 2025 to mid-August 2026. It moved above 28 times only in September 2026.[29] The road peers trade at 6 to 10 times.[31][32][34] If the market decides that WEL belongs with them, the bear multiple of 15 is not extreme. If it decides WEL belongs with Wabag, the bull multiple of 24 is reasonable. Evidence that would show us wrong is a sustained close above ₹842 with rising earnings estimates. That would mean the market is rating the company on a different scale from our median.

Weakest assumption behind the probability weights

The weights of 25, 45 and 30 percent are judgement, not measurement. The weakest one is the 45 percent on the base case, because that case needs the company to grow faster than it did in any of the last five quarters. Evidence that would show us wrong is a September quarter that grows by double digits, which would lift the bull weight, or one that falls again, which would lift the bear weight. Moving ten points of weight between the bear and bull cases moves the weighted value by about ₹51 either way. We will know after the September results.

The case against our conclusion: the red-team view

The sceptic's case is that the company is more fragile than the AA rating suggests. Revenue fell in four of the last five quarters. Management cut its FY26 guide three months after saying it was on track. The asset sale it promised for FY26 has slipped into FY27. Disputed claims against the group were already ₹164 crore, mostly tax, and two new notices add ₹87 crore of tax before interest.[10][9][6][1][21][22] Pune-Shirur needs about ₹1,500 crore of equity. Of that, ₹750 crore depends on warrant holders paying ₹525 a share, which they will do only if the price stays above that level.[16][8]

In that story the price slides to about ₹450, a 38 percent fall. The 190 shares would lose about ₹47,700 against their cost, which is 1.04 percent of net worth. We do not accept it as the base case. The order book, the AA rating and the ₹1,792 crore of cash all point to a business that can fund itself through a slow year.[4][16]
