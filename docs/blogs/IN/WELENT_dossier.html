This dossier answers one question. Should you keep, add to, or exit your Welspun Enterprises holding of 190 shares as of the latest completed market session, Friday 9 October 2026? It is written for a reader who has never used a moving average. Ten minutes gets you the verdict, the key figures and the main reason. One hour gets you the arithmetic behind each gate, the sources, and the conflicts between them.

The report uses a three-layer test. Layer 1 is a set of hard gates, and a company must pass every one. The gates cover return on capital employed (ROCE, the profit a business earns on all the money put into it), debt to equity (how much borrowed money sits beside the owners' money), three-year cash conversion (cash from operations divided by accounting profit over three years), a technical posture check (where the share price sits in its trend), and an asymmetry check (how much you could gain compared with how much you could lose). If any gate fails, the company is not scored further. Layers 2 and 3 are then skipped, and the report says so.

Cash from operations is the cash a business actually collects after paying its running costs. Profit is an accounting figure, and it can run ahead of cash when customers pay late or when work is done but not yet billed. A ratio near 1.0 means profit is turning into cash. The floor used here is 0.70. Below that level, the rule treats reported earnings as not backed by enough cash.

Stage is a four-season model of a stock's trend. Stage 1 is a base, a period of sideways trading. Stage 2 is the markup, when the price holds above a rising 50-day and 200-day average. Stage 3 is a top, and Stage 4 is the decline. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of trading days. The 20-day average is the fast line, the 50-day is the medium line, and the 200-day is the slow line. Each one smooths out daily noise, so a price that sits above a rising line is in an uptrend by this definition.

ATR, the average true range, measures a stock's normal daily move. Here the 14-day ATR is 29.76 rupees, so the stock moves about that much on an ordinary day. This report counts distances in ATRs. A gap of 30 rupees means little for a stock that moves 60 rupees a day, and a lot for one that moves 10. A stop-loss is a price you set in advance at which you exit. The CORE stop rule in the rules file places the stop below the lower of the recent swing low and the moving averages, minus one ATR.

Asymmetry is the ratio of the upside to a target divided by the downside to the stop. The floor for CORE_ALPHA names is 3.0, which means the possible gain must be at least three times the possible loss. The target has to be anchored to something you can check: forward earnings multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, or a measured move from a base. A target that cannot be checked fails the gate by rule, however attractive it looks.

Every holding receives one of four verdicts. KEEP_COMPOUNDING means every gate passes and the trend is intact. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP means the price has pulled back to verified support and you have room to add. TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING means the stock is stretched, has reached its bull target, or is too large for its size limit. REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER means support has broken, two or more operating tests fail, or the investment thesis is impaired. A replacement verdict makes the holding a candidate for a swap into a stronger challenger. It does not, by itself, tell you to sell today.

What the company does

Welspun Enterprises Limited, known as WEL, was formerly known as Welspun Projects Ltd. [1] It is part of the Welspun Group, a large conglomerate. The company works in infrastructure, with a focus on roads and water. Its main model is the Hybrid Annuity Model (HAM), in which it builds a project and the government pays for it partly during construction and partly over time through annuities. It also takes Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) toll projects and engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) contracts [1]. In plain terms, WEL is a builder and long-term operator of public works. It earns fees for building and it earns long-dated payments for running what it builds.

Revenue grew from 2,758 crore rupees in FY23 to 3,695 crore in FY25 and has been roughly flat since. [1] Consolidated revenue from operations was 3,615 crore rupees in FY26, against 3,695 crore in FY25, a fall of about 2 percent [12]. Profit did not fall in the same way. Consolidated profit after tax rose to 393 crore in FY26 from 354 crore in FY25 [12]. The difference came from margins, which widened, and not from more sales. A reader should keep that in mind when reading the growth numbers later in this report.

The order book and where the work sits

An order book is the value of contracts the company has won and not yet built or billed. It is the best guide to how busy the company will be over the next few years. WEL reported an order book of 18,729 crore rupees on 30 June 2026. That book is 55 percent water, 30 percent transport, 11 percent tunnelling and 4 percent other work. By contract type, 42 percent is EPC, 29 percent is O&M (operation and maintenance), 29 percent is BOT and 1 percent is HAM; the company's rounded shares add to 101 percent [7]. An order book of this size, set against FY26 revenue, is about 5.2 times the year's sales. That gives visibility, but it does not by itself prove the company can turn orders into cash. The rest of this dossier tests that.

The biggest water contracts are in Mumbai. The Dharavi to Ghatkopar tunnel is an 8.48-kilometre tunnel that the Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation awarded to WEL for 1,989 crore rupees [15]. WEL's bid at the time was 1,989.90 crore, lower than Afcons's bid of 2,045.70 crore [16]. The company's presentation says the Dharavi Ghatkopar tunnel has all clearances in place [7]. Management targeted commissioning of the Dharavi wastewater plant by July 2027 [12]. The 2,000 million-litre-a-day water treatment plant at Bhandup is about 20 percent complete and is targeted for completion by June 2029 [12]. A subsidiary, Welspun Michigan Engineers Limited (WMEL), reports its own order book. That order book was 2,356.66 crore rupees after a new sewer-rehabilitation award on 7 October 2026 [1].

Road assets, concessions and the cash they release

A HAM or BOT road is a long-lived asset. The company builds it, then receives payments over many years, and eventually it can sell the asset to a long-term investor. Selling a finished road is called monetisation, and it turns future payments into cash today. WEL has a definitive agreement to divest its Aunta-Simaria road asset at an enterprise value of about 1,000 crore rupees [6]. Management said the sale is expected to cut debt by about 800 crore rupees [8]. The company targeted completion in the first half of FY27, subject to valuation [12]. That sale is the single most important cash event in the next six months. It is also a reminder that part of the company's apparent cash comes from selling assets, not from operations.

The Pune to Shirur elevated road is a new BOT project. The sub-concession agreement was executed, and the company reported this in its June-quarter results [6]. Management expects financial closure in October or November 2026 and a contribution of about 500 to 600 crore rupees to FY27 revenue from early works [12]. Those revenue figures are management's own estimates. They are not verified here. Promoters hold 56.13 percent of the shares, and none of that holding is pledged [4] [21].

A figure of 16.7 percent means that for every 100 rupees of capital, the company earned about 16.7 rupees of operating profit, as Screener measures it. Cash conversion: the gate that fails

Cash conversion is the test that decides this verdict. The rule takes three years of cash from operations and divides it by three years of profit after tax. The result shows whether the reported profit has arrived as cash. A ratio of 0.70 or higher is the floor. It means that for every 100 rupees of profit over the three years, at least 70 rupees came in as cash from the business. Below that, the profit is either still sitting in receivables and unbilled work, or it is being used up in the same operations that produced it.

For WEL the three-year figures are poor on every basis we tested. Consolidated cash from operations was negative 344 crore in FY24 and negative 273 crore in FY25. It was positive 112 crore in FY26 [1]. Consolidated profit after tax was 319, 354 and 393 crore over the same years [1]. The three-year sum of cash is negative 505 crore, and the sum of profit is 1,066 crore. The ratio is therefore negative 0.47. The FY26 figure of 112 crore is confirmed by a second source, although that source describes it as a deficit [11]. The magnitude matches, and the sign follows the Screener cash flow table, in which the figure is positive.

Which basis does the rule require? The rules file does not name a basis. This dossier uses the consolidated basis because its data-gathering step starts from Screener's consolidated figures. The standalone result is shown beside it as a sensitivity, not as a substitute.

The standalone figures tell the same story. Trendlyne shows standalone cash from operations of negative 106 crore in FY24, 274 crore in FY25 and 353.9 crore in FY26 [3]. Standalone profit was 285.2, 307.7 and 308.3 crore [3]. That gives a ratio of positive 0.58, which is still below the floor. The standalone shortfall is about 109 crore of cash over three years: 0.70 times 901.2 is 630.8, against 521.9 of cash. If FY27 repeats FY26 on the standalone basis, the FY25 to FY27 window would reach about 1.06 and pass in May 2027. The consolidated shortfall is about 1,251 crore, so the consolidated result would need a far larger change. The conclusion does not depend on which basis you pick. A window one year earlier, covering FY23 to FY25, gives negative 0.19 on the consolidated figures, and 0.15 on standalone. The other three-year window we checked, FY23 to FY25, does not reach 0.70 on either basis, and no window reproduces the prior record's 0.78.

What would change this view

  • If operating cash flow for FY27 comes in at about 930 crore rupees, the FY25 to FY27 window would clear the floor at a profit of about 350 crore a year. That level of cash flow would be a large break from the last three years.
  • If operating cash flow for FY27 and FY28 together reaches about 650 to 715 crore rupees, the FY26 to FY28 window clears 0.70 at profit near FY26 levels. That window is the first one that can pass.
  • If the audited consolidated figures differ from the Screener table by about 1,251 crore in total, with profit unchanged, the consolidated result would change. The gate must then be re-run on the audited figures before any decision.

Two explanations are commonly given for a gap like this in asset-heavy builders. The first is timing. Under a HAM or BOT contract, the company spends cash building the asset, and the government's payments arrive later. Profit is recognised as the work is done, but the cash lags. The second is working capital. Receivables and unbilled contract assets can grow as the book grows. Both explanations may be partly true. We cannot confirm either from the audited cash flow notes, because the annual report PDF could not be read by the source cache. The rule does not allow a timing explanation to replace the number. A company can explain a fail, but it still fails.

Technical posture: the gate that passes

The technical gate asks whether the price is in a trend that can carry a holding. The test for a CORE_ALPHA name is Stage 1 base breakout or Stage 2 markup, and no Stage 4 breakdown. WEL is in Stage 2, the markup phase, according to the ledger. Its close of 735.90 rupees sits above the rising 50-day average of 689.97 and the rising 200-day average of 556.63 [1]. The ledger is the source for each technical figure in this dossier, and it is not a web page. The gate passes. The pass is narrower than it looks. The 20-day average, at 757.46 rupees, sits above the price. The stock fell 6.85 percent over the month to 9 October. A holding that is above its long averages but below its short average is in a pullback, not an uptrend. This dossier treats it as a Stage 2 stock that is cooling off, which is the reading the gate allows.

Asymmetry: the gate that cannot be verified

The asymmetry gate needs a target you can check, and a possible gain that is at least three times the possible loss. The loss side is set by the stop rule, which gives a stop of 526.87 rupees on the literal reading. That leaves 209.03 rupees of risk per share. The gain side needs a target. The rule allows two anchors, and neither can be completed here. The first anchor is forward earnings times a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio. We found no verified forward earnings consensus, and we found no verified three-year median multiple. The second anchor is a measured move from a base, and the ledger holds no base high or pivot price that would support one. The target is therefore unverified, and the gate fails by rule.   • A close below 556.63 rupees, the 200-day average, would be a Stage 4 warning, which fails the technical gate and would strengthen the verdict. The target would still need to be verified.

Why the target is not verifiable

The rule offers two anchors for a target, and each one needs inputs that the evidence does not supply. The first anchor is forward earnings times a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio. We found a single-analyst profit growth estimate of 34.9 percent for FY27, on an unstated base, and no forward earnings consensus [5]. Multiples published by aggregators cover other windows, such as Wisesheets' quarterly series [30] and Smart Investing's annual series [36]. The company's own profit has fallen in the June quarter, so an earnings base for FY27 is uncertain.

The second anchor is a measured move from a base. A measured move adds the depth of a completed base to the breakout point. The ledger holds the 63-day base low of 557.10 rupees and the base distance of 6.01 ATR. It does not hold a base high or a breakout pivot, so the move cannot be calculated from the ledger. A measured move built on a guessed pivot would be an arbitrary target, and the rule forbids that. The target is therefore not verifiable, and the gate fails. This is a finding about the evidence available, not a claim that the stock cannot rise.

Glossary

These are the terms used in this dossier, listed alphabetically. Each is defined in plain words, with its use in this report.

  • Asymmetry: the possible gain divided by the possible loss. The floor for CORE_ALPHA names is 3.0.
  • ATR (average true range): the typical daily move of a stock, in rupees. Here it is 29.76, and distances are counted in ATRs.
  • Cash conversion: cash from operations divided by profit after tax over the same years. The floor is 0.70.
  • CFO: cash from operations, the cash a business collects after running costs.
  • Consolidated and standalone: consolidated figures include subsidiaries; standalone figures cover the parent company alone.
  • CORE_ALPHA: the report's label for long-term compounders held as core positions. Its asymmetry floor is 3.0.
  • D/E (debt to equity): borrowed money divided by owners' money. The limit is below 0.70.
  • EBITDA: earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges.
  • EPC, BOT, HAM, O&M: engineering, procurement and construction contracts; build-operate-transfer toll projects; hybrid annuity projects where government pays partly during construction and partly through annuities; and operation and maintenance contracts.
  • EPS (earnings per share): profit attributable to each share.
  • Monetisation: selling a completed asset, usually a road, to turn its future payments into cash now.
  • Order book: the value of contracts won and not yet fully built or billed.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed): operating profit as a share of capital employed, as Screener measures it. The floor is 15.0 percent.
  • ROE (return on equity): profit as a share of owners' money.
  • RSI (relative strength index): a 0 to 100 gauge of recent momentum. A reading near 50 is neutral.
  • Show-cause notice (SCN): a tax authority's written notice that proposes a demand and invites a reply. It is not a confirmed demand.
  • Stage 1 to Stage 4: the four phases of a stock's trend, from base to markup to top to decline.
  • Stop-loss: a price set in advance at which the position is exited, here set by the CORE rule.
  • Trading window: the period when insiders are barred from buying or selling the company's shares, usually around results.

Unless the notes below change, the verdict and its rule stand as written above.

Methodology

Every technical figure in this dossier comes from the facts ledger built for the 9 October 2026 session. The ledger holds close, the 20-, 50- and 200-day averages, RSI with a 14-day window, ATR with a 14-day window, the 20-day swing low, the 52-week high and low, the Weinstein stage, and the 63-day relative strength. Distances to averages and to the swing low are the rupee gap divided by the ATR. A positive distance means the price is above the level. The 50-day and 200-day trend readings compare the average today with its value 21 and 63 sessions earlier.

The cash conversion ratio is the sum of three years of cash from operations divided by the sum of three years of profit after tax, for the same three fiscal years. The window used is FY24 to FY26, and a second window, FY23 to FY25, is shown as a check. The standalone version uses the Trendlyne standalone rows in the same way. Debt to equity uses the most recent gross debt and net worth in the company's June 2026 investor presentation, with the Screener March 2026 balance sheet as a cross-check. Market value of the company is scaled from Screener's header value at 726 rupees to the ledger close, because the header price is from the 12 October page.

Asymmetry follows the formula in the rules file: target base minus close, divided by close minus stop. The stop follows the CORE rule, which takes the lower of the 20-day swing low and the 50- and 200-day averages, and subtracts one ATR. The literal reading includes the 200-day average, and the alternative includes only the 50-day average. Both readings are shown in section 9. The target base is recorded as not verifiable, because neither the forward earnings times median P/E anchor nor a measured move from a base could be completed from the evidence.

Each research record in the source record quotes a passage that appears verbatim in a page held in the source cache. The value field carries only the scalars printed in that passage. Records with an as-of date older than 60 days are disclosed in the run notes. The source record holds 67 records, of which 64 are found and 3 are not found. Three queries were run for each not-found record, and the sources consulted are listed in the record.

Where a figure is derived rather than printed in a source, the dossier states the formula next to it. Examples are the cash conversion ratio, the debt to equity ratio, the price-to-earnings ratio at the ledger close, the stop, the risk per share, the asymmetry sensitivity rows and the market value scaled to the close. The derived figures are arithmetic on sourced inputs. They are not forecasts, and no derived figure stands in for a missing source.

Holdings come from the holdings transaction log, which is treated as authoritative. The position file nets buys and sells to 190 shares. The cost basis and market value come from that file, and the net worth figure of 4,689,840.82 rupees also comes from it. The allow-stale-holdings flag is recorded in the run notes as a freshness warning, not as a reconciliation to a broker statement.
