Greenlam Industries makes decorative laminates, the thin hard surfaces on kitchens and furniture, and sells them in India and in more than 120 countries.[1] Its return on capital employed is only 8.1 percent, well under our 15 percent floor, because new plywood and chipboard plants worth about ₹1,300 crore are still filling.[2][3] We rate it WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: at the ₹213.24 close the reward-to-risk ratio, the gain to our target for each rupee risked to our stop, is only 1.71 and the expected 12-month return is 8.2 percent.[4][5] We would buy only between ₹203.50 and ₹205.50, with a stop at ₹199.64, and not before the Q2 FY27 results, which are not yet dated.[6][7] The case is wrong if profit from chipboard and plywood stops improving, or if the price closes below ₹199.64[8][4].

1. What the company does

Laminate is a thin sheet made by soaking layers of paper in resin and pressing them hot until they set hard. The top layer carries the colour, wood grain or stone pattern. Furniture makers glue the sheet onto a plywood or chipboard panel to make a cabinet door, a worktop or a wardrobe shutter. Greenlam sells those sheets through more than 40,000 distributors, dealers and retailers in India, and ships to more than 120 countries.[1] The first laminate plant opened at Behror in Rajasthan in 1992.[9] Exports were about ₹1,336 crore, or 44 percent of gross sales, in FY26.[10] It has been India's largest laminate exporter for 16 years running, according to ICRA, a credit rating agency, and it estimates its share of India's organised laminate market at more than 17 percent.[1][11]

The money comes from volume times price. In FY26 the laminate business sold 20.65 million sheets at an average ₹1,144 a sheet, which made ₹2,433 crore, about 80 percent of the company's ₹3,046 crore of sales.[12] Out of every ₹100 of sales, about ₹54 is left after paper and chemicals (the gross margin), but only about ₹11 remains after wages, freight, marketing and rent (the operating margin, also called the EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) margin).[12] Paper and chemicals are about 52 percent of cost of sales, and 37 percent of them are imported, so the rupee and oil-linked chemical prices matter.[10] Prices are reset with a lag, which is why margins swing from quarter to quarter.

Since 2021 the company has built three new businesses beside laminates. It announced a ₹950 crore plan in December 2021 to add a third laminate plant, a plywood plant and a particle board (chipboard) plant.[13] Plywood started at Tindivanam in June 2023, a fourth laminate plant at Naidupeta in September 2023, and the chipboard plant at Naidupeta in January 2025.[1] It also sells decorative veneers, engineered wood floors and doors under the Mikasa brand.[10] Management says about ₹1,300 crore was capitalised across these projects, and that FY26 was the first year of running all five plants fully.[3][12]

Who pays? Furniture makers and contractors buy laminate and chipboard for factories and sites, and dealers stock it for small carpenters. Demand follows housing, office fit-outs and modular furniture, so it rises and falls with the real estate cycle.[10] The business is seasonal. Management calls the September and March quarters the stronger ones, and the June and December quarters usually softer, which matters when we read each new quarter against the last.[14][8] The new plants lose money until they fill, which is why sales grew 18.6 percent in FY26 while profit fell 18 percent to ₹56 crore.[12]

Where the three businesses stand in the cycle

Laminates are a mature, fairly steady business: capacity use was 86 percent in FY26 and management says it has reached more than 100 percent in past years, so the two new press lines due in Q4 FY27 add room for about ₹375 to ₹400 crore of revenue.[12][16] Plywood is a young business at 36 percent use, selling in the premium range in the south, west and east, and management does not expect to go pan-India before FY28.[11][12] Chipboard is the newest and the one the market watches most. It reached 61 percent use in Q1 FY27 and management says it can earn 18 to 20 percent margins at full use, but only around FY29.[8] These three ramp-up dates decide how fast profit recovers, so Chapter 2B scores them as KPIs.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit from running the business before financing and accounting charges.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business; it tells you the yearly return on every rupee invested.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid bills, less what the company itself owes suppliers.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money; above 1.2 is our hard limit in India.
  • Net debt is borrowings less cash held.
  • Stage 4 is the falling phase of the four-stage price cycle, when the price sits below both its 50-day and 200-day averages and both are falling.
  • Reward-to-risk (also called asymmetry) is the gain to our target divided by the loss to our stop.
  • Stop loss is the price at which we admit the thesis is wrong and sell.
  • Limit order is an order that fills only at our price or better.
  • PBILDT is the rating agency CARE's version of EBITDA: profit before interest, lease rentals, depreciation and tax.
  • DSCR (debt service coverage ratio) is cash earnings divided by the year's loan repayments plus interest; above 1.0 means the company can pay them from earnings.
  • Reverse DCF works backward from today's price to the yearly profit growth that price implies.
  • Forex means foreign exchange: the gain or loss from moving rupees against other currencies.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest part of the business is its reach. Greenlam has five plants, 17 company-owned regional distribution centres and warehouses, 23 branch offices and more than 40,000 dealers and retailers, and four international distribution centres.[1][17] It holds more than 17 percent of the organised laminate market in India and about 29 percent of India's laminate exports.[11][18] That scale matters because laminate is a design product: architects and carpenters specify a brand and a decor number, and a dealer who stocks the full range wins repeat orders. Management also says larger players are taking share from mid-sized and unorganised ones, which are under pressure on cost and cash flow.[12]

The weak part is pricing power. ICRA says intense competition from large organised and numerous small unorganised players restricts pricing flexibility, and the Q1 FY27 numbers show why.[1] Management raised prices twice in April and May 2026, cut some in June and again in July, and kept a net 7 to 8 percent of the rise while chemical costs moved by 30 to 80 percent.[8] The gross margin was flat at 52.9 percent against 53.1 percent a year earlier.[8] Century Plyboards, a larger competitor, raised prices 7 to 12 percent in April, then rolled some back to win volume, and the broker noted that high discounts prevail despite the hikes.[19] A company that cannot hold price has to win on volume and cost, and that is where the new plants matter.

Volumes are the live worry. Greenlam's laminate sales volume fell 6.4 percent in Q1 FY27 to 4.62 million sheets, with about ₹27 crore of export shipments postponed by container and vessel delays and a 7.5 percent fall in domestic volume.[18][8] Century's laminate volume fell 8.4 percent in the same quarter, so the weakness is industry-wide, not unique to Greenlam.[20] Revenue still grew because prices rose: Greenlam's laminate realisation was up 13.6 percent to ₹1,240 a sheet.[18] That is a fragile way to grow, because if demand softens at higher prices, volume falls first and prices follow.

The balance sheet is the third strength and weakness at once. The company is rated AA- by both ICRA and CARE, and CARE moved its outlook from Negative to Stable on 7 September 2026 because debt to operating profit improved from 4.42 to 3.81 times.[10] ICRA kept a Negative outlook in May 2026 and estimated a similar ratio at about 3.5 times for March 2026, falling to 2.6 to 2.8 times by March 2027.[1] Those are comfortable ratings, yet 3.8 times is high for a business whose returns are only 8 percent, and it leaves little room for another bad year.

The new plants: moat or burden?

Greenlam spent heavily to move from a laminate maker into plywood and chipboard, and the industry did the same at the same time. UnlistedZone reports that Merino, an unlisted laminate peer, made ₹219 crore of operating profit on ₹1,859 crore of laminates in FY26, but lost ₹120 crore in its new panel business and ended the year with a profit of ₹33 lakh.[21] Greenpanel and Rushil Decor also saw profits fall sharply in FY26, and the same note says panel capacity grew faster than demand and that pricing power is unlikely to return until industry operating rates pass about 80 percent.[21] That source is secondary commentary, so we use it only for direction. It matches ICRA, which names offtake risk for Greenlam's chipboard plant because other leading wood panel players have added capacity.[1]

The fair reading is that the laminate franchise is a real moat and the panel businesses are not yet one. Chipboard turned EBITDA positive in Q1 FY27 at ₹3.4 crore on 61 percent use, and management says it breaks even at about 50 percent use.[8][12] CARE puts the Q1 operating margin at 3.6 percent against a 10.8 percent loss in FY26.[10] That is progress, but a 3.6 percent margin is far from the 18 to 20 percent management hopes for at full use. Chipboard also competes with Century, which grew particle board volumes 156 percent in the same quarter and earned a 3.7 percent margin on them.[8][20]

What the market size and the peers tell us

India's decorative laminate market is worth about USD 1.39 billion in 2026 and is forecast to grow only about 5.35 percent a year to 2031.[22] Greenlam's own annual report sizes the market at ₹13,300 crore.[11] Against that slow market, Greenlam guides to 18 percent group revenue growth and 10 to 12 percent in laminates, which means it must keep winning share, through exports and new products, rather than ride the market.[12][8] The best-run laminate peer on the numbers is Stylam, which has a return on capital employed of 26.6 percent and almost no debt, so Greenlam's lower returns reflect its choices of expansion and capital use, not an industry limit.[23][24] Chapter 3 compares the five companies side by side.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A turnaround is a promise about the future. So we check what management promised one to four years ago and what the filings show. The record is mixed, and the pattern is worth naming. On growth targets for the year just ended, management delivered at the low end. FY26 revenue growth was 18.6 percent against a guide of 18 to 20 percent. Chipboard use was 39 percent against a target of 30 to 40 percent.[3][12] On projects and profit, it has been late and over budget.

The December 2021 plan promised plywood and laminate output by the fourth quarter of FY23 and particle board by the fourth quarter of FY24.[13] Plywood began in June 2023 and the Naidupeta laminate lines in September 2023. Chipboard began only in January 2025, and CARE says its cost rose from ₹775 crore to ₹875 crore.[1][10] In June 2025 management said debt had peaked and that better revenue and profit would strengthen the balance sheet. Debt did fall, from ₹1,199 crore to ₹1,160 crore. But FY26 profit fell 18 percent to ₹56 crore, because interest of ₹96 crore and depreciation of ₹142 crore rose faster than operating profit.[3][2][12]

The latest signals are fair but not yet proven. In January 2026 management said plywood and chipboard would break even in FY27. In June it said both would do so this financial year.[14][12] In Q1 FY27 chipboard did and plywood did not. The finance chief has now narrowed the plywood promise to one quarter of FY27, not the full year.[8] A broker note from the November 2025 plant visit recorded targets of 75 percent chipboard use in FY27 and full use in FY28. By August 2026 management spoke of an FY27 average near 70 percent and optimum use in FY29. The date has moved out a year.[26][8] Management states misses plainly on calls, which is a plus. The pattern still says to discount new dates by a few quarters.

How it was calculated

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest one for each. It says what evidence would show it wrong and when we will know. It then states the strongest case against buying, the one a sceptical reviewer would make.

The EPS estimate: a 12.8 percent margin in FY28

The weakest assumption is that the operating margin climbs from 10.7 percent in FY26 to 12.8 percent in FY28.[2][5] That needs laminates to hold 16 to 17 percent. It needs chipboard to move from a 3.6 percent margin toward the teens as use rises. It needs plywood to break even.[12][10][8] Evidence that would show it wrong: a Q2 or Q3 FY27 margin stuck at 10 percent or below, plywood still losing in the December quarter, or chemical prices rising again. Management would not give a margin outlook on the August call because of raw material uncertainty.[8] We will know from the Q2 FY27 results, expected in early November, and again in late January.[6]

A 28 times multiple on FY28 profit assumes the market will look through low returns and pay for growth. Greenlam's return on capital employed is 8.1 percent today. Input costs, freight and the West Asia conflict

Raw materials are about 52 percent of cost of sales, and 37 percent of that is imported. The balance sheet and the interest-cover flag

Debt is where a bad quarter does the most harm. Total debt to PBILDT is 3.81 times. CARE's downgrade trigger is a ratio above 3.50 times on a sustained basis. The single hinge claim is this. Consolidated EBITDA margin rises from about 10 percent in Q1 FY27 to about 12.8 percent by FY28, because chipboard and plywood stop losing money while laminates hold 16 percent. If that is true, FY28 EPS is about ₹8.45 and the stock is worth about ₹236.50. If the margin stalls near 11 percent, EPS is nearer ₹5.50 and the value is nearer ₹132 to ₹150.[15][5]

We moved the bucket from CORE_ALPHA, the seed suggestion, to TACTICAL_MOMENTUM. A core holding needs a return on capital employed of 15 percent or more and low debt. Greenlam has neither today. Its case is a recovery in operating profit over 12 to 24 months, not a compounding story.[2][25] Each day the ledger classifies the broad market into a regime.
