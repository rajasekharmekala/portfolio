Before you read

Anthem Biosciences is a Bengaluru company that makes drugs and drug ingredients under contract for global drug firms, and it listed in July 2025.[1][9] The business is strong: it earns 30.4% on capital, has almost no debt, holds ₹1,720 crore of net cash and has no pledged promoter shares.[31][7][14] We reject it at ₹832.15 because the price already assumes about 25% yearly profit growth for ten years, and our central case gives no gain in twelve months. Revenue fell 22.6% in the June 2026 quarter, yet analysts still expect the next three quarters to grow about 34%.[6][17] A strong second quarter on 3 November 2026 would give a reason to reopen the case, but only a lower entry price and firmer estimates would change the verdict[4][31].

1. What the company does

Anthem is a drug-making partner for companies that do not make their own drugs. A small biotech may have a promising molecule and no factory. Anthem works out how to make the molecule, tests it in its labs, makes the first kilograms for human trials, and later makes the commercial supply. The industry name for this work is contract research, development and manufacturing, shortened to CRDMO, a company that does all three jobs for someone else.[29]

The company was incorporated in Bengaluru on 13 June 2006 and runs three plants: Unit 1 at Bommasandra, Unit 2 at Harohalli and Unit 3, called NeoAnthem, a subsidiary also at Harohalli.[10][7] It employs 2,282 people, of whom 1,117 are scientists.[8] The current capacity is 425 kilolitres of chemical reactor volume and 142 kilolitres of fermentation tanks, according to the June 2026 slide deck.[7] On the May call the finance chief quoted 180 kilolitres of fermentation, so the two company sources differ and we use the filed slide figure.[2]

Anthem says it differs from other Indian peers in who it serves. It focuses on small biotechs and on the discovery side, not on the headcount-rental model where a customer simply pays for a team of chemists by the year.[2] Its platforms cover small chemical molecules (called new chemical entities, or NCE) and large biological molecules (new biological entities, or NBE), including peptides, antibody-drug conjugates, RNA interference and oligonucleotides.[31][2] Peptides are short chains of amino acids; the weight-loss drug semaglutide is one.

Revenue has two parts. The CRDMO business earned ₹17,727 million in the year to March 2026, or 83.4% of sales. The Specialty Ingredients business, which sells enzymes, probiotics and nutrition ingredients mostly to Indian companies, earned ₹3,516 million, or 16.6%.[8][4][10]

How a drug project turns into revenue

A project starts in discovery, where Anthem's chemists and biologists design and test candidate molecules for a customer. This is paid by the project. It then moves to development, where Anthem makes the drug in larger batches for clinical trials, and finally to commercial manufacturing, where it makes the approved drug in volume. Each step is stickier than the last, because once a regulator has approved a drug made at a given plant, the customer rarely wants to move it.[4]

The count of molecules shows where the pipeline stands. Anthem had 14 commercial molecules at March 2026 and four of them were commercialised during that year. It had 10 molecules in Phase III trials, the last test stage before approval, and more than 100 programmes at the early stage.[1] Those 14 commercial molecules generated 60.8% of revenue.[1] Management says new commercial supply takes 18 to 24 months to arrive from the late-stage group, and that sizeable volumes from the four newest products will take a couple of years.[4]

Who pays, and how concentrated they are

Anthem sells mainly abroad. In FY26, exports were ₹18,085 million of ₹21,243 million, or 85.1% of sales. Europe was 53.9% of revenue, North America 27.3%, India 14.9% and the rest of the world 3.9%.[1] Customers are emerging biotechs and large pharma companies, and management says it added two big pharma customers directly in FY26, on top of big pharma relationships that came through takeovers of its biotech clients.[2]

The customer list is short. Three customers made up 56.71% of consolidated revenue in FY26: 21.08%, 20.90% and 14.74%. A year earlier the same three-customer group was 61.18%.[1] At the time of the share sale in July 2025, the top five and top ten customers made up 70.92% and 77.33% of FY25 revenue, and ICRA put the top five products at 50 to 55% of revenue.[9][10] The company also depends on DavosPharma, an affiliate of one of its shareholders, for marketing in the United States.[9] A short list means one customer's inventory decision can move a whole quarter, which is exactly what the June 2026 quarter showed.

What it costs to make a rupee of revenue

In FY26 the cost of materials was ₹8,123 million, or 38.2% of revenue. Employee cost was ₹2,827 million (13.3%) and other expenses were ₹1,955 million (9.2%).[7] That leaves an operating profit of about 39% before other income, which matches the 39% reported on Screener.[31] The company's own EBITDA, which is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, was ₹9,896 million, or 43.4% of total revenues, because it counts ₹1,558 million of other income.[8]

That other income needs care. Of the ₹1,558 million, ₹634 million came from foreign exchange gains and a government export incentive, and ₹924 million from interest and other non-operating income.[8] Interest is earned on a cash pile that management plans to use for new factories, and exchange gains swing from year to year. We therefore judge the business on operating margin before other income, which means the share of sales left after running costs. That figure was 36.0% in the June 2026 quarter, 39% in FY26 and 36% in FY25.[31][6]

Why the quarters swing

Quarterly revenue is lumpy because customers order batches, not a steady flow. In FY26, quarterly sales were ₹540 crore, ₹550 crore, ₹423 crore and ₹611 crore, and the June 2026 quarter was ₹418 crore.[31][20] Management told analysts the June quarter was the softest of the year because customers deferred deliveries to later quarters and rebalanced stock between markets, and it asked analysts to judge the business over a full year.[4]

Bigger swings come from the science. In FY23 revenue fell 14.1% from the year before, partly because a Phase III molecule failed and a commercial molecule was withdrawn.[9][31] The business is therefore a bundle of projects, and each project can end early. This is why the next chapter weighs the quality of the pipeline and the customer list as heavily as the size of the profit.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • CRDMO is a contract research, development and manufacturing organisation: a company that discovers, develops and makes drugs for other companies.
  • Commercial molecule means a drug that has regulatory approval and is sold to patients, so the maker supplies it every year.
  • Phase III is the last human trial stage before a regulator decides whether to approve a drug.
  • NCE and NBE are new chemical entities (small molecules made by chemistry) and new biological entities (large molecules made by living cells or enzymes).
  • Kilolitre (kL) is a thousand litres of reactor or tank volume, the usual way to measure a contract manufacturer's capacity.
  • Order book means signed and expected orders for the year that the company has not yet delivered.
  • Other income is profit that does not come from selling the main product, such as interest on cash and foreign exchange gains.
  • ROCE is return on capital employed: profit before interest and tax divided by the money invested in the business.
  • EPS means earnings per share, the profit that belongs to each share. Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by EPS; a figure of 80 means investors pay ₹80 for each ₹1 of yearly profit.
  • FCF is free cash flow: the cash from operations left after spending on plant and equipment. Working capital is the money tied up in stock and customer bills, less what is owed to suppliers.
  • Guidance means the forecast a company gives about its own results.
  • RSI is the relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has moved. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought. ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price swing, which we use to measure distance from support and to place stops.
  • Stage 2 is the rising uptrend phase in a four-stage chart method, and Stage 4 is the falling downtrend phase. Stage 3, the topping or pullback phase, is where Anthem sits now.
  • A base is a sideways price floor where a stock rests before its next move. The pivot is the price at the top of that floor, and a breakout is a close above it on strong volume.
  • The entry zone is the price range where we would be willing to buy. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop; a compounder needs at least 2.0.
  • Reverse DCF is a method that starts from today's share price and works out the growth rate the price needs to be fair.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A contract drug-maker is strong when customers cannot easily leave and when it makes more profit per kilogram than rivals. Anthem has real evidence on both. Its plants have passed repeated inspections: the US Food and Drug Administration (USFDA) cleared Unit 1 three times and Unit 2 twice, in 2023 and 2024, and Brazil's ANVISA cleared both units in 2024.[1] ICRA, the rating agency, also notes inspections by Japan's PMDA, Europe's EDQM and Australia's TGA.[10] A drug approved with a given plant named in the filing is expensive to move, so that record is the core of the moat.

The second source of strength is cost. Anthem makes many of its own intermediates, which are the chemical building blocks of a drug, and it uses flow chemistry and enzyme-based steps to cut yield losses.[4] The result shows in the accounts. Materials took 35.0% of revenue in the June 2026 quarter against 42.7% a year earlier, even though sales fell.[7] In FY26 materials were 38.2% of revenue against 40.3% in FY25.[8] Management also cites an employee cost lower than the industry's and a young workforce, with 40% holding stock options.[2][4]

The third source is where the work sits in the drug cycle. Small biotechs that win a trial often get bought by a large drug company, and the buyer then inherits Anthem as the supplier. In the June 2026 call, management said one big pharma customer was newly added and a decent-sized biotech client had just been acquired by a large drug company.[4] The development contract is Phase III, so any commercial supply sits a few years away and depends on trial results.[4]

Where the edge is thinner

The company is mid-sized. ICRA calls it a mid-sized player in a highly competitive industry, with top-ten customers at about 75% of revenue.[10] Sai Life Sciences, a direct peer, reports 33 active commercial molecules and 14 late-phase molecules, against 14 and 10 at Anthem.[22][1] Divi's Laboratories is far larger, with sales of ₹3,080 crore in the June 2026 quarter against Anthem's ₹418 crore.[34][31] Anthem competes on science and flexibility, not on scale.

Capital needs are rising faster than sales. Gross fixed asset turnover, which is revenue divided by plant and equipment at cost, fell from 1.51 times in FY26 to 1.10 times in the June 2026 quarter.[1][7] Management plans about ₹700 crore of capital spending in FY27, then about ₹500 crore a year, and Unit 4 alone is ₹1,200-odd crore over two years.[2][4] Unit 2 ran at about 50% and Unit 3 at 30% to 35% in the June 2026 quarter, so the company is adding capacity before it has filled what it has.[4]

The peptide business is the biggest open question. Semaglutide's patent expired in India on 20 March 2026, and within weeks about 15 companies sold generics at about ₹1,290 a month, roughly a 90% fall from the branded price.[28] Anthem says it makes semaglutide at a cost that rivals Chinese makers but has not started commercial supply and is waiting for regulatory approval.[2][4] A price collapse in the finished drug usually passes down to the ingredient maker, so the size of this prize is unproven.

Industry growth and policy backdrop

The industry tailwind is real. The Frost & Sullivan data in the annual report puts the Indian CRDMO market at about USD 8 billion in 2024 and USD 15 billion by 2029, a 13.4% yearly pace against 9.1% for the global market.[1] India's development and manufacturing costs are about 30% to 40% lower than in the US or Europe, according to the same report.[1] The US BIOSECURE Act, signed on 18 December 2025, restricts US federal agencies from dealing with biotechnology companies of concern, mainly Chinese, and the first list of such companies is due by 18 December 2026.[24][1] The law helps Indian makers, but the benefit arrives with a lag.

Policy also cuts the other way. A US proclamation of 2 April 2026 imposes tariffs on patented drugs and their ingredients, with a 100% rate from 29 September 2026 for importers that have neither an approved onshoring plan nor a most-favoured-nation pricing deal.[25][26] Generic drugs and biosimilars are excluded for now, but the proclamation requires a review within a year of extending tariffs to them.[25][27] Anthem sells to innovators whose products are often patented, so the exposure is real. Management has not quantified it in the calls we read, and the tariff status of each customer is not public. We treat it as an unmeasured risk, not as zero.

What this means for an owner

The moat is narrow to moderate. It rests on approvals, process know-how and sticky customers, not on scale or on a network. Those are real, and they explain why operating margin has stayed between 36% and 41% in each year since FY23 while sales swung.[31] What the moat does not do is smooth the quarters or remove the dependence on three customers. The next section scores the specific measures that decide whether the strength is holding, and checks what management promised against what it delivered.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk asks whether management did what it said. The record is mixed but candid. At the start of FY26, management aimed for about 20% growth in revenue, EBITDA and profit. It missed on revenue, which grew 15.2%, and said so openly in the May 2026 call. It more than made up the gap on profit: EBITDA grew 30.8% and profit after tax grew 31.1%.[2][8] In February 2026 it had already cut the revenue aim to 15% to 16%, which proved accurate.[3]

The recent promises are harder to credit. In May 2026 the chief executive said the destocking by customers was behind the company and that everything would be positive this year. Two months later revenue fell 22.6%, and the explanation was that customers deferred deliveries.[2][30][4][5] Management also moved from 'about 20%' to 'we are not giving a guidance of any particular percentage' when an analyst asked on the June call whether the 20% aim stood.[4] Candour is a strength. A forecast that can be reset between two calls is weaker evidence of control over deliveries.

Capital spending promises have been kept in timing and missed in cost. ICRA's June 2025 report expected Unit 2's extra 130 kilolitres and Unit 3 to be done in the first half of FY26 and Unit 4 to cost about ₹1,000 crore and open in FY2028.[10] By May 2026, Unit 2 had grown from 246 to about 376 kilolitres, Unit 3 was running, and Unit 4 was priced at ₹1,200-odd crore for phase one, due in the latter half of March 2028.[2]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could be wrong. FY27 earnings of ₹11.80: the weakest assumption

The weakest assumption is that revenue grows 15% in FY27, which needs the last three quarters to be 27.8% above last year. Customers have deferred deliveries, but we are assuming they arrive within the year and in full for most of the shortfall. FY28 earnings of ₹13.76: the weakest assumption

The upside to this assumption is Unit 3 reaching breakeven this year, which management hopes for.[4]

The 12-month price of ₹826: the weakest assumption

The weakest assumption is the 60 times multiple. The stock trades at 78.9 times trailing earnings, which is 7% above the median of its four priced peers, and peer multiples depend on a sector that Syngene's results show can stay soft for a year.[31][33][21] If investors lose patience, 45 times is possible, as in our bear case. Reverse DCF of 25.5%: the weakest assumption

The weakest assumption is that profit is a fair stand-in for cash while the company spends heavily on plants. In FY27 the street expects free cash flow of ₹251 million against profit of ₹7,166 million, so profit overstates cash this year and the true growth needed is higher, not lower.[17] The assumption would be wrong in the other direction if Anthem earns very high returns on the new plants.
