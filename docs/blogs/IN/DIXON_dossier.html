Terms used in this dossier

ROCE (return on capital employed) measures how much operating profit the business earns on the money tied up in it, both shareholders' money and borrowed money. Think of it as the yield on the whole factory, not just on the owners' share. Screener reports ROCE for the consolidated group, and the gate asks for at least 15 per cent, with 18 per cent or more preferred for the Core bucket.[1]

Debt to equity compares borrowed money with the owners' capital. A ratio of 0.70 means the company owes 70 rupees for every 100 rupees of equity. The gate requires a ratio below 0.70, which is a conservative ceiling for a manufacturer.

CFO to PAT is cash from operations divided by profit after tax, summed over three years. Profit can be booked before the cash arrives, for example when customers pay late. A ratio of 0.70 or above is the gate. A ratio well above 1.0 means the cash is arriving faster than the accounts recognise profit.

Weinstein stages are a four-phase way of describing a share's life. Stage 1 is a base where the price moves sideways after a fall. Stage 2 is an advance, with the price above a rising 50-day average and a rising 200-day average. Stage 3 is a top, where the advance stalls and the averages flatten. Stage 4 is a decline. The tactical bucket buys only in Stage 2.

Moving averages (SMA20, SMA50, SMA200) are the average closing price over the last 20, 50 and 200 sessions. A falling average shows that the recent prices are below the older ones. A death cross is the 50-day average falling below the 200-day average, and it is treated as a warning when volume is heavy.

ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions, in rupees. It is the ruler the rules use. A stop one-half ATR below a level is a stop sized to the stock's normal noise, not a fixed percentage.

RSI (relative strength index) 14 is a 14-session momentum reading from 0 to 100. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought. A reading in the high 30s is neither; it says the stock has been weak but has not been crushed.

Relative strength (RS) compares the stock's return with the benchmark index over a window. A positive figure means the stock is beating the market. The rules use two windows, 63 sessions and 28 sessions, because a stock can lose leadership quickly.

Volume multiple is the average daily volume over the last 20 sessions divided by a longer baseline. A multiple of 1.0 or more means trading is busy enough to confirm a move.

Asymmetry is the reward-to-risk ratio: the rupees of upside to a verified target, divided by the rupees of risk to the stop. The rules require at least 3.0 to 1 for Core and 3.5 to 1 for Tactical. The target must be anchored, either to forward earnings times a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio or to a measured move from a completed base. A target that is not anchored counts as unverified.

Momentum gate is a code-run test with two alternatives, Test A for a stock already in an uptrend and Test B for a stock about to enter one. A candidate must pass one of them to receive any allocation. The thresholds are marked as needing calibration in the configuration file, which this file states openly.

OPM (operating profit margin) is operating profit as a share of sales, before other income, interest, depreciation and tax. It shows how much the core manufacturing work keeps. Other income is income that is not from making products, such as interest on cash and gains on investments. It can be real money, but it is not the same kind of earning.

What this means for you: these terms recur in every later section. When a number appears, the question to ask is which of these terms it measures and which gate it feeds. The next section sets out the sequence in which the terms are applied.

What the company does

DIXON
Chart • Screener is an electronics manufacturing services company. Such a company builds electronic products for other brands under contract, so the brand owner keeps the design, the marketing and the customer, and the manufacturer keeps the factory, the labour and the working capital. Screener describes the company as incorporated in 1993 and as operating across consumer electronics, lighting, home appliances, closed-circuit cameras and mobile phones.[2] The model matters because the manufacturer is paid a conversion fee on top of components, so its margin is thin and its volume is set by the brands it serves.

The mix has moved sharply toward mobile phones. Screener reports the mobile and EMS division at 90 per cent of FY26 revenue, up from 85 per cent in FY25, and consumer electronics and appliances at 6 per cent, down from 9 per cent.[1] The same page says the company holds a 37 per cent share of outsourced manufacturing in India and names clients including Xiaomi, Samsung, Hisense, Panasonic, Acer and several Indian appliance brands.[1] In plain terms, the business is now mostly a phone factory for a few large brands, and it is the phone brands' order books that decide the company's volume.

That concentration is the central business risk. A Multibagg report of a Phillip Capital note says Motorola accounted for more than 45 per cent of the company's FY25 revenue, and that the brand has begun diversifying its manufacturing partners.[20]

Why the business is strong or weak

A contract manufacturer earns its money in two ways. It earns a fee on each unit it assembles, and it earns a return on the factory and working capital it puts in. A strong manufacturer earns a high return on its capital because it fills its plants, keeps its customers and turns its inventory quickly. The Screener figures show the company earning high returns on its capital in the past few years. ROCE was 24 per cent in FY23, 29 per cent in FY24, 40 per cent in FY25 and 29 per cent in FY26.[1] A 29 per cent return on the whole capital base is strong for an assembly business, and it is the main reason the financial gate passes comfortably.

The balance sheet is also clean. Borrowings were 994 crore rupees at 31 March 2026 against reserves of 4,665 crore, which gives a debt-to-equity ratio of about 0.21, far below the 0.70 ceiling.[1] Cash generation has been strong. Cash from operations totalled 3,516 crore over FY24 to FY26, against profit after tax of 3,252 crore over the same years, so the three-year cash-to-profit ratio is about 1.08.[1] Good cash conversion means the reported profit is being collected, not just recognised. These are the qualities a long-term investor looks for, and they explain why the company is a serious candidate in the first place.

The weaknesses are about the nature of the earnings and the thinness of the margin. Operating profit margin, which is the core manufacturing margin before other income, was 3.8 per cent in FY26 and 3.0 per cent in the June 2026 quarter.[1] That is a conversion business with very little cushion. A one-point move in margin is a large move in profit, and the company has no proprietary product to protect its price. In the same fiscal year, other income was 731 crore rupees, against an operating profit of 1,873 crore, and the June quarter's other income of 537 crore was larger than that quarter's operating profit of 463 crore.[1] That is the core quality question for the business. Profit was helped by income that is not from making phones.

The client base is the second weakness. A few brands account for most of the volume, and a single brand's decision can move the revenue line. The Motorola figure cited earlier is the clearest example. The company also faces a market in which the phone brands are themselves under pressure. A December 2025 analysis by Univest recorded the stock trading below its 5, 20, 50 and 200-day averages at that time.[21] Industry pressure of that kind falls on a manufacturer more than on a brand, because the brand can move its orders.

What this means for you: the business is strong on capital efficiency, debt and cash conversion, and weak on margin depth, earnings mix and client concentration. Those weaknesses are not disqualifying on their own. They do mean that the price has to be right, because the company's profit is more sensitive to volume and to other income than a company with a higher margin would be. The financial gate passes; the question of quality is left open for the scorecard, which is not scored here because the chart fails first.

Layer 2 is a short set of three to five measures that drive a particular business. For an electronics manufacturing services company the watchlist skill names fixed-asset turnover above 4.0 times, client concentration below 40 per cent, and value-add margin expansion. A score of PASS, WATCH or FAIL is given to each measure, and at least three of four or four of five must pass before a company qualifies. The idea is simple. A manufacturer can have good returns for a few years on a single customer, and the scorecard is designed to ask whether the business is spreading its risk and widening its margin, not just growing.

The figures already in the record point in mixed directions, and the reader should see them clearly even though they are not scored. Fixed-asset turnover, measured as FY26 sales of 48,873 crore against closing fixed assets of 4,172 crore, is about 11.7 times, which would be a pass on the scorecard threshold of 4.0 if the measure is computed in that way. That is an indicative reading, because the closing fixed-asset figure is a balance-sheet number and the scorecard may define the measure on gross or average assets.[1]

Client concentration is the measure most clearly in difficulty. The reported figure for Motorola alone is more than 45 per cent of FY25 revenue, which is above the 40 per cent ceiling on its own, and the sources checked for this file do not give a newer concentration figure.[20] The value-add margin is the third measure, and the margin evidence points the wrong way. Operating margin fell from 3.9 per cent in FY25 to 3.8 per cent in FY26 and to 3.0 per cent in the June 2026 quarter.[1] A margin that falls as volume rises is the opposite of value-add expansion.

The reader should not take these readings as a score. They are not scored because the file is rejected at Layer 1, and the scorecard is a second opinion that the rules do not ask for once the first test has failed. They are included so the reader can see the shape of the business when the price structure eventually repairs. If the company were to pass Layer 1 on a future date, the first Layer 2 measure to check would be client concentration, because it is the most concrete and the most likely to decide the question.

What this means for you: the scorecard is not in play. The business has strong capital returns and a large fixed-asset base relative to sales, but it has high concentration and falling margin. Those are the two things a future reviewer would need to see improving before the company could be scored as a pass. Until the price structure allows a Layer 1 pass, the scorecard stays unscored, and no weight should be given to the indicative readings above as if they were a result.

The paragraphs below explain the timing check and record the one reading that would matter if the company later qualified, so that the reader does not mistake the reading for permission to buy.

The timing check measures how far the price sits from the nearest verified support, in units of ATR, and maps that distance onto a single ladder. A price within half an ATR of support falls in the full-size band, a price up to one ATR above support falls in the standard band, a price up to 1.2 ATR above support falls in a limit-order-only band, and a price further away is in a no-buy band where the rules say to wait for a pullback, a retest or a new base. The ladder exists so that a good business is not bought at the top of a move, because the stop distance then becomes too large for the reward.

On the ledger, DIXON
Chart • Screener sits about 0.16 ATR above its 63-session base low of 12,602 rupees. On the ladder, that would be the full-size band if the base were the right support to use. But the price is 3.24 ATR below its 50-day average, which means the stock is far below the average the rules use to define an advance. A stock below its average is not in a position to be bought on support. It is in a position where support has to hold and then recover the average before the entry is meaningful. The two readings conflict, and the rules resolve the conflict in favour of the trend test, which is Layer 1.

The timing check also depends on the stop. A stop placed below the base low with a half-ATR buffer sits near 12,423 rupees. A close below that level would be a break of the structure the entry was meant to rely on. That is a useful number to know, but it is a number for a position that is not being opened, so the file does not treat it as an entry instruction.

A common error is to treat a stock that is close to support as a stock that is cheap. Support is useful only when the trend above it is intact. A stock can sit on its base low for weeks while the base itself gives way, and each day it holds is not evidence of a turn. The entry rules therefore ask for the trend first and the distance from support second, which is the reverse of how a bargain hunter tends to think.

What this means for you: the timing question does not arise. A reader who sees that the price is close to its base low should not take that as a signal to buy. The base low is only useful as support when the trend has already turned up, and here the trend has not. The reader should wait for the Layer 1 triggers in the final section to be met before the timing question is asked at all.

Fatal flaws and the red-team case

A red-team case is the strongest honest argument against the file's own conclusion. Here the conclusion is REJECTED, so the red team asks whether the rejection is too harsh. The strongest version of the bull case is this: the company holds a 37 per cent share of outsourced manufacturing in India and grew sales 25.8 per cent in FY26, its returns on capital are high, its debt is light and its cash conversion is strong, and the joint venture with Vivo could add a second large phone customer. On that view, a chart in Stage 3 is a temporary condition, the market is pricing a cyclical trough, and the sensible response is to wait for the chart to turn and then buy. The rules already allow that, so the bull case is not a reason to change the verdict; it is a reason to watch the triggers.

The first fatal flaw is the price structure. The stock is below its 50-day average, that average is falling, and the 200-day average is also falling. The short relative-strength window is negative. A stock with that profile is not in an advance, and the rules say an advance is the only time the tactical bucket buys. The bear case is that the trend has not finished falling, and that buying now would be buying a falling knife. The file does not claim to know the bottom. It says the rules require a turn, and no turn is present.

The second flaw is the earnings quality. In the June 2026 quarter, other income of 537 crore was larger than operating profit of 463 crore.[1] In FY26, other income was 731 crore against operating profit of 1,873 crore. Those are not the earnings of a manufacturer that makes money from making things alone. A bear will say the headline profit is inflated by treasury income, and that the core manufacturing margin of 3.0 per cent in the latest quarter is the number that will matter when the other income falls. The bull will answer that cash from operations is strong, and that is true. The disagreement is about what the other income is and whether it recurs.

The third flaw is concentration. A reported figure puts Motorola at more than 45 per cent of FY25 revenue, and a note cited by Multibagg says the brand is diversifying its manufacturing partners.[20] The Vivo joint venture is the company's answer, but it is not yet a revenue line in the record. A bear case that rests on concentration does not need every brand to fail; it needs one large brand to shift volume, and the record shows that has already happened once.

The fourth flaw is the target. Without an anchored target, the asymmetry gate cannot pass. The consensus median of 14,980 rupees would pass the Tactical floor, and a bull will argue that the rules are too strict and that the consensus figure is a fair guide. The rule is stated for a reason. Consensus targets are set by the same analysts whose forecasts disagree by a wide range; the file records the high of 17,800 and the low of 8,157 as well.[15] The forward earnings estimate also conflicts with the company's own quarter, because the median FY27 estimate of 176.60 rupees is a fall of about 34 per cent on FY26, while the June quarter profit rose 156 per cent year on year.[9][15]

The hinge claim is this. The verdict depends on whether the price structure repairs before the business weakens. If the stock reclaims its 50-day average and that average turns up, the chart would support a Layer 1 re-test, and the target question would then decide the outcome. If the stock stays below the average and the margin keeps falling, the business case weakens while the chart stays weak. The file's position is that the chart is the test that can be checked today, and it fails.

What this means for you: the bear case is strong on the chart and on earnings quality, and the bull case is strong on the business. The rules side with the chart because it is the part that can be verified now. A reader who believes the bull case should not buy on belief. They should wait for the price to pass the Layer 1 technical test, and they should watch the other-income and margin figures in the next quarterly results.

What would change the verdict

A verdict should say what would change it. The levels and events that would move the answer are listed below, each with the rule it satisfies. None of these is a forecast. They are conditions the rules set, and the reader can check them without relying on anyone's opinion of the business.

The first trigger is a price structure repair. For the Tactical bucket, the stock would need to close above its 50-day average of 13,818.22 rupees, and that average would need to be rising over the 21-session window the rules use. The 200-day average would also need to stop falling. Today the close is 12,660 and the 50-day average is falling, so the distance to the trigger is about 1,158 rupees, or about 3.2 ATR. A reclaim of that level, sustained, is the first sign the trend is turning.

The second trigger is relative strength. The 28-session relative-strength figure is minus 7.62 points. Test B requires it to be rising and positive, which means the stock would need to begin beating the benchmark over that short window. A change in the sign of that figure, together with a close above the 50-day average, would clear two of the failing momentum conditions at once.

The third trigger is a base breakout on volume. The stock sits on a base low of 12,602 rupees. A confirmed breakout above a base pivot, on a volume multiple of at least 1.0, would satisfy the entry test. The rules are explicit that a conditional entry is allocated only on a confirmed breakout or on a retest that holds, and never before. A breakout that fails on low volume would not count.

The fourth trigger is an anchored target. The Tactical floor needs a target of at least about 13,489 rupees, and the Core floor needs about 15,405 rupees, with the stop set by the rules. The target must be built from forward earnings times a verified three-year median multiple, or from a measured move from a completed base. Until a three-year median is verified, the anchored target cannot be built. The reader who finds that median should check whether the stock's current forward multiple of about 72 times on the 176.60-rupee estimate is consistent with it.

The fifth trigger is the next quarterly result. The September 2026 quarter must be published within 45 days of quarter end, which the Univest preview gives as 14 November 2026. No board meeting for that quarter had been confirmed in the sources checked for this file. The last board meeting on record was the 31 July 2026 quarterly results meeting, and the board-meeting listings from Trendlyne, Economic Times, Financial Express and Mint show no later date.[5][6][7][8][11] The reader should look for two things in that result: operating margin above the 3.0 per cent of the June quarter, and other income that falls back toward the operating-profit level. Those two would weaken the earnings-quality bear case. A second large customer disclosed in that result would weaken the concentration case.

What this means for you: the verdict changes when the chart repairs and the target can be anchored. It does not change because the company reports a strong quarter alone, because the rules are built to ignore a good story on a broken chart. The reader who wants a timetable should expect a decision no earlier than the September quarter results, and only if the price has first reclaimed its 50-day average.
