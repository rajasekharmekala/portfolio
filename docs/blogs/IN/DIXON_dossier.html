1. What the company does

Dixon Technologies (India) Ltd is a contract manufacturer based in Noida, near Delhi. It builds products that other companies sell under their own brand names, which is why its tagline is "the brand behind brands".[2] It was set up in 1993. Today it makes smartphones, laptops, telecom and broadband boxes, television sets, refrigerators, washing machines and LED lights.[1] Its customers include Motorola, Xiaomi, Samsung and Bosch.[1][16] The money figures in this report are in rupees crore. One crore is ten million.

Think of Dixon as a factory for hire. A phone brand designs the phone and owns the buyer. Dixon buys the parts, assembles the phone, tests it and ships it back to the brand for a fee. Almost every rupee Dixon receives goes straight out again to pay for parts. In the June 2026 quarter, materials cost 94.2 percent of operating revenue, up from 92.5 percent a year earlier.[5] Management calls its contracts cost-plus with pass-through, which means that when parts get dearer, the brand pays more and Dixon's sales rise.[8] The profit does not rise with them. So Dixon kept about ₹3.0 of operating profit per ₹100 of sales in the June 2026 quarter, against ₹3.8 a year earlier.[1]

The business is large and growing fast. Sales for the year to March 2026 (FY26) were ₹48,873 crore, up from ₹38,860 crore, which is a rise of 26 percent.[1][6] The mobile and other contract-manufacturing division made ₹44,257 crore of that, or 90 percent.[39] Consumer electronics and appliances, mainly LED televisions and refrigerators, made ₹2,892 crore and fell 19 percent. Home appliances made ₹1,426 crore and grew 4 percent.[1][7] One division therefore supplies almost all the sales and almost all the growth. That is the first fact to hold on to, because it means Dixon's results follow how many phones its customers sell and how much they pay for memory chips.

Most of the business does not sit in the listed parent company. The parent's own turnover was ₹3,93,048 lakh, or about ₹3,930 crore, in FY26, against ₹48,873 crore for the whole group.[1][26] In the June 2026 quarter the parent booked ₹1,080 crore of revenue against ₹15,548 crore for the group.[1][35] The rest sits in subsidiaries and joint ventures where partners own a slice. Those partners' share of profit, called minority interest, took ₹205 crore in FY26 and ₹55 crore in the June 2026 quarter.[3][6] This matters later: a rupee of group profit is not a rupee that belongs to Dixon shareholders, and the gap will widen when the Vivo joint venture, in which Dixon owns 51 percent, starts to count.[22]

What a rupee of phone sales earns, and why the subsidy matters

In the June 2026 quarter Dixon made about 7.5 million smartphones, of which 0.6 to 0.7 million were exported. Exports were worth about ₹1,100 crore.[9] A government scheme called the Production Linked Incentive, or PLI, used to pay phone makers a slice of their sales. One broker put its contribution at about 60 basis points of Dixon's phone margin, which is 0.6 of a percentage point.[16] The scheme ended in March 2026.[9] Dixon's reply is to move deeper into the supply chain, so that it keeps the profit from parts that it now buys from others.

A new scheme, the Mobile Phone Manufacturing Scheme, was notified in August 2026. It runs five years, from FY27 to FY31, has an outlay of ₹62,500 crore and pays 2.25 to 5 percent.[20][38] Management expects the rate for Dixon's kind of work to run between 2.5 and 5 percent. It says the final rules were still awaited at the end of July.[9] Management also understands that the payment will be worked out brand by brand, and only on production above each brand's FY26 level.[9] Phone demand in India is falling this year, so growth above last year's level has to come mostly from exports. That is why the scheme helps the export plan more than the current run rate.

Where the next profit is meant to come from

Dixon plans to earn more per phone by making the parts inside it. Its camera-module arm, Q Tech, now makes about 70 million modules a year, and management wants 180 to 190 million over the next 15 to 18 months.[10] A display-module joint venture with HKC, a Chinese panel maker, is 74 percent owned by Dixon, under an agreement signed on 16 August 2025.[16][34] The building is finished. Trials were due to start in the third quarter of FY27, with full production at the end of that quarter or the start of the next.[8] Management says the camera and display parts will lift margins mostly from FY28, not this year.[9]

The second plan is to add products. Telecom equipment is expected to bring in ₹6,700 to ₹7,000 crore in FY27. Laptops, tablets and desktops are expected to pass ₹6,000 crore, according to one broker's note of its management meeting.[9][17] The third plan is the joint venture with Vivo Mobile India. Dixon would own 51 percent and Vivo 49 percent. It would make roughly two-thirds of Vivo's India volume.[16][22] One broker sized this as an opportunity of ₹18,000 to ₹20,000 crore of revenue a year.[16] The government approval letter is dated 8 July 2026, and the agreements were signed the next day. Management expects the venture to start adding revenue from the third quarter of FY27.[8][33] If all three plans work, Dixon becomes a broader and higher-margin business by FY28. If they slip, it stays a thin-margin phone assembler with a rich share price.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA — earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization: profit before finance costs and the accounting write-off of machines. In this report it is the closest measure of what the operations earn.
  • earnings per share (EPS) — the company's profit divided by the number of shares. Per-share figures let you compare profit with the share price.
  • ROCE — return on capital employed: profit before interest and tax earned on each rupee of capital the business uses. Above 15 percent is the portfolio's floor.
  • operating margin — the share of sales left after paying all running costs. For a contract maker, a margin of 3 percent means ₹3 kept from each ₹100 of sales.
  • gross margin — sales minus the cost of the goods sold, as a share of sales. Dixon does not report one, so we use operating margin.
  • net margin — the share of sales left after every cost, including tax and interest.
  • cash flow from operations — the cash the business itself generates, before spending on plants and before financing. The report also calls it CFO.
  • free cash flow (FCF) — cash flow from operations minus spending on plants and equipment. It is the cash left for owners.
  • capital expenditure — money spent on plants, machines and buildings.
  • working capital — the cash tied up in daily operations: stock and unpaid customer bills, less bills owed to suppliers. It is negative for Dixon in most periods, which means suppliers finance the business.
  • net debt — interest-bearing debt minus the cash held. A negative figure means net cash.
  • debt-to-equity — total borrowings divided by shareholders' money. The portfolio's red line is 1.20.
  • price-to-earnings — the share price divided by profit per share: the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit. A lower number is cheaper.
  • guidance — the company's own published forecast for a coming period.
  • consensus estimate — the average of analysts' forecasts for a number.
  • dilution — the fall in each shareholder's slice when a company issues new shares.
  • buyback — the company buying its own shares back, which shrinks the share count.
  • RSI — the relative strength index, a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has been moving. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR — the average true range: the average daily price swing, in rupees. It is the ruler used here to measure how far a price sits from support.
  • 20-day moving average — the average closing price over the last 20 trading days, a slowly moving line that prices often bounce off. The 50-day and 200-day moving averages are the same idea over longer spans.
  • Stage 2 — in the Weinstein stage scale, the confirmed rising phase. Stage 4 is the confirmed falling phase, Stage 1 is a flat floor after a decline, and Stage 3 is a top or pullback.
  • base — a sideways stretch the price builds after a fall; the platform a recovery would start from.
  • pivot — the top of a base; the price a recovery must clear.
  • breakout — the move that takes a price above a base on rising volume. A retest is the pullback to that level afterwards, to see whether it holds.
  • stop loss — the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold.
  • limit order — an order that fills only at your price or better. It does not chase the market.
  • entry zone — the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) — the gain you expect divided by the loss you risk: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). The portfolio needs 2.0 for a compounder.
  • measured move — a target found by adding the height of a base to its top.
  • book-to-bill — new orders divided by sales; above 1 means the order book is growing. Dixon does not publish it.
  • take rate — the share of a transaction a platform keeps. It does not apply to Dixon, which is paid per unit built.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Dixon's first strength is scale. The screening site's profile calls it the largest maker of original-design products in India, with a 37 percent share of outsourced television manufacturing, about 35 percent of semi-automatic washing machines and about 25 percent of consumer lighting.[1] In feature phones, the simple handsets, management says almost two-thirds of India's production is done by Dixon.[9] In smartphones it made about 7.5 million units in the June 2026 quarter while the market shrank by 10 to 12 percent. The chief financial officer said that holding last year's volume in a falling market shows that Dixon took share from other contract makers.[8][9] These are company statements. We have no independent share data, so treat them as claims, not measurements.

The second strength is how little capital the business needs. FY26 sales of ₹48,873 crore were made on fixed assets of ₹4,172 crore, so each rupee of plant produced about ₹11.7 of sales.[1] Customers pay in 49 days, stock sits for 31 days, and suppliers are paid after 86 days, so the cash conversion cycle was minus 7 days.[1] A negative cycle means that suppliers, not shareholders, fund the stock. That is how a 3 to 4 percent margin turns into a return on capital employed of 29.2 percent on the screening site's measure, and 34.1 percent on the company's own measure for the twelve months to June 2026.[1][5] The company's own measure was 44.8 percent in FY26, so the return is falling even though it is still high.[5][7]

The third strength is financial safety. At 31 March 2026, Dixon held ₹1,241 crore of cash and ₹468 crore of debt, a net cash position of ₹773 crore. By 30 June 2026 the net cash had fallen to ₹226 crore.[5][7] The credit-rating agency ICRA rates its long-term borrowing AA with a stable outlook, and its short-term paper A1+.[23] No promoter shares are pledged, which means the founding family has not borrowed against its stake.[24] The promoter stake has slipped from 33.63 percent in December 2023 to 28.50 percent in September 2026, a fall of 5.1 points, while Trendlyne's pattern shows no pledge in any quarter over the same period.[1][24]

Against that, the moat is moderate and narrowing in phones. A moat is a lasting edge that keeps rivals out. Dixon's edge is scale, supplier terms and government approvals, not a patent or a consumer brand, and a brand can move work to another contract maker. The old subsidy gave Dixon a margin edge that ended in March 2026.[8][16] One broker estimated that Motorola took 14.5 million of roughly 36 million phones in FY26.[16] The company filings we read give no revenue share for any single customer, so concentration stays a known unknown. We treat it as a risk and not as a measured number.

What protects the business

Approvals are part of the moat. India's rules on investment from land-border countries, known as Press Note 3, require government clearance before a Chinese partner can hold a stake in a joint venture. Dixon signed its Vivo term sheet on 15 December 2024.[22] Clearance came about 19 months later, in a letter dated 8 July 2026, and a broker had expected it by November 2025.[16][33] The delay hurt Dixon, but the same rule slows rivals. An approval is not a closing, though: the agreements were signed on 9 July 2026 and remain subject to customary conditions before the venture exists, so profit from it is still a forecast.

Breadth is the other protection. Dixon sells to phone, laptop, telecom, television, refrigerator, washing-machine and lighting brands. In the June 2026 quarter, consumer electronics revenue rose 47 percent, and laptop revenue in that one quarter matched about what the whole of FY26 produced.[4][9] If one customer or one product has a bad year, the others cushion it. But the cushion is thin while phones are 90 percent of sales. The mix will only change if the new lines keep growing faster than phones for several years.

What can hurt the business

The biggest outside threat is the memory-chip shortage. IDC expects global smartphone shipments to fall 16.7 percent in 2026 and the average selling price to rise 27.6 percent, with Android shipments down 24.3 percent.[21] Dixon's customers are mostly Android brands.[16] In the second quarter of 2026, IDC measured Xiaomi shipments down 26.4 percent, OPPO down 17.1 percent and vivo down 19.6 percent.[21] Counterpoint also found Xiaomi down 26 percent and Samsung up 17 percent.[37] Dixon can pass on higher part costs, but it cannot make buyers buy. A broker quoting management said the Indian phone market was down 15 to 20 percent in the September 2026 quarter.[17] The IDC figures describe the Android market as a whole, not a Dixon forecast. But taking share in a shrinking market does not protect a low-margin maker if its customers sell fewer and cheaper phones.

Earnings quality is the next threat. Reported profit before tax for the twelve months to June 2026 was ₹2,574 crore, and other income was ₹1,263 crore of it, which is 49 percent.[1] Most of that other income is a paper gain on a 2.38 percent stake in Aditya Infotech Ltd: ₹665 crore in FY26 and ₹519 crore in the June 2026 quarter.[2][6] Gains of this kind are not cash from selling phones, and they can reverse. We therefore use the company's adjusted figures, which strip the gain out, whenever we judge the operating business.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter turns Dixon's accounts into plain words. Two cautions come first. The first is that the profit line on most screening sites includes a large paper gain on a stake in another listed company, Aditya Infotech Ltd. Dixon marks that stake to its market price every quarter and books the change as income. The gain was ₹665 crore in FY26 and ₹519 crore in the June 2026 quarter.[2][6] The company shows its results both ways, reported and adjusted, and we use the adjusted view for anything that describes the operating business. One small difference to know: the screening site shows June 2026 quarter sales of ₹15,548 crore, while the company's presentation headline shows ₹15,557 crore, a difference of ₹9 crore between the two sources. We use ₹15,548 crore for sales and say so where we use the other figure.

The second caution is about the share price multiple. The screening site shows a price-to-earnings figure of 36.4 for Dixon.[1] That figure divides the price by reported profit, gains included. Profit for the twelve months to June 2026 after minority holders, with the gains taken out, is about ₹838 crore. That is our arithmetic: FY26 adjusted ₹845 crore, less Q1 FY26 ₹225 crore, plus Q1 FY27 ₹218 crore.[2][6] On that profit the multiple is about 81, not 36. The second estimate below, on underlying profit, shows the working. A screening-site median of 118.4 for the last three years exists, but it was computed on a much smaller and noisier profit base, so we do not use it as a target.[25]

Then the shape of the story. Sales rose from ₹10,697 crore in FY22 to ₹48,873 crore in FY26, a 4.6-fold rise in four years, while the operating profit rate stayed between 3.6 and 4.3 percent.[1] Growth came from more phones, not from a better margin. That is the pattern of a volume business, and it is why the margin question dominates what follows.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above rests on assumptions. This chapter takes the three that matter most, states the weakest part of each, and says what evidence would show it wrong and when that evidence arrives. Weakest assumption in the ₹235 earnings estimate: that margin recovers to about 3.4 percent

The estimate needs the phone-division margin to climb from 2.6 percent back toward 3.4 percent as camera modules, display parts and the new mobile scheme replace the old subsidy. Weakest assumption in the 50 times multiple: that growth stays above 25 percent a year

A multiple of 50 only holds if investors still believe in about 25 percent growth a year after FY28. The reverse DCF shows the current price already needs 25.4 percent for ten years. If Dixon proves to be a 12 to 15 percent grower after the Vivo venture and the component plants are built, investors will pay 30 to 35 times earnings, and the stock would fall even if profit rose as we expect.
