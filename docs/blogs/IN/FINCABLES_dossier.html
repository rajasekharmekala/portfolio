1. What the company does

Finolex Cables Limited was founded in 1958 and makes wires and cables at five sites, with Urse near Pune as its most diversified plant and Ponda in Goa as its copper-rod plant.[2][3] It employs about 1,609 people and sells through about 5,000 channel partners to more than 2,15,000 retailers.[2] The business has three reporting segments: electrical cables, communication cables and a small basket of consumer electrical goods.[3] Alongside the factories sits a large pool of financial assets. At 31 March 2026 the group held ₹2,763 crore of cash and investments and a 32.39% stake in Finolex Industries, a PVC pipe maker.[4][5] Any honest reading of this company has to keep the factory and the portfolio apart, because they earn money in very different ways.

Electrical cables are the engine. They brought in ₹5,490 crore of the ₹6,321 crore of FY26 sales, about 87%.[3][4] Most of that is building wire, the copper wire inside homes and offices. Management says about 60% of electrical sales are construction related. Automobile, agriculture and industrial uses are 10% to 12% each, and low- and high-voltage power cables are 7% to 8%.[11] Solar cables are new and ran near full capacity in FY26.[7] Copper is the main raw material, so selling prices move with the copper price. In Q1 FY27 sales rose 44%, and the company says part of that was higher copper passed on to buyers.[10] For an owner this matters because a copper-driven jump in sales adds little profit. The electrical EBIT margin, which is profit before interest and tax divided by segment sales, was 10.3% in FY26 and again in Q1 FY27.[3][9]

Communication cables are about 8% of sales and are the part of the business that changed fastest in 2026. The segment makes copper telephone and coaxial cables and optic fibre cables, the glass-strand cables that carry internet traffic. Buyers are telecom operators, with Bharti a large customer, plus government BharatNet tenders, which the company has not won so far, and since 2026 export buyers in the United States and Europe.[13][11] Fibre prices swung from $3 to $6 per kilometre in late 2025 to $17-18 in mid-2026 and are now about $12-13, as data-centre demand outran supply.[12][11] Q1 FY27 segment sales were ₹176 crore and segment EBIT was ₹52.5 crore, a 29.8% margin, against ₹1.3 crore a year earlier.[9] Management says the margin came from selling fibre bought at old low prices and will not last.[11] The company holds about 11% to 12% of the Indian market for optic fibre cable and is adding its own glass-rod (preform) plant and doubling its fibre-drawing capacity.[12][10]

The consumer basket (fans, lights, switches, water heaters, conduits) is 4% of sales and earned just ₹4.7 crore of EBIT in FY26.[3] Management still targets ₹500 crore of revenue in this basket by FY28, against ₹261.5 crore in FY26.[11][3] The group also owns 49% of Finolex J-Power Systems, a joint venture that makes extra-high-voltage cables and earned its first profit in FY26 (₹21.45 crore).[5][7] Profit from Finolex Industries and the joint venture is added to group profit as the share of net profit of an associate and joint ventures. That share was ₹204.5 crore in FY26, but the cash that actually reached the company was the dividend, ₹72.35 crore from Finolex Industries.[4][5] Chapter 3 separates these pieces so that the reader can see how much profit the factories earn on their own.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Optic fibre cable (OFC): a cable of hair-thin glass strands that carries data as pulses of light.
  • Preform: the solid glass rod from which fibre is drawn. Making its own preform lets the company stop importing it and pay no import duty.
  • Fibre draw capacity: how many kilometres of fibre the draw towers can pull from heated preform each year, counted in millions of fibre-kilometres.
  • EBIT: profit before interest and tax. Segment EBIT is the profit of one business line before the shared costs and treasury income are added.
  • Copper pass-through: raising selling prices to match a higher copper price so that the margin in rupees is protected.
  • Associate and equity method: when a company owns 20% to 50% of another, it adds its share of that company's profit to its own books even though the cash arrives only as dividends.
  • Treasury: the pool of cash, bank deposits, mutual funds and bonds the company holds apart from its factories.
  • FMEG: fast-moving electrical goods, meaning fans, lights, switches and water heaters sold to households.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Finolex is a strong brand with a weak growth record. Over three years its sales grew 12% a year, against 27% for Polycab, 19% for KEI Industries and 20% for R R Kabel.[1][19][20][21] Its operating margin, the profit left after operating costs as a share of sales, is 10% on trailing sales, against 14% for Polycab.[1][19] Polycab's sales in FY26 were ₹28,884 crore, about 4.6 times Finolex's ₹6,321 crore.[19][4] Management accepted the gap on its August 2025 call, saying the company had been "quite subdued on the power cable side" while peers grew strongly there.[14] In power cables it did only about ₹200 crore to ₹250 crore of sales in a ₹25,000 crore market, and it says it has chosen margin over volume.[13][14] That choice protects the margin, but it leaves the company smaller than the leaders in the fastest-growing part of the industry.

The strengths are real. The brand sells in more than 2,15,000 retail outlets through 26 depots, and management says it still commands a price premium over many rival brands.[2][13] The balance sheet is almost free of debt: borrowings were ₹19 crore against equity of ₹6,086 crore at 31 March 2026, and finance costs were ₹1.75 crore for the year.[1][4] Crisil rates the company's bank facilities AA+/Stable, reaffirmed on 28 November 2025, and the company's rating page lists no newer certificate.[16][37] Backward integration helps. The company makes its own copper rods and plastic compounds, and it has just commissioned its first glass-preform plant, only the second in India, to feed its fibre business.[3][11] These strengths protect the company in a downturn. They do not by themselves create growth.

The weaknesses show up in the cash and in the margin. Gross margin, which is sales minus the cost of materials as a share of sales, fell from 19.3% in FY25 to 18.5% in FY26 and was 19.4% in Q1 FY27.[10] Cash from operations was only ₹49 crore in FY26 against profit of ₹714 crore, because inventory rose by ₹306 crore and customer receivables by ₹128 crore.[4] Management says it chose to add about ₹300 crore of stock to secure copper and keep plants running during the Middle East disruption.[7] Supply problems also hurt in the June quarter: the copper-rod plant stopped for lack of LPG gas, and rod sales fell to ₹8 crore from ₹403 crore a year earlier.[11][9] A business that must tie up an extra ₹434 crore in stock and receivables to grow 19% is not yet converting growth into cash.

Competition is getting heavier, and the wire business is where it lands first

UltraTech Cement launched its wires and cables brand, Ultravolt, in September 2026, and Birla and Adani have both announced entries.[28][12] Polycab's management says the newcomer mostly targets wires, which are about 30% of a ₹1.1 trillion industry, and it is not cutting prices.[28] Jefferies expects Ultravolt to reach 5% to 6% of the industry by FY30. JM Financial warns that pricing discipline could break.[28] KEI Industries fell 11% on 22 October 2026 after its quarterly profit missed estimates, and it trimmed its margin guidance, which is management's own forecast, to 11% from 11-12%.[27] Its September-quarter margin on EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a profit measure taken before those accounting charges) was 10%, flat on the year and below the 11.5% that analysts polled had expected.[40] Finolex is more exposed than KEI or Polycab to this fight because about 60% of its electrical sales are construction wire.[11] Management itself told investors in February 2026 that "there would definitely be some pressure" on margin from new entrants.[12]

The shares fell with the sector. Finolex lost 16.65% over five sessions and 22.97% over twenty sessions to 26 October 2026.[30] One data provider noted that a company-specific reason for the drop had not been established.[33] Fibre stocks had also run hard before the fall: HFCL was up 240% in 2026 by 10 September.[26] The 63-day relative strength in the ledger is still +21.8%, so the shares have beaten the Nifty 50 over three months despite the fall.[31] That mix, strong past strength and a sharp recent break, is what a Stage 3 topping or pullback phase looks like, and Chapter 4 tests it.

The treasury and the Finolex Industries stake

At 31 March 2026 the group held ₹163.1 crore of cash, ₹5.0 crore of other bank balances, ₹1,875.3 crore of current investments and ₹719.6 crore of non-current investments, a total of ₹2,762.9 crore.[4] That is ₹181 per share and 15.5% of today's market value.[4][31] The 32.39% stake in Finolex Industries carried a book value of ₹1,489.7 crore and a market value of ₹2,999.4 crore at 31 March 2026, down from ₹3,616.3 crore a year earlier.[5] At Finolex Industries' price of ₹141 on 27 October 2026, close to its 52-week low of ₹140, the same 20.1 crore shares are worth about ₹2,834 crore.[18][5] Finolex Industries earned ₹599 crore in FY26 against ₹800 crore in FY25, and reports its September quarter on 30 October 2026.[5][18]

The two companies also hold each other. Finolex Industries owns 14.51% of Finolex Cables, which at today's close is worth about ₹2,583 crore.[6][31] For a shareholder the point is simple: about a third of the price paid for Finolex Cables buys financial assets and a minority stake, not cables. Those assets are worth what they are worth whether or not the cable business does well, so they set a floor under the value, but they earn little. The reported return on equity of 12.3% is held down by this idle capital.[1]

Governance: a family dispute with a court calendar

The Chhabria family feud is the main governance risk. Deepak Chhabria was not reappointed as executive chairman at the September 2023 annual meeting, with 72.34% of votes cast against him, and Ratnakar Barve became chairman in September 2024.[25] The secretarial auditor records that the company has appealed to the Bombay High Court against a Pune civil court order on the votes cast by Orbit Electricals (30.70% of the shares) and by Finolex Industries (14.51%).[6] In July 2026 the appellate tribunal upheld an order letting Deepak Chhabria amend his 2016 petition over 1,00,300 shares of Finolex Industries, and noted that the Supreme Court had told the tribunal on 12 January 2026 to dispose of the original petition within three months.[24] That deadline has passed without a final ruling in anything we could find.

The board has also churned. The related-party note lists six directors who served for a few weeks each in September to November 2024.[8] Mahesh Viswanathan, until then chief financial officer, became chief executive on 1 June 2026, and Sachin Naik became chief financial officer on the same day.[11][7] On the August 2025 call Mr Viswanathan said the dispute had "some impact, but not overall" on the business.[14] There is no promoter pledge, and promoters hold 35.86% as of 30 September 2026.[17][39] We therefore treat governance as a risk to watch, and not as the reason for the verdict.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in the last chapter rests on a weak assumption, and this chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong and says when we will know. We also give the strongest case against our own view, because a verdict that cannot survive its best counter-argument should not be acted on.

The June quarter may be the peak

The weakest assumption in the FY27 estimate is that communication-cable profit averages about ₹32 crore a quarter for the last three quarters of the year. The June quarter earned ₹52.5 crore partly because the company sold fibre bought at low prices in late 2025.[9][11] Management said a 30% margin is not a number that is sustainable and that long-term contract repricing had already happened in June.[11] Replacement fibre now costs more than the stock sold in Q1. Germanium, the raw material for preform, is a restricted item with long lead times, and the company holds enough for this calendar year.[11] If the second-quarter communication margin is below 20% and spot fibre is under $10 per kilometre, our FY27 estimate is too high.

A 22.2 times multiple on earnings that are 37% non-factory

The target applies the three-year median multiple to total earnings, including about 37% that comes from the treasury and the associate.[4][9] The associate is the weaker part. Finolex Industries' profit fell from ₹800 crore to ₹599 crore in FY26, its own shares trade at 14 times earnings near a 52-week low, and the cross-holding between the two companies makes a clean valuation hard.[5][18][6] A sum-of-the-parts check that prices the stake at market and the factories at 20 times gives ₹1,263 to ₹1,393 per share, below or at our target. On that range the ratio at ₹1,093, against the ₹899.34 stop, is between 0.9 and 1.5, and it is 0.9 if the market uses the lower value of ₹1,263. A 20.1 times multiple on our own earnings gives the same ₹1,262.

Cash conversion has not been shown to recover

The company says the ₹306 crore stock build and ₹128 crore rise in receivables in FY26 were deliberate and will unwind.[4][7] That is plausible, because the build came after a copper spike and during Middle East supply problems. But copper is still high, Q1 FY27 sales were 44% higher, and working capital rises when sales and copper both rise.[11][10] The hard-line test is measured on audited years, so a weak half-year is only a warning. When the FY27 accounts are published the three-year window becomes FY25 to FY27, and FY24's ₹577 crore of cash from operations drops out. 7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is REJECTED. The rule is the reward-to-risk floor of 2.5 to 1 that applies to the turnaround archetype. Against the stop of ₹899.34 explained below, the ratio to the ₹1,395 central target is 0.87 at today's close of ₹1,164.40, 1.56 at ₹1,093 and 2.29 at ₹1,050, so it fails at every price in the zone the support ladder allows.[31][32] This is not a technical rejection. The name is not in a Stage 4 downtrend, no hard red line is crossed, and Layer 2 qualifies. The ledger routes a name in a pullback above a rising 200-day average to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT only when the ratio passes inside the support zone. Here it does not, so the name is rejected and not left waiting.

Which stop applies. The thresholds file sets the core-alpha stop as the 20-day swing low or the 50-day average, whichever is lower, minus one ATR for a Stage 2 uptrend, and as the 63-day low minus one ATR for a Stage 1 or turnaround base.[32] This name is not in a Stage 2 uptrend, because Test A1 fails, and its archetype is turnaround. The second rule therefore applies: ₹967.00 minus ₹67.66 is ₹899.34. The swing-low rule would give a stop of ₹984.94 and a ratio of 2.79 at ₹1,093, but that rule is for Stage 2 uptrends and does not apply here. The 63-day low is also the lowest level that the pullback has to hold for the base to stay intact, so a stop above it would sit inside the normal range. At ₹1,093 the stop is 17.7% below the price, and at today's close it is 22.8% below.

The price at which the ratio would pass. The highest price that still clears 2.5 to 1 against the ₹899.34 stop is ₹1,041 on the 22.2 times median target and ₹1,003 on the stricter 20.1 times target. At today's 22.3 times trailing multiple it is ₹1,043.[23] Each of those prices is below the 20-day swing low of ₹1,052.60, and ₹1,041 is also below the 200-day average of ₹1,043.88. We do not move the zone down to rescue the ratio. The ladder measures the distance above an existing support, and a price under that support means the support has already broken. If the shares fell there and built a new floor, the ledger's support anchor would reset and we would rerun the whole case with a new target and stop. That is a reason to re-underwrite, not an order to place.

What the reader should do. Do not buy at today's price, and do not place a limit order (an order that fills only at your price or better) in the ₹1,050 to ₹1,093 zone. The position size is zero. Keep the name on the review list, because the business is sound and debt-free, and revisit it after the September quarter results.

The terms the table uses, in plain words. Risk-unit sizing sizes a position so that being stopped out costs a fixed small share of the whole portfolio. That share is the risk budget, counted in basis points, where 100 basis points is 1%. The tier is our conviction grade. TIER_1 is the highest and allows 7% to 10% of the portfolio, TIER_4 is the lowest and allows 1% to 2%, and TIER_0 means no allocation. The tier's maximum is the tier cap. The weight is the smaller of the cap and the risk budget divided by the stop distance, so a wide stop gives a small position. Here the verdict is REJECTED, so the tier is TIER_0 and the weight is 0%. Core is the long-horizon quality bucket, which the thresholds file sizes at 30% to 40% of the India portfolio. The let-winners-run rule says that a position that has risen is not sold only because it has grown large or looks overbought: it may grow to 22% of the portfolio while the stop moves up behind the price. The support ladder says how far above support we may buy: full size within 0.6 ATR units, standard size within 1.2 units, a reduced starter within 1.6 units, and no buying above that.
