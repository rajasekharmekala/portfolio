1. What the company does

Bharat Dynamics Limited (BDL) is a company owned 74.93% by the Government of India that builds guided missiles and underwater weapons for the Indian armed forces.[10][28] It was set up in Hyderabad in 1970.[12] CRISIL calls it the prime production agency for guided weapons in the country, which means the government treats BDL as its main factory for missiles that state laboratories design.[15] Its range covers the Akash and medium-range surface-to-air missiles, the Astra air-to-air missile, the Konkurs-M, Invar, Milan-2T and NAG anti-tank missiles, and lightweight and heavyweight torpedoes.[28]

Almost every rupee comes from one customer, the Ministry of Defence (MoD). The Annual Report says so in plain words: a high dependence on the Ministry exposes the company to changes in defence budgets and buying priorities.[4] Exports exist but are small and uneven, at ₹702.23 crore in FY26 after ₹1,270 crore in FY25.[3][40] Orders arrive as signed contracts. On 23 September 2026 the ministry signed ₹810.79 crore for 160 satellite-guided glide bombs for the air force, and on 15 October 2026 BDL signed about ₹1,307 crore for heavyweight torpedoes for the Navy, to be delivered within 42 months.[14][13]

BDL earns money by delivering what each contract names. The government pays an advance of 15% to 20% of the order value up front, which CRISIL says helps the company fund the parts it must buy before it can deliver.[15] At 31 March 2026 those advances, shown in the accounts as contract liabilities (money received for work not yet delivered), stood at ₹6,252 crore.[7] BDL's accounting moves each advance into revenue only as it completes the performance obligation in the contract, so revenue arrives in lumps.[7] The last five quarters show it: ₹248 crore, ₹1,147 crore, ₹567 crore, ₹480 crore and ₹572 crore.[26]

A second income stream sits beside the missiles. Because customers pay in advance, BDL holds a large pile of cash and bank deposits, ₹4,709 crore at 31 March 2026.[5] That pile earns interest. Other income, mostly interest, was ₹424 crore in FY26, against an operating profit of only ₹225 crore.[26][6] So about three-quarters of the company's pre-tax profit in FY26 (₹424 crore of ₹568 crore) came from interest on customers' money rather than from making missiles.[26][8] You need to keep that split in mind for every profit number in this book.

How an order becomes revenue

A missile order moves through five steps. A state laboratory (DRDO) designs the weapon. The armed forces run user trials, and the system receives a production clearance; the upgraded Akash received its clearance on 27 March 2026.[2] BDL then buys parts, many from foreign makers and some from single-source suppliers.[4] It assembles and tests the missile. The customer must clear each delivery, and ICICI Securities says Akash deliveries wait for such clearances on new versions.[16] BDL books the sale as it completes the contract's performance obligation.[7]

This chain explains why results are lumpy. Each step depends on a party outside BDL: the design body, the user, the foreign supplier or the inspector. If one step slips, finished work waits as inventory. At 31 March 2026 BDL held ₹4,626 crore of inventory, 1.9 times its FY26 revenue, up from ₹2,645 crore a year earlier.[12] That stock is the clearest sign of how much signed work sits between the factory floor and the revenue line.[5]

What this means for an owner

For an owner, BDL is a long-lived, government-backed order book attached to a delivery clock that has run slow. The cash pile cushions the shortfall, but it also makes reported profit look better than the missile business alone earns. 2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for BDL starts with how hard it is to replace. The government treats it as the main producer of guided weapons, and CRISIL says it is the exclusive source of Akash and Konkurs missiles.[15] BDL says it has made about 90% of its major missiles in India, which means most parts no longer depend on imports.[15] A new supplier would need design rights from DRDO, production clearances and years of user trials, so the position is hard to copy. The same Annual Report that describes this position also warns that private companies are entering defence manufacturing and could raise competition.[4]

The second strength is demand that is already signed. The order book was ₹26,176 crore at 31 March 2026, which is 10.7 times FY26 revenue of ₹2,442 crore.[3][26] The company lists about ₹15,000 crore of orders in its pipeline for FY27 and expects about ₹25,000 crore of fresh orders over the next two to three years.[12][3] The Defence Acquisition Council cleared about ₹1.10 lakh crore of purchase proposals on 7 September 2026, with 98% earmarked for Indian industry, which supports that pipeline.[33]

The third strength is the balance sheet. BDL has ₹5 crore of borrowings against ₹4,241 crore of net worth, ₹4,709 crore of cash and bank deposits, and a CRISIL short-term rating of A1+ on ₹600 crore of bank facilities.[27][5][15] CRISIL expects it to stay debt-free and notes that the government would be expected to support it if needed.[15]

Where the business is weak: delivery

The weak point is execution. Revenue fell 27% in FY26 to ₹2,442 crore. The company explains this as a shift in delivery dates for surface-to-air missiles and their ground support equipment, set by the customer's extended requirements.[2] Brokers describe a second cause: delays in supplies of radars, seekers and other parts for the Akash and Astra Mk-I missiles, and Akash deliveries waiting for clearances on new versions.[18][16] Both can be true at once.

The effect was sharp. In the March 2026 quarter revenue was ₹480 crore against ₹1,777 crore a year earlier, a fall of 73%, while inventory rose 75% year on year.[26][16] Nuvama said revenue, operating profit and net profit missed the street's estimates by 76%, 87% and 69%.[19] The December 2025 quarter was no better, with profit down 50.4% to ₹72.9 crore.[21] Quality of delivery, not quantity of orders, is the issue.

Where the business is weak: margins and suppliers

Margins are thin and shifting. CRISIL notes that BDL bids for defence projects on a fixed-contract basis with minimal profitability, and that delays in technical changes from DRDO leave BDL liable for damages.[15] Imported material consumed rose 164% to ₹290 crore in FY26, and Motilal Oswal expects a higher share of bought-out parts to keep weighing on margins.[2][20] The earnings-before-interest-taxes-depreciation-and-amortization (EBITDA) margin, the profit before those items as a share of sales, fell from 22.6% in FY24 to 9.1% in FY26.[12]

The company also leans on a few suppliers. The Annual Report warns of reliance on certain foreign makers, subcontractors and single-source vendors for critical sub-assemblies.[4] ICICI Securities names heavy dependence on a single source for the seeker, the part that steers the missile to its target, as a cause of the execution problems.[16]

Cyclicality: orders follow budgets, deliveries follow clearances

Defence orders do not follow a business cycle. They follow government budgets and delivery clearances. Revenue over the five years to FY26 was ₹2,817 crore, ₹2,489 crore, ₹2,369 crore, ₹3,345 crore and ₹2,442 crore, so it grew 5% a year from FY21 and was almost flat over three years.[26] Operating margin ranged from 26% in FY22 to 9% in FY26.[26] A single quarter can swing: the operating result was a loss of ₹45 crore in the June 2025 quarter and a profit of ₹188 crore in the next one.[26]

The honest summary is a very large, government-backed demand book and an unreliable delivery clock. Chapter 2B scores that tension with five measures and checks whether management's earlier promises were kept.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

We compared what management said in 2024 to 2026 with what happened. The pattern is clear. On revenue, BDL's targets were missed every year: it cut its FY24 target to ₹2,900 to ₹3,000 crore from ₹3,200 crore and still delivered ₹2,369 crore; it said FY25 would reach ₹4,000 crore and delivered ₹3,345 crore; it said FY26 would reach ₹5,000 crore and delivered ₹2,442 crore.[17][26] Those targets were relayed in a report by Elara Capital, not filed by BDL itself, so we treat them as management's stated view as reported.[17]

The decision is REJECTED. The rule behind it is the technical routing rule. A stock in Stage 4 with Test C failing is a hard red line. Even without the red line, the numbers would not clear. Our 12 to 24 month base value is ₹1,063. The stop is ₹960.95, which is the 63-day base low of ₹996.00 minus one average true range of ₹35.05. But ₹995 is below the ₹996 floor, so the arithmetic and the chart conflict.

Layer 3 decides how big a position could be and where the exit sits. It has three parts. First, how close the price is to its floor, measured in average true ranges (the typical daily swing). BDL is 0.29 of one swing above its floor, which falls in the closest band of the scoring ladder, the one that allows a full-size position (the FULL_SIZE band, anything under 0.6). Second, a conviction grade called a tier sets the largest share of the portfolio one stock may take. The best grade, TIER_1, allows 7% to 10%. The lowest buyable grade, TIER_3, allows 2% to 3.5%. TIER_0 means not buyable, so the cap is zero. Third, the risk-unit weight sizes a new position by how much of the portfolio you would lose if the stop is hit. That loss is counted in basis points, where 100 basis points is 1% of the portfolio, and a tier gives it a budget: TIER_3 gets 50 basis points, TIER_0 gets none, so the weight is 0.0%. The closest band matters only when Layer 1 passes. It does not today, so BDL is TIER_0.[1]

If BDL later qualified, a core name with a 3.0 out of 5.0 scorecard would sit in TIER_3, capped at 3.5%. TIER_3 is the lowest grade a core name can receive; TIER_4 exists for other kinds of names. Two labels would then apply. PASSED_CONDITIONAL means the business and the chart rule are acceptable but the entry timing is not yet right. WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT is the matching action: do not buy at the market, wait for the price to come back to its floor or place a limit order inside the entry zone. The let-winners-run rule would apply only after a position exists. It forbids a mechanical trim of a Stage 2 winner just because RSI is above 70. It trails the stop to the rising 21-day or 50-day average and lets the weight grow to 22%. The table that follows is generated from the scoring rules, so its stop row uses the long rule text. Here that rule means one thing: the stop is the 63-day base low of ₹996.00 minus one average true range of ₹35.05, which is ₹960.95. The table's -6.79% is measured from the top of the entry zone (₹1,031.00). From the close of ₹1,006.00 the stop is 4.5% away.
