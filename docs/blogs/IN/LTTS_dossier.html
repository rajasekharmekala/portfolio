LTTS is an Indian engineering services firm, 73.5% owned by Larsen & Toubro, that designs and tests vehicles, plants and medical devices for global clients[2][14]. We rate it WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: the business is sound and earns 26.7% on its capital, but after a 30% rise in one month the price of ₹4,174.20 offers only 0.29 of reward for each 1 of risk[1][2][42]. Our floor is 2.0. The September 2026 profit rise of 66.8% includes a one-time gain of ₹165 crore, and the real rise was 19.7%[3][4]. 1. What the company does

L&T Technology Services sells engineering brainpower. A manufacturer of trucks, medical scanners, mobile-phone masts or chemical plants hires LTTS engineers to design a product, write its embedded software, test it and keep it running. The company was incorporated in 2012 and is a listed subsidiary of Larsen & Toubro, which held 73.57% at March 2026 and 73.51% at September 2026[2][14]. In the September 2026 quarter it earned $317.1 million, or ₹3,040 crore, with 24,035 employees working from 21 design centres[3][4]. Its client list has 430 active names, including 69 Fortune 500 companies[4][3].

The money comes from billing for engineers' time and for finished projects. About 64.4% of revenue is time-and-material work, where the client pays per hour, and 35.6% is fixed price, where LTTS agrees a price for a result and keeps the gain or bears the overrun[4]. Offshore work, done from India at lower cost, was 55.9% of revenue[4]. People are the main cost. Employee cost was ₹1,752 crore, or 57.6% of revenue, in the quarter, and other costs were another 23.1%[27]. So profit depends on two things: the price LTTS gets for an engineer-hour compared with the salary it pays, and how fully its 22,449 billable engineers are used[4]. Wage rises are set for the December 2026 quarter, and management expects them to cost 50 to 80 basis points of margin (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point)[7].

Currency matters because most revenue is in dollars while most costs are in rupees. The rupee averaged 95.90 to the dollar in the September 2026 quarter, against 88.40 a year earlier[4]. That is why revenue in rupees grew 12.1% while revenue in dollars, excluding currency effects, grew 4.8%[3]. The 4.8% figure is the better measure of real demand. We call it constant-currency growth: the growth rate if exchange rates had stayed where they were a year ago.

The company reports three segments. Sustainability is the largest and the most profitable. Mobility and Tech make up the rest, and Tech is the one that is shrinking. Where the customers are, and how concentrated they are

North America gave 60.7% of revenue, Europe 17.7%, India 13.4% and the rest of the world 8.2%. Europe fell 3.9% over the year in dollars, while the rest of the world rose 18.3%[4]. Client concentration is low. The top five clients gave 15.7% of last-twelve-month revenue, the top ten 26.7% and the top 20 41.0%[4]. There are 197 clients that each paid more than $1 million a year, and 29 that each paid more than $10 million[4]. A low concentration means that one client's budget cut cannot sink a quarter, which is a contrast with some peers described in Chapter 3.

The business is cyclical in a specific way. Engineering budgets follow clients' product plans, not the economy in a given quarter. When European carmakers restructure, their design budgets fall for several quarters. Management says European auto should stay steady for six to nine months and recover in nine to twelve[7]. A single medical-device programme can also move a segment, as it did in the September quarter[7][29].

The strategy: Engineering Intelligence and the five-year plan

Management calls its main bet Engineering Intelligence, or EI: using artificial intelligence tools inside engineering work and inside the products clients ship. It reported an annualised EI-led revenue of $120 million, about 9.5% of quarterly revenue[3][7]. The company counts a deliverable as AI revenue when a significant part of it is created with its AI stack. That is a company definition and is not independently audited[7]. The five-year plan, called Lakshya 31, aims at 13% to 15% revenue growth a year and an EBIT margin of 16% to 17%[3][4]. EBIT margin means profit after depreciation but before interest and tax, as a share of revenue.

A newly announced five-year Nokia contract adds an offshore development centre in India for Nokia's research and development work, in the area of next-generation compute and AI infrastructure[20][30]. The press release gives no contract value, so we do not count it in our estimates until it shows up in revenue.

The exit from Smart World and Communication

LTTS bought a business called Smart World and Communication (SWC) in April 2023 for about ₹800 crore and agreed in March 2026 to sell it for ₹452 crore (the headline price used in every loss figure in this report) to a venture called AMI Paradigm[23][22][24]. SWC earned ₹1,027 crore, or 9.6% of group revenue, in FY25, at EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) margins of 8% to 10% by JPMorgan's estimate[23]. The sale closed on 1 September 2026. LTTS booked a gain of ₹165 crore after tax in the September quarter, shown under discontinued operations, which are businesses sold or closed and reported on a separate line[5][4]. This sale is the reason that the September quarter's reported profit jumped, and Chapter 3 separates it out.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A good engineering services business wins on three things: deep knowledge of its clients' products, a cost base below what the client could achieve alone, and long relationships that turn into repeat work. LTTS has all three, and the evidence is in the numbers. The company says about 90% of its business is repeat business, it holds 1,808 patents of which 1,085 are co-authored with clients, and it serves 57 of the world's 100 largest research-and-development spenders[15][3]. Those claims come from the company. What can be checked is the pattern of spending: the largest client is only a small slice of revenue, and large deals worth $855 million were signed in FY26, up 40% on the year[17][9].

The moat is domain knowledge and certification, not technology that a rival cannot copy. An aircraft part, a rail brake or a medical device must pass safety rules, and the engineers who understand those rules are scarce. That makes switching expensive for a client in the middle of a programme. It does not stop a client from moving new work to another supplier, and it does not protect the price per hour. Management says its AI toolkit and 'context' about client products put it nine to twelve months ahead of peers[7]. We cannot test that claim from filings, and we treat it as management's view.

The financial position is a clear strength. CRISIL, an Indian credit rating agency, reaffirmed its top rating of AAA with a stable outlook in June 2026[19]. The annual report says the company has no debt that makes the debt-to-equity or interest-cover ratios relevant, and the balance sheet at September 2026 held ₹2,735 crore of investments and ₹1,440 crore of cash[14][2][4]. The company paid ₹423.5 crore of dividends in the half year to September 2026, a sign that it returns cash instead of hoarding it[6]. A business with no debt, a payout near 48% and a return on capital employed of 26.7% is one that can fund its own growth.

Where the business is strong

Margin has turned up. EBIT margin reached 16.3% in the September 2026 quarter, up 60 basis points over June and 220 over a year, and every segment improved[4][7]. Management said it reached its mid-16% goal about two quarters ahead of plan, and Motilal Oswal and Nomura had both expected 15.6%[7][26]. The sale of SWC helped, because JPMorgan estimated that SWC's low margins had been dragging the group by about 70 basis points[23]. Cash collection has improved as well: customers took 78 days to pay in September 2026, against 114 days a year earlier[7][11]. A year-ago figure included SWC, so the two are not like for like, a point we return to in the walk-the-talk table.

Order flow supports the story. Large-deal wins were $203 million in the quarter, more than half of them AI-related, and management said the sales pipeline is up more than 25% over a year[3][7]. Large deals matter because they feed revenue for years. A $100 million deal runs five to seven years, a $50 million deal four to five, and a $10 million deal two to three, by the CEO's own description[7].

Where the business is weak

Growth is modest. Dollar revenue from continuing operations rose 4.8% over the year at constant currency and 2.7% over the June quarter[3]. For FY26 the dollar revenue of continuing operations grew 8.3% to $1,233 million, and 7.7% at constant currency[14]. Management had told investors in April 2025 to expect double-digit growth in FY26, and in January 2026 it cut that to mid-single-digit growth overall[13][10]. The Tech segment fell 8.5% over the year in September, and Europe fell 3.9%[4].

The second weakness is the pricing power of the whole industry. LTTS's own August 2026 presentation says that AI is compressing effort-based work and that value is moving from staffing to platforms and outcomes[15]. In plain terms, if a tool lets one engineer do the work of two, the client pays for fewer hours. LTTS says it will win by selling outcomes and platforms. The shift has begun, and the evidence is only 9.5% of revenue so far[7].

The third weakness is capital allocation. A business bought for about ₹800 crore in 2023 and sold for ₹452 crore in 2026 is a poor use of capital. JPMorgan called out the concern, and the sale price was 43% below the purchase price on CNBC-TV18's count[23]. The ₹165 crore profit gain does not mean the deal made money. At March 2026 the business sat on the balance sheet at about ₹323 crore of net assets (₹987 crore of assets held for sale less ₹664 crore of linked liabilities), and the headline sale price of ₹452 crore is 43% below the ₹800 crore paid in 2023. The cash flow statement shows ₹493 crore received from the transfer, and the company does not reconcile that figure to the ₹452 crore price[4][6][23].

How the three segments and the parent fit together

The parent matters. Larsen & Toubro's 73.5% stake means minority holders have little say, and the parent's finance chief sits as a permanent invitee to the audit committee[14]. That is normal for a subsidiary of a large group, and it also means that the share has a controlling holder that does not trade it. Only 26.5% of shares float, and foreign funds held 4.43% and domestic funds 13.76% at September 2026[2].

Competition comes from four directions: Indian engineering peers (Tata Elxsi, KPIT Technologies, Cyient and Tata Technologies, compared in Chapter 3), global engineering groups, the in-house teams of clients, and the large IT services firms that are entering engineering. LTTS says that IT-first competitors lack embedded depth and safety certification[15]. We do not rate the moat as wide, because the return on capital employed fell from 33% in FY23 to 27% in FY26 and sales growth has been uneven[2].

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A record of kept and broken promises tells us how much weight to give the next promise. Guidance is what management says it expects, such as a growth rate or a margin date. We read the last seven earnings calls, from April 2025 to October 2026, and listed each dated promise against what happened. The record is mixed. Management hits the targets it controls, such as margin, cash collection and the dividend. It missed the one about revenue growth and it lost money on the SWC acquisition.

The growth promise failed in two steps. In April 2025 the CEO said FY26 would be a better year than FY25 with double-digit revenue growth in constant currency, and in October 2025 he said, 'I said double digit, and I hold to that'[13][11]. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is more fragile than the rest. For FY28 earnings per share it is the 16.5% EBIT margin after a wage rise of 50 to 80 basis points. For the 28 times multiple it is that the market will keep paying more than the 21 to 23 times implied by the brokers' targets. For the probability-weighted value it is the 25% weight on the bear case. For the entry ceiling it is the stop at ₹2,892.94, which is 15.9% below the ceiling. Below we state the strongest case against our own verdict, three scenarios that would break the thesis, and the one claim on which everything hangs.

The red-team bear case against our verdict

The strongest honest case against WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT has two halves. The first half says we are too cautious. The ledger's reverse DCF assumes only 9.19% growth a year, estimates are rising, Jefferies expects a re-rating of Indian IT, and a debt-free business earning 26.7% on capital deserves a premium multiple[1][32][2]. If the stock holds above ₹4,000 for weeks and the December quarter confirms the acceleration, it may never touch ₹3,440, and waiting would cost the whole move. We accept that. A 5.8% expected gain does not pay for a 26% downside.

The second half says that even ₹3,440 is not safe. A stock that closes at 31 times trailing earnings with growth of 4.8% in constant currency has paid for the aim and not for the delivery. Brokers who follow the company daily set targets of ₹3,450 to ₹3,850[26]. If they are right, our zone will be touched and the price will keep going. Our stop at ₹2,892.94 would then lose 15.9% from the top of the zone. We address it by sizing: a full-size position stopped out costs 50 basis points of the portfolio, and the half-size starter about 25.

Fatal flaw 1: AI cuts billable hours faster than AI work arrives

Engineering services bill for people's time. If a client's tools let one engineer do the work of two, the client buys fewer hours. LTTS says 40% of the code in one major engagement is generated by AI and that time to market is cut by 50%[7]. That is good for the client and neutral for LTTS only if the saved budget is spent on more projects. Management says clients are redirecting savings to new AI work, and the Tech segment's 8.5% fall shows what happens when they do not[7][4]. In this case growth stays at 4% to 5%, FY28 EPS is about ₹147 and the value is near ₹3,090. The evidence we would see first is a second quarter of falling Tech revenue and a pipeline growth rate below 15%.

Fatal flaw 2: margin gives back its gains

Margin rose 220 basis points over a year, but part of that is the sale of SWC, which JPMorgan put at about 70 basis points[23]. Part is the weak rupee, which at 95.90 to the dollar versus 88.40 a year ago lifts rupee revenue against rupee costs[4]. The wage rise lands in the December quarter and costs 50 to 80 basis points[7]. If the rupee strengthened, if wage costs exceeded the guide or if the company spent more to build its AI platform, margin could return to 15% or lower. At 15.0% and the same revenue, FY28 EPS would be about ₹148. The company gives no rupee sensitivity in the calls we read, so we do not quantify it further. The level that would confirm the flaw is an EBIT margin below 15.5% for the December quarter.

Fatal flaw 3: weak cash conversion and another acquisition

The first-half statutory cash conversion is about 0.76, and the company's headline free cash flow of 108% excludes ₹296 crore that the sold business absorbed[6][4][42]. If the gap persists after the SWC cash is gone, reported profit is not turning into cash as it did. Management has also talked about 'consolidation deals' that could add to growth in Europe[7]. LTTS paid about ₹800 crore for SWC and sold it three years later for a headline ₹452 crore[23]. A second misjudged deal would hurt both return on capital and trust. The level that would confirm this flaw is statutory cash from operations below 0.7 of profit for the full year to March 2027, filed in April 2027. A full year at 0.76 would only move the cash KPI from PASS to WATCH. An acquisition announced above ₹1,000 crore without a margin target would also confirm it.

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule is the Core Alpha asymmetry floor of 2.0 to 1. The number that triggers it is 0.29 to 1 at the ₹4,174.20 close. The decision would change to ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP when the price reaches ₹3,440 or lower, and only if a refreshed valuation (FY28 EPS and multiple) still gives 2.0 to 1 at the fill price. At the top of the zone the stop is 15.9% away, which is wide. That is why the weight is small. We would use limit orders, not market orders. We would split the buy between ₹3,440 and ₹3,300.

The size follows the risk-unit formula. We assign conviction tier TIER_3, a 50 basis-point risk budget. Growth and deal conversion are on watch, and the Nifty regime is a downtrend. The weight is the risk budget divided by the stop distance from the entry high, capped by the tier maximum. It comes to 3.14% of the portfolio. In the current regime the starter is limited to half of that, about 1.5% of the portfolio, until the December quarter is known[41][42]. If the price rises after we buy, the rule is to let winners run. We do not trim only because RSI is above 70 or because the weight passes 15%. We allow an appreciated weight up to 22%. We trail the stop to the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average. We trim only if margin or cash conversion fails the levels in Chapter 6[41].

What this means for a reader who owns LTTS today: hold, do not add above ₹3,440, and use the stop of ₹2,892.94 on any new money. For a reader who owns none, the decision is a limit order and a date.
