IOL Chemicals and Pharmaceuticals makes bulk medicine powders, led by ibuprofen, plus industrial chemicals[3][13]. It has almost no debt, turns profit into cash and just had its strongest quarter of the last five (sales ₹756 crore, profit ₹64 crore)[17][5]. We reject the stock for now: its return on capital of 11.3% is below our 15% floor, and the ₹179.36 price already pays for the company's FY28 plan[17][33]. The view changes if the price falls to about ₹157.64 and holds, or once return on capital reaches 15% with a margin above 14.5% for three quarters; the 30 October results are only the first test[21][7].

1. What the company does

IOL Chemicals and Pharmaceuticals (IOL) is a single-site manufacturer in Barnala, Punjab. It makes two kinds of product. The first is active pharmaceutical ingredients, called APIs: the medicine itself, in powder form, before a drug company turns it into tablets. The second is specialty chemicals such as ethyl acetate (a solvent used in paints, inks and packaging) and acetic anhydride (a chemical used to make paracetamol).[4][13] Think of IOL as a bulk supplier to other manufacturers. It never sells a box of pills to a patient, so its customers are drug makers and chemical users, not households.

Ibuprofen is the product that built the company. IOL says it is the world's largest producer, with about 30% of global supply, and it has 12,000 tonnes a year of ibuprofen capacity in two fully backward-integrated facilities.[5][4][40] The company makes its own raw materials for ibuprofen (iso-butyl benzene, acetyl chloride and mono-chloro acetic acid), which means it does not depend on outsiders for the key inputs.[13] Ibuprofen was about 34% of FY26 sales, and ibuprofen plus ethyl acetate together were about 68%.[14] That concentration is the first fact to keep in mind: two products drive two-thirds of the revenue.

The second fact is that the mix is changing. Non-ibuprofen APIs were 43% of pharma revenue in the June 2026 quarter, up from 36% a year earlier.[6] The list includes paracetamol (a new 10,800-tonne plant that began on 24 March 2025 and cost ₹155.51 crore), metformin (a diabetes drug), clopidogrel (an anti-clotting drug), pantoprazole (an acid-reflux drug), fenofibrate and levetiracetam.[12][4] Management wants non-ibuprofen products to reach about half of pharma revenue soon, and 50% to 55% of API revenue by FY29.[8] More products mean less reliance on one price cycle, but the new products are mostly commodity-like, so they bring lower margins than ibuprofen did at its best.

Who pays? Pharma companies in India and abroad buy the APIs. The top ten customers were only about 19% of FY26 sales, so no single buyer can hurt the company, and exports were about 24% of sales, rising to 28.5% in the June 2026 quarter.[14][6] The chemicals side sells ethyl acetate entirely on the open market, while 50% to 60% of acetic anhydride is used inside the plant to make paracetamol.[9] A new 6,000-tonne triacetin plant (a chemical used in food, cosmetics and filters) started in May 2026 and could add about ₹120 crore of revenue a year at full use, according to the finance chief.[8] The board approved the plant on 20 May 2026 at a total cost of only ₹16.88 crore, paid from internal cash.[36] What this means for you: IOL is a volume-and-spread business. Its profit depends on how full its plants run and on the gap between what it pays for inputs and what it charges. The next chapters test how strong that position is.

How the company makes money

IOL's profit is the spread between input cost and selling price, multiplied by volume. Management says it passes most input cost changes to customers, and the part it cannot pass through is absorbed from a one-to-two-month order book where prices were fixed earlier.[1] After the war in West Asia lifted crude-linked inputs, prices of ethyl acetate and acetic anhydride rose sharply in March and then steadied.[8] The gross margin (what is left of revenue after the cost of materials) therefore moves with the price of oil-linked chemicals and with ibuprofen demand, more than with anything the company controls.

Fixed costs make the profit swing more than sales. Employee costs are about 10% of revenue, and spreading fixed plant costs over more output lifts the margin quickly.[9] That is what happened in the June 2026 quarter: revenue rose 37.1% while EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a measure of operating profit) rose 60.7%.[5] The same arithmetic works in reverse. If volumes fall, margins fall faster than sales, as they did from FY22 to FY25 when the EBITDA margin dropped from 13.0% to 10.7%.[3]

Where the plant sits and how it is run

All production is at one integrated complex of more than 180 acres in Barnala, with eleven production units, a 17 MW power plant and a zero-liquid-discharge effluent system.[4][13] One site gives low cost and tight control, but it also means one fire, one flood or one regulatory action touches everything. The company bought 101 more acres on the Chandigarh-Bathinda highway for a second site, with environmental clearance in hand and other approvals pending as of May 2026.[9]

Management guides to a capital outlay of ₹1,200 crore to ₹1,400 crore over four to five years on the new land, spread at about ₹200 crore to ₹250 crore a year and paid from internal cash.[9] In the August 2026 call management said the first big project on that land would probably not start within FY27.[8] Management said a buyback (a company repurchasing its own shares) is a second option behind growth projects, and it has not been discussed by the board.[9] For you as an owner, that means free cash is likely to be spent on plants for several years, not returned, so the dividend (₹1 per share in FY26, about 0.55% of the price) stays small.[2][17]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

IOL has a real advantage in one product and a real weakness in how much it depends on a few. The advantage is cost: it makes its own ibuprofen inputs, runs the largest ibuprofen plant in the world and keeps its ibuprofen and chemicals plants at 90% to 100% of capacity. The new paracetamol plant is the exception, at 55% to 65%.[13][4] The weakness is that ibuprofen and ethyl acetate are commodity-like. CARE Ratings, the credit rating agency, says the sharp fall in ibuprofen prices after the COVID period 'demonstrates the inherent cyclicality associated with the industry'.[14] Both sentences are true at once, and the rest of this chapter weighs them with evidence.

The rating agency view is a useful anchor. CARE reaffirmed CARE A+ (Stable) for long-term bank lines and CARE A1+ for short-term lines (letter dated 30 June 2026, published 6 July 2026), and says IOL's edge in ibuprofen 'is expected to sustain in the medium term'.[13] CARE would raise the rating if revenue grows more than 15% with EBITDA margin sustained above 15%, and would cut it if revenue fell more than 20% or margin stayed below 10%.[14] Those triggers are a good scorecard for the margin question in Chapter 3. CARE's July 2025 note blamed the FY25 dip on volatile finished-goods prices in both pharma and chemicals, and interest cover moved from 15.20 times in FY24 to 14.27 in FY25 and 18.86 in FY26.[38][13]

Position in ibuprofen: scale, cost and regulatory standing

PharmaDB lists 37 manufacturers with an active ibuprofen filing, and only 16 of them hold a US master file or a European certificate of suitability.[25] IOL is one of the 16, with one US file and two European certificates for ibuprofen.[25] It also holds approval from China's drug regulator for ibuprofen (April 2025) and, in 2026, for clopidogrel.[14][6] These approvals take years to earn and are a barrier to small entrants, which is why IOL can sell to large generic and over-the-counter drug makers in Europe and elsewhere.[9]

The US Food and Drug Administration inspected the Barnala site in April 2015 and August 2019. Both inspections were classified Voluntary Action Indicated, the mildest outcome that still allows approval, with no citations posted, and the FDA database shows no warning letter and no Form 483 record for the company.[24] Management also says the US FDA has approved five of its products and approves further ones through its customers' filings without visiting.[8][10] The caution is age: the last US FDA inspection was in 2019, and the newer plants (paracetamol, the expanded clopidogrel and pantoprazole lines) have not been inspected by the US agency.[24]

The ibuprofen price cycle and China

Ibuprofen prices rose and then collapsed after COVID, and CARE says that fall is why the business is cyclical.[14] Management's own account is that demand was weak for three or four quarters because of overstocking in 2023 and 2024 and the arrival of a new competitor, and that demand recovered from October 2025.[10] In the May 2026 call an analyst said another listed ibuprofen maker had stated that prices were 'not remunerative' and that it wanted to close the business.[10] Management declined to comment on the rival and said its own ibuprofen plants run at 90% to 95% of capacity.[10]

Public price data is weak. Expert Market Research puts Indian ibuprofen at USD 14.9 a kilogram in the June 2026 quarter, up 5.7% from USD 14.1, and the United States at USD 16.4.[26] IMARC puts the United States at USD 11,749 a tonne, which is USD 11.75 a kilogram, and China at USD 13,737, the opposite order from Expert Market Research, which shows China lowest at USD 9.9.[27][26] PharmaDB, built from customs data, shows a median export price of USD 10.80 a kilogram in 2025 with a range of USD 9.02 to USD 14.35.[25] Three sources, three different levels. The honest conclusion is that nobody outside the company can measure the ibuprofen price cycle with confidence, and IOL does not publish ibuprofen prices, so the margin line is the only evidence we have.[8]

China is both a rival and a market. Chinese plants have large ibuprofen capacity, which is why Chinese prices are usually the lowest, and IOL still imports a metformin raw material (dicyandiamide) from China because no Indian maker supplies it.[26][8] At the same time China's regulator approved IOL's ibuprofen and clopidogrel, which opens a new sales channel.[14][6] Trade defence is spreading: in October 2026 Pakistan proposed provisional anti-dumping duties of 8.71% on Chinese ibuprofen and 7.77% on US ibuprofen.[28] That case does not touch IOL directly, but it shows that ibuprofen is a price-sensitive product that governments watch.

Diversification, customers and exports

The diversification is real in the numbers. Non-ibuprofen products were about 18% of API revenue in FY21 and about 37% in FY26.[4] The company expects clopidogrel, pantoprazole and fenofibrate to reach ₹100 crore to ₹150 crore each within two years, while paracetamol and metformin are described as high-volume commodity products.[9] On 24 September 2026 the European regulator granted a second certificate for fenofibrate made by a new process, which widens its sales to regulated European buyers.[37] Paracetamol is the swing factor. The new plant was described as running at 55% in the May 2026 call and again in the August call, while the June-quarter press release says 65%, up from 60% the quarter before. It is guided to 70% to 75% in FY27 and full use in FY28.[9][6][8]

Customer risk is low and export reach is wide. The top ten customers were about 19% of FY26 sales and exports about 24%, spread across Europe, Latin America, Asia and the Middle East.[14] In the June 2026 quarter exports rose to 28.5% of sales.[6] The 2025 aim of 40% exports over the medium term has not yet been met.[11] Management says the buyers are 'all A-grade customers', mostly large generic and over-the-counter makers.[9] What this means for you: the commercial position is better than the financial returns suggest, and the open question is whether better pricing and fuller plants will lift returns to a level that justifies the share price.

Ownership, governance and balance sheet quality

The promoter group (the Gupta family that founded the company, and its companies) owned 62.28% in September 2026, up from 52.62% in September 2025, with none of the shares pledged as loan security in any of the last twelve quarters.[16][17][13] The Trendlyne figures show the promoter share count rose from 154,463,080 to 182,800,000, which is 28,336,920 more shares, about 9.7% of the company, and a filing shows one promoter entity buying on the open market on 17 March 2026.[16][15] Founders buying that much stock, with no pledge, is a positive signal about their view of value.

Foreign investors also arrived: their share rose from 1.72% in March 2026 to 7.26% in September 2026, while public holders fell from 45.10% to 30.16% over the year.[17][16] The flip side is a small free float: only about 37.7% of shares are held outside the promoter group, which can make the price jumpy.[16] The auditors gave an unmodified opinion, contingent liabilities were only ₹5.66 crore and CARE rates the company A+ with strong liquidity.[2][13] Nothing here suggests a governance concern.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management gave specific targets in the May 2025 call for FY26. We compare each with what the FY26 accounts and the later calls show. The number

Our own estimate of IOL's return on capital employed is about 14.6% for FY27 and about 15.9% for FY28, compared with 11.2% for FY26 on the same method. On our base case the 15% floor is crossed during FY28, not now.

How it was calculated

Return on capital is EBIT (profit before tax plus finance cost) divided by the average of opening and closing capital employed (equity plus borrowings plus leases). We built FY27 and FY28 profit from management guidance with a small haircut, then added retained profit to capital.

The inputs

FY26 EBIT ₹210.2 crore and capital employed of 1,798.4 + 132.0 + 3.4 = ₹1,933.8 crore; FY25 capital 1,687.4 + 117.0 = ₹1,804.4 crore.[3] FY27 model: revenue ₹2,700 crore (management target), EBITDA margin 14.0% on total revenue of ₹2,725 crore (₹381.5 crore), depreciation ₹86 crore, finance cost ₹16 crore, tax 25.5%, giving profit before tax ₹279.5 crore and profit after tax ₹208.2 crore.[9][7][3][5] FY28 model: revenue ₹3,105 crore (up 15%), margin 14.5%, depreciation ₹100 crore, finance cost ₹18 crore, giving profit before tax ₹335.9 crore and after tax ₹250.2 crore.[7] Capital employed ends FY27 at ₹2,112 crore (1,933.8 + 208.2 − about 30 dividend) and FY28 at about ₹2,327 crore.[2] The 25.5% tax rate is FY26's 25.3% (46.7 ÷ 184.4) rounded up.[3]

The range

If the margin stays at FY26's 12.4% and revenue grows only about 6% a year, FY28 EBIT would be about ₹230 crore on about ₹2,000 crore of capital, which is about 11.5%, no better than today. If revenue grows 18% a year and the margin reaches 16%, EBIT would be about ₹421 crore on about ₹2,300 crore, which is about 18%. The spread is wide because margin drives almost all of the answer.

If the number is off

If the margin is one point lower than our base (13.0% instead of 14.0% in FY27), FY27 EBIT would fall by about ₹27 crore and the return on capital would be about 13.3%, so the floor would not be crossed in FY28 either. If the margin is one point higher, FY28 would reach about 17%. That is why the 30 October results matter: a second quarter at or above 14% margin keeps the base case alive.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions. This chapter names the weakest of each, says what would show it wrong, and says when we will know. A bear case that is easy to imagine is stronger evidence for caution than one that is hard to imagine, and for IOL the bear case is easy to imagine because the company has lived through it: margins fell for three years until FY25.[3]

Weakest assumption in the earnings estimate: a 14% margin holds

Our base case needs EBITDA margin of 14.0% in FY27 and 14.5% in FY28. The evidence is two quarters (15.2% and 14.6%) after eleven quarters in which Screener's operating margin sat between 8% and 13%.[3][17] Management says the June quarter did not rely on inventory gains and attributes the rise to higher plant utilisation, mix and efficiency.[8] But the same management also said in the May call that 'the uptick in the prices' helped, that finished-goods prices and raw-material prices both rose, and that the spread was 'more or less same'.[9] A war-driven price spike in chemicals is the kind of gain that reverses when the spike fades.

The check is simple. If the second-quarter margin is 13% or lower and both years follow it down (13.0% in FY27, 13.5% in FY28), our FY27E earnings fall to about ₹6.40 and the FY28E base value to about ₹165, below the close. Cutting only the FY28 margin by one point gives ₹164.78, 8.1% below the close. We will know on Friday 30 October 2026, when the board approves the half-year results, and on Monday 2 November at 11:30 IST, when management takes questions.[22][23] What this means for you: a low-margin quarter would not break the business, but it would remove the reason to own the stock at this price.

Weakest assumption in the target: the 21.3 times multiple

The three-year median of 21.3 times comes from a period when earnings were low and growth was slow. If IOL's return on capital reaches 15% or more, the market may pay a peer-like multiple, as it does for Granules (35.1 times) and SMS (43.5 times).[18][29][30] If instead the ibuprofen cycle turns, the multiple could fall below 17 times. The median is a rule from the scoring file, not a law of nature, and we show the spread rather than hide it: at 30 times the base value would be ₹255.7; at 17 times it would be ₹144.9.

The evidence that would show us wrong is a sustained re-rating above 30 times with rising estimates, not just a rising price. Only one analyst covers the stock, and no revision history is published, so we cannot see estimate momentum in the way we can for large companies.[19][20] We will know after the second-quarter results, when analysts may initiate or update coverage.

Weakest assumption in the probabilities: 30/50/20 is a judgment

Weights are subjective. With 25/50/25 the expected value is ₹190.4 and the return from ₹170.83 with dividend is 12.1%, which just meets the hurdle; with 35/50/15 it is ₹168.9 and the return from ₹170.83 is negative. The reward-to-risk verdict does not depend on these weights, because it uses the base target and the stop, but the return-hurdle test does. We say this openly because a reader who disagrees with us on the weights could reach a different answer on the return hurdle and should be able to see why. With 20/50/30 the return from ₹170.83 with dividend is 18.3%, a clear pass. The verdict rests on the reward-to-risk rule, which does not use the weights, so treat the return test as supporting evidence and not as the deciding fact.

The red-team bear case

The strongest case against owning IOL runs as follows. Ibuprofen is a commodity made in India and China at scale, and CARE itself says prices collapsed after COVID.[14] Chinese prices are the lowest in the Expert Market Research tracker, and an analyst in the May call said that another listed ibuprofen maker called prices 'not remunerative'.[26][10] Paracetamol is a commodity too, and the new plant is running at 55% to 65% of capacity.[8] The company plans ₹1,200 crore to ₹1,400 crore of greenfield spending over four to five years, which is about 23% to 27% of today's market value, and earned only 9.7% on its capital in FY26.[9][1] In that world, margin returns to 12.4%, the multiple falls to 17 times and the stock reaches about ₹92, down 49%. That is a 30% chance on our weights, and the single largest risk to a buyer today.
