1. What the company does

Solar Industries India Limited, based in Nagpur, was founded in 1995 and today runs 40 plants and sells in more than 90 countries[25]. Its oldest business is commercial explosives: bulk and packaged explosives, detonators and the electronic and non-electric systems that set them off. Mines, quarries and infrastructure builders buy them to break rock[3][1]. The group says it holds about 24 percent of the Indian explosives market, and Coal India alone supplies 9 to 10 percent of group revenue[3]. A broker put its Indian volume share at about 26 percent in FY25[12].

The second business is defence, which the group entered in fiscal 2010[3]. It makes high-energy materials, propellants, ammunition, rockets, missiles, drones, loitering munitions (small drones that circle and then strike) and counter-drone systems[3]. Defence brought in ₹2,634 crore in FY26, about 27 percent of group sales, up from about 5 percent in fiscal 2021[8][3]. In the June 2026 quarter defence sales were ₹933 crore, 26 percent of the quarter's ₹3,668 crore[23][1]. The third strand is international explosives, which made up about 39 percent of group revenue in FY26 and 37 percent in the June 2026 quarter[3][23].

Who pays, and when, shapes the numbers. Mining customers pay for explosives that are used in the quarter, on ordinary credit terms. Defence customers sign long contracts and often pay in advance. At 31 March 2026 the group held ₹1,359 crore of customer advances on its books, which the accounts call contract liabilities[5]. Advances are part of working capital, which is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills, less what suppliers are owed. They help cash flow when they arrive and hurt it when the work is delivered and the cash is already spent. Chapter 3 shows how that played out in FY25 and FY26.

The raw material that matters most is ammonium nitrate, a fertiliser chemical that is also an explosive. It makes up about 65 percent of raw material cost, and the law treats it as an explosive, so making, selling and storing it needs a licence[3]. Most contracts carry price-escalation clauses that pass cost changes to the customer, and the group makes most other inputs itself[3]. That mix, in-house inputs plus escalation clauses, is why the operating margin — the share of each rupee of sales left after running costs — has held in the high 20s while ammonium nitrate prices moved[3][8].

The order book is the stock of signed work. It stood at ₹21,350 crore on 30 June 2026, including defence orders above ₹18,000 crore[3]. A broker splits that defence figure into about ₹11,000 crore of international orders and a ₹6,000 crore Pinaka rocket order[12]. The total was ₹5,100 crore at the end of FY24[14]. On 14 September 2026 the group also agreed to buy Omnia Holdings of South Africa for about $1.355 billion (₹12,951 crore) in cash, with completion expected in early to mid-2027 and not yet approved[15][3]. Omnia sells mining explosives, fertiliser and chemicals in 23 countries, and management's FY28 view of ₹31,000 to ₹32,000 crore of combined revenue is more than double Solar's own ₹14,000 crore this year[15][7].

How the money is made, in one line

Solar buys chemicals, turns them into explosives or munitions at plants close to its customers, and sells them at a price that moves with its costs. In FY26 it earned about ₹2,750 crore before interest, tax and depreciation, a figure analysts call EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), on sales of ₹9,838 crore[8][1]. Profit for each share, called EPS (earnings per share), was ₹185.38[1]. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying plant, and it was negative in FY26[1]. Return on capital employed, which measures profit earned on the money tied up in the business, was 38.1 percent[1]. Those returns are high for a manufacturer, which is what the later chapters test and price. Chapter 2 turns to how strong that business really is.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Solar's strength is scale in a business that is hard to enter. The explosives trade needs industrial licences and clearances from the Chief Controller of Explosives and mine-safety regulators, and ammonium nitrate itself is licensed[3]. Solar's bulk explosive plants sit 50 to 60 km from major mining regions, and the group makes most of its inputs, which the rating agency credits for stable margins[3]. Its Nagpur complex is described as one of the largest integrated explosives sites in the world[3]. A newcomer would need years and licences to match that.

The second strength is the defence order book, which grew from ₹5,100 crore to ₹21,350 crore in two years[14]. Defence orders above ₹18,000 crore are about 6.8 times the defence revenue of FY26[3][8]. The Pinaka order of ₹6,084 crore came from the Ministry of Defence through the subsidiary Economic Explosives[26], and a first batch of Pinaka rockets was flagged off for Armenia on 18 January 2026[27]. Orders from abroad are large: new export contracts of ₹2,039 crore (December 2024), ₹830 crore (January 2026) and ₹1,076 crore (May 2026) are all on record[30][28][25]. A defence order book this large gives sales cover for several years if the plants can deliver.

The third strength is the balance sheet before the Omnia deal. Borrowings were ₹1,524 crore against equity of ₹6,277 crore at 31 March 2026, so debt-to-equity, which is borrowings divided by shareholders' equity, was 0.24[5][1]. Management says net debt, which is borrowings less cash, will stay below 2 times EBITDA after Omnia[7][3]. Interest cover, how many times profit before interest covers the interest bill, was above 21 times by the rating agency's count[3]. CRISIL rates the debt AA+, one notch below the top[3]. Chapter 3 keeps the reported figures apart from the pro forma debt after Omnia.

The weaknesses are as concrete. Domestic mining demand did not grow in FY26[8], and one customer, Coal India, is 9 to 10 percent of revenue[3]. Defence revenue arrives in lumps: the company said in May 2025 that FY26 defence revenue would pass ₹3,000 crore, and it delivered ₹2,634 crore[10][8]. Cash conversion weakened sharply in FY26, with operating cash flow of ₹621 crore against profit of ₹1,737 crore[5]. And the ownership has a split: a family trust holding 29.23 percent of the company, which received its shares in a March 2026 gift transfer, may have voted against every resolution at the August 2026 annual meeting, on a secondary page's reading of the vote split[22][20].

Competition, margins and what the rating agency says

CRISIL expects Solar's operating margin to stay at 26 to 27 percent over the medium term, and to fall to 22 to 23 percent for the combined company after Omnia, because Omnia earns lower margins[3]. Management itself says BME's margin is 13 to 14 percent against 18 to 19 percent for global explosives and sees room to lift it[7]. A broker puts Omnia's margin at about 11 to 12 percent and expects a rise of 2 to 3 percentage points over a few years[11]. The agency would cut its view if the company took on a major debt-funded acquisition that weakened its financial profile, with total outside liabilities reaching 1.5 to 1.85 times net worth[3].

What this means for an owner: the moat is real and measurable in margins and returns, but the growth from here depends on two things the company has not yet proved: delivering defence orders on time and digesting a purchase that doubles its size. Governance and the family dispute

The Supreme Court disposed of a long-running appeal between the company and Kailash Chandra Nuwal, an early director, on 26 August 2025, after noting that his directorship term from 2016 to 2021 had expired[19]. CRISIL says the dispute has not had a measurable effect on business or results but will keep watching it[3]. The feud still shows in how the shares vote. On 11 August 2026 the promoter group, with 66,191,271 shares, split: 60 percent of the votes in favour and 40 percent against, as read from a secondary page. That is consistent with the 29.23 percent family trust, which holds 40 percent of the promoter group's shares, voting against every item, but the page does not name the voters[20][22]. The special resolution to alter the articles of association failed, and an independent director was approved only under a rule that deems a majority of public holders enough[20]. We score this as a watch item, not a failure, because no court or regulator has found fault with the accounts or the business. It is an ownership dispute that can resurface in votes on funding, such as any later special resolution tied to the Omnia deal.
