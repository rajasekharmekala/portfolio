Solar Industries makes explosives for mines, and rockets, ammunition and drones for armies. The verdict is REJECTED at the 16 October 2026 close of ₹20,011. The gain to our target is only about twice the distance to our exit price, below the minimum of three, and only three of five operating checks pass. The business is strong: profit is growing fast, the order book is about 2.2 years of sales, and debt is low today. The call would be wrong if cash flow recovers and the funding for the ₹12,951 crore Omnia purchase stays inside the debt ceiling management has promised, or if the price falls to about ₹19,000, where the same target and exit price clear the minimum.

1. What the company does

Solar Industries India, based in Nagpur, makes two kinds of products. The first is industrial explosives: bulk and packaged explosives, detonators and the other devices that start a blast. Coal mines, quarries and road and building projects use them to break rock. The second is defence products, a business Solar entered in 2010, which now covers propellants, rockets, ammunition, loitering munitions (small drones that crash into a target) and counter-drone systems.[3][4] In the year to March 2026 the group sold ₹9,838 crore of goods, which is about 27% defence, about 39% international explosives, and the rest, roughly 34%, domestic explosives (our subtraction).[1][4]

The mechanics of the explosives business are simple to describe. A mine signs a supply contract, often for several years, and Solar builds a bulk plant 50 to 60 kilometres from the pit so the product travels a short way.[4] The key raw material is ammonium nitrate, which CRISIL says is 65% of raw material cost.[4] Contracts carry price escalation clauses. The chief executive said in May 2026 that these now adjust monthly, and that in the current price spike customers agreed to 15-day adjustments, so a rise in ammonium nitrate is largely passed on.[17] That protects the percentage margin but means part of the sales growth is price, not volume: for FY27 he expects the explosives business to grow about 35%, with 20% to 22% points coming from price and about 15% from volume.[17]

The defence business sells to the Indian Army and Navy and to foreign buyers. Its anchor is the Pinaka rocket launcher system: in February 2025 the Ministry of Defence signed contracts worth ₹10,147 crore with Economic Explosives Limited (now Solar Defence and Aerospace, a Solar subsidiary) and Munitions India for Pinaka rockets, and Solar's own order from that programme is ₹6,084 crore.[22][3] The chief executive says Pinaka now brings in ₹500 to ₹550 crore a year, and about 70% of FY27 defence revenue is meant for international buyers.[17] Other products are the Nagastra-I loitering munition, 155-millimetre artillery shells, and two items still in trials: the Rudrastra drone and the Bhargavastra anti-drone system.[3][18] The market is large: India's defence production reached a record ₹1.78 lakh crore in FY2025-26.[23]

Two facts describe who pays Solar and how steady the demand is. First, no single customer dominates revenue: Coal India contributed 9% to 10% of consolidated revenue in FY26, and the Coal India and Singareni Collieries order book was ₹3,500 crore at March 2026.[4][3] Second, the order book has shifted to defence. At 30 June 2026 the order book was ₹21,350 crore, of which defence orders were above ₹18,000 crore, at least 84%.[4] Domestic mining demand is cyclical and was flat in FY26 by the company's own words, while defence orders arrive in lumps and are delivered over four to ten years.[18][19] So the order book gives visibility, but it is concentrated in one buyer type: governments.

On 14 September 2026 Solar signed a definitive agreement to buy 100% of Omnia Holdings, a South African mining-and-agriculture chemicals group, for about US$1.355 billion (₹12,951 crore) in cash. Completion is expected in early to mid-2027.[10] Omnia had FY26 revenue of about US$1.41 billion, up from US$1.18 billion in FY24, and sells to more than 40 countries, but 54% of its revenue comes from agriculture, a business Solar has never run.[10][33][14] A much smaller deal followed on 15 October 2026: a Solar subsidiary agreed to buy Sivasakthi Industrial Explosives for ₹115.02 crore in cash, and the target had no turnover in FY26.[27] This purchase changes the size, mix and leverage of the company, which is why it sits at the centre of the underwriting in later chapters.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • order book is the total value of signed contracts that have not yet been delivered and turned into sales.
  • relative strength index (RSI) is a meter from 0 to 100 that shows how fast a price has been moving. Above 70 is often called overheated and below 30 oversold.
  • average true range (ATR) is the typical size of one day's price swing, in rupees. We use it to measure how far a price sits from support.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is operating profit before those four items.
  • return on capital employed (ROCE) is the yearly profit before interest and tax as a share of the capital the business uses. It tells you how well each rupee of capital is put to work.
  • earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit divided by its number of shares.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and paying for plants and equipment.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business brings in from its own work, before buying assets and before financing.
  • working capital is the money tied up in day-to-day running: stock, bills customers have not yet paid, and money owed to suppliers.
  • guidance is a company's own forecast for the coming period.
  • consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow a stock.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by the profit per share. It shows how many rupees you pay for each rupee of yearly profit.
  • book-to-bill is new orders divided by sales in the same period. A figure above 1 means the order book is growing.
  • operating margin is the share of each rupee of sales left after the running costs of the business.
  • debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Zero means the company has no borrowings.
  • net debt is borrowings minus the cash the company holds. A negative figure means more cash than debt.
  • capital expenditure (capex) is spending on plants, machines and software that last for years.
  • dilution is the fall in each existing shareholder's slice of a company when it issues new shares.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing prices over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. They are slowly moving lines that a price often bounces from. In the text we call them the 20-day, 50-day and 200-day averages.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the four-stage chart scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • base is a sideways stretch of trading that a stock builds before a larger move. (In this report "base case" and "base target" mean the central scenario, not this chart pattern.)
  • pivot is the top of a base, the level a price must clear to start a new leg up.
  • breakout is a move that takes the price above a pivot on rising volume.
  • stop loss is the price at which we admit the idea was wrong and sell. We write it as the stop.
  • limit order is an order to buy only at a chosen price or lower, so it never chases the market.
  • entry zone is the band of prices where we would be willing to buy.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain we expect divided by the loss we would take. The formula is (target − price) ÷ (price − stop).
  • measured move is a price target found by adding the height of a base to the level where the price breaks out of it.
  • PEG ratio (price-to-earnings-to-growth) is the P/E divided by the yearly profit growth rate. A figure above 2 means the price is more than twice the growth rate.
  • Verdict labels: REJECTED means our rules say do not buy at today's price; WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT means a sound company to buy only on a pullback to the entry zone; ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP means add on weakness inside the entry zone.
  • Conviction tiers (TIER_0 to TIER_4) set position size: TIER_0 is no position, TIER_2 is a medium position with a 75 basis-point risk budget (0.75% of net worth lost if the stop is hit) and a 6.5% cap.

  • Ammonium nitrate: the fertiliser-grade chemical that makes up most of an explosive's cost; in India it is regulated as an explosive.
  • Price escalation clause: a contract term that moves the selling price when a raw material price moves.
  • Loitering munition: a small drone that circles over an area and then dives into its target.
  • Standalone and consolidated: standalone figures cover the parent company alone; consolidated figures add all subsidiaries.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Solar's strongest advantage is scale plus location. CRISIL puts its share of the domestic explosives industry at around 24%, it has 32 manufacturing sites in India, and its bulk plants sit within 50 to 60 kilometres of the main mining regions.[4] It also makes most of its own raw materials except ammonium nitrate, including detonator components, emulsifiers and sodium nitrate.[4] Because explosives are licensed and regulated, a new entrant needs permits as well as plants, so entry is slow. The chief executive also says the group is the world's largest maker of packaged explosives; that is a company claim, which we did not verify.[9]

The evidence for the advantage is in the returns. Return on capital employed was 38% in both FY25 and FY26, against 25% in FY22.[1] The operating margin, which is operating profit divided by sales, rose from 19% in FY22 to 28% in FY26 and was 27.7% in the June 2026 quarter.[1] CRISIL attributes the improvement to the rising share of defence and export business and to the ability to pass on raw material swings, and expects the margin to hold at 26% to 27% over the medium term.[4] Net worth, the shareholders' money in the business, grew from ₹1,914 crore to ₹6,277 crore over the same four years (equity capital plus reserves).[1]

The weak points are specific. Standalone sales grew 19.4% in Q1 FY27 while consolidated sales grew 70%, so the headline growth comes from subsidiaries rather than the original domestic business.[8] Domestic mining volume did not grow in FY26 by management's own account.[18] Ammonium nitrate is volatile and ICRA lists it as the main rating constraint, along with exchange-rate risk and licence rules.[3] Pass-through is also not complete: on the Q4 FY26 call, as summarised by Perivis, management described cover of about 60% of a cost increase on some contracts, with a lag of up to a quarter.[18]

Governance needs a plain statement. The promoters hold 73.15% of the shares, with Manish Nuwal personally holding 38.93% and a family trust holding 29.23%.[1][2] Promoters had pledged 2.27% of their holding at June 2026, up from none in September 2023; that is far below the 20% red line, but the trend is up.[2] ICRA and CRISIL both note a long-running dispute with a former director, K. C. Nuwal. The Supreme Court disposed of the company's appeal on 26 August 2025 and the matter stays pending before the National Company Law Tribunal. ICRA says management expects no material effect and calls the outcome a rating monitorable, and CRISIL says the dispute has had no measurable adverse effect so far.[3][4] A new rival may also appear: an analyst asked on the May 2026 call about the Kalyani Group entering explosives, and management declined to comment on competition.[18]

Omnia changes the competitive picture and the margin picture at the same time. Its own operating margin was 9.0% in FY26, and the chief executive puts the margin of its mining arm, BME, at 13% to 14%, against 26% to 28% for Solar.[16][9] CRISIL expects the operating margin of the combined group to fall to 22% to 23% after the purchase.[4] The offer price was a 70.69% premium to Omnia's share price at the end of 2025 and about 16 times its FY26 earnings, according to Jefferies.[12][14] Jefferies also expects defence to fall from a planned 35% to 40% of revenue to 22% to 25% by FY30, because the new business is large.[14]

What this means for an owner

For an owner the position is clear: the core franchise is high quality and its returns are proven, but the part of the story that earned the high price, defence, is becoming a smaller share of a bigger and lower-margin group. That is a reason to value the business on cash and leverage, which Chapter 2B tests, and not only on growth. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Over four years Solar's sales grew from ₹3,948 crore in FY22 to ₹9,838 crore in FY26, a pace of 25.6% a year, and net profit grew from ₹455 crore to ₹1,737 crore, or 39.8% a year (our compound-growth arithmetic from the table below).[1] The three-year pace is slower, 12.5% a year for sales and 28.9% for profit from FY23, because FY23 sales of ₹6,918 crore were a high starting point that fell 12% in FY24 before the climb resumed.[1] Trailing twelve-month sales to June 2026 are ₹11,351 crore, up 42%, and trailing profit is ₹2,050 crore, up 58%.[1]

Our variant perception

Our variant perception is where we say what we think differs from what the price assumes, and why. What the market assumes is that Solar keeps growing profit near 27% a year for ten years. At that pace trailing profit of about ₹1,991 crore would reach roughly ₹21,500 crore a year by FY36 (1,991 × 1.269 raised to the tenth power). What the evidence supports is two good years, not ten. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could fail. This chapter names the weakest one under each estimate, then argues against our own verdict as hard as the evidence allows, and ends with the single claim on which the case turns and the date we will learn the answer.

The best case against our own verdict (a red-team case)

Here we argue against our own REJECTED verdict as hard as the evidence allows, so you can see the best case for buying. The first flaw is the stop. The Core rule places it under the lowest of the swing low and the 50-day and 200-day averages, minus one ATR, which is ₹16,017.48, 20% below the price. A stop one ATR below the 20-day swing low, as a Tactical-style rule might place it, would be ₹17,969.69, and the same target would give 3.96. The rule is applied as written, but a wide stop is a property of a rising 200-day average, not of the business, and it can make a good stock look unattractive.[24]

The second flaw is the anchor and the street. Consensus profit may be too low: the June quarter alone annualises to ₹288.44 against ₹281.58. Management projects 11% to 15% profit-per-share accretion from Omnia by FY28, and Goldman Sachs holds a ₹26,550 target.[13][15] Jefferies expects 4% to 6% dilution in FY28 and FY29 on its own assumptions, yet still holds ₹28,160, noting that the price is about 16 times Omnia's FY26 earnings for a net-cash business.[14][16] If they are right, the leverage fear that took the price from ₹22,290 to ₹18,480 was overdone.[11][14]

The third flaw is quality. Return on capital employed of 38%, interest cover near 19 times, 24% of the domestic market and defence orders from governments are not features of a failing business. If working-capital days fall back by the second quarter and the Omnia funding is mostly paid by Omnia's own cash and cheap rand debt, Layer 2 returns to 4 of 5 within weeks. Our REJECTED label then looks like an expensive way to miss a rally. For you, the point is that the verdict depends on the stop rule and on two unproven items, not on a fault in the business.

Three ways the verdict could cost us

These are three ways acting on REJECTED could leave you worse off than buying today. Scenario one: the stock re-rates. If the market holds a 90-times multiple on FY28 profit, the price in October 2027 would be about ₹31,500 and a buyer on 16 October gains 57%. Scenario two: the pullback never comes. The stock drifts up from its 50-day average to ₹22,700 without touching ₹19,000, and the entry zone is never offered. Scenario three: the cash and leverage tests pass in November. Layer 2 moves to 4 of 5, the verdict becomes WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT (our label for a sound company to buy only on a pullback to the entry zone) or better, and the wait has cost the move from today's price to wherever the stock stands then. For you, this is the price of caution: a wait that ends well still costs something.

For now the reader's decision is to wait for a better price or better cash evidence, whichever arrives first.
