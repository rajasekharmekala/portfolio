1. What the company does

EPACK Prefab Technologies Limited is an Indian maker of pre-engineered steel buildings. Think of a warehouse, a factory shed or a solar-cell plant. Instead of building every part on site, EPACK designs the building on computers, cuts and welds the steel frame in its own factories, and ships numbered parts to the customer's land. A site crew then bolts the pieces together. The company calls this the prefab business, and it supplied more than 90% of revenue in the year to March 2026.[3] The company was founded in 1999, listed on the NSE and BSE on 1 October 2025, and is run by members of the Singhania and Bothra families.[3][6]

Prefab has three product lines. The first is the steel building frame itself. The second is insulated sandwich panels: two metal sheets with insulating foam or rock wool between them, used for walls, roofs, cold stores and data centres. The third is modular and light-gauge steel housing, such as site offices and worker camps.[7][4] Management says the three lines are sold together in one project and are not reported as separate revenue lines.[4] The smaller business, about 9% of revenue, makes foam packaging blocks and shapes from expanded polystyrene. LG is a named customer of that division.[3][5] Some descriptions of the group also mention electronics manufacturing services (EMS). The filings fetched show no EMS segment, and ICRA places the promoters' electronics and consumer-durables interests in separate group companies, so this report does not analyse one.[3]

Customers pay in stages, and the stages explain the cash flow. The CFO described the standard terms as 20% advance, 10% when the general-arrangement drawing is approved, and about 60% before the steel leaves the factory.[5] Contracts are mostly fixed price.[4] That protects the customer but exposes EPACK when steel gets dearer between signing and delivery. Management says it manages this by booking new orders every week at the steel price of that week, and it quotes the steel price rise since the Middle East war began as 12% to 14%, with only about 1% hitting its profit.[4] The first-quarter margin of 9.4%, against 10.5% a year earlier, shows the protection is partial.[10]

The company runs three factory locations: Greater Noida in Uttar Pradesh, Ghiloth in Rajasthan, and Mambattu in Andhra Pradesh, with design offices in Greater Noida, Hyderabad and Vizag. At 30 June 2026 it could fabricate 147,122 tonnes of steel structures a year, 1.31 million square metres of sandwich panels a year and 8,400 tonnes of foam packaging a year.[7] It was using 75.15% of its steel capacity and 44.25% of its panel capacity in the June quarter.[7] Management is adding about 73,000 more tonnes of steel capacity, mostly in a new Gujarat plant, and a new continuous panel line at Ghiloth, which is meant to lift totals to roughly 220,000 tonnes and 2.11 million square metres by March 2027.[5]

How the company makes money, and where it can lose it

Revenue is booked as a project progresses. A typical job runs six to nine months from order to handover, and management says the whole opening order book has to be completed within the year.[5] That makes revenue easier to forecast than it is for most manufacturers, because the order book is a signed list rather than a hope. The average pending order is about ₹12 crore to ₹13 crore, up from ₹6.5 crore last year, because of one ₹165 crore order for a solar-cell and module plant.[4][25] A large order lifts the headline, but it also concentrates risk: one delayed customer site can hold back a quarter.

Profit comes from the gap between the price agreed with the customer and the cost of steel, labour and freight. Steel is the largest cost. In the year to March 2026 materials cost ₹1,049 crore on revenue of ₹1,525 crore, and employee costs were ₹150 crore.[13] Management guides to an EBITDA margin of 10.5% to 11.5%. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization; here it is the profit from running the factories and sites before financing and accounting charges.[4] The stock market focuses on this margin, because a 1-point change on ₹1,850 crore of revenue is about ₹18.5 crore of profit before tax, or about 15% of last year's ₹122.5 crore profit before tax.

Two further features shape the economics. First, the business is asset-light for what it does: management says every ₹100 crore spent on a new plant yields about ₹500 crore to ₹600 crore of revenue at full use.[5] Second, customers rarely stay for life. The company says about 40% to 45% of its order book comes from repeat customers, and that it wins roughly 20% of the jobs it bids for.[4] That win rate is above the 10% to 12% management says is typical, but it also means four in five bids go to rivals.[4]

Who buys, and what is changing in the order mix

ICRA, the credit-rating agency, lists Waaree Renewables, Reliance Industries, the Aditya Birla Group, Adani Ports, Royal Enfield, Centuryply and Larsen & Toubro among customers.[3] By sector, management put renewable energy at 25% to 28% of the order book in January 2026, electronics, semiconductors and electrical at about 18%, and logistics and warehousing at 22% to 25%.[6][4] Data centres are about 4% to 5% of the book today, mostly insulated panels, and the company wants a first complete data-centre building order in the current year.[4] It lost two data-centre bids in the June quarter on design and price, which management said reflected its inexperience in designing and pricing such buildings.[4]

This mix matters because each sector spends on a different cycle. Solar-factory building runs on government incentive schemes and on what the customers can raise. Warehousing follows consumption. Auto and cement follow the broad economy. A company whose orders come from many sectors is less likely to have all of them stop at once, but the largest-order dependence this year is on renewable energy, so a slowdown in solar manufacturing would show up first. Chapter 6 comes back to this risk.

The regional mix has also moved. South India was 43% of prefab revenue in the June quarter, while West India was 32% of it in FY2025, 17% in FY2026 and 12% in the June 2026 quarter.[7][5] Management says it subsidises freight to serve the west from northern plants, and that the new Gujarat plant near Vithalapur is meant to fix that.[5] The plant is a bet that the west will grow, and it is also a cost-saving move.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive case rests on three things: speed, scale among organised players, and a clean balance sheet. On speed, management says prefab is at least 60% faster than conventional concrete construction, and cites a record of 151,000 square feet built in six days.[5] Speed matters most to customers whose factory earns nothing until it opens, which is why solar-cell and module makers keep returning. In January 2026 the CEO said one repeat solar customer had just given a price about 2% to 2.5% higher than earlier orders, which is the clearest sign in the filings that speed is worth money to some buyers.[6]

On scale, EPACK has about 5% to 6% of the Indian PEB market in FY2026 on management's estimate, and aims for 7% to 7.5% by March 2027, while the market leader has about 12%.[4] ICICI Securities calls it the third-largest player with about 147,000 tonnes of capacity.[16] Screener's profile of the number-two player, Interarch, shows 221,000 tonnes of installed capacity.[29] The industry itself is small relative to construction: PEB is 3% to 5% of overall construction by management's account, and CRISIL, via the company's presentation, sizes the Indian PEB industry at ₹210 billion in FY2025, rising to ₹330 billion to ₹345 billion by FY2030.[4][7] A rise from 210 to about 338 is a compound growth rate of about 10% a year, which is the market tailwind before any share gain.

The weakness is that steel buildings are close to a commodity. The company's own director said the product is the same steel bought from the same mills as anyone else, and that the edge is in design and installation service.[5] ICRA agrees that the segment is tender-driven and competitive, with limited pricing power, and that margins are exposed to steel prices.[3] The company's 20% win rate and the 9.4% June-quarter margin both fit that picture. The business is good and growing, but it is not a pricing-power franchise, and the report treats it that way.

The financial strength is real. ICRA rates the company [ICRA]A+ (Stable) for long-term debt and [ICRA]A1 for short-term, reaffirmed on 21 July 2026, and says total debt was 0.7 times operating profit at March 2026 against 1.8 times a year earlier.[3] The company reports net cash of about ₹103 crore at June 2026.[10] Promoters hold 64.96% of the shares and have pledged none of them.[14] Cash is the reason the company can build capacity without borrowing, and it is why a bad quarter would hurt the share price before it hurt solvency.

Where the moat is thin, and where it is real

A moat is whatever lets a company keep earning more than its cost of capital after rivals arrive. For EPACK the real parts are execution record, factory spread and the habit of repeat customers. Between 40% and 45% of the order book is from repeat buyers, and the company now has factories in the north, south and soon the west, which cuts freight and lead time.[4][5] A rival must build the same footprint and win the same trust, and that takes years.

The thin parts are price and product. Management itself says the product has no premium, and that insulated panels bring priority in bids rather than a higher price.[5] The sandwich-panel line at Mambattu ran at about 25% in FY2026, which management called a miss. It rebuilt the sales team after that, and utilisation reached 44.25% in the June quarter.[5][7] Whether that improvement holds is one of the five operating tests scored in Chapter 2B.

There is also a people question. Filings show the resignation of a General Manager for Sales on 15 December 2025, the company secretary in January 2026, the Vice President for Operations on 30 May 2026 and the Vice President for Sales and Business Development on 14 July 2026, and a further company secretary resignation effective 14 October 2026 appears on Screener's announcement list.[23][24][22][21][17] Management says it hired an experienced leader from the steel-building industry to strengthen the team.[10] Turnover in sales roles in a business that wins on relationships is worth watching, and Chapter 2B treats it as part of the walk-the-talk audit.

What would show the business is weaker than it looks

The first warning sign would be an order book that stops growing faster than revenue. At 30 June 2026 the book stood at ₹1,376.4 crore, up 40.4% from a year earlier, while Q1 revenue rose 23.9%.[7] If the book fell below roughly six months of revenue, the growth story would be at risk. The second would be falling utilisation as new capacity arrives: PEB utilisation was 75.15% on 147,122 tonnes, and the plan adds about 50% more tonnage within nine months.[7][5] If orders do not grow with the capacity, utilisation, and then margin, would fall.

The third would be cash quality. The company's cash from operations was ₹135.7 crore in FY2026, but trade payables to suppliers rose by about ₹174.5 crore in the same year, and customer receivables and inventory rose by ₹103.4 crore and ₹114.3 crore.[13] Suppliers are paid on letters of credit that run about 120 days, so the company is partly funded by its vendors.[5] That is normal in this industry, but it means a squeeze on supplier credit would hit cash first. ICRA also flagged that receivables older than six months have grown.[3] Chapter 3 and Chapter 6 return to this point.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management gave specific promises on the January 2026 call, the May 2026 call and in the May and August presentations. The test is simple: did the result match the words? The score card is mixed. The company hit its revenue target for FY26 and its balance-sheet promises tied to the listing, and it booked a record quarter of orders in the June quarter, as it had said it would. It missed on margin, on staff costs as a share of sales, and slightly on the timing of its first new factory line. The misses are small in rupees but they cluster in one area, cost control, which is exactly where a fixed-price contractor is most exposed.[6][5][4]

Candour is a point in management's favour. In May 2026 the CEO volunteered that the Mambattu panel line had run at only about 25% and that the sales engine had been rebuilt.[5] The May call said only that the first quarter might see a little impact; in August he said the company had estimated a cut of about 200 basis points, and the actual cut was about 100.[5][4] A team that admits a miss and then beats its own warning is more believable than one that does not. The counterweight is that on 22 January 2026, with steel prices already rising, the CEO said he did not foresee any impact on the operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of revenue, and the next two quarters showed one.[6]

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on the future, and the bet can be read backwards. If you know what the company earns today and what rate of return investors demand, you can solve for the growth rate the price already assumes. That is a reverse discounted-cash-flow calculation: the framework uses a 12% yearly return for India, a ten-year horizon, and a final-year value of 20 times that year's earnings. If the growth it solves for is low, the market expects little and good news can lift the price. If it is high, the price leaves no room for a miss. Management's own published forecast, called guidance, is one input to that bet.[1]

The standard version uses free cash flow, which is the cash left after running the business and buying plant. For EPACK that cash is negative: Screener shows -₹30 crore for FY25 and -₹11 crore for FY26.[17] A calculation cannot start from a negative number. So the calculation below starts from net profit instead, and it is labelled a profit proxy. It flatters the company, because profit overstates what an owner can take out while the company is still investing heavily. The result is a ceiling on how much comfort to take from a low growth figure, not a clean valuation.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption, and some assumptions carry more weight than others. This chapter takes each main estimate in turn, names the assumption most likely to be wrong, says what evidence would show it, and says when that evidence will arrive. It also sets out the strongest honest case against the report's own conclusion, built as if by someone paid to argue the opposite.

The order matters. Margin is first because it moves the most rupees. The multiple is second because it moves the target and, through the target, the add rule. The bear case comes third because it collects the risks that sit outside the two numbers. The last part is a calendar, because an estimate that cannot be tested soon is not much use to someone deciding whether to hold.

The earnings estimate: the margin recovery is the weakest assumption

The central estimate assumes the EBITDA margin averages 10.0% in FY27 and reaches 10.5% in FY28. That rests on one claim, that steel-driven cost pressure is temporary and that repriced orders will bring the margin back to the guided range from the September quarter. The evidence for it is real: the CEO said on 3 August that the old-rate orders had either been executed or repriced, and that new orders carry the new prices.[4] The evidence against it is also real. The margin has fallen in each of the last three quarters, from 10.1% to 9.8% to 9.4%, the company's contracts are mostly fixed price, and in January the same CEO saw no impact from rising steel.[11][10][4][6]

The size of the error is easy to state. Each point of FY28 margin is about ₹22 crore of pre-tax profit and about ₹1.64 of earnings per share, so a margin of 9.6% instead of 10.5% gives FY28 earnings per share of about ₹12.01 and a target near ₹240 at 20 times. Evidence that the assumption is wrong would be a September-quarter EBITDA margin below 9.5%, which would also turn the margin KPI from WATCH to FAIL. Evidence that it is right would be a September-quarter margin of 10.5% or more. Both come from the next results filing.

The target: the 20 times multiple is a judgement, not a measurement

The target price of ₹270 multiplies FY28 earnings per share by 20. The scoring framework prefers a three-year median multiple, but the shares have traded only since 1 October 2025, so no three-year median exists. The 20 times is therefore a judgement. It is the framework's final multiple for India, it matches the 20 times ICICI Securities applies to later earnings, and it falls between Interarch at 18.3 times and EPACK's own 24.1 times trailing multiple. Pennar, a more diversified peer, prints 14.4 times.[1][16][29][30]

The sensitivity is steep. At 18 times the target drops to ₹243 and the reward-to-risk at ₹220.1 falls from 2.73 to 1.24, which is below the floor of 2.0. A 9.2% margin at 20 times gives a target near ₹227 and a ratio of 0.39. Neither is an observed price; each is one assumption changed at a time. At 22 times the target is ₹297. The ratio reaches 2.0 at ₹220.1 only if the target is at least ₹256.66, which is about 19.0 times FY28 earnings. The group multiple looks under pressure, not just EPACK's: the stock is 34% below its 52-week high, and Screener's one-year price changes (page prints dated 23 October) are -27% for Interarch and -41% for Pennar.[1][29][30] The test of this assumption is the recalculation after the next results, which is also when rules T2 and T4 can act.

The red-team bear case: the strongest argument against holding

The bear case starts with the business model. A fixed-price contractor that buys steel on credit is paid for taking steel-price risk, but it is also the first to be squeezed when that risk goes against it. The margin has already shown that. The company's cash flow depends on suppliers: payables rose ₹174.5 crore in FY26 on letters of credit of about 120 days, and without that rise cash from operations would have been negative.[13][5] If banks tighten letter-of-credit limits or suppliers shorten terms, the company would have to fund the gap from its own cash. A one-month cut in supplier terms alone would absorb about ₹87 crore, one twelfth of the ₹1,049 crore annual materials bill, which is close to the whole of the ₹103 crore of net cash at June 2026, a figure already down by almost half in one quarter.[13][10][11]

Second, the company is increasing steel capacity by about 50% in nine months while its own panel line, built in the last two years, ran at 25% in FY26 and 44% now.[5][7] The Gujarat plant is a ₹100 crore to ₹110 crore bet on a western market where the company's share of revenue fell from 32% to 12%.[5][7] Third, concentration is rising, not falling: one ₹165 crore order is about 12% of the entire order book, and renewable energy is the largest sector at 25% to 28%.[25][7][6] A pause in solar-factory building would hit the book quickly. Fourth, the new data-centre subsidiary has an approved investment of up to ₹75 crore and no funding plan, after two lost bids.[20][4] Fifth, five senior and secretarial resignations in ten months, including two sales leaders, sit alongside a plan to grow sales by 30%.[23][24][22][21][17] Sixth, ownership: promoters hold 64.96%, and a foreign private-equity holder whose name is cut off in the filing as 'South Asia Growth Fu' holds 10.23%, against the 12.9% ICRA reported for GEF Capital after the listing, so a further sale is possible.[14][3] The filing shows 27.44% of shares locked in, but the lock-in end date is not in the filings fetched.[14]

Finally, the price action. The stock fell 15% in two weeks with no filing that explains it, on volume that was quiet on the last day.[1] A holder can read that as noise or as someone knowing something. The honest answer is that the filings do not say. Weighing the bear case, I put its probability at one in four and its value at about ₹130, near the 52-week low of ₹132.17. The balance sheet limits the damage, because net cash and an A+ rating mean the business is not at risk, but the price could fall a long way without the business failing.[3][1]
