1. What the company does

EPACK Prefab Technologies designs steel buildings, makes the parts in its own factories, and then puts them up on the customer's site. The core product is the pre-engineered steel building, shortened to PEB. Instead of pouring concrete on site, the company cuts and welds the frame, roof and wall sheets in a factory from a computer design, trucks the pieces out and bolts them together. Think of a very large flat-pack wardrobe: the thinking and the cutting happen indoors, and the site work is assembly. Management says this is at least 60 percent faster than conventional concrete construction, and speed is what management says its customers buy.[19] The company was incorporated in 1999 as Epack Polymers, listed on the NSE and BSE on 1 October 2025 after raising about ₹300 crore of new money in its offer,[20][22] and is led by Sanjay Singhania as managing director and chief executive. The promoter families, Singhania and Bothra, hold 64.95 percent of the shares, and none of those shares is pledged to lenders.[22][24]

Beyond the steel frame, the company makes sandwich insulated panels: two thin steel sheets with an insulating core, used for the walls of cold stores, data halls, clean rooms and building facades. It also sells modular buildings such as site offices and housing, light-gauge steel frames, metal doors and aluminium windows.[9] A second, older business moulds expanded polystyrene, the white foam known as thermocol, into protective packaging, mainly for consumer-durable makers such as LG.[22] The first business is called Prefab in the company's reports and the second Packaging here. Prefab earned ₹1,382 crore of the ₹1,525 crore in revenue for the year to March 2026, which is 90.6 percent, and Packaging earned ₹143 crore.[4]

How an order turns into revenue

Almost every Prefab contract is signed at a fixed price and for a fixed delivery time. The customer places an order; EPACK's team of more than 100 engineers in three design centres draws the building. The company buys steel against that order, makes the parts, ships them and erects the building, usually inside six to eight months.[20][17] That cycle explains why the company talks about its order book: the order book is the value of signed work that has not yet been billed, so it shows how much future revenue is already booked. On 30 June 2026 the pending Prefab order book was ₹1,376 crore, which management says is about eight months of work.[9][17]

Steel is the largest cost. Cost of materials was ₹1,049 crore on revenue of ₹1,525 crore in FY26, or 68.8 percent of every rupee of sales.[15] A simple illustration shows why that matters. If steel rises 12 percent and the company could pass none of it on, the cost would rise by about 8 percentage points of revenue (12 percent of 68.8 percent), which would wipe out about four-fifths of the 10.5 percent operating margin. Management says steel rose 12 to 14 percent in the four to five months after the Middle East war began, yet the hit to the company was only about 1 percent.[18] The difference comes from three habits: it signs new orders almost every week at the steel price of that week, it orders steel only after an order is confirmed, and it asked customers to pay more on older contracts and succeeded on most of them.[18][19] ICRA, the credit rating agency, adds that the protection is partial, because a gap between the day steel is bought and the day the price can be reset always remains.[22]

Unit economics: what a factory line earns

A plant is the unit that matters. A new continuous sandwich-panel line costs roughly ₹70 crore at Mambattu in Andhra Pradesh, where the land was already prepared, and about ₹90 crore at the new Ghiloth site in Rajasthan once land and building are included.[19] Management's rule of thumb is ₹1,700 to ₹2,000 of selling price per square metre, which it translates into ₹125 to ₹140 crore of panel sales from one line, or about ₹250 crore if matching steel-fabrication capacity is added beside it.[18] Those are management estimates for a line running well, not audited results. The first Mambattu line ran at only about 25 percent in FY26, which management itself calls a miss, and at 44.25 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[19][10] A line earns its keep only when it is busy, so utilisation, not capacity, is the number to watch.

For the steel business, the company says it earns a margin per tonne that is about the same whatever the customer's industry, because every segment has several bidders.[18] The group's operating margin, which is operating profit before interest, depreciation and tax as a share of sales, was 10.5 percent in FY26 (EBITDA of ₹160 crore) and 9.4 percent in the June 2026 quarter. EBITDA here means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, and it is the company's own headline profit measure.[9] Fixed assets turned over 4.26 times in FY26, meaning each rupee of factory and equipment supported about ₹4.26 of sales, and ROCE (return on capital employed, the pre-tax profit earned on the money tied up in the business) was 19.4 percent by the company's own measure.[4] Spending on new plant, called capital expenditure in accounts, is planned at about ₹150 crore in FY27, funded from cash on hand and internal accruals, according to ICRA.[14][23]

Customers, concentration and the order book

Who pays? Management says logistics and warehousing form 22 to 25 percent of the order book, automobile makers about 10 percent, and data centres 4 to 5 percent, mostly insulated panels. Energy is the fastest-growing group and includes solar, wind, transformers and cables.[18] ICRA names Waree Renewables (the solar maker Waaree), Reliance Industries, Aditya Birla Group, Adani Ports, Royal Enfield, Centuryply and Larsen & Toubro among customers.[22] In the June quarter the company booked ₹578 crore of new orders, up 146.4 percent on ₹235 crore a year earlier (management rounds these to ₹580 crore and ₹240 crore on the call), including its largest single order ever, ₹165 crore from a solar-cell and module maker.[10][17] That one order is about 12 percent of the ₹1,376 crore order book, and the company is the sole supplier on it.[18]

Concentration is a real feature. About 40 to 45 percent of the order book comes from repeat customers, the rest from new ones, and the average pending order has risen to ₹12 to ₹13 crore from ₹6.5 crore in FY26.[18] The company's own risk note in the annual report says its receivables are owed by few customers, which is a concentration of credit risk.[6] The company also says its win rate on bids is close to 20 percent, which it calls high because it chooses which quotations to work hard on. Management puts typical rivals at 10 to 12 percent.[18] Four of every five bids are lost, so new orders depend on a steady flow of enquiries, which is why the departure of the vice president of sales in July 2026 is noted in Chapter 6.[25]

Seasonality and the business cycle

Work on a building site stops in heavy rain, and customers cannot take delivery until their civil work is ready. Management says the company has done about 47 percent of a year's revenue in the first half and 53 percent in the second half, and it asks readers to compare each quarter with the same quarter a year ago, not with the one before.[17][20] The quarterly sales figures show why: ₹434 crore in September 2025, ₹325 crore in December 2025 and ₹471 crore in March 2026.[28] A single weak quarter is therefore normal, and a margin that falls for three quarters in a row is the thing to examine.

Over several years demand follows private-sector spending on new plant, which swings with interest rates, steel prices and confidence. Sales dipped from ₹245 crore to ₹241 crore in FY21, the pandemic year, before growing every year since, to ₹1,525 crore in FY26.[28] The company says the Indian PEB industry grew about 8.3 percent a year from FY19 to FY26 while its own Prefab revenue grew 34.2 percent a year from FY22 to FY26, so the gap is share gain and new customers, not just a rising market.[15][16] CRISIL, a research agency whose figures the company quotes, sizes the industry at ₹210 billion in FY25 and ₹330 to ₹345 billion by FY30.[16] Because the company is growing far faster than its market, a slowdown in industrial spending would show up here first as fewer new orders, and the order book is the early warning.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

EPACK is a good operator in a business that has little pricing power. That sentence carries the whole chapter. The evidence for "good operator" is that sales grew from ₹450 crore in FY22 to ₹1,525 crore in FY26, a compound rate of about 36 percent a year, profit after tax rose from ₹20 crore to ₹93 crore, and the credit rating moved from [ICRA]A in August 2025 to [ICRA]A+ in October 2025 and was reaffirmed in July 2026.[28][23] The evidence for "little pricing power" is just as concrete: ICRA says the Prefab segment is tender-driven and competitive, which limits pricing flexibility, and the chief executive says the industry has three or four large players and that margins cannot be commanded because of competition.[22][19] A reader should hold both facts together.

What the company has that rivals find hard to match is speed and reach. It accepted a ₹165 crore solar-plant order that the customer wanted finished in about four and a half months, against the normal six to eight, although the customer's own civil work has since delayed the start.[18] It has steel-building plants at Greater Noida and Ghiloth in the north and at Mambattu in the south, and a fourth in Gujarat is due to start production from April 2027, which lets it quote near more sites. Management says capacity added in the south was taken up so fast that it had to expand again within 18 months, and it expects the west to repeat that.[19][18][13] It sells steel structure and insulated panel together, which spares a customer from managing two vendors. Management says this does not earn a higher price, but wins preference when both are needed.[19] On installed capacity the company is the third-largest PEB maker with about 5 percent of the Indian market, according to ICICI Securities, and management puts its FY26 share at 5 to 6 percent against a leader at about 12 percent.[26][18]

Against that, four facts limit how strong the position is. First, the win rate is close to 20 percent, so most bids go elsewhere.[18] Second, Prefab, which is 90.6 percent of revenue, is mostly fixed-price work whose largest cost is steel, so the company, not the customer, carries a steel price shock until contracts are reset.[18][4] Third, the newest and most-promoted capacity, the sandwich-panel line at Mambattu, ran at about 25 percent in FY26, and two data-centre bids were lost on design and price.[19][18] Fourth, receivables that are more than six months past due rose to 31.6 percent of the total at March 2026 from 26.0 percent a year earlier, which ICRA calls monitorable (detail in Chapter 2B).[6][22]

How the competitive position compares with the nearest listed peer

The cleanest comparison is Interarch Building Solutions, the second-largest PEB company by installed capacity, with 221,000 tonnes a year.[29] In FY26 Interarch had sales of ₹1,898 crore, operating margin of about 10 percent and net profit of ₹135 crore, against EPACK's ₹1,525 crore, 10 percent and ₹93 crore.[29][28] EPACK's operating margin is close to Interarch's (10.5 percent against 9.7 percent), so the gap in net profit comes from below the operating line: EPACK paid ₹31 crore of interest in FY26 while Interarch paid ₹9 crore, and EPACK's profit margin after tax was 6.1 percent against 7.1 percent.[28][29] Interarch also carries almost no debt (borrowings of ₹18 crore against reserves of ₹864 crore), a comparison EPACK is now catching up to after repaying ₹70 crore of loans from its IPO money.[29][12]

The market currently pays more for the smaller profit. EPACK trades at 25.6 times its last twelve months of profit and Interarch at 19.7 times.[35][29] That premium is the market's bet on faster growth: EPACK's sales grew 32 percent a year over three years against 19 percent for Interarch.[28][29] It is also the reason this report cares so much about whether the 2026 margin dip is temporary, because a premium multiple on a company whose margin is falling is the pattern that disappoints owners.

What the weakness means for an owner

The business does not need a moat to be worth owning, because a fast-growing fabricator with net cash can compound shareholders' money for years. But without a moat the margin is only as good as the steel contract the company signed last week. That is why the margin and the receivables are scored as separate tests in Chapter 2B, and why the next steps in Chapter 7 turn on the next quarter's margin, not on the long growth story. The next chapter sets out the five operating tests and then checks whether management did what it said it would do.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Five numbers decide whether EPACK is running well, and each was chosen because of how this particular business earns money. It sells fixed-price buildings made mostly of steel, so the order book shows whether sales are coming, the operating margin shows whether steel is being passed on (the gross margin, which is sales less the cost of materials as a share of sales, is where a steel shock shows first), and the book-to-bill ratio, which is orders received divided by work billed in the same period, shows whether the order book is growing or shrinking. A factory business needs its plants busy, so capacity utilisation is the third test. Project customers pay late, so working capital and overdue receivables are the fourth. The fifth test is whether profit turns into cash flow from operations, which is the cash a business generates from its normal activity after tax, and whether the balance sheet can carry the planned plant spending.[9][4]

The scoring rules are written in the table so that a reader can check each one. A test passes at or above the stated line, is a watch inside a stated band, and fails below it. The portfolio standard needs at least four of five to pass to qualify, and two or more fails is a reason to replace the holding. EPACK passes three, is on watch on one and fails one, so the score is 3 of 5. That is short of the qualifying score but above the replacement line, and the health label is WATCH.

3. The numbers, in plain words

The five-year record is one of fast, mostly self-funded growth followed by a balance-sheet reset. Sales rose from ₹450 crore in FY22 to ₹1,525 crore in FY26, a multiple of 3.4, and profit after tax from ₹20 crore to ₹93 crore, a multiple of 4.6.[28] Operating margin moved from 8.0 to 10.5 percent, so most of the profit growth came from selling more, and roughly a fifth came from a wider margin (operating margin 8.0 to 10.5 percent, net margin, which is net profit as a share of revenue, 4.4 to 6.1 percent). In FY26 the IPO added about ₹300 crore of equity, which more than doubled shareholders' funds from ₹353 crore to ₹734 crore and cut borrowings from ₹216 crore to ₹115 crore.[28][2]

The FY26 accounts were audited by Talati & Talati LLP.[8] Two cautions apply to every table that follows. First, Screener.in rounds to whole crore and its definitions differ slightly from the company's, so where the audited annual report gives a figure to the lakh we use that, and where we use Screener we say so. Second, 'consolidated' means the parent company and its subsidiaries together. In FY26 the consolidated and standalone revenue and profit are the same to the first decimal (ICRA's standalone figures are ₹1,525.3 crore and ₹92.6 crore), so the subsidiaries add nothing yet, and the new data-centre subsidiary approved on 1 August 2026 comes after the year-end.[1][23][33]

5. What could happen next

A reverse DCF turns the usual valuation question upside down. Instead of forecasting profits and asking what they are worth, it takes today's price and asks what growth rate for the next ten years the price already assumes. The ledger's version starts from trailing net profit of ₹94.99 crore (market value divided by the trailing price-to-earnings ratio of 25.59, which is the share price divided by a year's profit per share). It counts each year's profit as cash received by owners, discounts that cash at 12 percent a year, adds a sale of the business at 20 times profit in year ten, and solves for the growth rate. The answer is 9.91 percent a year.[35][37] In plain words, the price assumes that profit grows about 10 percent a year for a decade, which is well below what the company has done and what analysts forecast.

That basis has limits. It uses past profit, not the cash the business throws off, so it ignores both the growth already in the order book and the capital the company must spend to deliver it. A cash-flow version is not usable here, because FY26 cash from operations of ₹135.7 crore was almost entirely spent on plant (₹129.3 crore), leaving free cash flow of ₹6.4 crore. That is a yield of 0.26 percent, shown as the FCF yield (free cash flow divided by market value) in the table below.[3] A better test uses forward profit. Analysts quote profit per share as EPS (earnings per share), which is net profit divided by the number of shares. Starting from ICICI Securities' FY27 forecast of ₹115.7 crore, the same discount rate and exit multiple imply only 7.3 percent a year. [27] Both versions say the share is priced for modest growth. Against that, sales grew 32 percent a year over three years and profit 57 percent, and the company's FY27 revenue target of ₹1,900 to ₹1,950 crore is growth of about 25 to 28 percent, which management rounds to 30 percent.[28][14]

Where we differ is on what growth is worth. The market pays 25.6 times trailing profit and 21.3 times FY27E earnings of ₹11.35, and about 17.1 times FY28E earnings of ₹14.10.[35] Our evidence for being careful is specific: the margin has fallen for three quarters in a row, management's January statement about steel proved wrong, overdue receivables are rising, and the one close peer with higher returns trades at 19.7 times. In short: the street's ₹325 target and our ₹282 differ by about half a year of earnings, not by a different view of the company. The case for owning is that growth is cheap. The case against adding is that the margin, not growth, is what moves the share, and the margin has not yet turned. We would be wrong on the upside if the September 2026 quarter margin is 10.5 percent or more and orders keep arriving, in which case the bull value in the table below becomes the central case. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one weak assumption, and this chapter names it, shows what would prove it wrong and says when we will find out. The weakest link overall is that the operating margin returns to at least 10 percent. The central target, the FY27 earnings estimate and the bull case all lean on it. The red-team bear case at the end of this chapter is the strongest honest argument against the holding that the evidence supports.

The margin may not recover

The margin has fallen from 10.5 percent in the June 2025 quarter, and from a September 2025 high of about 11.5 percent on rounded figures, to 10.1, 9.8 and 9.4 percent in the last three reported quarters, and management has said twice that it would recover (January: no impact expected; May: 10 percent plus all year).[20][21][9][19] The August explanation is that older, lower-priced orders are now executed. New orders carry higher prices, so the June quarter should be the low point. That is plausible. The company books orders weekly at that week's steel price.[17][18] The weak spot is competition: management itself says margins cannot be commanded because three or four large players bid for the same work, and ICRA says the Prefab segment is tender-driven.[19][22] If rivals have lower steel costs or bid thinner, price rises on new orders will stick less than management says. Evidence of this would be a September 2026 margin under 10 percent. That is due with the results by 14 November 2026.[34]

Fixed-price contracts leave steel risk with the company

Most Prefab orders are fixed price, and management says a pass-through clause would not work because steel is bought weeks before it is used and installed months later.[18] The company held about 32,000 tonnes of steel inventory in May, and management has blamed a minimum import price, anti-dumping duties and the Middle East war for the rise in steel prices.[19][17] A second price shock before the order book is repriced would repeat the June pattern. A fall in steel price would reverse it only partly, because customers would then ask for lower prices on the next bid. The measurable signal is the gross margin: management says it fell about 2 percentage points in the June quarter, and a further fall in September would break the recovery story.[18]

Receivables and the quality of the order book

ICRA flags that receivables over six months past due have risen in recent years and that the company's ability to manage them is a monitorable item. The audited figures show the share rising from 26.0 to 31.6 percent in a year. That is the trend ICRA means.[22][6] The presentation warns that 'some of the order book received during the year may be cancelled'.[11] The loss allowance is only ₹3.5 crore, 1.1 percent of receivables, against ₹37.2 crore that is more than 12 months past due.[6] A write-off of the oldest bucket would cut FY26 pre-tax profit by about 28 percent. Falling debtor days, now 74, over the next two quarters would show this concern to be wrong.[28]

New capacity and the data-centre bet

PEB capacity is planned to rise from 147,122 tonnes a year to about 220,000 by the end of FY27, an increase of about 50 percent, and the sandwich-panel capacity from 13.1 to about 21.1 lakh square metres.[26][13] On top of about ₹150 crore of plant spending, the board has approved up to ₹75 crore for a new data-centre subsidiary, whose funding, location and revenue are not yet set. The chief executive said so on the call and promised numbers by the next call.[33][18] The first two data-centre buildings it bid for were lost on design and price. Data centres are only 4 to 5 percent of the order book.[18] If orders do not grow by about half in a year, utilisation, now 75 percent, will fall and the extra depreciation will press the margin. The check is the utilisation figure in each quarterly presentation.

People, win rate and thin evidence

The vice president of sales and business development, a senior management post, left on 14 July 2026, and management says it has brought in a senior leader from a large steel-building business.[25][9] He had been with the company nine years and cited a wish to pursue new directions. In a business where four of five bids are lost, the sales team is the engine, so continuity matters. The estimates are also built on thin evidence: two analysts, one year of listed history, no three-year median multiple, and a 200-day average that is only 256 days old.[35] The check is the next two quarters' order intake against the ₹2,000 crore target, and the number of analysts who publish estimates by March 2027.

The red-team bear case

The strongest case against the holding runs like this. EPACK is a steel fabricator with no pricing power, priced at 25.6 times profit against 19.7 times for the closest and better-capitalised peer. Its margin has fallen three quarters in a row, and the stated recovery depends on rivals accepting higher bids at a time when it wins one bid in five. Its net cash of ₹103 crore is IPO money that is being spent: ₹176 crore of the ₹300 crore raised was already used by June, plant spending continues at about ₹150 crore this year, a data-centre subsidiary will absorb up to ₹75 crore, and free cash flow was ₹6.4 crore last year.[12][9][33][3] A third of its receivables are more than six months overdue. If private capex slowed, the new 220,000 tonnes of capacity would sit half-empty, depreciation and interest would stay, and a company earning about 10 percent before those costs would see profit fall by more than sales. In a falling market, with a beta of 1.62, the multiple could compress to 17 times the FY27 forecast of ₹11.35, which is ₹193, a price 20 percent below today's and below the entry zone. The reply is that none of this has yet shown up in cash flow, order intake or the credit rating, and that the stop at ₹206.56 would cap the loss at about 14.5 percent from today's price if it can be executed at that level.[35][22]

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is to trim the holding from 1,050 shares to 653 and to keep the remainder under the stop. The verdict is TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING, and it needs a plain explanation, because the gain is small. The position is up only 6.0 percent on cost and the share is 29.8 percent below its high, so there is no large profit to harvest. This is a trim for position size and for the entry-ladder band, not a victory lap. One taxonomy rule triggers it: the holding taxonomy treats a holding in the NO_BUY band, beyond 1.6 ATR from support, as extended, and the ledger puts the price 2.44 ATR above its support anchor. That band is the only rule behind the TRIM label. Our risk budget is a separate judgement that decides how many shares to sell: the holding is 5.55 percent of net worth, and its loss at the stop would be 0.80 percent of net worth against the 0.50 percent that a TIER_3 position may risk when it is bought.[37][35][36]

The 2.44 figure depends on which support level is used, and the ledger gives two answers. The ladder, and the A4 and B3 gate tests, use the ledger's support-anchor rule. When the 50-day average (₹238.59) is within 2.5 ATR of the 63-day low (₹213.22), as it is here (2.19 ATR apart), the anchor is the lower of the two, the floor of the base at ₹213.22. That gives 2.44 ATR. That is the ledger's rule, and we do not claim to know the reason for it. The scoring configuration describes support differently, as the highest level at or below the price, which would be the 50-day average. [37] The turnaround test, C3, measures instead from the nearest average or low below the price, which is the 50-day average, and gets 0.25 ATR. On that reading the share would sit in the FULL_SIZE band and the NO_BUY trigger would not be met. [35] We follow the ladder anchor because the ledger computes it and the gate tests A4 and B3 use it. The choice matters. On the 50-day-average reading no taxonomy trigger is met, and the trim would be a discretionary size decision based on our own risk budget, not a rule. We make it on the ladder reading and say so.

How big is the trim? A TIER_3 position may risk 50 basis points of net worth, which is ₹4,573,985.12 x 0.005 = ₹22,869.93. Each share loses ₹34.99 between ₹241.55 and the stop of ₹206.56, so the budget allows 22,869.93 / 34.99 = 653.6, or 653 shares. As a cross-check, the stop is 14.49 percent below the price, so the sizing formula gives 0.50 / 14.49 = 3.45 percent of net worth, and 3.45 percent of net worth at ₹241.55 is also 653 shares. We therefore sell 397 shares. That is 37.8 percent of the holding, inside the 30 to 50 percent trim range of the taxonomy. [37][36] The orders are limit orders, not market orders. Sell 200 shares at ₹247.64, the 20-day average, and 197 shares at ₹241.55, the 16 October close. If both fill, proceeds are ₹49,528.00 + ₹47,585.35 = ₹97,113.35 and the realised gain over cost is about ₹6,672 before brokerage and tax. If the first order has not filled by the Q2 FY27 results date, it is reset to the price of the second order, so that the reduction is intended to be complete before the results are known. A limit order cannot guarantee that. If the second order has also not filled by then, we reset it to the then-current price.

Why keep 653 shares and not sell all? No red line has fired, the order book is 40.4 percent higher than a year ago, cash exceeds debt, and the valuation implies only about 10 percent growth a year. The retained size risks 0.50 percent of net worth at the stop, which is exactly the TIER_3 budget, and the existing exit and second-failure triggers below still apply. The valuation sensitivity also matters for the size. If the market paid only 16.4 times FY28 earnings of ₹14.10, the multiple of Pennar Industries, the central price would be 14.10 x 16.4 = ₹231.24, which is 4.3 percent below ₹241.55. With the same 20, 50 and 30 percent weights the probability-weighted value becomes 0.2 x 325 + 0.5 x 231.24 + 0.3 x 168 = ₹231.02, or 4.4 percent below today's price, so a lower multiple alone removes the expected gain. At 653 shares a fall to ₹231.24 costs 653 x (241.55 - 231.24) = ₹6,732, or 0.15 percent of net worth, and a fall to the stop costs 0.50 percent. We accept that at the retained size. At 1,050 shares the stop loss would be 0.80 percent, which is why the trim comes first.[30][35]
