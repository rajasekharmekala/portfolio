1. What the company does

Beta Drugs Limited was founded in 2005 and is run from Panchkula in Haryana. The Batra family still controls it: Neeraj Batra alone holds 58.21% of the shares.[8] Its factories are in Baddi (Himachal Pradesh) and Dera Bassi (Punjab), and it owns two wholly owned subsidiaries. Adley Formulations makes finished medicines, and Adley Lab makes the active ingredients (the chemicals that do the work in a drug).[27][20] Beta makes tablets, capsules, ordinary injections and freeze-dried (lyophilised) injections, nearly all for cancer care. Management counts more than 135 oncology products, and the July 2026 company presentation lists over 180 oncology product codes (SKUs).[24][23]

Think of Beta as a specialist factory with three ways to be paid. First, it sells its own brands to cancer doctors and hospitals. These brand sales, which management calls branded oncology, were ₹123 crore in the year to March 2026 (FY26) and carry the best margin. Management says branded oncology earns an operating profit of 35% to 36% on each rupee of sales. That profit is called earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA).[24] Gross margin is the share of each rupee of sales left after paying for materials. Management puts it at about 70% for branded products, 50% for contract work and 55% to 60% for exports.[30] Second, it makes cancer drugs for other Indian drug companies under contract (called CMO or CDMO work). That was ₹149 crore in FY26 at a thinner 17% to 18% margin, and the named customers include Glenmark, Intas, Alkem, Cadila and Torrent.[24][26] Third, it exports mostly to tender buyers in Latin America, Asia and Africa, which was ₹71 crore in FY26 at 20% to 21%.[24][23]

Two smaller lines sit beside these. Beta makes about 70% to 80% of the active ingredients for its own formulations (Brickwork Ratings puts it at 65% to 70%), and it also sells to other drug makers; this API line earned ₹25 crore in FY26.[24][21][29] It also sells skin-care and cosmetic products (₹16.58 crore in FY26) and, since April 2026, owns 66.09% of Nivian Lifesciences, a company that sells fertility (IVF) medicines. Nivian had sales of ₹45 crore to ₹46 crore in FY26 and is part of Beta's accounts only from FY27.[24][15] Beta paid ₹69.4 crore for the stake: ₹20.8 crore in cash and the rest in new Beta shares issued at ₹1,712.49 each.[15]

Who pays? Cancer hospitals, drug distributors, other drug companies and foreign government tenders. Management says its brands reach more than 85% of corporate and government hospitals in India, and it is empanelled (approved as a supplier) with the Tata Memorial Hospital for five products.[23][21] Government and institutional business is slow to pay. Management says it can take up to six months, although government bodies do not write the bills off.[30] That matters later, when we look at cash.

The mix is moving towards brands. In FY21 contract work was half of sales. In the first half of FY27 (April to September 2026) management's slide shows brands plus exports at 54% of sales and contract work at 40%. On the table above, contract work is ₹95 crore of ₹263.04 crore, or 36%, and own brands, Nivian and exports together are ₹145 crore, or 55%. The slide appears to use a different base, so we quote both and rely on the table.[21] Management wants brands plus exports to reach 81% by FY30 and revenue of ₹850 crore to ₹900 crore, against ₹385 crore in FY26.[21][24] Our job in this report is to test whether that story is already in the share price. How the price rule for cancer drugs touches Beta

India's price regulator, the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA), sets or caps prices for medicines. In October 2026 it capped the trade margin (the markup between the maker's price and the price a patient pays) at 30% for 137 anti-cancer drugs, and press reports say prices of some drugs could fall by as much as 70%.[33][34] Beta told the stock exchange that it has studied the notice and sees no significant effect on its selling prices or margins, and it kept its guidance for branded oncology and contract work. Guidance means the growth forecast that management publishes.[20] We cannot test that claim yet, because no quarter has been reported under the new rule. We treat it as the largest outside risk in this report.

There is an older example. Beta has held back platinum-based cancer drugs, and a summary of the company's disclosures says price controls make the supply hard to sustain.[32] Management said in May 2026 that contract sales would have been about 7% higher in FY26 had it supplied them (about ₹10 crore), and in October it said platinum business stayed flat and is not expected to grow soon.[24][30] So price rules have already cost Beta some sales, which is why the new cap deserves attention.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • CMO / CDMO — contract manufacturing (or development and manufacturing): making a medicine for another company that sells it under its own name.
  • CCD — compulsorily convertible debenture: a loan that must turn into shares on a set date. Beta's ₹117 crore of CCDs, issued in November 2024, turned into shares by 25 May 2026.
  • PAT — profit after tax: the profit left for owners, before paying minority shareholders of a subsidiary.
  • CFO — cash flow from operations: cash the business generates from its day-to-day work, shown in the cash flow statement.
  • NDDS — new drug delivery system: a newer form of an old drug (for example a nano-particle or a ready-to-use liquid) that patients tolerate better.
  • TTM — trailing twelve months: the latest four reported quarters added together.
  • ₹ crore — ten million rupees. ₹1 lakh is one hundred thousand rupees, and 100 lakh equals ₹1 crore.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Beta's strength is that it is a focused specialist in a field where doctors choose brands carefully and patients cannot postpone treatment. It reaches most big Indian cancer hospitals, it makes a large share of its own active ingredients, and its profit margin has moved up. Gross margin was 55.5% in FY26 against 52.7% a year earlier, and 56.21% in the first half of FY27 against 51.35%. Operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales, and it has held between 20% and 23% for five years.[3][24][20] Management points to higher selling prices across its businesses, a richer mix of brands and exports, and cost cuts, and it plans to hold the margin by making more intermediate chemicals in-house.[20][30]

The second strength is the balance sheet after the debentures turned into shares. Borrowings were ₹148 crore at March 2026 but only ₹49 crore at September 2026, while cash and bank balances were ₹104 crore, so Beta now has net cash of about ₹55 crore (cash minus borrowings).[4][21][20][31] Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, and it fell from 0.60 at March 2026 to about 0.11 at September 2026. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is the profit a company earns on the money invested in it, and Screener shows 19.2%.[1][4] The rating agency Crisil rated the group BBB+ with a positive outlook in January 2026 and treated the debentures as equity.[27] Promoters have pledged none of their shares, and they held the same 6,551,392 shares in December 2025, June 2026 and September 2026; their percentage fell only because new shares were issued.[8]

The weaknesses are just as real. Contract work (36% of H1 FY27 sales on our table, 39% in FY26) is low-margin and depends on large Indian drug companies, and Beta does not disclose how much any single customer pays. Beta says it has more than 104 contract clients and that 95% of business from key customers is repeat business, but that is not a concentration figure.[23] Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying plant and equipment. It was minus ₹16 crore in FY26.[5] Exports are lumpy because tenders slip: management says tender delays cost ₹20 crore to ₹25 crore of FY26 exports, which fell 11% from ₹79 crore to ₹71 crore.[24] Skin care took years to reach break-even, and the new fertility business adds a third field for the same management team to run.[26][24]

Regulatory status is mixed, and we state it without rounding up. All three plants are certified under the World Health Organization's good manufacturing practice (WHO-GMP) standard, and one plant also holds approvals from Brazil (ANVISA), Colombia (INVIMA) and Mexico (COFEPRIS).[23] The active-ingredient plant has also cleared an audit under the PIC/S scheme, an international factory-inspection standard.[24] What Beta does not have is clearance from European regulators: the European Union audit has slipped repeatedly, and management now says it will come within two to four months, after four European dossiers were filed.[30] We searched eight company documents (the FY25 and FY26 annual reports, three call transcripts, the July and October 2026 presentations and the Q2 results filing) and found no mention of the US Food and Drug Administration (USFDA), of any Form 483 inspection finding or of any warning letter. We read that as "no US filings disclosed", not as a clean US record, so USFDA status is N/A.

A final point is how rating agencies see Beta. Brickwork Ratings had Beta in its "issuer not cooperating" category, at a low BWR B rating, as late as April 2025. On 6 July 2026 it assigned BWR A-/Stable to ₹42.89 crore of bank facilities, citing the debenture conversion and the Nivian deal.[29] Crisil's September 2026 update, in contrast, says it is still waiting for adequate information from the company and names information availability as a rating risk.[28] The company now has almost no bank debt to rate, and a new A- rating is a good sign. Even so, a long non-cooperation record is a governance watch item, because a company that wants outside investors should answer a rating agency promptly.

Where the competitive edge is thin

Beta's moat is partly brand and partly cost. Doctors choose cancer drugs by trust, and Beta has built that with new-format products such as nano-albumin-bound paclitaxel and ready-to-use docetaxel.[23] But the products are mostly generics, other Indian makers sell the same molecules, and the regulator can now cap what the trade earns on 137 of them.[34] The edge that is hardest to copy is the combination of dedicated cancer plants, in-house active ingredients and a field team of oncology specialists. The edge that is easiest to erode is price.

Concentration inside the brand business is low. Management says the largest single brand is just over ₹10 crore against a branded run-rate of about ₹150 crore, so no product can sink the segment on its own.[30] The Nivian fertility franchise is more concentrated: its top five brands make 63% of its sales.[21] A buyer must accept that Beta is a collection of niche franchises, not one dominant product.

What this means for an owner

For an owner, the business is better than the headline numbers of FY26 suggest, because FY26 profit carried ₹9.36 crore of interest on debentures that no longer exist.[24] It is worse than the first-half growth suggests, because about half of the growth came from buying Nivian. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means checking what management said it would do against what it did. We compared the May 2025, November 2025 and May 2026 earnings calls with the audited numbers and the October 2026 results. The record is mixed. Financing steps and product launches arrived on time. Sales forecasts were missed more than once, and the European audit date has moved twice.[26][25][24][30]

The most telling example is the FY26 sales plan. In May 2025 management said it would grow 23% to 30% a year. By November 2025 growth was 13% for the half-year. An analyst on the May 2026 call said management had since cut the FY26 plan to ₹400 crore to ₹420 crore, and management accepted that correction. The final number was ₹385 crore, which is 6.2% above FY25.[26][25][24][11] Management blamed delayed export tenders and the platinum-drug decision. Both reasons are plausible, and both are the kind of lumpy events that we cannot forecast either.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter puts numbers on the business. All figures are consolidated (parent plus subsidiaries), in ₹ crore, for years ending 31 March. Years FY24 to FY26 come from the audited annual reports; FY22, FY23 and the trailing twelve months (TTM, the four quarters to September 2026) come from Screener.in, which rounds to whole crores.[11][19][3] Where our own calculation differs from a source, we show the working in the estimate boxes.

Growth has been strong on sales and slower on profit. Sales grew 27% a year over five years and 19% a year over three years, but profit grew 29% a year over five years and only 11% a year over three.[3] In FY26 sales rose 6.2% to ₹384.83 crore while profit after tax slipped from ₹42.42 crore to ₹41.48 crore.[11] Three costs explain the gap: finance costs more than doubled from ₹6.99 crore to ₹15.31 crore because of the coupon on the debentures, depreciation rose from ₹12.51 crore to ₹17.15 crore, and a one-time ₹4.57 crore share-issue cost sat in FY25.[11][19][26] Management says ₹9.36 crore of the FY26 interest was debenture interest that has now disappeared.[24]

That is why TTM profit of ₹53 crore, or earnings per share (EPS) of ₹48.26, is so much higher than FY26's ₹41 crore. The September 2026 quarter alone earned ₹19 crore and EPS of ₹16.68, against ₹12 crore the year before.[2][3] Part of this jump is real growth, part is the end of debenture interest, and part is profit from Nivian, which Beta did not own before April 2026. We separate these three pieces in Chapter 5.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions, and this chapter names the weakest one in each, says what evidence would show it is wrong, and says when we will know. We also give the red-team case, which is the strongest honest argument against our own verdict. We hold the view that the business is good, that value on our scenarios sits well above the close, and that timing is the problem. So the red team argues that even a wait-for-support call is too generous.

The red-team argument is short. Beta's profit jump in FY27 comes from three things that will not repeat in FY28: the end of debenture interest, a first full year of Nivian and a rebound in exports after tender delays. In FY28 profit grows about 18% and the stock trades at 40 times profit. Management has missed its own sales plan in FY26, the European audit is late, the trade-margin cap on cancer drugs is untested, and cash conversion is weaker than profit suggests. On this view a buyer pays a full price for a plan that has not yet arrived, in a stock that trades only 13,000 shares a day.[24][30][34][23]

The price cap on cancer drugs may cost more than management says

The weakest assumption in the base case is management's statement that the 30% trade-margin cap has no significant bearing on prices or margins.[20] The rule covers 137 anti-cancer drugs and press reports expect price cuts of up to 70% on some of them.[34][33] Beta's selling price to distributors may not change, but distributors and hospitals may push back on discounts. The evidence would be branded oncology sales below ₹36 crore a quarter, or gross margin below 54%, in the December quarter. We will know when the Q3 FY27 results are filed (date not yet announced; February 2027 is our expectation).

Second-half sales may arrive late again

Management needs about ₹272 crore of sales in the second half to reach ₹535 crore; our base case uses ₹250 crore.[20] Branded oncology needs ₹80 crore in H2 against ₹62 crore a year ago, and contract work needs ₹85 crore to ₹95 crore.[30] Past plans slipped on tenders, platinum drugs and audits. Sales below ₹125 crore in the December quarter would confirm our caution, and sales above ₹135 crore would favour the bull case.

The multiple may fall faster than profit rises

The base value assumes a P/E of 40.8, the three-year median. The reward-to-risk floor fails at the zone top below about 35.4 times, and the three-year low was 24.7 times.[10] A smaller stock with slowing growth and no analyst coverage can lose its multiple quickly. The evidence is a fall in the P/E toward 30 times on stable profit. We would see it in the price within weeks, so it can happen before any filing.

Cash conversion may be worse than it looks

The reported ratio of cash flow to profit is 0.87 over three years, but cash from operations is only about half of operating profit (49% in FY26), inventory days rose from 127 to 159, and customers take 116 days to pay.[5][6][30] The cash flow statement does not show tax paid on its own line, so the strict reading is 0.53.[13] The evidence would be another year of negative free cash flow or a further rise in inventory days. The FY27 annual report, due around September 2027, will settle it; the half-year cash flow in the 22 October filing is not usable in our scan, so it is N/A here.

7. What we would do, and when

Our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule is the support-distance ladder. A price within 0.6 average daily swings (ATR) of a verified support is an immediate buy. Within 1.2 swings it is an accumulate. Within 1.6 swings it is a small limit order. Beyond 1.6 swings it is a no-buy band, and we do not chase. Beta's close of ₹1,894.60 is 2.05 swings above the ₹1,616 swing low, so it sits in the no-buy band.[39][40]

Value is not the problem here. On our scenarios the base value is 55% above the close, and the reward-to-risk clears the floor. The buy is deferred by the no-buy band and the momentum gate, which are timing rules. The upside also depends on FY28 EPS of ₹72 at a 40.8 median multiple that the stock already trades near, so a better entry price matters more than a better story.
