Before you read

HBL Engineering makes special batteries and the Kavach system that stops trains from colliding, and it earned ₹814 crore on ₹3,303 crore of sales in FY26 with almost no debt[1][4]. The business is clean, with ROCE (return on capital employed) of 59%, no pledged promoter shares and cash conversion of 0.92 over three years[1][18]. We still do not buy at ₹809.95, because FY26 was a peak margin year, management says Kavach profit may fall from FY29, and our weighted value is about ₹755[2][30]. The verdict is WAIT FOR SUPPORT, with a buy order planned at ₹696 to ₹706 after the November results, where the gain could be about two and a half times the loss[31]. The idea is wrong if the stock closes below ₹683.20 or if the results due on 7 November 2026 show Kavach margins sliding toward the FY25 level[1].

1. What the company does

HBL Engineering Limited is a Hyderabad company that designs and manufactures things other companies cannot easily buy abroad: batteries for submarines, aircraft and railway coaches, and the electronic units that stop a locomotive from running into another train. [2] The annual report dates the company's start to 1977, when it began with batteries for defence aircraft, while the CARE rating note dates the promoter's company to 1986. Since then it has added products one at a time, each one a niche that was too small for large companies and too hard for small ones. [2][4] The company was called HBL Power Systems until it changed its name, and the old name still turns up in filings and older articles. [2][3] It trades on the National Stock Exchange as HBLENGINE, and the promoter group led by the founder, Dr A J Prasad, which is made up of family trusts and relatives, holds 59.11% of the shares. [1][19]

The company earns money in three ways. Batteries are sold to railways, telecom towers, data centres, oil and gas plants and defence buyers, and they wear out and get replaced, which gives a steady stream of repeat orders. [2] Electronics, which is now almost half of sales, means the Kavach system and the train management software that sits on top of it, sold mainly to production units owned by Indian Railways and to the railway zones. [10][13][14] Defence products are batteries and, increasingly, electronic fuzes, which are the triggers that make an artillery shell or grenade explode at the right moment. [2]

The best way to see the mix is the table below, which comes from the segment note in the FY26 annual report. Electronics grew from ₹298 crore to ₹1,626 crore in one year, which is why the whole company's sales rose 68% and its profit tripled. [2][4] The segment margins matter as much as the sales: the credit rating agency CARE puts electronics at a 50.33% profit margin before interest and tax and batteries at 24.09%, so each rupee of electronics sales, which is mostly Kavach, earns about twice what a rupee of battery sales earns. What Kavach is and why the railways are buying it

Kavach is India's own automatic train protection system. It slows a train by itself if the driver misses a signal or exceeds a speed limit, and it works through radio tags on the track, towers and a computer unit in every locomotive. [15] The railways approved version 4.0 of the design on 16 July 2024, and by 31 July 2026 it had been commissioned on 2,633 route kilometres of the Delhi–Mumbai and Delhi–Howrah corridors. [15] Track-side work has been taken up on 21,794 route kilometres in total, and 6,290 locomotives had the unit fitted, with work started on another 7,190 locomotives and 1,200 suburban trains. [15] That means the railways have ordered a great deal, but only about 12% of the route kilometres under work are finished with this version. [16]

Why HBL has a head start and where the head start is thin

HBL says it began Kavach research in 2005 with a very small budget and that it led the work with the railways' own design body, the Research Designs and Standards Organisation. [2][7] Only a handful of firms are approved to supply it, and CARE sees that approval as the main entry barrier for the business. [4] In September 2025 the chairman told shareholders that only two of the five approved firms had qualified for version 4.0 and that HBL led the other by eight to nine weeks. [7] By January 2026 the railway locomotive works at Chittaranjan had given Kernex Microsystems an order of ₹2,465.71 crore and Quadrant Future Tek one of ₹287.82 crore for the same on-board equipment. [17] So the lead exists, but it is a lead of months, and rivals are now winning very large orders.

Batteries: the older business that pays for the research

HBL calls itself the world's second largest supplier of industrial nickel-cadmium batteries, the kind used for railway signalling, pantographs on electric locomotives, metro trains and oil and gas plants. [2] It is also the only Indian maker of pure lead thin plate batteries, and sales to data centres doubled in FY26 on orders from operators such as STT, NTT and Colt. [2] The weak spot is telecom: mobile tower companies are switching to lithium-ion packs, which cut demand for the two-volt lead-acid cells HBL built for them, and the annual report says the industry now has spare capacity and falling prices. [2][3] Management chose not to chase telecom lithium because the margins did not justify years of warranty. [3]

Defence products, electronic fuzes and the new ventures

In defence, HBL sells batteries for aircraft, missiles, armoured vehicles, torpedoes and submarines, and it has spent about ₹200 crore on a plant that makes lithium cells for the Navy under a licence from a government laboratory. [2] Electronic fuzes are the next product the chairman expects to become the second-largest business by FY30, but Army approval for the main types is only expected during 2027. [2][3] The company also plans electric drive trains for 55-tonne trucks, with road approval by March 2027 and pilot sales from July 2027, and it formed a joint venture with Cochin Shipyard for electric tugs and ferries. [2][10] None of these three ventures produced material sales in FY26, so they are options on the future and not part of today's profit.

How the cash comes in

Customers are mostly governments and large organisations, so sales arrive as large orders delivered over twelve to twenty-four months. [4] The company reports consolidated sales, and the quarterly numbers jump around: ₹602 crore in June 2025, ₹1,223 crore in September 2025, ₹874 crore in December 2025, ₹604 crore in March 2026 and ₹638 crore in June 2026. [1] The reason is that Kavach revenue is booked when locomotives are fitted at railway sheds, and the railways, not HBL, decide when a locomotive is available. [4] This is the single most useful fact for an investor to carry through the rest of the report, because it makes any one quarter a poor guide to the year.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

HBL is strong in the places where it is hardest to copy and weak in the places where it depends on someone else's timetable. The strengths are real: a deep technical record, a customer list that returns, and a balance sheet that needs no lenders. The weaknesses are about concentration and timing, not about quality. This chapter weighs them in turn and gives the evidence for each claim.

Strength one: technology that took twenty years to build

Nearly all of HBL's products took ten to fifteen years from research to a commercial sale, and Kavach research began in 2005, so the first real profit arrived in FY26. [7] That patience is the moat. A new entrant would need to match the technology, win railway approval and then fit hundreds of locomotives, and the railways have said they will approve only a few firms. [4][7] The chairman's own summary is that the company enters niche markets that are too small for big companies and too difficult for small ones, and it avoids capital-heavy products. [3] The evidence that this works is the return on capital employed: 59% in FY26 and 27% in FY25, on a business whose capital spending in FY26 was about ₹144 crore, which is operating cash flow of ₹738 crore less free cash flow of ₹594 crore. [1]

Strength two: a balance sheet that needs no one's permission

Borrowings were ₹67 crore at 31 March 2026 against shareholders' funds of about ₹2,215 crore, which is a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.03, and the company repaid its last term loan so that CARE withdrew that rating. [1][4] Cash and liquid investments were ₹570.25 crore and the company produced ₹858.86 crore of gross cash profit in the year. [4] The rating agency raised the long-term rating to AA minus on 9 October 2026 and expects the financial risk profile to stay strong. [4] For an investor this means the company can fund new plants and a slow quarter from its own cash, and that a lender can never force a sale of assets.

Strength three: promoters who stay and do not pledge

The promoter family holds 59.11% and that figure has been the same in every quarter from December 2023 to September 2026. [1] A data source that tracks pledges states that none of the promoter holding is pledged. [18] That removes a common Indian small-company danger, where a falling price forces promoters to sell the shares they pledged for loans. [18] The chairman has said openly that the company must run without depending on one person, and a succession plan that makes each of nine business units a separate team is under way. [2][7]

Weakness one: half of the sales come from one product and one buyer group

The annual report says Kavach produced almost 50% of FY26 sales and should produce about the same share in FY27. [2] Of the ₹5,902 crore order book, ₹4,440 crore or about 75% is Kavach and train management software, and most of it is placed by railway entities. [4] That is a concentration of risk in one customer type, and the customer has already cancelled work once: HBL delivered 76% of the earlier Chittaranjan order inside the time allowed and the balance was cancelled under the order terms. [5] CARE lists this as the first key weakness and warns that locomotive availability at railway sheds, which HBL does not control, can delay revenue. [4]

Weakness two: the margin was a peak and management says so

Operating margin was 11% in FY22 and FY23, 19% in FY24, 20% in FY25 and 34% in FY26. [1] The 34% came from an electronics segment, mostly Kavach, that earned a 50.33% profit margin before interest and tax, and the company itself says that Kavach contracts differ in profitability for reasons beyond its control and that competitive bidding makes margins vary. [2][4][6] The annual report goes further and says that from FY29 competition would increase and both sales and profit could decline. [2] The quarterly record shows how wide the swings are: 44% in September 2025, 12% in March 2026 and 23% in June 2026. [1][12] The honest reading is that the business can earn very high margins on some contracts and that no one can say how long that lasts.

Weakness three: competition is arriving and pricing is the weapon

Kernex Microsystems won a ₹2,465.71 crore order for 3,024 on-board sets from the Chittaranjan works in January 2026, while HBL's own order from the same works, worth ₹1,714 crore, came only in May 2026. [13][17] A market commentary on HBL records that management has said it lost certain Kavach tenders because competitors quoted lower prices. [29] The annual report expects competition to increase and says that in most industries the top two firms earn profits and the third survives. [2] That is a reasonable view of how this market will end, and it is also a warning that margins in the current contracts may be higher than margins in the next round.

Weakness four: the other businesses have not yet picked up the slack

Battery sales were ₹1,416 crore in FY26 against ₹1,397 crore in FY25, which is flat, and in the June 2026 quarter defence and aviation battery sales fell 48.5% to ₹37.93 crore. [2][9] The new ventures are late: electric truck sales were meant to start in October 2025, were moved to October 2026 and are now set for pilot sales in July 2027. [2][3] The fund that was to invest surplus cash in small technology companies, Mittelstand Technology Partners, was dropped because the regulations were too restrictive. [2] The annual report expects cash surpluses larger than internal needs, and how that cash is used will matter for returns.

How it was calculated

We added reported cash from operations for FY24, FY25 and FY26 (₹270 crore, ₹247 crore and ₹738 crore) and divided by the sum of profit for the same years (₹280 crore, ₹276 crore and ₹814 crore).[1]

The range

If FY26 cash from operations had been 10% lower, at ₹664 crore, the ratio would be 0.86. If the large FY26 inventory release reverses and FY27 cash from operations is only ₹250 crore, the next three-year ratio (FY25 to FY27, using our own FY27 profit estimate of ₹748 crore) would be about 0.67, below the 0.70 target and above the 0.25 red line.

A ratio below 0.70 would turn KPI 2 from PASS to WATCH and cost half a point, which would drop the Layer 2 score to 2.5 out of 5.0 and the dossier to FAIL. This is the second most important number in the KPI check.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is weaker than the others. This chapter names that assumption, says what evidence would show it is wrong and gives the date by which the evidence will be known. We also write the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, and then single out the one claim on which the whole verdict depends.

Weakest assumption in the profit estimate: Kavach margins of about 27%

Our FY27 estimate needs a blended operating margin of 27%, and FY28 needs 25%. The last four quarters printed 44%, 35%, 12% and 23%, so the average is fine but the spread is enormous and we are forecasting a number that no one quarter has produced. [1] Management says margins vary by contract and that some Kavach work was priced competitively. [2][6] If the unexecuted order book is mostly lower-margin work, FY27 profit per share could be closer to ₹21. The evidence will arrive with the September 2026 results on 7 November 2026 and the December results in February 2027. [1]

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF: a normal starting cash flow

We used FY26 free cash flow of ₹594 crore because it is the only audited figure. CARE says part of FY26's cash came from selling inventory built while Kavach approval was delayed, so some of that cash was a one-time release and is not repeatable. [4] If the true normal level is lower, the implied growth needed is higher and the price looks more demanding, which supports our view. If FY26 cash flow was held back by a slow-paying customer, the real figure could be higher. The half-year cash flow statement filed with the September 2026 results will show which it is. [1]

Weakest assumption in the target: a 27 times multiple and a 25% weight on the bear case

We have no published consensus and no public three-year median to check our multiple against, so the 27 times is judgement guided by the stock's own recent range and its peers. [1][23] If the market keeps paying 30 times or more for Kavach visibility, we will be too cautious. We also gave the bear case a quarter of the weight, which is judgement. The evidence for it is a margin of 12% in March 2026, a partly cancelled order and a larger rival order, but the evidence against it is a stronger credit rating and an order book that has doubled. [4][5][12][17]

Red-team bear case: the strongest honest argument against buying even at ₹706

The bear argument is simple. FY26 profit was a one-time boom from catching up on delayed Kavach orders, and the stock at ₹809.95 pays 28.8 times trailing profit. If the Kavach margin falls to its FY25 level and sales stay at ₹3,700 crore, profit per share is about ₹20 and the multiple on that is 40 times. [1] A railway market with five suppliers that have received orders, at least two of them now winning large ones, tends to see prices drop as capacity grows. [4][17] HBL's own annual report predicts exactly that. [2] In that world a buyer at ₹706 has a stop only 3.2% away but a gap risk far bigger than the stop. The 7 November results come before any planned fill, so the exposure arises later: the December-quarter results in February 2027, or an order or regulatory announcement over a weekend, could open the stock well below ₹683.20 and fill the stop at a worse price.

Red-team case against our own caution

The opposite argument is also fair. Management said on 24 May 2026 that FY27 sales and profit would be significantly better than FY26. [6] The order book of ₹5,902 crore is close to double its December level, and CARE says an annual operating income above ₹4,500 crore at current margins would trigger a further upgrade. [4] The share price has already come off from ₹1,122 to ₹809.95 and the stock is up 13.64% in two weeks on strong relative strength. If September-quarter profit is well above the ₹213 crore a quarter that our base case needs, say ₹260 crore with a margin of 29% or more, our base case is too low, and a buyer who waited for ₹706 would have missed a fair entry. We accept that cost, because it is smaller than the cost of buying a possible peak.

One-variable stress: a 23% margin in FY28

The June 2026 margin of 23% is the cheapest stress test. If FY28 margin is 23% instead of 25% and nothing else changes, operating profit is ₹966 crore, profit after tax is about ₹723 crore and profit per share is about ₹26.1. At 27 times that is about ₹705, which is below the ₹706 top of our entry zone and 13% below today's close. [1][10] The point is not that 23% will happen. It is that the 2.54 to 1 reading has almost no slack, because the base target of ₹764 is only about ₹1 above the ₹763 needed to meet the floor at ₹706. That is why a purchase needs a refreshed margin estimate after the results as well as a price touch, and why the ratio is a planned price-level figure, not a guaranteed payoff.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, which means nothing is bought at ₹809.95. The rule is the support distance ladder: the price is 4.56 ATR above the ledger's support anchor, and any distance above 1.6 ATR is the NO_BUY band. Layer 1 passes with room to spare, Layer 2 qualifies at exactly 3.0 out of 5.0 with one FAIL, and the asymmetry floor of 2.5 to 1 is met only at the entry zone, not at today's price. [31] Because this is a Tactical Momentum name it is sized as TIER_3. A tier is a size class for a position, and TIER_3 is a small one, 2% to 3.5% of net worth, which suits a name whose Layer 2 score has no room to spare.

The entry zone comes from the chart. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut, and for a tactical name the rule puts it below the 20-day swing low, less half an ATR. The swing low is ₹701.00 and half an ATR is ₹17.77, so the stop is ₹683.23, which we round to ₹683.20. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and the entry zone is the band where a buyer is willing to fill. Ours is ₹696.00 to ₹706.00, which holds the swing low and ends 22.8 rupees above the stop. At the zone high of ₹706 the reward to the base target of ₹764 is ₹58 against a risk of ₹22.80, which is 2.54 times, just above the 2.5 floor. [30][31]

Asymmetry means the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss, measured from your entry to the target and to the stop. At the ₹809.95 close it is minus 0.36 to 1, because the base target is below the price. At ₹750 it is 0.2 to 1, and at ₹715 it is about 1.5 to 1, so the zone is narrow and the margin over the floor is thin. If our base profit per share is 5% too high, the target falls to about ₹726 and the ratio at ₹706 drops to about 0.9. We would rather say so than widen the zone to flatter the arithmetic, and the 2.54 figure is a planned price-level ratio, not a promised payoff. [30]

Position size follows the risk-unit rule. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so a TIER_3 risk budget of 50 basis points means a stop-out should cost about 0.5% of net worth. The stop is 3.23% below the zone high, which would allow a raw size of 15.5%, and the tier range of 2% to 3.5% caps it at 3.5%. [31] The sequence is: no order rests in the market today. Once the results of 7 November 2026 are filed and our estimates refreshed, a resting limit order for half the tier weight, 1.75% of net worth, goes in at ₹696 to ₹706, but only if the refreshed asymmetry still clears 2.5 to 1. The second half waits for information that does not exist yet, namely the December-quarter results in February 2027.

Once a position exists, a gap below the stop becomes possible. The December-quarter results in February 2027, or an order or regulatory announcement made over a weekend, could open the stock below ₹683.20. A stop only protects against a close below it, and the sale then happens at the next open, which can be lower than the stop. That gap risk of a filled position, not the 7 November results, which arrive before any fill, is why the first fill is half size.
