Before you read

Marsons Limited makes the large steel-and-copper machines, called power transformers, that raise and lower voltage on the electricity grid.[20] It restarted in 2022 after an insolvency process, and sales grew from ₹6.46 crore in FY24 to ₹245 crore in FY26, but the latest quarter grew only 4.7%.[16][6][3][1] This report concludes REJECTED: the balance sheet is clean, yet three years of operating cash flow are negative, and the price already assumes about 18% growth a year for ten years.[2][39] Our central-case value is ₹86, below the price of ₹117.40; that price is the 26 October close, one session behind the 27 October as-of date. The conclusion would be wrong if profit grows much faster than ₹2.62 a share in FY28, if customers pay within 90 days, or if the price falls toward ₹70.

1. What the company does

A transformer changes the voltage of electricity. Power stations send current at very high voltage because less energy is lost on long wires, and transformers step it down again near towns and factories. Marsons designs, builds, tests and ships these machines from a 45,000 square-metre plant at Maheshtala, Kolkata, with a stated installed capacity of 12,000 MVA a year.[20] The range runs from small distribution units of 10 kVA up to 160 MVA at 220 kV, and the company says it is the only maker of extra-high-voltage (EHV) units, meaning 132 kV and above, in Eastern India.[6][35]

The company was incorporated in 1976 and later went through insolvency. The tribunal in Kolkata approved a resolution plan on 25 January 2022, and new owners took over.[16] The restart was fast. Standalone sales were about ₹5 crore in FY23 and ₹6 crore in FY24, then ₹168 crore in FY25 and ₹245 crore in FY26.[2] The jump was paid for partly by a share issue to outside investors on 18 April 2024. It raised ₹80.25 crore for 32.1 million new shares, about ₹25 a share, and it cut the promoter stake from 66.2% to 53.7%.[15][7][3]

Three kinds of customer pay Marsons. State power utilities such as the West Bengal transmission and distribution companies buy through tenders. Contractors that build power projects for others, known as EPC firms, buy for their own jobs. Renewable developers such as Inox Wind and GH2 Solar buy special transformers for wind and solar plants.[6] The rating agency puts state utilities at nearly 30% of revenue, with payment cycles of 45 to 90 days.[6] By August 2026 the company said renewable projects were more than half of its current orders, helped by an NTPC Renewable Energy approval, and that it had won its first United States orders worth about USD 5 million.[4]

Marsons earns the gap between the price it quotes and what the materials cost. In FY26 materials cost ₹185.57 crore on revenue from operations of ₹245.03 crore, or 75.7%.[3] The main inputs are electrical steel (about 35% of raw-material cost), copper (about 25%) and transformer oil (7% to 8%).[8] Orders are often priced when signed and delivered months later, so a jump in copper or oil costs in between comes out of the margin. The operating margin is the share of sales left after the running costs of the business. It fell to 14.7% in April to June 2026, when the company said oil supply was disrupted and prices rose.[4][1]

The model also swallows cash. Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company itself owes suppliers. At 31 March 2026 customers owed Marsons ₹107.29 crore, up from ₹68.28 crore a year before, and stock stood at ₹58.01 crore, up from ₹14.95 crore.[3] Suppliers were owed ₹56.05 crore and customers had paid ₹14.77 crore in advance, which funded much of the build-up.[3] For an owner this is the central tension: reported profit is high, but a large share of it sits in receivables and steel rather than in the bank.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • MVA (megavolt-ampere) is the unit that measures a transformer's size. A 100 MVA unit can carry about 100 million volt-amperes of power.
  • kV (kilovolt) is the voltage class. 132 kV and 220 kV units sit in the extra-high-voltage group and are much harder to build than 33 kV or 11 kV units.
  • EHV means extra high voltage, the 132 kV and above class used on the main transmission grid.
  • CRGO is cold-rolled grain-oriented electrical steel, the core material of almost every transformer. India imports about 90% of its CRGO.
  • IDT and GSU are inverter-duty and generator step-up transformers, the types wind and solar plants use to feed the grid.
  • EPC means engineering, procurement and construction: the contractor that builds a power project for the owner.
  • NCLT is the National Company Law Tribunal, the court that approves resolution plans for insolvent companies in India.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Marsons has a regional edge, not a national moat. The edge is real: it says it is the only EHV transformer maker in Eastern India and the North Eastern Region, and on 15 May 2026 it won Power Grid Corporation of India vendor approval for units up to 132 kV, which the company calls the only such approval in Eastern India and ten neighbouring states.[9][10][34] It also runs a test laboratory accredited by NABL, India's testing-accreditation body, which the rating agency calls the only privately owned one of its kind in the east.[6] Those approvals take years to earn, and they keep out small local rivals on the larger orders.

The edge is thin when you step back. Voltamp Transformers sold ₹2,154 crore in FY26, about nine times Marsons' ₹245 crore.[25][3] Transformers and Rectifiers (India) reported a record order book of ₹6,630 crore and an EBITDA margin of 19.16% for the June 2026 quarter. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit. Marsons' own operating margin for the quarter was 14.7%.[28][4] The rating agency names ABB India and Kirloskar Electric among larger rivals with scale and long utility relationships, and the company's own annual report warns that small unorganised makers sometimes win big orders by quoting lower.[6][3]

What supports the business

Demand for large transformers is tight in the United States, which is why the new joint venture matters. A US-based private equity firm and Marsons said on 6 October 2026 that lead times for transformers there exceed 24 months, and Wood Mackenzie estimated in 2025 that US supply would fall about 30% short of demand, with imports about 80% of supply.[17][18] Marsons already has first US orders of about USD 5 million at margins the company calls meaningfully higher than at home.[4] In India the order flow is also strong: disclosed wins since May 2026 include ₹18.88 crore from Ram Taranga Solutions, ₹33.19 crore from Vikran Engineering for an NTPC Renewable Energy project, and ₹17.94 crore from S.T. Electricals, each including GST.[11][12][13] Together these three come to ₹70.01 crore, which is 28.6% of FY26 revenue from operations (70.01 divided by 245.03).

The raw-material squeeze is shared by every maker, which makes it a sector risk and not a Marsons fault. About 90% of India's electrical steel is imported, only one domestic plant makes it, and an anti-dumping investigation could raise import costs further.[23][36] Peers saw the same squeeze in the June 2026 quarter: Shilchar Technologies' operating margin fell to 16% in the quarter from 33% a year earlier.[24] The conclusion is that Marsons' April to June margin dip is partly industry-wide, but a company with firm-price orders and less scale feels it more.

What weakens the case

Marsons' growth comes from a very small starting point, and its customers pay slowly. The market itself was flat for years: the company's own 2025 slides put the Indian transformer market at ₹304 billion in FY20 and ₹324 billion in FY24, about 6.6% higher over four years.[8] Marsons grew from a near-zero starting point on a plant that already existed. That is much easier than growing from a ₹245 crore level. Capacity also needs care: recorded capital spending on equipment was only ₹1.65 crore in FY26, so the rise in stated capacity from 10,000 MVA in August 2025 to 12,000 MVA was not paid for by visible new machinery, and the cost of the new 26,000 MVA expansion is not disclosed in the filings we could read.[3][38][14]

For an owner, the competitive position supports a good business at a fair price, not a great business at any price. The price already assumes, as Chapter 5 shows, that the edge turns into growth for a decade. Chapter 2B turns to the operating numbers that decide whether that is plausible.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management promises are the best test of whether a young company can plan. The February 2025 investor slides said renewable and special transformers had the potential to supply 30% to 40% of revenue by FY27.[8] By August 2026 more than half of current orders came from renewable projects, which points the right way, although the filings do not give a renewable share of FY26 revenue.[4] The rating agency recorded that revenue of ₹107 crore in the first half of FY26 was 49% of the projected full-year figure, which implies a projection of about ₹218 crore (107 divided by 0.49); the company delivered ₹245.03 crore, about 12% above it.[6][3]

The record is weaker on cash and on the first test of FY27 guidance, which is the company's own published forecast for the period ahead. The rating agency described the working-capital cycle as an optimum 80 days in FY25 and expected the company to strengthen it, but days of customer dues were 148 in FY25 and 160 in FY26 on the Screener calculation, and trade receivables rose 57% against revenue growth of 46%.[6][1][3] In August 2026 the company said it remained confident of sustaining growth through FY27, yet Q1 revenue rose only 4.7% and the margin fell, which it blamed on transformer oil and copper.[4][1] The company did use its issue proceeds as stated: it reported no deviation from the stated purposes and had deployed ₹65.91 crore of the ₹80.25 crore by March 2026.[15][14]

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter sets two kinds of evidence side by side. Levels say where the company stands today, for example how much profit it earns on its capital. Trajectory says which way each number is moving. A business can pass on levels and still be a poor buy if the direction is wrong, and it can fail on levels yet be a good buy if the direction is sharply up. The ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses. Screener shows 24.4% for Marsons, which clears the 15% floor in the scoring rules.[1]

The five-year record is short because the company was dormant after insolvency. Standalone sales were ₹2 crore in FY22, ₹5 crore in FY23 and ₹6 crore in FY24, then ₹168 crore in FY25 and ₹245 crore in FY26.[2] Profit before tax was ₹42 crore in FY26, but net profit was ₹46 crore because the company booked a deferred-tax credit of ₹4.07 crore and paid current tax of only ₹0.001 crore.[3] Profit after a normal tax charge would have been closer to ₹33 crore, which is why the earnings per share used later in this report are lower than the headline figure.

The number

Normalised annual profit is about ₹32 crore, or ₹1.88 per share, compared with the ₹44 crore reported for the last twelve months.

How it was calculated

We took the last four quarters of profit before tax (9.19 + 6.48 + 18.54 + 7.39, about ₹41.6 crore)[1][4], applied a 22% tax charge, and divided by 17.21 crore shares.[3]

The inputs

The quarterly profits come from the filed results.[1][4] The 22% sits between the 19.8% rate in the June 2026 quarter[1] and the 25% rate that peers such as Shilchar and Voltamp pay.[24][25] The share count is 17.21 crore.[3]

The range

At a 19.8% tax rate the figure is ₹33.4 crore (₹1.94 per share). At 25% it is ₹31.2 crore (₹1.81 per share).

If the number is off

If the true tax rate stays near zero because of loss relief, earnings stay near ₹41 crore and the price-to-earnings multiple falls to about 49 at the close; our central values would then rise by about a quarter.

The number

Growing sales to ₹290 crore in FY27 would add about ₹20 crore to customer dues at today's 160 days, which would absorb about 44% of the year's central-case operating profit.

How it was calculated

Dues at 160 days of ₹290 crore are 290 × 160 ÷ 365 = ₹127.1 crore. Today's dues are ₹107.29 crore, so the increase is ₹19.8 crore. We compare it with central FY27 operating profit of ₹45.0 crore (sales of ₹290 crore at 15.5%): 19.8 ÷ 45.0 = 44%.

The inputs

Dues of ₹107.29 crore and sales of ₹245.03 crore come from the annual report.[3] The ₹290 crore of sales and 15.5% margin are our central assumptions explained in Chapter 5.

The range

If days fall to 120 (Voltamp reports 46 days), dues would be 290 × 120 ÷ 365 = ₹95.3 crore, which is ₹12.0 crore less than today, and cash would come back instead.

If the number is off

If customers keep paying at 160 days, operating cash flow will stay near zero for a third year even while profit grows, which weakens the case for any buy and supports a lower multiple.[25]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

The red-team bear case is that Marsons is a good small company that the market has priced as a great one, and the weak link is cash. Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could be wrong, and the four flaws below name them, say what evidence would prove each wrong, and when that evidence arrives. The next date is the September 2026 quarter result, due by mid-November.[1]

Flaw 1: profit is not turning into cash

Customers owed ₹107.29 crore at March 2026, up 57% in a year, and ₹27.9 crore (26%) was more than six months past its due date.[3] Stock rose from ₹14.95 crore to ₹58.01 crore, and the cash flow statement shows ₹39.00 crore tied up in receivables and ₹43.06 crore in inventories during FY26 alone.[3] Suppliers funded much of it: trade payables doubled from ₹24.99 crore to ₹56.05 crore. If suppliers or customers stop funding the cycle, a growth year turns into a cash drain. The rating agency lists delayed receivables as a trigger for a negative outlook.[6] Evidence against this flaw would be positive operating cash flow of ₹20 crore or more in FY27, and the half-year cash flow statement due with the September results.

Flaw 2: the price already pays for success

At ₹117.40 the stock trades at about 46 times reported profit and 62 times normalised profit, against 29.6 to 34.7 times for four peers.[39][24][25][26][27] Peers with far larger sales and cleaner cash flow trade lower. If the multiple fell to the peer median while profit grew as we expect, the share would be worth about ₹86, 27% below the price. The June 2026 quarter, with sales up 4.7% and profit down 26%, is the first data point that the multiple does not yet reflect.[1][4] Evidence against the flaw would be two consecutive quarters of sales growth above 20% with margins above 16%.

Flaw 3: firm-price orders and imported steel

The company said the June 2026 quarter was hurt by firm-price orders executed while transformer oil and copper costs rose.[4] About 90% of India's electrical steel is imported, and an anti-dumping case may lift costs again.[23][36] Marsons has less pricing power than TARIL, whose margin was 19.16% in the same quarter.[28] Evidence against this flaw would be a September-quarter margin back above 16% without a rise in advance payments or price clauses; the company's order terms will show up in its quarterly press release.

Flaw 4: governance and reporting quality need work

Several small signs add up. The statutory auditor was paid ₹1.50 lakh for FY26 for a company with ₹245 crore of sales.[3] An independent director resigned on 16 July 2026.[22] The consolidated audit report refers to a 'consolidated loss' for a year in which the group made a profit of ₹46.33 crore, which looks like a drafting slip but is not what a reader expects.[3] The Directors' report labels total income of ₹247.07 crore as 'net revenue from operations'.[3] The promoters hold their 53.65% through one private company, and institutions held only 1.24% in September 2026 (1.06% foreign plus 0.18% domestic).[3][1] Another 23.36% sits with bodies corporate; if these are the buyers from the 2024 issue at about ₹25 a share, they are holding a gain of about ₹297 crore at today's price (32.1 million shares × (117.40 - 25)), and selling by them would pressure the stock.[3][15] The filings do not say who these holders are, so that part is a risk, not a fact. Evidence against the flaw would be a clean audit-fee and independence picture in the next annual report and a stable shareholder register.
