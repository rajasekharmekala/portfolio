Chapter 0

Netweb Technologies India Limited (NSE: NETWEB) is India's only scaled, listed, Indian-origin original equipment manufacturer of high-end computing solutions. It designs, engineers and manufactures AI systems, supercomputers, private-cloud infrastructure, high-performance storage and data-centre servers under its Tyrone and Skylus brands. Q2 FY2027 revenue of ₹1,323 crore grew 335.7% year-on-year on strong AI-systems demand. The first-half PAT of ₹222 crore already exceeded the whole of FY2026.

The business is genuinely strong and genuinely cyclical. It earns a 37.5% ROCE and holds a ₹2,629 crore confirmed order book that rose even after billing ₹2,143 crore in a single half-year. It is net-debt-negative with ₹403 crore of unutilised QIP proceeds. But it is an integrator, not a technology owner. It buys the components, adds integration and support, and sells into a price-led market at a 13–15% operating margin. Its revenue is now 58% dependent on AI capital expenditure. Its historical cash conversion has been poor: roughly ₹382 crore of cumulative profit against ₹27 crore of operating cash flow from FY2021 to FY2025. Chapter 1

Netweb Technologies India Limited was founded in 1996 as a proprietorship by Sanjay Lodha and was incorporated as a company in 1999 in Faridabad, Haryana. Its founding bet was unfashionable at the time: rather than importing finished servers and reselling them, Netweb would design, engineer and assemble high-end computing machines inside India. For its first two decades the company stayed deliberately small and highly specialised, servicing national research institutions, defence laboratories and public-sector undertakings that needed bespoke supercomputers but could not buy them off a global catalogue.

That specialisation produced a sequence of nationally visible engineering milestones. In 2004 Netweb deployed KABRU at the Institute of Mathematical Sciences, Chennai, which entered the world's Top 500 supercomputers at commissioning. In 2013 it deployed PARAM Yuva-II at C-DAC, Pune, then among the most powerful machines in the country. In 2019 it deployed PARAM Ambar for ISRO, and in 2023 it delivered AIRAWAT, which was India's largest and fastest AI supercomputer at the time of commissioning. Over its life the company has installed more than 600 supercomputing systems and placed machines in the global Top 500 list seventeen times, and it has shipped over 10,000 accelerator or GPU-based AI systems.

The corporate evolution since 2019 has been a story of stacking two durable adjacent businesses on top of that supercomputing base. The first was private cloud and hyperconverged infrastructure, launched as a commercial line around 2015 and now sold under the Skylus brand. It let enterprises and banks run their own compute and storage estates instead of renting public cloud. The second, and the one that changed the company's economics, was AI systems. Netweb qualified under the Government of India's PLI 1.0 scheme for IT hardware in 2021. It qualified under PLI 2.0 for telecom and networking products in 2022. It listed on the NSE and BSE on 27 July 2023. In November 2023 it became a manufacturing partner for NVIDIA's Grace CPU Superchip and GH200 Grace Hopper MGX server designs, committing to build more than ten server variations under its Tyrone range.

The stock has lived through three distinct cycles. It listed on 27 July 2023 at ₹950 against an issue price of ₹500, a listing gain of about 89%. But the post-listing euphoria faded quickly. The shares drifted to an all-time low of ₹738.60 on 26 October 2023 as early investors exited and the market discounted a small, lumpy, project-based order book. The second cycle, spanning 2024 into 2025, was a slow re-rating. The AI narrative, the NVIDIA partnership and a visibly expanding order book pulled the stock steadily higher. The sovereign-AI order worth ₹1,734 crore, announced on 3 September 2025, was the turning point. That order, for NVIDIA Blackwell-based systems under the IndiaAI Mission, made the market treat Netweb as an AI-infrastructure proxy rather than a supercomputing integrator.

The third cycle is the one investors are living through now. The stock made an all-time high of ₹5,813 on 24 August 2026. That came days after the company completed its first equity raise since listing — a ₹1,200 crore qualified institutions placement priced at ₹4,790 per share on 20 August 2026. It then gave back a large part of that gain. Global anxiety about the durability of AI capital expenditure peaked, and the market began to scrutinise the company's rising inventory and lengthening cash-conversion cycle. By early November 2026 the share price was trading in the mid-₹4,000s, roughly 28% below the August peak, even as reported earnings were accelerating. History here is a warning against both extremes. This is a business whose operating results have compressed a decade of growth into three years, and whose share price has repeatedly swung far ahead of, and then back toward, those results.

The Pattern to Notice: Operating Leverage Arrives Late and Unevenly

Look at the shape of the two series in Table 1.1. Revenue multiplied nearly seventeen-fold from FY2019 to FY2026, but operating margin did not expand in a straight line — it climbed from 5% to 16% between FY2019 and FY2023, then settled back to 13% as the mix shifted toward very large, lower-margin AI systems deals. This is the signature of a systems integrator, not a software compounder: each new growth engine arrives with a different gross-margin profile, and the overhead base is shared across all of them.

That is precisely why the cash-flow line matters more than the profit line in this company's history. Netweb reported profits every year, but between FY2021 and FY2025 it converted a cumulative operating profit into negative free cash flow, because executing large orders meant buying expensive components — GPUs, memory, switches — long before customers paid. Only FY2026, when customer advances and faster collections arrived alongside the orders, turned the cash engine positive. Any future AI downcycle would first show itself in exactly this line.

Two structural pivots define where the company stands today. The first is the shift from a domestic research-and-defence installer to a commercial OEM with a full-stack portfolio. That portfolio now spans AI systems, HPC, private cloud and HCI, high-performance storage, data-centre servers and software/services, sold under the Tyrone and Skylus brands. The second is the geographic and balance-sheet pivot underway in 2026. The board approved a wholly owned R&D subsidiary in Taiwan to access hardware-design talent near the major silicon vendors' design centres. And the ₹1,200 crore QIP turned the company from net-debt-negative on operating cash to comfortably funded for a working-capital-heavy expansion. Both moves say the same thing. Management believes the AI-infrastructure window is a multi-year platform shift and is willing to finance inventory and hire ahead of it.

Chapter 2

Netweb sells six distinct product lines, but they share one commercial logic: the company engineers a complete computing system — compute, interconnect, storage, cooling, virtualisation and a software layer — rather than shipping a bare server. That integration is what lets a research institution, a bank or a sovereign cloud operator buy a working capability from Netweb instead of assembling one from components. The revenue mix has been transformed by AI over the last two years, and understanding each segment's role is essential to judging how durable the growth is.

Two years ago AI Systems was a minority of revenue; by Q2 FY2027 it is 58.4%. The old backbone — HPC and private cloud — remains a genuine ~37% of revenue and is the part of the business least exposed to the AI-spending cycle.

AI Systems. This is now the engine. Netweb takes NVIDIA's reference architectures — most recently the Blackwell-based GB200/GB300 NVL systems — and adds its own chassis, liquid-cooling design, storage, networking and management software to deliver a validated cluster. Concrete example: in FY2026 Netweb won the order to build the AI infrastructure for India's foundational large language model, deploying a system powered by more than 5,000 GPUs. The customer is a sovereign AI programme that needed a nationally housed training cluster. The problem was that no off-the-shelf imported solution combined the scale, the confidential-computing requirements and the local-manufacturing mandate. Netweb engineered, assembled and integrated the cluster in India and supports it in place. Units are sold per node. For the ₹1,734 crore sovereign-AI order announced on 3 September 2025, each node carries an 8-GPU HGX-B200 configuration under the Tyrone Camarero platform, priced per node with a full-system service contract.

High-Performance Computing (Supercomputing/HPC). This is Netweb's oldest and most defensible line. Concrete example: the PARAM Yuva-II supercomputer deployed at C-DAC, Pune, in 2013. The customer is a national research and development agency. The problem is running large-scale scientific simulation — weather, materials, genomics — that a general enterprise server cannot handle. Netweb designed a hybrid architecture combining conventional processors with accelerators, built the cluster, installed the scheduling and middleware layer and tuned it for the specific workloads. It was paid on a project basis with an ongoing maintenance contract. More recently, in 2025 Netweb deployed a marquee supercomputing system for ISRO at its Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre. Winning these projects is a reference-based business. The customer will pay a premium for a proven domestic integrator, but will also cancel if a delivery slips.

Private Cloud & Hyperconverged Infrastructure. Concrete example: a regulated Indian bank that cannot place customer data on a public cloud. State Bank of India and IDFC First Bank appeared for the first time among Netweb's disclosed customers in FY2026. The problem is data sovereignty, security and regulatory compliance. The product is the Skylus private cloud and HCI stack, which bundles compute, storage, networking, virtualisation and automation into an on-premise estate the bank controls. Pricing is per rack or per node plus a software subscription and support, with a multi-year service element. This is the segment where recurring service revenue and switching costs are highest. Once a bank runs its core infrastructure on Skylus, ripping it out is expensive.

High-Performance Storage, Data Centre Servers and Software & Services. These are smaller but strategically important. The Tyrone ParallelStor VELOX storage platform uses parallel file systems and GPUDirect Storage to feed GPU clusters. A concrete example is a large-scale AI training job where the GPUs starve unless storage can stream data at hundreds of gigabytes per second. The storage is therefore sold as an integral part of the AI cluster rather than as a standalone box. The company's 2017 deployment of servers across 204 locations in 23 states for a public-sector surveillance project illustrates the Data Centre Servers line. There the challenge is distributed deployment and remote management rather than raw compute. And the Software & Services line — KUBYTS container images, Skylus.ai orchestration, FMOcean data pipelines — is the glue that lets Netweb claim 'full-stack' rather than 'component supplier'.

Is the AI Segment a Moat or a Mix Shift?

Notice a subtlety in the mix. AI Systems jumped from a minority to nearly 60% of revenue in six quarters, but that jump came disproportionately from a handful of very large deals — two strategic orders worth roughly ₹2,184 crore announced in FY2026, plus the sovereign LLM cluster. Meanwhile HPC and private cloud, the slower, stickier businesses, have kept growing but now look small beside the AI line.

This matters because it splits the company's durability question in two. The HPC and private-cloud segments have decade-long customer relationships, 75.6% repeat revenue and genuine switching costs — that is where a defensible business lives. The AI segment has explosive demand and world-class technology partners, but it is also where competition is fiercest (global OEMs and Taiwanese ODMs sell comparable boxes), where the customer decides on price and delivery speed, and where a single quarter's revenue can swing on the timing of one order. The mix that looks most impressive on the income statement is not necessarily the mix that is most protected.

Management's own framing reinforces the concentration question. On the Q2 FY2027 call, an analyst asked how fast Netweb grows outside its top five customers. The CFO pointed to 449 new customers added in the first half alone. He stressed that the top-five and top-ten lists are not static cohorts but rotating groups of 25 to 30 accounts. That is a reasonable mitigation, but it is not the same as diversification. 75.6% of FY2026 revenue still came from repeat customers, which is a double-edged statistic. It proves retention but also implies a concentrated base. The honest reading is that Netweb has a diversified customer list but a concentrated revenue base, and the two should not be confused.

Chapter 3

Netweb's go-to-market model is a hybrid of direct enterprise selling, government and public-sector tendering, and channel-led distribution. All of it wraps around OEM partnerships with the silicon vendors. The company maintains manufacturing and R&D facilities in Delhi-NCR (Faridabad and Gurugram) and Hyderabad, with eighteen offices across India and an office in Singapore. Large sovereign and institutional orders are typically won directly through tenders or negotiated strategic contracts. Enterprise orders arrive both directly and through distributor and system-integrator partners. For example, Netweb's 2026 sovereign cyber-secure compute facility for CERT-IN was disclosed as processed through the company's distributor. Because the customer base is dominated by government, defence, research, banking and large enterprise buyers, the sales cycle is long, qualification-heavy and reference-driven.

The supply chain is the crux of the model and the crux of the risk. Netweb is an OEM, not a chipmaker. It buys GPUs from NVIDIA, CPUs from Intel and AMD, memory from Samsung, and power and cooling components from partners such as Vertiv. It then integrates them with its own chassis, board designs and software. That means its gross margin is set by the spread between component cost and the system price it can command. Its working capital is set by how long it must hold expensive components before delivery. Management has been explicit that it is deliberately carrying more inventory. Inventory days stood at 117 at September 2026 versus 86 at March 2026. The purpose is to secure critical components against longer lead times and to safeguard execution of a ₹2,629 crore order book. The flip side is equally explicit: if component prices fall or customers defer, that inventory loses value.

Pricing varies by segment. AI systems are priced per node or per rack with a service wrapper. Management has said the gross-margin profile of a very large deal is structurally different from a small one. Quarterly gross margin is therefore a function of deal mix and should not be read sequentially. HPC supercomputing projects are priced on a fixed-scope project basis plus a multi-year maintenance contract. Private cloud and HCI carry a hardware-plus-software-subscription-plus-support structure, the closest thing in the portfolio to recurring revenue. Standard execution runs 10 to 16 weeks per order. Billing on large institutional orders is milestone-and-advance based. That is why receivables improved to 62 days in Q2 FY2027 while inventory ballooned. Customers pay promptly once a milestone is met, but Netweb has already funded the components.

The cash-conversion mechanics deserve particular care. They are where this business differs most from a software compounder. Receivable days were 112 in FY2026 on Screener's annualised basis and improved to 62 in Q2 FY2027. Inventory days rose to 117. The cash-conversion cycle was 94 days in Q2 FY2027 against 84 days for FY2026. Management guides the cycle to run between 90 and 110 days for the foreseeable future. Against that, customer advances on large strategic orders and the ability to stretch payables have kept the company net-debt-negative most of the time. Unit Economics: What a ₹100 AI Order Actually Earns

Strip the project accounting away and Netweb's economics per order are those of an advanced integrator. A large AI system carries roughly 13–15% operating EBITDA margin — so on a ₹100 system, the company keeps around ₹13–14 of operating profit. From that it funds engineering, R&D, sales, interest on the working-capital borrowing used to buy the components, and tax, leaving roughly ₹8–9 of bottom-line profit if collections are timely.

The critical variable is not the margin percentage; it is the time between paying the component supplier and being paid by the customer. If that gap is 90–110 days, the company must finance a full system's worth of components from its own balance sheet and its borrowings for a quarter. That is why a ₹1,200 crore equity raise was necessary even while the company reported record profits: growth, not profitability, is what consumes the cash. The unit economics work as long as order intake keeps growing and customers keep paying on schedule; they reverse sharply if either stops.

Two structural features of the distribution model are worth flagging as durable positives. First, the OEM relationships are multi-sourced and chip-agnostic. Netweb partners with NVIDIA, AMD and Intel, so it is not hostage to a single accelerator roadmap. It can adapt its designs as the market shifts between GPU and alternative architectures. Management described a two-to-three-year product roadmap and the ability to re-schematic quickly if component supply shifts. Second, the company's qualification under the PLI 1.0 and 2.0 schemes gives it a structural cost and eligibility advantage. It is one of the few Indian OEMs manufacturing IT hardware at scale. On government and sovereign tenders where local manufacturing is mandated, the customer often cannot legally or politically award the contract to an importer. That eligibility is the distribution moat.

Chapter 4

Netweb competes in the Indian data-centre server market. Mordor Intelligence sizes it at roughly USD 6.55 billion in 2025, growing to about USD 10.94 billion by 2032 at a 7.6% compound rate. Rack servers hold about 37% of that revenue and x86 processors about 61%. The longer-term context is a national capacity build-out. India's live data-centre capacity grew from about 296 MW in 2016 to over 1.6 GW by 2025. Beneath those headline numbers, the market splits into two very different arenas. Netweb sits in both.

The first arena is the commodity server market. There, global OEMs such as Dell, HPE and Lenovo, together with IBM, Fujitsu and the Taiwanese original design manufacturers (Wistron, Foxconn, Quanta and Pegatron), compete on price, supply-chain scale and brand. Here Netweb is a small player with no cost advantage and no proprietary silicon. Its only edges are local manufacturing eligibility, faster service and the ability to customise. The second arena is integrated high-end computing — supercomputing, sovereign AI clusters and regulated private cloud. There, the buyer needs a validated full system, a domestic vendor for political and security reasons, and long-term on-site support. Here Netweb is genuinely competitive, because the global OEMs often bid a standard product while Netweb engineers to the customer's workload and manufactures in-country.

Historical Failure-Mode & Base-Rate Twin: Sun Microsystems (United States (1982–2010))

Shared Vulnerability: Sun built genuinely superior integrated systems — its own SPARC microprocessors, the Solaris operating system, the Java platform and complete workstations and servers — and earned premium margins for two decades on the strength of that full-stack integration and deep relationships with research, telecom and government customers. Dilution, Dividends and the Quality of Capital Allocation

Netweb has funded its growth with a mix of internal accruals, short-term debt and equity. The dividend is token — a 0.07% yield, with a payout that fell to 8% of earnings in FY2026 — which is correct for a company that can reinvest at 30–40% growth.

The deeper question is whether the equity raises were necessary and well-timed. The QIP was priced at ₹4,790 per share in August 2026, near the top of the stock's range, and was explicitly for working capital. Raising equity at a high multiple to fund inventory that supports a ₹2,629 crore order book is defensible capital allocation. The counter-argument — which sceptics make — is that a business this profitable should not need to sell shares to fund routine growth. Both are true: the growth genuinely consumes cash, and the balance sheet is now strong enough to absorb it. The test will be whether the ₹1,200 crore is deployed into orders that earn the guided margin or into inventory that ages.

Pulling the forensics together, the earnings quality verdict is nuanced rather than damning. Netweb's profits are real, audited, and backed by genuine revenue; its customers are creditworthy and its receivable days are improving; it is net-debt-negative and earns returns above its cost of capital. What is less clean is the conversion of those profits into cash, which was poor for four of the last eight years and only normalised in FY2026, and the rising inventory that any future slowdown would convert into a write-down risk. This is the profile of a fast-growing, capital-hungry integrator with sound accounting and a genuinely cyclical cash engine — not a fraud, and not a fortress.
