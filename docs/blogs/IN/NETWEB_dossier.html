1. What the company does

Netweb Technologies India Limited, based in Faridabad near Delhi, designs and builds the large computer systems that run supercomputers, private clouds and AI projects. A fair comparison is a company that makes the engines for data centres rather than the data centres themselves. It was founded in 1999 and listed on the NSE and BSE in July 2023.[5] Its customers include government agencies, research institutes, universities, technology services firms and newer cloud providers. CRISIL, the credit rating agency, names the Indian Institutes of Technology, ISRO, Railtel, the University of Delhi, Infosys, Yotta, Zoho and Tata Consultancy Services.[15]

The company sells hardware that it designs and assembles itself, together with the software that manages it. A typical order is a cluster: many servers fitted with graphics processing units (GPUs), wired together, cooled and loaded with management software. Revenue is booked when control of the goods passes to the customer, generally on delivery,[1] so one large order lands as a lump in the quarter it ships. The company groups its sales into three pillars. AI systems are the newest. Supercomputing is called HPC, short for high-performance computing. Private cloud and hyperconverged infrastructure, called HCI, packs computing, storage and networking into one appliance.[6] Storage, data centre servers, software, services and spares are small lines.

The mix changed fast. In the quarter to June 2026 (the first quarter of the financial year 2026-27, written Q1 FY27), AI systems brought in ₹510.6 crore, which is 62.3 percent of revenue. Private cloud and HCI brought in ₹135.3 crore and HPC ₹125.3 crore.[6] In FY25, AI was ₹169.4 crore or 14.7 percent of ₹1,149.0 crore revenue, and in FY26 it was ₹947.8 crore or 43.4 percent of ₹2,183.6 crore.[6][10] In two years the company moved from mainly building supercomputers to mainly supplying AI servers, and that move explains most of the growth in the numbers later in this book.

Who pays, and when, shapes the cash story. On the May 2025 call the chairman said about half the business came from government and half from enterprises.[14] CRISIL says buyers usually pay within 70 to 90 days, and that 30 to 40 percent of a year's revenue arrives in the last quarter of the financial year, which leaves large amounts owed by customers at each March year end.[15] In November 2025 the company announced two national-scale AI orders worth about ₹2,184 crore, to be delivered by FY27, and it reports them separately from its regular business.[13]

Why buyers choose Netweb

Netweb says it is one of a small number of manufacturers worldwide that design and build the newest generation of AI GPU systems under an OEM partnership with Nvidia, and that it also works with AMD, Intel, Samsung and Vertiv. OEM means original equipment manufacturer, a company that builds systems under its own brand using partner chips.[6] It runs a factory at Faridabad, employs 125 people in research and development, and holds 17 registered or applied patents and designs.[6] Its supercomputers have been listed 17 times in the world's Top 500 list, and it has installed more than 600 private cloud systems.[6]

Government policy pushes buyers toward Indian-made systems. The company qualified for the production-linked incentive scheme for IT hardware.[6] Management says the next phase of the National Supercomputing Mission moves from buying to designing and building in India, which makes domestic design a buying criterion.[5][6] The IndiaAI Mission has an outlay of about ₹10,300 crore over FY25 to FY29 according to the company's presentation, and demand from cloud providers building GPU capacity adds to it.[6] These are claims by the company and its advisers. Chapter 2 tests them against numbers and against outside facts.

What the order book says about the next year

On 30 June 2026 the confirmed order book was ₹2,506.9 crore. A further ₹848.0 crore was in L1 status, meaning Netweb had been declared the lowest qualified bidder but had not yet received the purchase order. The company also counts a pipeline of ₹10,410 crore of opportunities it is chasing.[6] Management says about 60 percent of the pipeline converts to orders within 18 to 24 months, and that orders now take 16 to 20 weeks to execute, compared with 8 to 12 weeks before.[7][25] The order book is a promise from buyers, not revenue yet, and some orders can be delayed or cut.

Put next to the sales figures, the order book plus L1 of ₹3,355 crore is about 1.5 times the whole of FY26 revenue of ₹2,184 crore. That is a strong start to a year, and it is the main reason the report treats near-term growth as likely. It is not the same as profit or cash, which chapters 3 and 5 take up.[6][1]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ROCE — return on capital employed: operating profit before interest divided by the money invested in the business (equity plus debt). The company calculates it on the average of the opening and closing figures.[6]
  • EBITDA — earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges. Netweb's own "operating EBITDA" is profit before tax plus depreciation and finance cost, minus other income.[6]
  • EPS — earnings per share: the company's profit divided by the number of shares. A rise in the share count lowers it even when profit is unchanged.
  • RSI — relative strength index: a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been rising or falling over 14 trading days. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR — average true range: the typical daily price swing over 14 trading days, in rupees. Support distances in this book are measured in these units.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average — the average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 trading days. Each is a slowly moving line that the price tends to respect.
  • Stage 2 and Stage 4 — the two ends of the Weinstein trend scale. Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend with price above rising averages, and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend with price below falling averages. Stage 3 is a topping or pullback phase between them.
  • base — a sideways price range that a stock builds before its next move, like a floor. In this book the 63-day base low is the lowest price of the last 63 trading days.
  • pivot — the top of a base, or the level a price must clear to start a new advance.
  • breakout — a move above a base or pivot, normally on higher trading volume.
  • stop loss — a pre-set price at which the holding is sold because the reason for owning it no longer holds. Here it sits just below a recent low.
  • limit order — an order that fills only at your price or better, so it does not chase a rising price.
  • entry zone — the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry and reward-to-risk — the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, both measured from the same entry price. A ratio of 2.5 means the target is 2.5 times as far away as the stop.[35]
  • operating margin — profit from running the business, before interest and tax, as a share of revenue.
  • debt-to-equity — borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Below 0.70 is comfortable in the scoring rules, and 1.20 or more is a hard red line for a non-financial company.[35]
  • net debt — borrowings (and lease obligations, in the company's definition) minus free cash. A negative number means net cash.[6]
  • price-to-earnings — the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit. A ratio of 100 means the market pays ₹100 for each ₹1 of trailing profit.
  • free cash flow (abbreviated FCF) — free cash flow: cash from operations minus spending on plant and equipment, the cash left after running and maintaining the business.
  • cash flow from operations — the cash the business itself generates in a period, after changes in what customers owe and what the company owes suppliers, and after income tax.
  • guidance — management's own forecast for a coming period.
  • measured move — a price target found by adding the height of a base to its top. This book does not use it, because the earnings-based target in chapter 5 is better supported.
  • dilution — the rise in the number of shares when new shares are issued, which shrinks each existing share's slice of profit.
  • capital expenditure — spending on factories, machines and other long-lived assets.
  • working capital — the money tied up in day-to-day operations: inventory and amounts customers owe, less amounts owed to suppliers and customer advances.
  • consensus estimate — the average of professional analysts' forecasts for a number such as next year's profit.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A fair summary of the competitive position is that Netweb has a real design and customer advantage in a small niche, earns margins far above its direct rivals, and carries risks that come from a few big customers, a few big suppliers and a business that needs a lot of money tied up in stock and receivables. Each of those claims is tested below against numbers or outside facts rather than taken from the company's own description.

On advantage, the evidence is mixed in a useful way. CRISIL describes Netweb as one of the few players that provide end-to-end supercomputing and cloud solutions, with more than 300 made-in-India HPC units installed, and says its research team spends 2 to 3 percent of revenue on software and design. Long relationships with Nvidia, Samsung, AMD and Intel give it access to chip roadmaps 9 to 12 months ahead.[15] The company's own deck lists a 125-person research team and 17 supercomputer entries on the Top 500 list.[6] These are signs of engineering depth that a simple box assembler would not have.

On profit, the figures back the claim of a premium. Operating EBITDA margin was 14.16 percent in FY24, 13.84 percent in FY25 and 13.04 percent in FY26, and 14.70 percent in Q1 FY27.[6] Rashi Peripherals, an Indian distributor of technology hardware, earned an operating margin of about 2.9 percent in FY26.[29] Super Micro Computer, a large US maker of AI servers, earned an operating margin of 7.09 percent over the last twelve months.[32] Management says its competitors sit in single-digit margins, which the two comparisons support.[14] A margin that is roughly twice the global server maker's is the clearest sign of a differentiated product mix, with design, software and services in the price and not only the box.

What the margin does not show is stability. The company says the deliberate pricing policy is value for money rather than taking advantage of scarcity, and that very large orders carry a profit-before-tax margin about 2 percentage points lower than ordinary orders.[7][12] Because AI orders are now 62 percent of sales, a few large orders can pull the whole margin up or down in a single quarter, which is one reason the scoring in chapter 2B tests the margin against a floor and not only against last year.

Where the strength looks real

The strongest evidence is financial. The return on capital employed was 37.5 percent in FY26 on the company's own definition and 48.2 percent annualised for the June 2026 quarter.[6] CRISIL rates the company A+ with a stable outlook for bank facilities of ₹2,420 crore, up from ₹700 crore, and calls liquidity strong.[15] Interest cover, meaning profit before interest and tax divided by interest paid, was 22.3 times in FY26 and the borrowing ratio was 0.38 times, both well inside the scoring limits.[15] Repeat customers produced 98.9 percent of Q1 FY27 revenue, which suggests buyers come back rather than shop around each time.[6]

The second strength is that capacity is not the constraint. The factory runs at 65 to 70 percent utilisation and was built for revenue of ₹3,000 crore or more, so growth up to about 37 percent above the FY26 level needs only light capital expenditure, in management's words.[11][7] Gross fixed asset turnover, which is revenue divided by factory and equipment at cost, was 43.9 times annualised in June 2026.[6] Low need for new factories is what lets a fast-growing company show a return on capital this high, and it is a feature of a design-led assembler rather than a heavy manufacturer.

Where the strength is thinner than it looks

Concentration is the main weakness and it is rising. The five largest customers were 50.3 percent of revenue in FY24, 50.8 percent in FY25, 62.2 percent in FY26 and 81.2 percent in Q1 FY27, and the ten largest were 87.7 percent in Q1 FY27.[6] CRISIL puts the long-run level at 65 to 70 percent for the top five customers and says the top three suppliers account for 65 to 70 percent of purchases, with many parts imported from Taiwan.[15] A cancelled or delayed order from one large buyer therefore moves a quarter's results, and a supply problem at one chip or memory supplier can stop shipments.

Money tied up in operations is the second weakness. CRISIL notes that current assets (stock, receivables and similar items) equalled 268 days of revenue at March 2026, up from 205 days a year earlier, with 112 days of customer dues and 155 days of inventory.[15] The company's own measures are lower because they are calculated differently, but they point the same way: the cash conversion cycle, which is receivable days plus inventory days minus payable days, rose from 84 days in March 2026 to 96 days in June 2026.[6] The third weakness is a shrinking promoter stake. Promoters held 71.00 percent in September 2025 and 64.16 percent in September 2026, after a block sale in February 2026 and a share issue in August 2026.[24][17]

Demand, the cycle and what could break it

Demand has two legs: government programmes and private cloud providers building GPU capacity. The government leg is real but uneven. A newspaper report from 2 October 2026 says the IndiaAI Mission has access to about 30,000 GPUs against commitments of 45,000, because several private providers could not deliver their promised capacity as equipment costs rose, and that the Centre is adding government-owned capacity of about 3,000 GPUs with C-DAC and about 10,000 through a UAE-funded supercomputer.[28] For Netweb the first fact is a warning that cloud buyers can fall behind on their own plans, and the second is a possible source of new orders.

The company says global shortages of memory and other parts will not ease for a year or so, and that it holds extra inventory on purpose to hedge.[7] That raises costs and ties up cash, but management says it passes new prices through on new orders. Analysts quoted on the Q1 call also raised the risk that the AI spending boom may normalise.[25] Nothing in the filings can settle that question. What can be measured is how much of the next year's revenue is already ordered, which chapter 2B turns into a score.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record on growth and margins is good. In May 2025 it guided for 35 to 40 percent revenue growth a year and a 13 to 14 percent operating EBITDA margin, and for a profit margin near 10 percent.[14] FY26 revenue grew 90.0 percent, the margin was 13.04 percent, and profit after tax was 9.3 percent of total income, a little under the target.[1][6][10] The company also said in May 2025 that AI could reach about 20 percent of revenue by the end of that year or early the next. It reached 43.4 percent in FY26. That is a large beat, and it also shows that management's forecast for its own product mix was far too low.

The record on cash and funding is weaker. In May 2025 an analyst noted that the company had been expected to convert 30 to 35 percent of EBITDA into operating cash for FY25, yet operating cash flow was negative at ₹13.2 crore; management replied that this was a cost of fast growth and that the cash conversion cycle would not improve much.[14][2] In November 2025 it described the extra short-term working capital needs of the large orders as transitionary and very short term, backed by customer advances and letters of credit.[13] By June 2026 the company reported net debt of ₹199.9 crore, and on 20 August 2026 it raised ₹1,200 crore of new equity for working capital.[6][16][27] Management did warn on the Q1 call that it might raise capital, and it had asked shareholders for an enabling approval, so the raise is consistent with what it said. The need was larger and longer than the November description suggested.[7]

The reverse DCF: is profit a fair stand-in for cash?

The reverse DCF treats each year's profit as if it were cash that could be paid out. For Netweb that is generous, because over three years only 45 paise of each rupee of profit became operating cash, and the best single year, FY26, owed its cash flow to customer advances and supplier credit.[1] If a fair cash measure were used, the implied growth would be higher, about 36 percent on FY26 free cash flow. The weakest assumption is therefore that profit will turn into cash at a normal rate once the business stops growing so fast.

The evidence that would show it wrong is the cash flow statement for the half year to September 2026. Regulations require a cash flow statement only in the half-year and full-year results, so it is expected, not confirmed, in the 24 October release, and a quarterly figure for Q1 FY27 was not published.[35] If half-year operating cash flow is clearly positive and larger than the ₹171.5 crore reported for FY26 while inventory days stay near 110, the cash concern eases. If it is negative, which is plausible after free cash fell from ₹406.6 crore to ₹73.4 crore in one quarter, the case for a lower multiple strengthens.[6][1]

The FY28 profit forecast: will the order book convert at a 13.5 percent margin?

The forecast needs revenue of ₹3,700 crore in FY27 and ₹4,736 crore in FY28, with the margin steady at 13.5 percent.
