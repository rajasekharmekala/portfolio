1. What the company does

Netweb Technologies India Limited is a Faridabad-based company, founded in 1999, that designs, builds and installs what it calls high-end computing solutions. In plain terms it makes the large, specialised computers that universities, space agencies, banks, telecom firms and now national AI programmes buy when an ordinary office server is not enough. The range covers supercomputers (known as high-performance computing, or HPC), private cloud and hyperconverged infrastructure (a rack that bundles computing, storage and networking into one box), AI systems built around graphics processors from Nvidia and AMD, storage, and data-centre servers.[6][1] The company employed 569 permanent staff at 31 March 2026 and says 125 of its people work in research and development.[1][6]

Who pays, and for what? Every sale is a one-off purchase of equipment, so there is no subscription income that repeats by itself. Buyers named in the CRISIL rating note include IIT campuses, ISRO, Railtel, the University of Delhi, Infosys, Yotta, Zoho and Tata Consultancy Services.[17] Government and defence work matters most today: The company calls its largest IndiaAI Mission orders strategic orders. ICICI Securities puts their original value at about ₹2,180 crore, and Netweb's own slide shows ₹1,625 crore of them still to ship at 31 March 2026.[22][9] Once an order is signed, delivery takes 16 to 20 weeks, which the company says has lengthened from the 8 to 12 weeks it used to quote.[7]

The money comes from the gap between what Netweb pays for parts and what it charges for a finished, tested and installed system. In FY26 it spent ₹1,728 crore on materials, net of the change in stock, against revenue of ₹2,184 crore, which leaves a gross margin (the share of sales left after direct material cost) of about 20.8 percent. After staff and other running costs, the company's own measure of operating profit, called operating EBITDA, was 13.04 percent of revenue.[1][6] Operating EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, with other income removed. Netweb needs little plant to do this: gross fixed assets turned over 43.9 times in the June 2026 quarter on an annualised basis, so each rupee of factory and equipment supported ₹43.9 of sales.[6]

The business has one structural feature that shapes every later chapter. Customers are few and large. The top five customers made up 81.20 percent of revenue from operations in the June 2026 quarter, up from 62.23 percent in FY26 and 50.84 percent in FY25.[6] CRISIL, the rating agency, also notes that the top three suppliers account for 65 to 70 percent of purchases and that many components are imported from Taiwan.[17] So Netweb sits between a few powerful suppliers (Nvidia, Intel, AMD, Samsung) and a few powerful buyers, and it earns its 13 to 14 percent operating margin by being the Indian builder that can design, integrate and support the whole system.

How an order turns into cash

Netweb counts demand in three buckets. The order book is purchase orders already received but not yet delivered; it stood at ₹2,507 crore on 30 June 2026. L1 means the company has been declared the lowest qualified bidder, or chosen as the preferred vendor, but the formal order has not yet arrived; that was ₹848 crore. The pipeline is the total of live opportunities, ₹10,410 crore, and management expects about 60 percent of it to convert over 18 to 24 months.[6][7] Think of the order book as signed contracts, L1 as handshakes and the pipeline as conversations.

Cash arrives unevenly. CRISIL says Netweb books 30 to 40 percent of its yearly revenue in the last quarter and usually collects payment within 70 to 90 days, while it must hold stock early because most raw material is imported.[17] Customer advances smooth this: the audited balance sheet shows ₹413 crore of money paid in advance by customers at 31 March 2026, against ₹2.5 crore a year earlier.[1] That advance is a loan from customers that reverses as the machines ship, which matters for the cash-flow story in chapter 3.

Cyclicality and the lumpy part of the revenue

Hardware refresh cycles and government programmes drive the timing. The company has grown revenue by 80, 63, 59 and 90 percent in FY23, FY24, FY25 and FY26, from ₹247 crore in FY22 to ₹2,184 crore in FY26.[4][3][2][1] Part of the FY26 jump is the strategic order: Netweb states that FY26 included ₹559 crore of strategic-order revenue, so growth without it was about 41 percent, which matches the 35 to 40 percent it had guided.[9][11] The June 2026 quarter included about ₹430 crore of the same order, which means growth without it was near 29 percent, assuming the year-earlier quarter had none, as its flat sales around ₹300 crore a quarter suggest.[7][6] That second figure is our inference from two company statements, not a number Netweb reports.

In July 2026 management said it would stop reporting strategic and regular orders separately because strategic orders are now the new normal.[22][7] That is a fair business view if India keeps buying AI machines at this pace, but it also removes the cleanest view investors had of what the base business is doing.

Who owns the company and how it was funded

The Lodha family founded and still controls the company. Promoters held 64.16 percent at September 2026, down from 66.98 percent at June 2026, with no shares pledged to lenders; the fall came from dilution, because the promoters' own share count was unchanged at 38,141,542 while the company issued new shares.[19][18] On 25 August 2026 Netweb completed its first equity raise since listing, a qualified institutions placement (a sale of new shares to large institutions) of about ₹1,200 crore at ₹4,790 a share, allotting 2,505,219 shares, and said the money is for working capital.[20] Before that, the promoters' share count fell from 40,221,477 at September 2025 to 38,141,542 at June 2026, a sale of 2,079,935 shares, which is why their stake slid from 71.00 percent at December 2025 to 66.98 percent at March 2026.[28][19]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Netweb's competitive position rests on one fact. It is the Indian-owned company that designs the system, builds it in Faridabad and installs it. Systems it delivered include the Airawat supercomputer and machines for ISRO and the Centre for Development of Advanced Computing. The company reports 17 registered or applied patents and designs, up from 9 at the start of FY26.[6][22] It also describes itself as one of a select few makers worldwide that design the latest Nvidia GPU systems under an OEM partnership.[6] The National Supercomputing Mission now asks for systems designed and built in India. That local record is a real advantage, and it means Netweb need not be the cheapest bidder to win.[6]

The evidence that the advantage is real shows up in four places. First, return on capital employed (ROCE) was 37.53 percent for FY26 on the company's definition. ROCE is operating profit before interest divided by the money invested in the business. Our own recomputation from audited statements gives 37.5 percent.[6][1] Second, repeat customers gave 98.92 percent of June-quarter revenue.[6] Third, operating margin stayed between 12 and 15 percent for five quarters while revenue rose almost three-fold, which suggests the company can pass on component costs.[15][16][5] Fourth, CRISIL rates the bank facilities Crisil A+/Stable, with interest cover of 22.3 times in FY26.[17]

The evidence against the advantage is just as specific. First, customers. Five buyers took 81.20 percent of June-quarter revenue, so one delayed order moves the whole result.[6] Second, suppliers. The top three suppliers provide 65 to 70 percent of purchases, and memory and GPU supply is tight worldwide.[17][7] Third, margin structure. Gross margin has fallen two years in a row. The 13 to 14 percent operating margin held only because staff and overhead costs grew slower than revenue.[3][2][1] Fourth, market share. Netweb does not publish a share figure, and three searches found no independent one. So the claim that it leads the Indian AI server market cannot be checked.[7]

Competition is largely foreign. Server makers such as Dell, HPE and Lenovo sell similar machines. Indian box-sellers and distributors sell Nvidia systems without designing them. On the July 2026 call, management said Netweb does not chase volume or box selling. It said it wins on end-to-end solutions, and that 62 percent of June-quarter revenue from AI shows the numbers speaking.[7] That is a fair argument about the past quarter. It is not evidence about the price Netweb will get in 2028, when more local builders may have Blackwell-class designs.

Where the demand comes from

Netweb's presentation, citing a 1Lattice report, sizes India's AI infrastructure market at ₹115 billion in FY26 and ₹283 billion by FY30, a rise of about 25 percent a year, and says the IndiaAI Mission has an allocation of about ₹103 billion over FY25 to FY29.[6] These are market-size forecasts from a vendor-supplied source, so treat them as direction, not fact. The firmer evidence is Netweb's own order book of ₹2,507 crore plus ₹848 crore of L1 at 30 June 2026, which together equal 1.24 times trailing twelve-month revenue of ₹2,702 crore.[6][9] Management also says demand should stay strong for about two years, which is a time limit worth noting.[7]

Quality of the reported numbers

Two accounting choices help a reader trust the profit line. Netweb expenses all research and development as it is spent, with a team of 125 people, instead of capitalising it, so the balance sheet carries no software asset that could later be written off.[7] The auditor, S S Kothari Mehta and Co. LLP, gave an unmodified opinion on the FY26 accounts, and its main audit focus was whether year-end revenue was booked in the right period.[1] Against that, Netweb changed its inventory valuation method in FY26 from first-in, first-out to moving weighted average cost, and restated FY25 profit from ₹1,144.75 million to ₹1,137.51 million, which the company calls not material.[6][1][2] That change is small, but it is the type of accounting change worth watching in a company whose stock of components rose from ₹221 crore to ₹805 crore in one year.[1]

What this means for an owner

The business is real, profitable and well placed in a national priority, so the question for an owner is not whether it is good but how much of its quality is already in a price near 100 times earnings, and how much the cash flow and the concentration reduce what can safely be paid. The next chapter turns these strengths and weaknesses into five scored operating tests and checks whether management did what it said it would do.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 of the underwriting asks which few numbers actually run this business, and whether they are healthy. For a company that assembles expensive hardware from imported parts, three questions matter most: is there enough signed work (order cover), is the margin holding as the product mix changes (gross margin and operating margin), and does profit turn into cash instead of sitting in stock and unpaid bills (cash conversion). A fourth question is how many customers share the risk (concentration). We chose five measures that answer those questions, scored each PASS, WATCH or FAIL against a threshold set before reading the result, and cited every measured value.[32]

The thresholds are our own and are stated in the table so that a reader can disagree with them. Order cover passes at 0.8 times trailing-year revenue or more. Operating margin passes when the full-year figure sits inside or above management's 13 to 14 percent guidance. Gross margin passes when it moves less than one point over two years, and is a WATCH for a drop of one to five points. Cash conversion passes at 70 percent of operating EBITDA or more over three years, is a WATCH from 40 to 70 percent and fails below 40 percent. Customer concentration passes when the top five buyers take 50 percent or less of revenue, is a WATCH up to 70 percent and fails above that.

One choice needs a plain explanation, because the verdict depends on it. Layer 1 tests cash conversion as cash flow from operations divided by profit after tax, which is 0.447 over FY24 to FY26 and sits between the 0.40 red line and the 0.70 floor. The Layer 2 KPI divides the same cash flow by operating EBITDA instead, which gives 32.3 percent and falls below 40 percent. We use EBITDA for the KPI because it is the measure management itself quotes when it talks about cash conversion, and because profit after tax is lower than EBITDA by interest, tax and depreciation, which flatters the ratio. A third version removes the ₹4,103 million rise in customer advances in FY26. On that basis cash flow over profit after tax is minus 0.59 and cash flow over EBITDA is minus 43 percent, and both fail on any threshold.[1][2][3][6] So two of the three versions fail and the third, the reported ratio over profit after tax, is a WATCH. We say so openly: if only the reported ratio over profit after tax were used, the KPI would be a WATCH, only one KPI (customer concentration) would fail, and the replacement trigger would not fire on the KPI count alone.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has given the market a lot of numbers in the last two years, which makes a promise-by-promise audit possible. Netweb gives guidance once a year and says it will follow through, and on growth and margin it largely has.[12] The track record is weakest on cash, and on one point of communication, the QIP.

The clearest hit is growth. In May 2025 the company guided for a 35 to 40 percent revenue CAGR (compound annual growth rate) and said it would keep that guidance for regular business when the strategic order arrived.[11][13] FY26 revenue grew 90 percent, and after removing the ₹559 crore of strategic-order revenue it grew about 41 percent, so both readings beat the guide.[1][9] Margin is similar: the 13 to 14 percent guide was met for the year at 13.04 percent, and management's January 2026 warning that strategic orders would carry about 200 basis points lower PBT margin did not show up in the June 2026 quarter, when operating EBITDA margin was 14.70 percent with ₹430 crore of the strategic order shipped.[14][6][7]

The record on cash is weaker. In May 2025, after operating cash flow turned negative in FY25, management said that at 35 to 40 percent growth the company would definitely be cash positive, and put the cash conversion cycle at 70 to 90 days.[11] FY26 growth was 90 percent, not 35 to 40, and operating cash flow was positive at ₹171 crore, but only because customers advanced ₹413 crore.[1] The cycle was 84 days in March 2026, then 96 in June 2026, and in May 2026 the stated range was widened to 90 to 110 days.[6][8] Moving the range is not a breach of a promise, but a reader should note that the yardstick moved when the result approached the old edge.

The pattern is a management team that guides low on growth and mix and then beats, which is good for credibility on those lines. It also guides loosely on cash and capital, which is where the risks in this report sit. The table cannot say whether the next beat will repeat once the strategic order ends.

Put together, the Layer 2 result is 2 of 5 PASS with two FAIL scores. The weak-form REPLACE rests on exactly those two FAILs: cash conversion on the cash flow over operating EBITDA measure (32.3 percent), and top-five customer concentration (81.20 percent). Under the portfolio rules, failing two operating KPIs is one trigger for REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER, and a holding that misses the 4-of-5 hurdle cannot be labelled KEEP_COMPOUNDING.

The two-FAIL basis is defensible but not beyond argument. Concentration is a clear fail on any reading. Cash conversion fails on the EBITDA measure and on the advances-adjusted measure, which is the stricter and more honest one because the FY26 positive cash flow came from customers paying ₹413 crore in advance. It is a WATCH only on the reported ratio over profit after tax. A reader who prefers that measure should treat the verdict as a review rather than a replacement: one KPI fails, the asymmetry and chart tests still fail, and the next step is the October results.

The caveat is that both FAIL scores trace to growth that is faster than the company can fund. A business that grows revenue 172 percent in a year needs to buy parts first and collect later, and the customers who pay least promptly are large government and enterprise buyers. If the June-quarter cash outflow is a one-time stock build rather than a pattern, the cash score improves. The October 24 results are the first document that will show whether it did, because they carry the half-year cash flow statement, as the Q2 FY26 results did a year earlier.[29] The test is cash flow from operations after removing the change in customer advances. Concentration will not improve before the March 2027 annual report.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in the previous chapter rests on a weakest assumption, and good analysis names it. This chapter does that for each number, states the evidence that would show it wrong, says when that evidence arrives, and then makes the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, because the report's verdict is a rules-based call that could itself be mistaken.

Cash conversion of 0.447: the assumption that cash conversion will improve once shipments convert

The weakest assumption behind the red-line pass is that cash will arrive from customers as machines ship, so that FY26's reliance on ₹413 crore of advances was a one-time bridge. If advances are simply replaced by new advances, reported cash flow can stay positive while the business still does not fund itself. If they unwind faster than orders replace them, the three-year ratio falls under 0.40 and the red line breaks.[1] The test is therefore not the sign of reported operating cash flow. It is operating cash flow after removing the change in customer advances, together with receivable and inventory days that are not rising. A positive adjusted figure supports the company and would trigger a re-run of the gates. A negative adjusted figure, or a positive reported figure that rests on advances still above ₹400 crore, supports the verdict. The 24 October results will show the half-year figures.[28]

The 5,813.00 target: the assumption that an old high is reachable

A prior high is a place where sellers have been before, so it can be a ceiling instead of a goal. The target is therefore the least certain input: it is a chart level, not a valuation, and the ICICI valuation target of 5,360 is lower. Evidence of being wrong would be a failed rally that stalls under the 50-day average of 4,944.35 on falling volume. We would know within three to six weeks.

The FY27 earnings estimate: the assumption that parts are available

The estimate needs Netweb to ship about ₹3,714 crore in three quarters. Management says memory and graphics-processor shortages have not yet hurt it and that it has built stock, with inventory days at 110, to cover them.[7] If supply tightens, shipments could slip across quarters while the stock of parts stays on the balance sheet. Earnings delivery and cash recovery are linked risks here: a component delay lowers profit and traps cash at the same time. Evidence would be revenue below ₹1,000 crore in Q2 or Q3 with rising inventory.

Red-team bear case: why the next year could be worse than the table says

The strongest case against owning Netweb here is that the quarter that looks like a breakthrough is the peak of a one-off. About ₹430 crore of the June quarter revenue came from the strategic order, and by our inference growth without it was near 29 percent, which is below the 35 to 40 percent management guides for regular business.[7][6] ICICI Securities itself models FY28 revenue at ₹4,599 crore, only 1.4 percent above FY27, which means a stock priced at 61 times FY28 earnings could face a year of flat sales.[22]

Second, the company chose to sell new equity at about 100 times trailing earnings while describing itself as a zero-net-debt business on its May 2026 call. In the three months to June 2026 its free cash fell by about ₹333 crore and borrowings stood at ₹262.5 crore, so the ₹1,200 crore QIP looks like a response to the cash cycle, not an optional extra.[8][6][20] Promoters also reduced their holding by 2,079,935 shares between September 2025 and June 2026.[19] None of that proves trouble, but together they fit the pattern of insiders and the company raising money from strong buyers at a high price.

Third, 81.20 percent of revenue from five customers means the result is closer to a project business than to a product business. CRISIL names customer and supplier concentration, working-capital intensity and growing competition as the main weaknesses, and sets downside rating triggers at revenue below ₹2,000 crore or operating margin below 11 percent.[17] Neither is near, so the bear case is about the multiple, not about solvency: a fall from 100 times to 50 times earnings on unchanged profit would halve the stock.

ICICI's FY28 EPS of ₹77.7 at the report's own bear multiple of 50 times gives 3,885.00, which is 17.9 percent below the close and ₹230 below the 4,115.00 chart anchor.[22] The 50 times multiple is a scenario assumption, not a proven historical average. 7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER. The portfolio's taxonomy has no weak or strong form; 'weak-form' is this report's own shorthand for a position whose expected value is positive and whose stop has not been hit. Here those are cash conversion at 32.3 percent of operating EBITDA over three years, and top-five customer share of 81.20 percent. The verdict does not say Netweb is a bad company. The business is growing fast and earning a 37.5 percent return on capital, no stop has closed, and the stock sits on its 20-day average.
