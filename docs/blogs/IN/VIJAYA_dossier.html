1. What the company does

Vijaya Diagnostic Centre Limited is a Hyderabad company that tests blood and other samples and takes medical scans, such as X-rays, MRI and PET-CT [2][3]. It was founded in 1981 by Dr S. Surendranath Reddy, who is chairman, and Suprita Reddy leads the company as managing director and chief executive [2]. At 30 June 2026 it ran 166 centres in 27 cities and towns: 51 hubs, 115 spokes and about 26 processing labs that sit inside the hubs [2][3]. A hub is a large centre with advanced scanners and a full lab. A spoke is a smaller centre that sends samples to a hub, but even the smallest one has an ECG or X-ray room, because the company does not run plain sample-collection points [3].

Who pays, and for what? Mostly the patient. In Q1 FY27, the quarter to June 2026, 92% of revenue came from walk-in patients with a doctor's prescription or a health package, and the other 8% from all other sources [2]. Pathology (lab tests) was 63% of sales and radiology (scans) 37% [2]. Wellness packages, which are bundles of tests that healthy people buy for a check-up, made up 14.8% of sales, up from about 8% before the Covid years [2][3]. Each patient visit brought in ₹1,860, and each visit included about 3.7 tests [2]. Management says a pure lab chain earns ₹900 to ₹950 per patient from 2.5 to 3 tests, which is why a scan-and-test centre earns about twice as much per visit [3].

Where is the business? Hyderabad gave 67% of Q1 FY27 sales, the rest of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana 20%, Pune 6%, West Bengal 4% and everywhere else 3% [2]. Hyderabad's share was 70% a year earlier, so the mix is slowly widening [25]. The company bought the Pune chain PH Diagnostics more than two and a half years before the August 2026 call, runs seven hubs in Kolkata, opened its first two Bengaluru hubs in the spring of 2025, and opened a flagship hub at JP Nagar, Bengaluru, in July 2026 [2][3][4][8]. On 10 September 2026 it signed an agreement, announced on 11 September 2026, to buy the Arya Wellness Centre business in Guwahati for about ₹46 crore, which would add a seventh state [15].

Why is the profit margin so high? The materials the company consumes cost only about 11% of sales, so the gross margin is 88.9% [2][3]. Management says 60% to 70% of its costs are fixed, such as rent, machines and salaries, so each extra patient adds sales at little extra cost [3]. Operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, was 42.7% in Q1 FY27 [2]. One accounting rule changes that picture. Under Ind AS 116 the company shows rent as depreciation plus interest, so rent does not reduce the headline operating profit. Management says the margin would be about 35% under the old rules [4]. We show both numbers throughout and explain the gap in Chapter 3.

How one hub earns its keep

Think of a hub as a small hospital department that does not admit patients. Doctors in the area send patients for scans, and the same visit often adds blood tests. A mature older hub in Hyderabad books about ₹10 crore to ₹15 crore of revenue a year, while the newer hubs are larger and are built to earn more [4]. Setting up a hub costs about ₹15 crore to ₹16 crore, with the Bengaluru flagship at about ₹30 crore because it includes a central lab, a 3 Tesla MRI and a digital PET-CT [3]. Because the cost base is mostly fixed, a hub loses money until it has enough patients and then earns a high margin on each extra one.

Management has said new hubs break even in about a year in new cities and about three quarters in its home markets [4]. The company says the hubs it opened in Khammam and Nandyal reached break-even in two quarters [4]. In Q1 FY27 management said the majority of the ten hubs opened in FY26 had broken even. It named three or four that had not, and said their burn was about half a percent of revenue [3]. The exact number of hubs at break-even is not disclosed. That is the cost of growth, and Chapter 2B tests whether it is under control.

What this means for an owner

An owner holds a franchise that sells a necessary service to people who pay in cash, with a cost base that rewards volume. The same facts mean the company must keep opening hubs to grow, and every new hub brings a long lease. Chapter 2 asks whether the advantage is real, and Chapter 3 shows what the leases do to the numbers.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Hub and spoke: a hub is a large centre with full lab and scanners; a spoke is a smaller centre that feeds it.
  • Footfall: the number of patient visits. This is the count of people, not tests.
  • B2C: business to consumer, meaning the patient pays, not an insurer or a hospital.
  • Wellness package: a bundle of tests and scans that a healthy person buys for a routine check-up.
  • Ind AS 116: the lease accounting rule that puts rented property on the balance sheet as an asset and a debt.
  • RSI (relative strength index): a 0 to 100 gauge of recent buying pressure. Readings above 70 are called overbought and below 30 oversold.
  • ATR (average true range): the typical size of one day's price swing. We use it to measure distance from support and to place stops.
  • Stage 2 is the markup phase of a share, with the price above a rising 50-day average that sits above the 200-day average.
  • Stage 4 is the markdown phase of a share, when the price sits below falling 50-day and 200-day averages.
  • Asymmetry is the reward-to-risk ratio, which means the gain we expect divided by the loss we would take if the stop is hit. A Core Alpha idea needs at least 2.0.
  • Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by profit per share. It is the number of years of today's profit that the price pays for.
  • Pivot: the price level a share must clear to complete a base, which is a flat resting range after a fall.
  • EPS (earnings per share): profit divided by the number of shares, so the profit that belongs to one share.
  • Surplus cash: the company's own measure of cash and investments after deducting money owed to equipment suppliers [2].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive advantage rests on four things, and each has evidence. The first is the one-roof model. A patient who needs a scan and a blood test gets both in one visit, so Vijaya collects ₹1,860 per patient visit against the ₹900 to ₹950 management quotes for pure lab chains [3]. That is a claim by management and not an audited figure, but the company's own reported revenue per visit supports it: it rose from ₹1,276 in FY22 to ₹1,740 in FY26 [2].

The second is density in the home market. Hyderabad grew about 17% in Q1 FY27 without any new hub in the last couple of years, and spokes more than five years old grew at a higher double-digit rate [3]. Management also says the number two and number three chains in Hyderabad are now spending their capital outside the city, because Vijaya already holds the larger share [3]. We could not verify market share, because no source publishes it, and management says so itself [3]. The third is brand and pricing without discounting: the company says its maximum discount on wellness packages is about 20% off list price and that it does not push upsell packages [3]. The fourth is advanced imaging. The Bengaluru hub houses a 75 cm wide-bore 3 Tesla MRI that the company calls the first of its kind in Karnataka [2].

The weak points are just as clear. One city still gives 67% of sales [2]. Growth is uneven from quarter to quarter: in Q2 FY26 revenue rose only 10.2% and Hyderabad grew in the low single digits, because a wet monsoon cut fever testing and the festival season came early [6]. Pune, bought more than two years earlier, took longer to fix than management expected, and management admits it is the one guidance that did not come true [4][3]. The company has not raised prices since June 2025, when it raised some test prices in Hyderabad [3]. A pure price increase is therefore not a source of growth at present, and every point of growth comes from volume and mix.

The industry offers a long runway but no free ride. Crisil Market Intelligence & Analytics expects Indian diagnostics to grow 10% to 12% a year from FY24 to FY28, and a PhillipCapital study says organised chains hold only 15% of the market, hospital labs 37% and small standalone labs 48% [20][21]. Vijaya's own revenue growth was 19.5% in FY26, so it grew well above the industry [2]. Management says it has run 4 to 5 points above the industry for years [3]. Whether that gap lasts as Vijaya enters cities where it has no history is the central question for Chapters 2B and 5.

What this means for an owner: the advantage is genuine inside south India and unproven outside it. Hyderabad pays for the expansion, and the expansion is where the risk sits. The next chapter picks the operating numbers that show whether the new hubs and the old ones are both doing their jobs.

Capital allocation tells the rest of the story. The company has no bank borrowings, paid a dividend of ₹2 per share for FY26 (about 12% of profit), and plans to fund the ₹46 crore Arya acquisition from internal accruals at about 8 to 9 times Arya's EBITDA [12][15][2]. The Arya business earns a 21.4% EBITDA margin on ₹26 crore of revenue, against 42.7% at Vijaya, so it lowers average margins even though it adds profit [15]. It will also buy a plot of land in Andhra Pradesh for ₹8 to ₹10 crore, a break from the habit of leasing, which the company explains as a one-off because it could not secure a lease [3].

Two governance facts belong here. The chief financial officer changed between the Q4 and Q1 calls: Ankit Shah was listed as CFO on 8 May 2026, and on 7 August 2026 the managing director welcomed Narasimha Raju back, noting that he had rejoined as CFO [4][3]. Separately, Crisil lowered its rating on ₹40 crore of old bank facilities to B on 25 June 2026 because the company did not supply information. What this means for an owner: management does what it says on operations and often does better. The things to watch are the speed at which its plans grow and the quality of its disclosure, not its honesty about the business. The next chapter turns to the financial statements.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Layer 1 compares where each number stands today (its static level) with the direction it is moving (its trajectory), and then applies the hard red lines in our rulebook. A business can fail a level and still qualify if the trend is strong, and a business can pass the level and fail on the trend. For Vijaya the levels and the trends both pass, so the archetype is COMPOUNDER: steady growth, high returns and no solvency problem. The weak point is the price, which Chapters 4, 5 and 7 take up.

Ind AS 116 is the accounting rule for leases. Vijaya's stated strategy is to lease its centres, so the rule matters more here than at most companies [3]. The balance sheet shows lease liabilities of ₹422.6 crore at March 2026 (₹400.0 crore long-term and ₹22.6 crore due within a year) against zero bank borrowings, and a right-of-use asset of ₹367.2 crore [2][1]. Screener's 'Borrowings' line of ₹423 crore is therefore the lease liability, not bank debt [32][2]. In FY26 the company paid ₹52.1 crore for leases, ₹21.5 crore of principal and ₹30.7 crore of interest, discounting at 8.5% [1]. Those payments sit in financing cash flow, so they never reduce reported cash flow from operations. Contracted future lease payments before discounting add up to ₹691.7 crore, of which ₹417.5 crore falls after five years [1].

Three adjustments follow from that rule, and each is shown in the tables below. First, we subtract lease payments from cash flow from operations, which cuts the three-year cash conversion from 1.56 to 1.25 times profit. Second, we subtract lease payments from EBITDA, which cuts FY26 margin from 41.4% to 35.0%, matching management's own figure of about 35% [4]. Third, we show debt-to-equity both with and without leases, because a reader who sees 0.44 might think it is bank debt when the bank part is zero.

Two more cautions apply to the profit trend. Screener reports ₹187 crore of profit over the last twelve months, up 24% [32]. But the company changed its depreciation method from reducing balance to straight line from 1 January 2023, which raised profit by ₹6.8 crore in FY23 and ₹29.1 crore in FY24 [2]. Growth measured from the FY23 base therefore overstates the underlying trend. From FY24 to FY26 profit grew 20.7% a year, which is the figure we trust [2]. Return on capital employed (ROCE, the profit a business earns for each rupee of capital it uses) also needs care, as the table of tests shows: three sources give 20.5%, 25% and 15.6% for the latest year because each counts capital differently [32][2][18].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 argues against our own conclusion. For each estimate we name its weakest assumption, the evidence that would show it is wrong, and the date by which we will know. We then give the strongest honest bear case and the single claim on which the whole verdict hinges. The point is to find the way the report could fail before the market does.

Weakest assumption in the earnings estimate: revenue still grows 17% in FY28 at a 42.5% margin

Our FY28 profit per share of ₹24.4 needs two things at once: revenue growth that fades only to 17%, and a margin that stays above 42% while nine more hubs open. The company's own long-run guide is 15% a year, and Emkay splits that into 10% to 11% from existing centres and 4% to 5% from new ones [3][19]. If Hyderabad slowed to the low single digits again, as in Q2 FY26, growth would fall toward our bear path of 17% in FY27 and 14% in FY28, and EPS toward ₹20.7 [6].

The evidence would be a quarter in which Hyderabad grows less than 10% or the EBITDA margin falls below 40% with no one-off cause. We will know part of it on 3 November 2026, when Q2 FY27 results are due, and more on 4 November, when management takes questions at 11:00 IST. The Q3 FY27 results should follow in February 2027, based on last year's call on 13 February 2026, but no date has been announced [13][14][23].

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF: owner cash flow of ₹198.1 crore is the right base

We built owner cash flow from operating cash flow of ₹270.6 crore, lease payments of ₹52.1 crore and maintenance capex of 2.5% of sales [1][4]. Two parts are soft. Maintenance capex is a management estimate that we cannot audit, and lease payments will keep rising: contracted payments before discounting are ₹691.7 crore, and FY26 lease liabilities rose 32% [1]. If true owner cash flow is ₹150 crore, the implied growth rate rises to about 28%. The harsh view, using actual FY26 free cash flow of ₹35.7 crore, gives 49%.

The evidence would be the half-year cash flow statement filed with the Q2 results, which lists operating cash flow, lease payments and capex for April to September 2026. Because a full cash flow statement is required only with half-year and full-year results, the next one arrives on 3 November 2026 and the following one with the March 2027 results [13].

Weakest assumption in the target: a multiple of 60 times holds

A multiple is a mood, and this one is high. At ₹1,442.80 the stock trades at 79.1 times trailing earnings, 66 times the street's FY27 estimate and 54 times its FY28 estimate. Peers trade at 47 to 57 times trailing [32][33][34][35]. Foreign portfolio investors held 19.95% in December 2023 and held 10.67% in September 2026, while mutual funds rose from 19.4% to 31.4% [28][32]. A stock this heavily owned by domestic funds can fall quickly if inflows slow. It traded near ₹1,025 in November 2025, around the weak Q2 FY26 result, which is 29% below today's price [30].

The size of the risk is easy to state. On unchanged EPS of ₹24.4, a multiple of 50 times gives ₹1,220, which is 15.44% below the 28 October close and 9.09% below the zone top. The evidence would be a fall in the multiple without a fall in earnings, such as a close below ₹1,281.03 with estimates unchanged. We watch it every day, and the shareholding pattern for the December 2026 quarter, due in January 2027, will show whether funds are still buying [28].

Red-team bear case: a lease-funded growth story priced for perfection

The bear says Vijaya is a very good business that grows by signing long leases, and that the accounts hide the cost. Lease liabilities grew 32% in FY26 against equity growth of 20%. Capex nearly doubled to ₹182.7 crore, free cash flow after leases and capex fell to ₹35.7 crore, and management raised its FY27 capex plan by a third within three months [2][1][3]. A 79 times multiple pays for twenty-odd percent growth for years, yet Q2 FY26 showed that one wet monsoon and an early festival can cut quarterly growth to 10%.

The bear adds soft governance signals. The chief financial officer changed within three months, Crisil's rating on old facilities fell to B because the company stopped supplying information, and promoters pledged 1.96% of their shares, up from zero in June 2025 [4][3][16][28]. None is serious alone. Together they suggest a company that is busy growing and less careful about disclosure. In the bear's numbers FY28 EPS is ₹20.7, the multiple falls to 45 times and the price falls to ₹932, which is 35.4% below the 28 October close.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, and the rule behind it is the asymmetry floor. The ledger puts the 28 October close of ₹1,442.80 0.51 average true ranges (ATR, the typical daily price swing) above the 20-day average of ₹1,418.63, which is inside the 0.6 band that our support ladder calls full size, so the price is at support by distance alone. The support ladder is our scale for how close the price sits to a floor, measured in ATR. The closer to support, the larger the position it allows: under 0.6 ATR is full size, under 1.2 is standard size, under 1.6 is a small starter or a limit order, and beyond that we do not buy at market. But a Core Alpha idea must offer at least 2.0 times as much reward as risk. At the close the base target of ₹1,464 offers ₹21.20 of gain against ₹161.77 of risk to the stop, a ratio of 0.13 to 1. Support at the 20-day average does not pay enough because the target is too close to the price. The ratio reaches 2.0 to 1 only at ₹1,342.00, so the zone from ₹1,328.10 to ₹1,342.00 is where the rule is met. That level is 7.0% below the close and sits just above the 63-day base low of ₹1,328.10, the floor of the turnaround reading from Chapter 4. The zone comes from the 2.0 rule and from nothing else. We treat it as a place to re-examine the stock, not as an order to buy.

The stop is ₹1,281.03, which is the 63-day base low of ₹1,328.10 minus one ATR of ₹47.07, as our rule for turnaround bases requires. A stop of ₹1,287.93, built from the 20-day swing low of ₹1,335.00 less one ATR, is within ₹7 of it, so the two rules agree. We use the lower one. The three prices that frame the trade are the base target of ₹1,464 (FY28E EPS of ₹24.4 at 60 times), the bull target of ₹1,715 (₹26.8 at 64 times) and the bear target of ₹932 (₹20.7 at 45 times). At ₹1,342.00 the base case gains 9.1%, the bull case 27.8%, the bear case loses 30.6%, and the stop loses 4.54%. The expected value of ₹1,394 is 3.9% above that entry, against 3.4% below the 28 October close. Both the 9.1% base gain and the 3.9% expected gain are below the 12% yearly return target used in Chapter 5. So this is a modest-edge entry for a high-quality business, and we accept that only because the position is small.

We do not reject the idea, because Layer 1 and Layer 2 both pass. The business has no bank debt, three-year cash conversion of 1.25 times profit after leases, a 42.7% margin and 20% revenue growth. Layer 2 scores 3.5 out of 5, above the 3.0 line. The only failing test is price. That is why the stock stays on the shortlist with a price zone and stated triggers, and is not archived. A fall into the zone is not enough to buy. The momentum test must be run again on the new prices, because today's pass would not carry over: Test C needs the price more than 1% above the 63-day low (₹1,341.38) and at or above the 20-day average (₹1,418.63), and the whole zone sits below both. The earnings case must also be rechecked, because a fall may come with results that lower our ₹24.4. A close below the ₹1,328.10 floor suspends buying even before the ₹1,281.03 stop is reached. If any of this changes, we recompute the support, stop, target, ratio and size before acting.

Sizing follows the conviction tier. We choose TIER_3 (2% to 3.5% of net worth), not a higher tier, because the price is rich and the margin of safety is thin. Our risk budget for this tier is 50 basis points of net worth if the stop is hit. From the zone top of ₹1,342.00 the stop is 4.5% away, so the formula allows 11.0%, but the tier cap of 3.5% binds. At the capped weight a fill at the zone top that then hits the stop loses 3.5% × 4.54% = 0.16% of net worth, and a fill at ₹1,328.10 loses 0.12%, both inside the 0.50% budget if the stop fills where it is set. A stop does not guarantee that. If the shares gapped down to our bear price of ₹932, which is 30.6% below the zone top, the loss at the 3.5% cap would be about 1.07% of net worth, more than double the budget. The stop limits the loss only if it can be executed. The let-winners-run rule applies after purchase: do not trim only because the RSI (relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of recent buying pressure) passes 70 or the weight passes 15%; let a rising position run to 22% of net worth while the stop trails the rising 21-day average or 50-day average.

What this means for an owner: this is a good company at a full price. If you want to own it, the plan is patience with a fixed price and a fixed risk, not a chase. No position exists in the portfolio today, because the ledger lists Vijaya only as a watchlist name.
