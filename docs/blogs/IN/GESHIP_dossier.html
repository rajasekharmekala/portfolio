Before you read

The Great Eastern Shipping Company owns oil, gas and dry-cargo ships and some offshore rigs.[28] Its latest quarter was its most profitable ever, and the shares cost only 5.84 times the last twelve months of profit.[9][1] We still reject the stock for new money, because no verified profit forecast exists to set an upside target, and the best target on file gives 1.87 rupees of gain for each rupee of risk against our floor of 3.5.[34] The call would be wrong if freight rates stay at record levels for years, though the company itself warns that new ship orders are building.[22]

What the company does

Great Eastern Shipping, usually called GE Shipping, is a Mumbai-based company that moves things by sea for other people. It has two businesses. The shipping business carries crude oil, refined petroleum products, gas and dry bulk cargoes such as grain and coal. The offshore business rents out drilling rigs and support vessels to oil companies through a subsidiary, Greatship (India) Limited.[28]

The customers are oil traders, refiners, gas shippers and commodity merchants. They pay a daily hire for a ship, or a price per voyage. A ship that earns a high daily rate in a tight market earns a few thousand dollars a day in a loose one. The company has little control over that rate. Management says it is "predominantly spot market", which means most of its ships are hired voyage by voyage at whatever the market pays that week. Only 25% to 26% of its capacity is hired out on longer fixed-rate contracts.[24][17]

The money flows in a simple way. Revenue is the daily hire multiplied by the days at sea. The costs are crew, fuel, repairs, insurance and the cost of owning the ship. Because these costs barely move, a rise in the hire rate drops almost entirely into profit. In the June 2026 quarter, sales of ₹2,005 crore turned into an operating profit of ₹1,338 crore, a 67% margin, against 54% a year earlier.[9] The same effect works in reverse when rates fall.

The company buys and sells ships all the time. In the past year it sold older vessels such as the 2009-built "Jag Lokesh" and took delivery of younger ones.[27] Its stated plan is to "replace, not expand", which means it swaps old ships for newer ones and does not add capacity at a high point in the cycle.[23][20] A new-building Suezmax crude tanker contract was announced on the company's page in the financial year that began in April 2026. The price, the shipyard and the delivery date are N/A because they were not in the page text we could fetch.[26]

The idea reached our watchlist through a weekly market commentary that mentioned a purchase of a "Swissmax" tanker and high tanker rates.[33] We could not confirm the name "Swissmax" on the company's own announcements, so we treat the link between that comment and the Suezmax contract as N/A.

Glossary of terms used in this report

ROCE means return on capital employed, the operating profit earned each year per rupee of capital tied up in the business.

EPS means earnings per share, which is profit divided by the number of shares.

RSI means relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast the price has moved lately; above 70 is hot and below 30 is weak.

ATR means average true range, the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions, used here as a ruler for distances and stops.

free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after the company pays for its ships and equipment; FCF stands for free cash flow.

price-to-earnings is the share price divided by the profit per share; a low number means each rupee of profit costs little, but only if the profit lasts.

debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' funds; a low number means the company relies little on lenders.

net debt is borrowings minus cash and investments; a negative figure means the company holds more cash than it owes.

cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates from its day-to-day work, before ship purchases and dividends.

operating margin is operating profit divided by sales.

working capital is the money tied up in unpaid bills and day-to-day running costs.

capital expenditure is money spent on ships and long-lived equipment.

buyback means the company buys its own shares from the market, which shrinks the share count.

dilution is a fall in each owner's share of the company when new shares are issued.

guidance is a forecast of future results that a company gives to investors.

consensus estimate is the average of professional analysts' forecasts for a company's profit.

gross margin is sales minus the direct cost of sales, divided by sales.

net margin is final profit after all costs and tax, divided by sales.

take rate is the share of each transaction a platform keeps as its fee; it does not apply to a shipping owner.

book-to-bill is new orders divided by sales billed in the same period; it does not apply to a shipping owner.

20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing prices over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days; a rising average shows the trend direction.

Stage 2 is the markup phase of a stock's life cycle, when the price sits above rising 50-day and 200-day averages; Stage 4 is the breakdown phase, when the price falls below falling averages.

A base is a flat resting period in the price; the pivot is the high point of the base that the price must clear; a breakout is a close above the pivot on strong volume; a retest is a pullback to the old pivot that holds.

A measured move is a target set by adding the height of the base to the pivot.

A stop loss is the price at which we would sell to cap the loss; an entry zone is the price range in which we would buy; a limit order is an order that fills only at a price you set or better.

asymmetry and reward-to-risk are two names for how many rupees we could gain for each rupee we could lose, which is (target minus entry) divided by (entry minus stop).

TCE means time charter equivalent, the daily earning of a ship after voyage costs; NAV means net asset value, here the company's own estimate of what its ships, cash and other assets are worth per share.

Why the business is strong or weak

The balance sheet is the strength. Borrowings fell from ₹4,655 crore in March 2022 to ₹1,087 crore in March 2026, while investments alone stood at ₹2,392 crore.[5] Management said cash was about $700 million (roughly ₹6,000 crore) with net cash of $600 million, and that all debt is repayable by November 2028.[18] The company has been net cash for more than three years. A shipping company with this much cash can buy ships when others are forced to sell.

The second strength is fleet quality. Management says ships built after 2013 burn 20% to 25% less fuel than older ones, worth about $1,000 to $2,500 a day depending on ship size.[21] The company spent ₹1,200 crore on fleet changes in the year to March 2026, ₹300 crore in the June 2026 quarter and a further ₹250 to ₹300 crore in July 2026.[20] It pays a steady dividend: ₹14.40 a share for the June 2026 quarter, the highest ever and the 18th quarter in a row.[16]

The weakness is that the business has no pricing power. Tanker rates are set by world supply and demand. Management credits the latest rates to the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a key oil route, and to longer trading routes after refinery disruptions in other countries. It also calls part of this "not quite a structural… temporary phase".[17][23] A shipper that earns peak rates for a few quarters is not the same as a shipper with a lasting edge. Five years of profit show the problem: ₹630 crore in the year to March 2022, then ₹2,575 crore, ₹2,614 crore, ₹2,344 crore and ₹2,943 crore.[4]

Supply is the other weak point. The order books for new ships stand at 27% of the existing fleet for crude tankers, 35% for very large gas carriers, 20% to 21% for product tankers and 14% for dry bulk carriers.[19] Management said in its own words that "as we get further into this cycle, the chances of the market coming off due to oversupply increase".[22] It expects deliveries next year of about 7% of the product tanker fleet and 4% of the bulk fleet.[22]

The offshore business adds a smaller and slower stream. Three drilling rigs come up for new contracts in the second half of the financial year, and one has been awarded a three-year contract.[25] Management said it had not yet seen enough tenders to commit more money to offshore.[25] Revenue split between shipping and offshore is N/A because the segment table sits behind a paywalled page.

How it was calculated

The stop follows the tactical rule: below the lowest of the 20-day swing low (₹1,358.80), the 20-day average (₹1,487.36) and the 50-day average (₹1,397.35), minus half an ATR. The scenario probabilities. The weakest assumption is the 30% bear weight. Hormuz-driven disruption may last longer than we think, or a ship-supply shock may arrive sooner. The evidence that would show the bear case is too high is the next two quarterly results showing crude and product tanker earnings at or above the $90,000 and $45,000 a day management reported.[17] The evidence that would show it is too low is a fall in spot rates or a rise in delivered ships. The normal-year profit. The weakest assumption is that the last ten years show what is normal.
