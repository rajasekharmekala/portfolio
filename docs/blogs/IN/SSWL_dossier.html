Before you read

Steel Strips Wheels makes steel and alloy wheels for Indian car, truck and tractor makers. It has just reported its best quarter ever, with profit up 208.5% on sales up 60.7%[1]. The shares rose 28.4% in one week to ₹528.90, which is far above their nearest support[30]. Our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, because our central target of ₹487 is below the price, and the gain-to-loss ratio reaches our 2.5-to-1 minimum only near ₹394. 1. What the company does

Steel Strips Wheels Limited (SSWL) is a Chandigarh-based manufacturer of wheel rims. It started operations in 1991 and today runs five wheel plants with a combined capacity of about 26 million wheels a year: 21 million steel wheels, 5.0 million alloy wheels and 0.5 million aluminium steering knuckles[1]. A wheel rim is the metal part a tyre is mounted on. Steel rims are cheap and tough, so they go on trucks, tractors, farm and construction machines and entry-level cars. Alloy rims are made of aluminium, look better, weigh less and cost more, so they go on premium cars and SUVs[1]. A knuckle is the aluminium part that connects the wheel to the suspension; SSWL began selling it in 2025 as a first step away from pure wheels[1].

The company sells almost entirely to vehicle makers, not to the public. Its customers include Maruti Suzuki, Hyundai, Mahindra, Tata Motors, Ashok Leyland, Honda, Kia, MG Motors, Escorts, Sonalika and JCB, and the company reports its share of each customer's wheel needs: for example 32% of Maruti Suzuki, 56% of Mahindra and 86% of Honda in passenger-car steel wheels[1]. Brokers put its FY26 domestic share of steel wheels at 39% in passenger vehicles, 53% in medium and heavy trucks and 43% in tractors[15], and management says it holds more than 64% of passenger-car alloy wheels outside Maruti Suzuki[2]. Those shares are the reason the company has pricing power with customers that are much larger than it is.

Money comes in per wheel, and the price of a wheel is mostly metal. In the first half of FY27 the company sold about 111 lakh wheels and knuckles (one lakh is 100,000) for ₹3,439.7 crore of revenue, roughly ₹3,100 per piece[1]. Steel and aluminium are bought at market prices and the contracts say the customer pays the metal back, with a delay. Management says aluminium is now settled monthly, so the company is covered within about 30 days, whereas steel settlements take longer[5]. That is why the company talks about profit per wheel, not profit margin: when metal prices rise, revenue rises and the margin percentage falls even if the rupees earned per wheel are unchanged[5].

The product mix is the second money lever. In the first half of FY27, steel wheels brought in ₹1,927 crore (56% of revenue), alloy wheels ₹1,439 crore (42%) and knuckles ₹74 crore (2%)[1]. Management says an aluminium wheel earns about twice what a steel wheel earns on a like-for-like passenger-car basis, and about 40% more on a blended basis across all segments[2]. Exports are the third lever: export revenue was ₹314 crore in the first half, up 16%, with the second quarter alone at ₹187 crore, up 69%[1]. The story of the stock is therefore a story about mix: more alloy, more trucks and tractors, more exports, against a steel business that grows slowly.

Where the plants are and why capacity matters

The wheel plants are at Dappar (Punjab), Chennai, Jamshedpur, Mehsana (Gujarat) and Bhuj (Gujarat), plus a hot-rolling mill at Saraikela in Jharkhand that feeds steel wheels[1]. The Bhuj plant came from the bankrupt AMW Autocomponent through a tribunal-approved resolution plan in January 2024 for about ₹138.15 crore, and it is where the new aluminium wheel and knuckle lines are being built[13]. Capacity is the binding limit today: steel plants ran at 85% and alloy plants at 89% of capacity in the first half, up from 76% and 80% in FY26[1], and management says every unit is close to full[5]. A business running near full capacity earns more per extra wheel, but it also cannot grow volume without spending on new plants, which is the central tension in this report.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is helped by having few competitors in aluminium wheels. Management says only two serious players dominate aluminium wheels in India and that SSWL, being technically superior, earns a premium on some programmes[5]. It has a 64% share of passenger-car alloy wheels outside Maruti Suzuki[2]. Two strategic shareholders back it: Tata Steel holds 6.9% and Nippon Steel (formerly Sumitomo Metal) holds 5.4%, which helps with steel supply and technology[1]. A new wheel design must be qualified by the vehicle maker, and management says a new export programme takes about nine months to qualify after it is won[2]. That delay protects existing suppliers.

The weakness is that the customers are bigger and that the industry is cyclical. Truck demand collapsed in the second quarter of FY26 to a five-year low, exports to the United States were hit by tariffs, and profit per wheel stayed flat at ₹262 for the full year[7]. Full-year FY26 profit after tax actually fell 3.8% to ₹202.1 crore on a 17.0% revenue increase, because depreciation and interest rose as new plants were built[1]. The EBITDA margin was 12.7% in FY22 and 9.9% in FY26[1], so the five-year history is of a business that grew its sales by 45.6% but not its profit.

The competitive moat is real but narrower than the share numbers suggest. Management admits that domestic alloy-wheel margins are under pressure because competitors are accepting orders below cost, and says SSWL's answer is to sell more outside India[5]. In exports the main rivals are in Turkey, and management says SSWL is cheaper on manufacturing and on freight to Europe, while Chinese suppliers face anti-dumping duties in its export markets and compete in America, where SSWL does not sell aluminium wheels[2]. Those are management claims. We could verify the export revenue figures but not the cost comparison with Turkish producers.

Three structural risks sit behind the numbers. First, electric vehicles help alloy wheels (management cites rising electric share) but steel wheel volumes grow only about 4% a year against 12% for alloy, by the company's own estimate[1]. Second, the new hot-stamping line, a press that forms car body parts from hot steel, puts SSWL against Gestamp, a large Spanish group, and others, for one anchor customer in Maharashtra[2]. Third, the business is capital hungry: each rupee of fixed assets turns over only 3.7 times a year[1], so growth needs steady spending on plants.

Customer concentration and bargaining power

The company reports its share of each customer's business but does not publish the share of its own revenue that any one customer represents, so concentration by revenue is not published. What the share-of-business slide does show is that SSWL is deeply embedded: 100% of MG Motors, 100% of Suzuki scooters, 81% of Mahindra three-wheelers, 65% of Ashok Leyland and 60% of Tata Motors truck wheels[1]. That depth is a strength when the customer is growing and a risk when the customer cuts output, as the truck makers did in mid-FY26. The Q2 FY27 call also drew a question on lower share of business at Escorts, HMSI and Tata Alloy. Management said it had lost no share and that tractor share rose from 43% to 45%[2]. Price increases were the most important bargaining result of the last two years: management says it won price revisions for non-metal cost inflation after years of absorbing them, which is the main reason it gives for the jump in profit per wheel[2].

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A good test of management is whether last year's promises became this year's results. We checked nine statements made between August 2025 and October 2026. The short answer is that operating promises were mostly kept or beaten, but the guidance numbers have been set so low that they are not useful forecasts, and project timelines and the capex bill have moved the wrong way.

On the positive side, the company said in June 2026 that profit after tax would grow 15% to 20% in FY27 and profit per wheel would be close to ₹300[7]. In the six months to September 2026 profit after tax was up 115.3% and profit per wheel was ₹362[1][2]. That is a large beat, but a forecast that is wrong by a factor of about six in four months tells us the company either has little visibility or guides very cautiously. We therefore do not anchor our estimates on management guidance.

On the negative side, the August 2025 plan to add 65 lakh steel wheel capacity by FY26 was cut to 5 lakh by January 2026 and the actual figure was 207 lakh against 205 lakh a year earlier[13][10][1]. The Bhuj trial runs, due between October 2026 and January 2027, are now a knuckle start in the last quarter of FY27 and aluminium wheels in early April 2027[7][2]. The FY27 capex figure has risen from about ₹500 crore to over ₹600 crore in four months, before the new ₹1,400 crore plant[7][5][2].

Capital allocation is the other half of the audit. The company pays out little: the three-year dividend payout is 8.25% of profit, and the interim dividend of ₹1.50 a share for FY27 is 0.28% of the price[22][2]. It acquired the Bhuj plant out of bankruptcy for about ₹138.15 crore and is now spending several times that on new capacity[13]. The promoter group, led by managing director Dheeraj Garg with 29.51% personally, holds 61.21%, and one promoter bought 164,605 shares in the open market in September 2026. None of the holding is pledged[23][21]. We treat the promoter buying as mildly encouraging and the funding plan for the next ₹1,400 crore, which relies in part on unnamed 'strategic partners', as the largest open question[2].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A forecast is only useful if it says how it could fail. This chapter takes each estimate in Chapter 5, names its weakest assumption, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when we will know. It then states the red-team bear case, which is the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion, and names the single claim on which everything hinges.

Weakest assumption 1: profit per wheel of ₹350 is a floor-like base

Our base case assumes ₹350 per wheel, between FY26's ₹262 and Q2's ₹404. The weak point is that nobody outside the company can separate how much of the jump is permanent. Management lists the causes as richer mix, cost savings, price revisions for non-metal costs and higher volume[2], and ICICI Securities says ₹400 is not sustainable[15]. If the true level is ₹300, which is the original guide for FY27[7], FY28 profit falls to about ₹330 crore, earnings per share to about ₹21.0 and the value at 18 times to about ₹378.

The evidence that would show us wrong is profit per wheel in the next two quarters. Q3 results are expected in late January 2027 (the Q3 FY26 presentation was dated 22 January 2026. The FY27 date is not confirmed)[10]. The company also publishes monthly turnover notices, such as the September record of ₹625.81 crore net turnover up 52.4%[19], which give early volume evidence but not profit.

Weakest assumption 2: the catch-up invoices carry no profit

Management says the ₹183 crore of supplementary invoicing is purely a metal pass-through with no effect on EBITDA, and repeated that three times on the call when analysts asked[2]. Revenue includes about ₹140 crore of it in Q2, gross margin fell from 35.5% to 33.0% year on year while EBITDA margin rose, and receivables rose by ₹305 crore[1]. Those facts fit a pass-through. They also fit a quarter in which cost was booked earlier and revenue followed, which would make Q2 look better and Q1 worse than the underlying run rate, and then the half-year figure of ₹362 is the honest one.

We cannot test the claim from the filings we have: the full cash-flow notes and the segment data are not published, and management says it cannot give a steel-versus-alloy margin split[5]. If a year from now receivables are still above 50 days of revenue, the claim will look weaker.

Weakest assumption 3: the capex plan is funded without strain

The plan is over ₹600 crore this year, with commitments already signed for ₹155.1 crore, and about ₹1,400 crore over 60 months at Sambhajinagar, funded by 'a mix of debt, internal accruals, and also likely strategic investments from strategic partners' that management did not name[2][14]. Operating cash flow was ₹365.2 crore on a trailing basis (FY26 ₹331.2 crore plus H1 FY27 ₹130 crore less H1 FY26 ₹96 crore)[1], which covers only part of this year's capex, so borrowings must rise. ICICI models debt rising from ₹826 crore to ₹1,096 crore in FY27[15]. The rating agency already expects net adjusted leverage above 2.5 times through FY27[14].

Red-team bear case

The strongest argument against our verdict is that we are being too cautious. The company has beaten every guide for two quarters, its capacity is sold out, the new plants are pre-sold, exports are recovering and management says it is extremely confident of a very strong second half[2]. If profit per wheel stays at ₹400 and the stock never pulls back below ₹430, waiting costs the full move to ₹692.

The strongest argument against owning it at any price is the opposite: the profit jump comes from a single quarter that includes catch-up invoices, the share price has tripled from its low in a market where 82% of the stocks in our tracked universe are below their 50-day average, big domestic funds were sellers into the run, and the company has approved a ₹1,400 crore plant that is about 3.8 times its trailing yearly operating cash flow of ₹365.2 crore. The weak spot in our own analysis is that the base multiple of 18 is borrowed from one broker, and 22 times in July suggests the market could pay more for the same earnings.

A closing-basis stop does not cap every loss. The bear value of ₹248 is 30.6% below the ₹357.27 stop, so a gap down on bad news, such as a poor Q3 print, could fill a sale well below the stop. A position bought at ₹394 that gapped to ₹248 would lose 37.1%, which is about 1.3% of the portfolio at a 3.5% weight. That is why the weight is capped and why we do not add to a position that is falling toward the stop.

The next paragraph explains the Layer 3 table that follows. Layer 3 is the timing check: it asks how far the price is above support and how much of the portfolio to risk on the idea. Two terms matter. A basis point is one hundredth of one percent, so a risk budget of 50 basis points means we accept losing at most 0.5% of the whole portfolio if the stop is hit, which is ₹50,000 on a ₹1 crore portfolio. A conviction tier caps the size of the position: Tier 3 allows 2% to 3.5% of the portfolio. The formula divides the risk budget by the distance to the stop. From ₹394 that distance is 9.32%, so 0.5% divided by 9.32% allows a 5.36% position, and the Tier 3 cap holds it to 3.5%. If the stop is hit on a full 3.5% position, the loss is about 0.33% of the portfolio, or ₹33,000 on ₹1 crore.

The 3.5% weight shown in the key figures and in the table applies only if the pullback entry at ₹394 or lower happens. At today's price the size is zero.
