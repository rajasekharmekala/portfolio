1. What the company does

Bliss GVS Pharma Limited was incorporated in 1984 and is listed on the NSE and BSE[11]. It makes finished medicines, and its specialty is the suppository and the pessary. A suppository is a small solid dose that is placed in the body and melts there. A pessary is the same idea in a form meant for women's health. The company also makes capsules, tablets and syrups[1]. The annual report calls it one of the world's leading makers of suppositories and pessaries, with a particularly strong footprint in Sub-Saharan Africa[1]. Anupam Rasayan's press release says the company has more than 150 brands and six plants in Maharashtra[9]. Its best-known African brands are P-Alaxin, Lonart, Funbact and Lofnac, which treat malaria, fungal infections and inflammation[12]. The filings call these branded formulations and do not split sales between over-the-counter and prescription channels, so the over-the-counter share is not published.

The customer is a distributor in another country, not a patient. In the year to 31 March 2026 the company reported ₹927.11 crore of consolidated revenue[1]. The annual report splits it by region: Africa ₹669.8 crore, Europe ₹125.4 crore, Asia ₹81.8 crore, North America ₹30.6 crore, Central and South America ₹13.0 crore and Oceania ₹6.5 crore[1]. That makes Africa about 72 percent of the total. Revenue from customers in Nigeria and in Mauritius each topped 10 percent of the company's revenue[1]. A June 2024 credit rating report said exports were about 94 percent of sales and that the top three customers were around 10 percent[12]. A smaller line of business is contract manufacturing of suppositories and pessaries for Sun Pharma and Mankind, whose products may not be exported[11].

The business makes money in three steps. First, it makes the medicine at a cost that left 54 percent of revenue as gross margin in FY26, up from 49 percent a year earlier (our calculation from the filed cost lines)[1]. Second, it sells through distributors and spends money to support the brands: ₹26.52 crore went to business development in FY26, and the auditors flagged how that cost is spread across distributors as a key audit matter[1]. Third, it waits to be paid. The notes to the accounts say customers can take between 0 and 240 days to pay[1]. That wait is the single biggest feature of the business, and chapters 2B and 3 return to it.

The demand behind the Africa franchise is real. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates 282 million malaria cases and 610 000 deaths worldwide in 2024, with the African Region carrying the greatest burden[18]. Its regional office puts the African share of cases at 94 percent in 2022[19]. WHO also warns that partial resistance to artemisinin medicines is confirmed or suspected in at least eight African countries[18]. For a seller of anti-malarial brands that cuts both ways: a very large patient pool, and a treatment mix that may have to change.

The ownership is brand new. Anupam Rasayan, a custom synthesis and specialty chemicals company with its registered office in Surat[9][5], and its wholly owned subsidiary Mates Visa Consultancy Private Limited were designated promoters of Bliss on 28 September 2026[5]. Together they hold 5,32,36,264 shares, or 50.07 percent[5]. The company's leadership has changed several times in a year. Managing director Gagan Sharma resigned on 6 November 2025[1], a new chief executive was appointed in July 2026[17], and managing director Narsimha Kamath resigned on the day of the handover[10].

What changed on 28 September 2026

A mandatory open offer is the public bid that Indian takeover rules force on a buyer who takes control of a listed company. Anupam signed a share purchase agreement on 23 May 2026 for 4,58,03,024 shares, which was 43.30 percent of the company at the time[27], at ₹299 a share, or about ₹1,369.5 crore[31]. The buyer also held a call option on a further 51,81,571 shares at ₹299[3]. The completed block of 5,09,84,595 shares equals the agreed shares plus the option shares, so the option was used in full[5]. The offer to the public was for 26 percent of the expanded share count at ₹299 a share, a maximum cash bill of ₹829.03 crore[3]. The tendering window ran from 28 July to 10 August 2026[3]. Only 1,669 shares were tendered, which is 0.00 percent of the company, which fits a market price far above ₹299[5].

On 28 September 2026 the buyer completed the purchase of 5,09,84,595 shares, or 47.95 percent, and the same day bought another 22,50,000 shares on the market at ₹723.2883 each, for ₹162.74 crore[5][30]. The second purchase took the group to 50.07 percent, past the 50 percent line the filing treats as control[29][33]. Anupam says the total cost was about ₹1,750 crore, including about ₹50 crore of expenses[32]. The Letter of Offer says the buyers do not intend to delist the company, and they reserve the right to restructure its businesses and assets later[3]. It also says that a higher price paid within 26 weeks of the offer closing must be shared with tendering holders, except for ordinary open-market purchases[3]. The ₹723 purchase was on the market, and on 1,669 shares the gap would be only about ₹7.1 lakh in any case (1,669 shares × ₹424.29).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strengths are specific and checkable. Suppositories and pessaries are a niche dosage form, and the annual report says Bliss was the first Indian suppository maker to win EU-GMP certification[1]. It also records a US FDA approval for the suppository unit at Palghar[1]. Large Indian drug companies, Sun Pharma and Mankind, buy its contract production[11]. The balance sheet is clean: borrowings fell from ₹70.97 crore to ₹2.93 crore in FY26, a 96 percent drop[1], while cash and bank balances were ₹166.87 crore[1]. Operating cash flow was ₹138.72 crore, up from ₹105.91 crore[1].

The earnings trend backs this up. Consolidated profit after tax rose 49 percent to ₹134.73 crore in FY26[1]. Gross margin rose from 48.9 to 54.2 percent (our calculation from the filed cost lines)[1]. In the June 2026 quarter revenue grew 37.6 percent to ₹285.58 crore, and operating profit before other income grew about 85 percent to ₹76.4 crore, a margin of 26.8 percent against 19.9 percent a year earlier (our calculation)[2]. CRISIL, the rating agency, had rated the company BBB+ with a stable outlook and noted interest cover of 19.79 times for FY26[11]. ICRA assigned A- with a stable outlook in April 2026[13].

Where the money goes is worth seeing in rupees. Of each ₹100 of FY26 revenue, about ₹45.9 paid for materials, ₹14.7 for staff and ₹21.7 for other costs, which left ₹17.7 of operating profit before other income (our arithmetic on the filed lines)[1]. The company also spent ₹26.52 crore on business development, which is 3.6 percent of standalone revenue[1]. The auditors describe it as marketing and business development cost that is spread across the company's international distributors. The company does not publish profit by region, so we cannot say what the African franchise earns on its own.

The weaknesses are just as specific. The first is the wait for cash. Consolidated trade receivables were ₹517.19 crore against ₹927.11 crore of revenue, which is about 204 days of sales[1]. On the standalone books, only ₹235.07 crore of the ₹506.62 crore gross receivables was not yet due. Another ₹224.56 crore was overdue by less than six months, ₹33.96 crore by six to twelve months, and ₹13.03 crore by more than a year, including ₹12.52 crore under dispute and treated as credit impaired[1]. The company holds a ₹49.51 crore loss allowance against these balances[1]. In FY26 it wrote off ₹13.41 crore of bad debts and charged another ₹14.66 crore of expected credit loss[1].

The second weakness is how much profit comes from outside the core business. FY26 other income was ₹73.53 crore, against profit before tax and one-off items of ₹192.08 crore, so 38 percent of pre-tax profit was not from selling medicines (our calculation)[1]. The largest piece was a ₹51.78 crore net currency gain, which is after a ₹34.81 crore fair-value loss on forward contracts[1]. Africa is also the home of currency risk: one dollar bought about ₦1,380 in the official Nigerian market in mid-July 2026, and the IMF still called the naira undervalued by 25.6 percent[20]. A company that is paid in dollars and naira over 200 days is exposed to exchange-rate swings for most of a year.

The third weakness is history with related parties and leadership churn. In FY25, Greenlife Pharmaceuticals Limited was a related party that bought ₹108.06 crore of goods and owed ₹106.13 crore at year end[1]. On 1 April 2025 a Bliss subsidiary sold its 51 percent of the Nigerian business to its partner for US$1.3 million (₹11.14 crore) and booked a ₹3.57 crore loss[1]. The auditors named three key audit matters beyond revenue cut-off: a ₹43.74 crore exposure to the Singapore subsidiary, the ₹26.52 crore business development cost, and information technology controls[1]. In June 2026 CRISIL moved its rating from BBB+ to B because the company did not give it the information it asked for, and then withdrew the rating at the company's request. The agency warns that non-cooperation may be a result of deterioration in credit quality, but its own table shows interest cover of 19.79 times, so we read this as an information gap and not as distress[11].

How the franchise compares with its peers

A fair question is whether Africa and emerging-market franchises can be high-return businesses. Caplin Point Laboratories, which sells in Latin America and Africa, earns a 35 percent operating margin and a 24.2 percent ROCE, with debtor days of 138[24]. Marksans Pharma shows debtor days of 80 and ROCE of 18.8 percent[25]. Bliss has a 204 day wait and a 16.9 percent ROCE[23]. So the sector can earn more than Bliss does, and it can collect faster. Bliss's niche dosage forms are an advantage, but its slow collections and its heavy reliance on other income keep its returns closer to the middle of the pack than the top.

What this means for an owner: the business is real, profitable and debt free, and it is moving in the right direction on margin. The moat is the suppository know-how and the African brand names. The weak spots, slow cash and other income, are the same two numbers that chapter 2B scores as KPIs. The next chapter shows how they score.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record on money is mixed. It promised, and delivered, a clean balance sheet and a higher dividend. It also promised better collections and did not deliver them. In June 2024 CRISIL wrote that management had changed its debtor policy, that this had moderated debtors and improved realisation, and that sustaining it would be monitorable[12]. Debtor days then went from 192 in FY24 to 198 in FY25 and 204 in FY26[23]. The FY26 annual report says debtor days fell to 188[1]. The balance sheet, read the standard way, shows 204. We do not know how the 188 was built, and the gap is worth asking about.

On capital allocation the record is better. Borrowings fell 96 percent, from ₹70.97 crore to ₹2.93 crore[1]. The FY26 dividend of ₹1.50 a share was three times the ₹0.50 paid in each of FY23, FY24 and FY25[3]. Capital spending of about ₹69 crore on a semi-solid plant and a suppository expansion was reported, and cash spent on property, plant and equipment was ₹56.83 crore, down from ₹76.29 crore[1]. The buyer's promises also matter, because it now decides the strategy. On 14 August Anupam said the deal would close in the first half of September[8]. It closed on 28 September[5], about two weeks late. Anupam also said it would fund the deal with about ₹300 crore of debt and an equity-linked instrument it could buy back[8]. The filings match that[6][9], but they also include a ₹1 call option that the call did not mention[10].

The estimate of ₹14.50 assumes that the last three quarters of FY27 earn 20 percent more than a year earlier. The weak point is other income. In FY26, other income was 38 percent of pre-tax profit, and in the June 2026 quarter it was 12.7 percent[1][2]. If currency gains fade and operating margins slip back from the 26.8 percent of June to the 17.7 percent of FY26, the estimate would be too high. If margins hold above 25 percent, it would be too low. The evidence is the September quarter. The three cases in chapter 5 imply earnings per share for that quarter of ₹2.58 with no growth on last year's ₹2.58, ₹3.10 with 20 percent growth (the base) and ₹3.61 with 40 percent growth. We would call the estimate too high if the quarter came in below ₹2.58, and too low if it came in above ₹3.61. The results are due within 45 days of 30 September, which is by 14 November 2026, and the date has not yet been announced[15].

Weakest assumption in the base value: the market keeps paying 42.3 times

The base value of ₹613.35 assumes the multiple stays where it has been since the deal was announced. That multiple was set during a period of heavy trading, with 16.3 million shares changing hands on 26 May and 2.8 million on 28 September[22]. When the volume fades, as it has to 0.39 times the 20-day average, the multiple may fall. The evidence is the price itself: a close below ₹615.33 would say the market is already paying less. We will know every day.

Weakest assumption in the bull value: that a breakout will follow

The bull value of ₹845.45 only exists after the price closes above ₹739.90. Today the price is 11.9 percent below that level, in a market the ledger calls a downtrend[34]. A measured move is a pattern, not a law, and it measures only the height of one range. The evidence that would show it wrong is a failed breakout, where the price closes above ₹739.90 and falls back below it within five sessions.

The controlling owner's own borrowing

The control of Bliss now rests on a parent that is borrowing at more than one level. Anupam issued ₹160 crore of debentures at 10.25 percent in May 2026, due on 26 June 2027, secured by a pledge of 31,50,000 of its own shares held by its promoter Anand Desai[7]. CRISIL placed Anupam's A+ rating on watch with developing implications in June 2026 because of the Bliss deal[14]. None of this says the parent cannot pay. It does mean the same group is pledging at the parent, at the subsidiary and over the Bliss shares at once. The evidence that would show the risk is real is a rating action, a new promoter pledge at Anupam, or a missed debenture payment. Anupam's next results and the CRISIL review will show the first two.

The red-team case against our own verdict

The strongest honest case for keeping the share runs as follows. The pledge is how Indian acquirers routinely secure acquisition debt. Here the debt is ₹300 crore and the pledged block is worth about ₹3,324 crore, 11.1 times as much[4][6]. The money behind Anupam includes about ₹1,450 crore from Bain Capital-led investors[9]. The business grew revenue 37.6 percent, lifted operating margin 6.8 points and has no debt[2][1]. The controlling owner paid ₹723.29 on the market, which says it sees value well above ₹652[5]. If the stock reached ₹845.45, a holder would gain ₹193.45 a share, and our rule would have sold the position before.

We keep the verdict for three reasons. The red line is written to be binary, and its purpose is to remove judgement where the downside is a forced sale, not a gradual loss. The filings that could support a narrow-debt exception, the debenture series secured and the enforcement terms, are not published[4], and even then the rule would need a deliberate override. And the price is above every base-case value we can anchor. The cost of being wrong is small: one share worth ₹652, which is 0.01 percent of net worth[34].

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER. It is triggered by rule, not by a view of the company. The rule is that a Layer 1 red line failure routes a holding to replacement. The red line that failed is the promoter pledge, which is 95.8 percent of the promoters' holding against a 20 percent limit, and the asymmetry floor failed as well at -1.05 against 3.5. The verdict changes if the pledge falls below 20 percent of the promoter holding, and if at the same time the KPI score reaches 4 of 5 and the price offers 3.5 to 1. A filing that proves the pledge secures only the ₹300 crore debentures would not clear the red line by itself. It would be evidence for a deliberate override, and the other gates would still have to pass. At the current 5.6 percent stop distance, that price is about ₹512 against the ₹613.35 base value: (613.35 − 512) ÷ (512 × 5.6 percent) is 3.5.

The practical step is to sell the one share at market or place a limit order inside the ₹630 to ₹652 range, and to put no new money into Bliss. The action is small, ₹652 of a ₹45,73,985.12 portfolio, so brokerage on a single share is a real part of the proceeds. The reason to act is discipline: the position carries a tail risk that our rules refuse to keep, however small. We hold Tanfac Industries at 4.26 percent of net worth[34], a company Anupam controls[9], so the portfolio already has exposure to this group's credit story.

What would not change the verdict is a rising price alone. A close above ₹739.90 on strong volume would turn on the ₹845.45 target.
