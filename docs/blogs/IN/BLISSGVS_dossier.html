1. What the company does

Bliss GVS Pharma makes finished medicines in dosage forms that most drug companies leave alone. Its best-known products are suppositories and pessaries, which are medicines shaped to be inserted into the body, where they melt and release the drug. It also makes capsules, tablets and syrups, and it was incorporated in 1984[1]. Crisil describes formulations made mainly for sale in Africa in anti-malarial, anti-fungal, anti-bacterial, anti-inflammatory, contraceptive and anti-diabetic groups, and says the company also makes suppositories and pessaries under contract for Sun Pharma and Mankind, which do not have rights to export them[7].

The customers are overseas. In FY26 ₹836.18 crore of the ₹903.32 crore of goods sold went outside India, which is 92.6 percent on our division of the two filed figures[3][42]. A secondary source puts Africa at 72 percent of FY26 revenue and Europe at 14 percent, a split the annual report does not give[28]. One customer took more than 10 percent of group sales, and the filed amount of ₹277.09 crore is 30.2 percent of the ₹917.18 crore of contract revenue[3][42]. Customers can take up to 240 days to pay[3], and that is why unpaid bills decide so much of the analysis in this report.

Capacity is large against sales. ICRA lists five plants in India, four at Palghar and one at Ambernath, with installed capacity of about 3.05 billion tablets and 341.4 million suppositories and pessaries a year; the plants are WHO-GMP and EU-GMP accredited and one Palghar unit also holds USFDA approval[6]. Other sources count differently: Anupam Rasayan says six plants in Maharashtra[10] and Crisil says seven units, two of them in Nigeria[7]. They also differ on brands, with more than 150 (Anupam, PTI) against more than 250 (Crisil)[20][7]. We use the capacity figures and treat the counts as unsettled. Anupam says the plants run at about 30 percent of capacity and that 60 to 70 percent is reachable in two to three years[10][12]. That is the buyer's view, not a figure Bliss has filed.

The money comes from three places. The first is the factory: group revenue from operations was ₹927.11 crore in FY26, up 14.5 percent, and EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), the profit from running the business before those charges, was ₹164.04 crore[3]. The second is other income of ₹73.53 crore, of which ₹51.78 crore was a currency translation gain[3]. The third is interest on bank deposits, ₹16.54 crore[3]. In the June 2026 quarter revenue rose 37.6 percent to ₹285.58 crore, the operating margin, which is the share of sales left after paying the running costs of the business, rose to 26.75 percent from 19.92 percent on our recompute, and profit after tax was ₹51.37 crore[5][42].

One idea runs through the rest of the report. Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company owes its suppliers. Bliss's consolidated trade receivables were ₹517.19 crore at March 2026, 55.8 percent of the year's revenue, or about 204 days of sales[3][42]. Of the ₹579.10 crore gross, 62 percent was past its due date, and the company had set aside ₹61.91 crore, 10.7 percent of the gross, for expected losses[3][42].

Who owns the company now

On 23 May 2026 Anupam Rasayan India, a Surat specialty-chemicals maker, signed an agreement to buy shares from the founding promoters and three other holders at ₹299 a share[9][20]. The agreement forced an open offer, which is a legal invitation to every other shareholder to sell to the buyer at a stated price, here for up to 26 percent of the company at the same ₹299[9]. Only 1,669 shares were tendered, because the market price was ₹482.50 to ₹483.50 on 17 July, far above the offer[9][22].

The deal closed on 28 September 2026. Mates Visa Consultancy, Anupam's wholly owned subsidiary, took 5,09,84,595 shares, and with the open-offer shares the buyers held 47.95 percent[8]. Anupam also holds 22,51,669 shares in its own name, so the September 2026 shareholding table shows the group at 50.07 percent[24][13]. The outgoing promoters, who held 34.82 percent, were reclassified as public shareholders[19].

Anupam says it paid with a ₹300 crore term loan and about ₹1,450 crore raised through non-voting instruments from investors led by Bain Capital, with Trust Group and Investec[10]. The ₹300 crore is raised by Mates as secured, unrated, unlisted debentures with a maximum life of 48 months. Anupam guarantees them, pledges all of Mates's shares and its 15 percent stake in the investor vehicle Purebliss Pharma Solutions to the debenture trustee, and has granted Purebliss an option to buy all of Mates for ₹1 on certain events of default, a price Anupam says reflects "the distressed-scenario nature of the trigger conditions"[11]. A pledge is a promise of shares as security for a loan, so the lender can sell them if the loan is not repaid. The debenture trustee, Catalyst Trusteeship, is reported to hold a pledge over the 5,09,84,595 Bliss shares held by Mates, 47.95 percent of the company, as at 30 September 2026; we read this through a Whalesbook summary, and the promoter table behind the 95.77 percent figure is the September 2026 shareholding table as shown by Trendlyne and Screener, with the trustee filing not fetched[16][13]. We infer that this pledge secures the ₹300 crore of debentures, because the trustee and the dates are the same, but the secured amount was not read in the trustee's own filing.

Moat & Margin reports that the terms of the roughly ₹1,450 crore instruments are not in any filing it read[19], and we did not find them either. That gap matters, because outside investors that put up 83 percent of the funds usually hold a claim on the upside. The management change followed at once: the managing director and promoter, Mr Narsimha Kamath, resigned on 28 September, and the board added three non-executive, non-independent directors, one of whom was an independent director of Anupam from 2021 to 2024[8]. Their appointment awaits a postal ballot with voting from 16 October to 14 November and results on 17 November 2026[1]. A chief executive from outside, Rahul Adakmol, was named on 3 July and took office on 15 July[29].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Suppository and pessary: a medicine shaped to be inserted into the body, where body heat melts it; suppositories are used in the rectum and pessaries in the vagina.
  • Open offer: the legal invitation, required when a buyer takes control of a listed company, for other holders to sell to the buyer at a stated price.
  • Pledge: shares handed to a lender as security; the lender may sell them if the borrower defaults.
  • Debenture: a loan note; secured means backed by named assets, unrated means no rating agency has graded it.
  • Call option: the right, but not the duty, to buy something at a fixed price; here the price is ₹1.
  • ASM and trade-for-trade: an exchange surveillance step that can raise margins and forces every trade to be settled by delivery, with no same-day buying and selling.
  • Circuit limit: the largest one-day price move the exchange allows before trading in the share stops for the day. The next chapter turns from these terms to the strengths and weaknesses of the business.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when it earns a high return on what it has invested, owes little, and is hard for rivals to copy. Bliss has two of the three. It owes almost nothing: current borrowings were ₹2.93 crore against equity of ₹1,231.76 crore at March 2026, and the company held ₹35.14 crore in cash and ₹131.73 crore in other bank balances[3]. Crisil's adjusted debt-to-net-worth reading was 0.00 times and interest cover 19.79 times; ICRA shows total debt at 0.1 times operating profit and interest cover of 17.2 times[7][6]. The return it earns on its capital is lower and less steady, which is the weak spot.

The niche is the main strength. Suppositories and pessaries need special moulding and cooling equipment and quality systems, and the company's own annual report calls its position in them "a specialized entry barrier, providing an operational moat against standard formulation manufacturers"[3]. That is a company statement and should be read as one. Outside evidence points the same way: Sun Pharma and Mankind buy suppositories and pessaries from Bliss under contract[7], and ICRA records that the plants carry WHO-GMP and EU-GMP accreditation and, at one Palghar unit, USFDA approval[6]. A USFDA inspection at Palghar in March 2023 ended with three observations the company called minor and procedural, none on data integrity[30].

Where the business is weak

Growth has been slow for most of the past decade. Screener shows compound sales growth of 5 percent over ten years, 10 percent over five and 7 percent over three, and a return on equity near 10 percent over three years[1]. Sales were ₹752 crore in FY23 and ₹927 crore in FY26[1]. The recent jump, up 21 percent over the latest twelve months and 37.6 percent in the June 2026 quarter, is real but short, and it follows three years of low single-digit growth (1, 2 and 5 percent)[1][5].

Collections are the second weakness. The consolidated balance sheet carries ₹517.19 crore of net receivables against revenue of ₹927.11 crore, up from ₹438.80 crore a year earlier, a rise of 17.9 percent against revenue growth of 14.5 percent[3]. Only 38 percent of the gross balance was not yet due, and ₹64.39 crore, 11.1 percent, was more than six months past due[3][42]. We read this as exposure to the credit of a few distributors and to the currencies they earn in.

Concentration is the third. Outside India the company sold 92.6 percent of its goods[3][42], and one customer took 30.2 percent of contract revenue[3][42]. A single large buyer paying late, or one African currency falling sharply, would reach the profit and loss account at once. The company also records currency gains and losses as a normal part of its results, which leads to the next subsection.

What the earnings are made of

FY26 profit before tax was ₹189.59 crore, and ₹51.78 crore of it, 27.3 percent, was a gain on currency translation, after a ₹34.81 crore fair-value loss on forward contracts had already been netted off[3][42]. Remove that gain after a 29 percent tax charge and group profit falls from ₹134.73 crore to about ₹97.9 crore, which is earnings of about ₹8.7 a share instead of the reported ₹12.23[3][42]. We treat currency translation as real money that can reverse, not as a permanent part of earning power.

The June 2026 quarter shows why this matters in both directions. Other income fell from ₹35.45 crore in the June 2025 quarter to ₹9.60 crore, so profit after tax rose only 15.8 percent, from ₹44.36 crore to ₹51.37 crore[5]. Underneath, operating profit rose from about ₹41.3 crore to about ₹76.4 crore, up 84.9 percent, on our recompute[5][42]. The core business is therefore improving faster than the headline profit shows, and the headline in FY26 flattered it. A reader should keep both facts in mind: the operating margin of 26.75 percent is the best of the thirteen quarters on Screener, and it is one quarter[1].

What this means for an owner

For an owner of the business, the picture is of a sound, nearly debt-free exporter with a real niche, a rising operating margin, slow historic growth and an unusually long collection cycle. None of that is in doubt. The harder question is what the shares are worth. The answer depends on what the new owner does with the plants and the customers, on whether currency gains repeat, and on whether the receivables turn into cash. It also depends on a fact that sits outside the accounts: the shares were bought by a controlling owner at ₹299 and now trade at more than twice that. 3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter lays out the filed numbers and then applies the hard rules. A few measures recur, so we define them once. EPS (earnings per share) is a company's profit divided by its number of shares. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit a business earns on each rupee of capital it uses. The price-to-earnings ratio, written P/E, is the share price divided by a year of profit per share, so a P/E of 20 means paying ₹20 for each rupee of yearly profit. The debt-to-equity ratio is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before any financing.

Unless stated otherwise the numbers are consolidated, which means they add Bliss's subsidiaries to the parent company. We state one choice up front because it decides every red line: the red lines are read on the reported consolidated statements and on the September 2026 shareholding table, not on standalone or adjusted figures. Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 combines hard rules with the state of the trend and the price. We need a few terms from chart analysis, so here they are in one place. A 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 sessions, and the 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average work the same way over longer spans, which makes them lines that trace the short, medium and long trends. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend, with the price above rising 50-day and 200-day averages, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend; the ledger labels Bliss GVS as Stage 3, the topping or pullback phase between them. A base is a sideways stretch of trading from which a rally starts, the top of that stretch is the pivot, a breakout is a move above the pivot on heavy volume, and a retest is the pullback that checks the pivot from above. RSI (relative strength index) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been moving, where under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought. ATR (average true range) is the average size of a day's price swing, which is ₹32.88 for this share.

Three terms set the trade levels. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut. An entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned, normally filled with a limit order, which is an order that fills only at your price or better and does not chase the market. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the gain from price to target divided by the loss from price to stop, so a ratio of 2.5 means ₹2.50 of gain for each ₹1 of loss. The floor for the tactical bucket is 2.5, and the target must come from forward EPS times a median P/E, a discounted-cash-flow value, or a measured move, which is the height of a base projected above its pivot[43].

If the probabilities were 40, 40 and 20 the value would be ₹383; if they were 10, 40 and 50 it would be ₹286. Neither is near the price.

If the number is off

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A central value of ₹310 against a price of ₹623.35 is a strong claim, so this chapter lists what could make it wrong, in the order in which each item could change the verdict. Several risks are about the stock and not the company, and we separate them: the business can be fine while the price is not.

The premium could last

The first risk is that the share trades above its value for much longer than we expect. About half the shares are in public hands, trading is delivery-only and the exchange has already moved it to trade-for-trade settlement; buying interest in a recently acquired share can also persist for months. Since the pledge was reported the price has fallen about 10 percent, but it has stayed above ₹600 in every close, and the open offer at ₹299 drew only 1,669 shares because the market price was far above it[43][22]. If the premium lasts we sell a share that later rises, and on one share the cost is small. The risk to a larger holder would be much greater, which is the reason the verdict is the same at any size.

Earnings could be far higher than our central build

Operating margin in the June 2026 quarter was 26.75 percent, and the buyer thinks plant use can double. If those hold, our bull value is ₹539 and the buyer's full plan gives about ₹660 in three years. We weight that path at 20 percent for the bull case. We show the full plan separately because it needs sales and cash to grow together while receivables already take 204 days. The September results on 29 October will give the first evidence. The check is on three numbers together: operating margin, receivable days and other income. On our base build a margin of 25 percent gives about ₹349 and 27 percent about ₹380, and a margin back at 20 percent gives about ₹271 (arithmetic in chapter 5). Receivable days are not an input to those values; falling receivables would raise our confidence in earnings quality and cash, which is a qualitative gain and not a rupee value.

The pledge may be a financing security and not a warning

The pledge reading is secondary-source: the September 2026 shareholding table as shown by Trendlyne and Screener, with the trustee filing not fetched. We infer that the pledge secures the ₹300 crore of debentures, because the trustee (Catalyst) and the dates match, but the secured amount was not read in the trustee's own filing. If it secures only the ₹300 crore, the loan is about 9.4 percent of the pledged shares' value of about ₹3,178 crore at today's price, and the shares would have to fall about 90 percent before the collateral equalled the loan[11][42][16]. Anupam also guarantees the debentures. Against that, the debentures are unrated, the investors' terms for the roughly ₹1,450 crore of non-voting instruments are not public, Purebliss can take Mates for ₹1 on default, and Anupam has itself borrowed ₹145 crore for 13 months at 10.25 percent[11][19]. The rule we apply has no exemption for a loan that is small against its collateral, and we do not invent one. We record the point because it explains why the failure does not signal distress in the operating business, even though it still decides the verdict. The exit is a policy decision on the pledge reading. The verdict stands unless a primary filing showed a figure under 20 percent, and the fields that would confirm or overturn it are the promoter shares, the pledged shares, the effective date and any release. Cash conversion and corporate debt do not measure acquisition-vehicle risk.

Source quality and the rating withdrawal

The pledge percentage comes from the September 2026 shareholding table as shown by Trendlyne and Screener, both secondary aggregators, and the trustee's filing was read through a news summary[13][16]. The Africa share of revenue is from a secondary article[28]. The Crisil withdrawal is the hardest item to read. Crisil marked the company issuer-not-cooperating at B and withdrew at the company's request with a bank no-objection certificate[7]; that can be a housekeeping step after loans were repaid (borrowings fell 96 percent) or a sign of an unwillingness to share information. ICRA, which kept an A- rating on watch, noted no change in operations for 8 to 12 months and no new debt for Bliss[6]. We scored it as a watch item for that reason.

Transition risk for the business

The managing director has gone, three directors linked to the buyer have joined and a new chief executive started in July, so the top of the company has changed within three months, and we do not yet know whether pricing and credit practice will change with it[8][29]. Receivables are the largest asset and are most exposed to that change in either direction: a new owner can tighten credit, which would lower sales growth, or loosen it, which would raise the balance and the expected-loss provision. Currency is the other exposure; 92.6 percent of goods are sold abroad and one customer takes 30.2 percent of contract revenue[3][42].
