Before you read

Eris Lifesciences sells branded prescription medicines in India, mostly for long-term illnesses such as diabetes and heart disease. We reject the stock because promoters have pledged 21.25% of their shares as loan security while the price sits in a long downtrend, which breaks a written rule[14]. The possible gain is also only 1.19 times the possible loss, against a 2.0 floor. The domestic business is healthy, with sales up about 14%, but the injectables plants and the debt are weak points, and net debt (borrowings minus cash) of ₹2,144 crore is falling only slowly[3]. We would revisit the call if the pledge is released and the margin returns to 36%, and a price near ₹1,135 would restore the 2.0 times ratio.

1. What the company does

Eris Lifesciences is a pharmaceutical company based in Ahmedabad. Doctors prescribe its medicines, patients buy them at pharmacies, and the company earns the price of each pack after trade margins. About ₹809 crore of the ₹892 crore it booked in the September 2026 quarter came from its domestic branded formulations business, which is the company's name for prescription brands sold in India[2]. The rest came mainly from Swiss Parenterals, a maker of sterile injections that it sells in Africa, Asia and Latin America[2].

Most of what Eris sells is taken every day for years. The company's own slide puts 73% of domestic revenue in chronic therapies, 13% in sub-chronic therapies and 15% in acute (short-course) medicines, so 86% is chronic or sub-chronic[2]. Management expects that share to rise toward 90% after it licensed about ₹100 crore of acute and injectable sales to Zorvia Healthcare for a 7% royalty[2][3]. Long-term medicines matter because a patient who starts a diabetes tablet usually stays on it, which makes sales steadier than for a cough syrup.

Diabetes is the largest therapy at 37.3% of revenue, and it grew 18.5% in the September quarter against 16.1% for the whole Indian market[2]. Cardiac, dermatology and vitamins-minerals-nutrition each add roughly 15% of revenue[3]. Ten brands produce 44% of domestic sales and three brands each sell more than ₹200 crore a year[2]. That concentration is useful, because a few well-known brands let a small sales force reach specialist doctors, but it also means one banned or copied product can dent the year.

The company grew by buying brands and a plant-owning subsidiary. It bought Biocon's India formulations business, which brought the insulin brands, and a stake in Swiss Parenterals[10]. Those deals are why borrowings rose from ₹84 crore in FY22 to ₹2,359 crore in FY26[4]. Management now says the buying phase is over: it told analysts in July 2026 that it is not looking for any acquisition and is spending on factories instead[8]. The next chapters test whether the borrowed money is earning its keep.

Who pays, and how the money flows

Patients pay pharmacies, pharmacies buy from distributors, and distributors buy from Eris. Eris spends about 17% of revenue on employees and about 22% on other costs such as launches and regulatory filings, and keeps roughly a third as operating profit[2]. Government price controls reach about 17% of Eris sales, the same share as for the Indian market as a whole[13]. Because most of the portfolio sits outside price control, Eris can raise prices a little each year, which supports margins when volumes are steady.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is operating profit before interest, tax and the write-off of old purchases. It shows what the business earns before the cost of its debt.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is the profit that belongs to one share. A rising EPS is what a share price usually follows over several years.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) measures profit earned on every rupee of capital tied up in the business. Acquisitions lower it because the purchase price sits in the denominator.
  • Free cash flow, or FCF, is cash from operations minus spending on plant and equipment. It is the cash left for debt repayment and dividends.
  • net debt is borrowings minus cash. Dividing it by EBITDA shows how many years of operating profit it would take to repay.
  • debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' funds. A figure above 1.20 is a red line in our rules.
  • gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of making the product, as a share of revenue. operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue.
  • price-to-earnings is the share price divided by the profit per share. A lower figure means each rupee of profit costs less.
  • guidance is a forecast that management gives about its own results. Chapter 2B checks how often it came true.
  • Pledge means shares that promoters have given to lenders as security for loans. If the share price falls far enough, lenders can sell them.
  • Stage 4 is the downtrend phase of a share price, when the price sits below falling 50-day and 200-day averages. Stage 2 is the opposite, a rising phase.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has risen or fallen. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical size of one day's price swing. We quote distances in ATR so that a calm stock and a wild one are judged fairly.
  • A base is a flat resting area on a price chart, and the pivot is the price where a breakout from that base would start.
  • The entry zone is the price range where we would be willing to buy. reward-to-risk (also called asymmetry), is the gain to our target divided by the loss to our stop.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest part of the moat is distribution into specialist doctors. Eris ranks among the top 20 companies in the Indian pharmaceutical market and is the youngest of them[4]. It is number one in injectable semaglutide, the diabetes and weight-loss drug whose Indian patent ended in March 2026, measured by units and by prescriptions, and number two by value[3]. Management put the generic semaglutide market at about ₹40 to 45 crore a month and expects it to reach ₹80 to 90 crore a month next year[3]. Eris booked ₹16 to 18 crore from the drug in the September quarter[3]. That is real but small beside ₹892 crore of revenue.

Insulin is the second pillar. In the year to March 2026 Eris's share of rapid human insulin cartridges rose from 13% to 24%, and its insulin sales grew 32% against 6.7% for its covered market[7]. The weak point was supply. Management admitted that moving manufacture to its own Bhopal plant ran nearly a year late and cost about ₹50 crore of sales in FY26 after a similar loss in FY25[7]. Bhopal now makes vials and cartridges, but the full shift of products there is planned only for the second half of next year[2]. A moat that depends on a supplier or a plant that keeps slipping is narrower than the market-share charts suggest.

Margins are the evidence of pricing power, and the picture is mixed. Eris earns a gross margin of 74.7% and an operating margin near 35%[2]. Those are high for Indian pharma. But domestic EBITDA margin fell from 37.6% to 35.7% in a year, because other expenses jumped 29.8% on launches, filings and regulatory work[2]. Management says the cause is a heavy launch season. A fair reader should treat that as an unproven claim until the third-quarter margin arrives.

The injectables business is the weak spot. In March 2026 European inspectors visited the two Swiss Parenterals plants. Management told investors the observations were largely procedural[7]. The European inspection record lists 3 critical, 12 major and 2 other deficiencies, including weak control of germs during sterile handling[27]. Critical findings of that kind are not clerical. The plant will shut for repairs in November and management hopes to invite inspectors back by year-end[3]. Management says the pipeline for a European contract-manufacturing business worth ₹800 to 1,000 crore is intact with no cancellations, but revenue cannot start until the plants are re-approved[7][2].

Competition and price pressure

Eris competes with larger and better-known Indian drug companies. Five peers in the matrix in chapter 3 trade at 26 to 54 times profit, while Eris trades at 22 to 23 times[4][28][29][30][31][32]. Screener shows 21.8 times for Eris and the ledger shows 22.9 times at the same price, which implies the two sources use different profit figures. We use the ledger's 22.9 for Eris's own valuation and Screener's 21.8 in the peer table so that all six names come from one source. The discount reflects three things the market can see: debt, a lower return on capital than Ajanta or Alkem, and the Swiss regulatory problem. The business is not a commodity, but it earns a smaller reward for its quality than the peer group does.

Regulation and legal risk

Eris sold four products at risk, meaning it launched generic copies before patent challenges were settled. It has now settled with customers and wrote off ₹35.9 crore of receivables[1][3]. The same strategy once produced good sales, so a future at-risk launch is possible and so is another write-off. India's government also promotes cheaper generic medicines, which Ind-Ra lists as a risk to branded pricing[13].

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

The second half of Layer 2 asks a plain question: when management gave a number, did it deliver? Eris gave unusually clear targets in August 2025, so the record is easy to check. The domestic business kept its word. The financial targets that depended on acquisitions, plants and the injectables unit did not.

The August 2025 presentation promised FY26 revenue of ₹3,325 to 3,500 crore, EBITDA of ₹1,190 to 1,255 crore, EPS growth of 50%, an adjusted ROCE of 22% and net debt of ₹1,800 crore by March 2026[10]. Actual revenue was ₹3,129 crore, operating profit was ₹1,120 crore and net debt was ₹2,255 crore[4][7]. In February 2026 the deadline for the debt goal moved to December 2026[11]. With net debt at ₹2,144 crore in September and a planned paydown of only ₹150 to 200 crore for the full year, that date will also be missed[3].

The injectables guidance was cut twice. In May 2026 management guided Swiss revenue growth of 18% to 20%[7]. By July it said growth would be neutral to low single digits with margins down a couple of hundred basis points[8]. In October it said revenue would be within plus or minus 5% and the margin about 25%[3]. One basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point.

The pattern has a consequence for valuation. When a company misses its own debt target for a year and then moves the date, the market reasonably asks for a lower price on every later promise. That is part of why Eris trades at 22 to 23 times reported profit while peers with cleaner records trade between 26 and 54 times[4][31][28]. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of figures show a company that more than doubled sales by buying businesses, paid for them with debt, and is now earning its way back. Revenue rose from ₹1,347 crore in FY22 to ₹3,129 crore in FY26[4]. Operating margin stayed between 32% and 36% throughout[4]. Profit, however, did not follow sales until FY26, because interest and the cost of writing down purchased brands took the gains.

Reported FY26 profit of ₹648 crore is flattered. Management said a one-off tax gain added ₹150 crore, and profit from continuing operations was ₹498 crore[7]. The same pattern repeats in the September 2026 quarter, where a tax settlement and a one-off write-off both moved profit. The reader needs the clean numbers, so the tables below show both.

The number

We estimate that Eris will earn ₹43.6 of adjusted profit per share in FY27, less than 1% below the analyst average of ₹43.77[35]. The two estimates that follow are our own. They test the debt and cash gates in the table above, and chapter 5 reuses the EPS estimate.

How it was calculated

We took the first-half revenue of ₹1,766 crore, added a second half of ₹1,744 crore (11.5% growth over last year's second half), applied the 36% second-half margin management guided, and took off depreciation, interest and a 20.5% tax rate.

The inputs

First-half EBITDA ₹606 crore and revenue ₹1,766 crore[2]; second-half margin guide of about 36%[3]; tax rate guide of 20% to 21%[3]; first-half depreciation ₹145 crore and finance cost ₹93 crore[1]; 13.9 crore shares[17]. Full-year EBITDA comes to about ₹1,234 crore, profit before tax about ₹762 crore and adjusted profit about ₹606 crore.

The range

If the second-half margin is 35% instead of 36%, EPS falls to about ₹42.6. If it is 37%, EPS rises to about ₹44.6.

If the number is off

A one-point miss on margin moves FY27 EPS by about ₹1.0, or 2%. That is small for the year, but a miss would signal that the gross-margin recovery is not arriving, which would hurt the multiple far more than the earnings.

The number

We estimate net debt of about ₹2,080 crore at March 2027, which is 1.7 times FY27 EBITDA and above management's target of less than 1.5 times by December 2026.

How it was calculated

We started from the ₹2,255 crore at March 2026 and subtracted the midpoint of the ₹150 to 200 crore paydown management guided for FY27, then divided by our FY27 EBITDA of ₹1,234 crore.

The inputs

Opening net debt ₹2,255 crore[7]; guided paydown ₹150 to 200 crore for this year and about ₹400 crore next year[3]; remaining capex of about ₹100 crore in H2[3]; September 2026 net debt ₹2,144 crore[3].

The range

At the high end of paydown (₹200 crore) net debt is ₹2,055 crore and the ratio is 1.67 times. If the Swiss shutdown costs cash, net debt could stay near ₹2,144 crore and the ratio near 1.74 times.

If the number is off

Ind-Ra would cut its rating outlook only if the ratio stays above 2.0 times on a sustained basis[13]. A 1.7 times reading is therefore safe for credit, but it keeps the earlier 1.5 times promise unmet for a second year.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate rests on one assumption that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest one for each, shows what evidence would disprove it, and says when we will know. It then gives the best argument against our own conclusion and picks the one claim the whole call hangs on.

Weakest assumption in the base EPS: the 36% margin

We accepted management's 36% second-half margin because the gross margin rose 134 bps from Q1 to Q2 and the Zorvia licence starts to show from the third quarter[2][3]. But the same management said in May that domestic margin would be 37% and delivered 35.2% for the half[7][2]. Other expenses rose 27% in Q2[2]. The Q3 results, due around mid-February 2027 (the company has not announced the date), will show whether the 36% holds. If the margin stays at 35% and the multiple is 22 times, FY28 EPS is about ₹50.8 and the price about ₹1,118, which is 5% below today.

Weakest assumption in the multiple: 25 times is the right level

The market has paid 44 times median trailing earnings and 21 times EV/EBITDA for Eris over three years[5]. If the Swiss plant is re-approved and margins recover, the stock could re-rate to 28 to 30 times and reach our bull case. If our 25 times is too cautious, we understate the base by about ₹160 a share. The Investor Day on 19 November 2026 and the Swiss re-inspection, expected around the turn of the year, are the next tests[3].

Weakest assumption in the rejection: the pledge reading

The rejection depends on two facts. The first is that 21.25% of promoter shares are pledged, which comes from two aggregators that agree with each other because the exchange's own shareholding file was not reachable from our environment[14][15]. The second is that the pledge rule counts a Stage 4 label even when Test C passes. If a reader decides that Test C should cancel the Stage 4 trigger in the pledge clause, the verdict would move to wait-for-support, not a buy, because the reward-to-risk ratio still fails. Promoter holding rose from 54.14% to 54.74% in the same quarter that the pledge rose by 4.4 points, which suggests the promoters bought shares and borrowed against them[14]. That is our inference only. A 0.6-point rise in holding does not by itself explain a 4.4-point rise in the pledged share, and re-pledging existing shares would look the same. We do not use the inference to raise the bear probability. The exchange filing is the first reopen trigger.
