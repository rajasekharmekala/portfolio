1. What the company does

Surya Roshni Limited is an Indian manufacturer with two businesses under one roof. The older one turns flat steel coils into steel pipes and strips. The younger one makes light bulbs, ceiling lights, fans, water heaters, induction cooktops and, since August 2025, house wires. The company was started in 1973 as Prakash Tubes with one pipe unit at Bahadurgarh in Haryana, and it added lighting in 1985[13]. Today it calls itself the largest exporter of welded (ERW) pipes in India and the largest maker of galvanised (GI) pipes[1].

Steel pipes are the larger business by far. In FY26 the pipes and strips segment earned ₹5,731 crore of the group's ₹7,540 crore sales, which is 76 percent[8]. The segment sold 9.04 lakh tonnes in FY26 from plants in Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat and Andhra Pradesh, with an installed capacity of 14.21 lakh tonnes a year[9][8]. The pipes go into farm irrigation, water lines, fire-fighting, scaffolding, fencing, building frames, and oil and gas pipelines. The company says it has more than 21,000 dealers and retailers and more than 250 distributors across India[8].

The money in this business comes from a spread. Surya buys hot-rolled steel coil, shapes and welds it into pipe, coats or galvanises some of it, and sells it at a price that moves with steel. What it keeps after paying for steel, power, labour and freight is its profit per tonne. That number is the best single gauge of the business. It was ₹4,648 per tonne in FY22, ₹6,496 in FY23, ₹5,401 in FY24, ₹5,392 in FY25 and ₹4,553 in FY26[8]. A higher share of value-added pipes, such as API line pipe, coated pipe, galvanised pipe and large hollow sections, lifts the profit per tonne. Those products were 43 percent of FY26 volume and about 47 percent in Q1 FY27[8][1].

Who pays? Management gave a rough split in August 2026. About 53 percent of steel sales go through domestic trade and consumer channels, about 20 percent are exports, about 10 percent is cold-rolled strip, and about 15 percent is government and end users. Management also said that 75 to 80 percent of the steel business behaves like a distribution business with steady repeat buyers[2]. Exports became more important this year. A new US market for API pipe opened in 2026, and the company reported 78,000 tonnes of US export orders on 1 July[1].

The lighting and consumer durables segment earned ₹1,809 crore in FY26 and ₹156 crore of EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, which is operating profit as the company counts it), a margin of 8.6 percent[8]. Its professional lighting arm, which sells to governments and builders, earned ₹473 crore, and the company makes about 80 percent of what it sells in its own factories at Kashipur and Gwalior[9][2][20]. Wires and cables are the newest line: ₹38 crore in FY26 and a ₹260 crore target for FY27[9]. The group has almost no borrowings, so the owners' return depends on how well these two businesses earn on the money they use. Chapter 2 asks whether that earning power is strong or weak.

Where the plants are and why that matters

Pipe is heavy and cheap per kilogram, so freight decides who can sell where. Surya runs steel plants at Bahadurgarh (Haryana), Malanpur near Gwalior (Madhya Pradesh), Anjar near Kandla and Mundra ports (Gujarat) and Hindupur (Andhra Pradesh)[20]. The Gujarat plant sits near the ports, which suits export orders and the coated API pipe line. The Andhra plant serves the south. Management plans to add three direct forming technology mills at Anjar, Malanpur and Bahadurgarh between August and December 2026, and a four-mill block at Hindupur from January 2027 for about ₹60 crore[1][2]. Direct forming technology shapes square and rectangular section pipe in one pass, which saves steel and time.

The capacity plan is large. Management wants about 16 lakh tonnes in FY27 and about 20 lakh tonnes by FY28 to FY29, against 14.21 lakh tonnes today, and the annual report puts the three-year capital plan at ₹500 crore[2][6]. That is about 2 to 3 lakh tonnes of new capacity a year. The company plans to fund it from its own cash, and management says the 14-to-16 lakh step costs about ₹100 crore[2]. A plan funded from cash is good for safety. It also uses cash that could return to shareholders, which matters in chapters 3 and 7.

  • price-to-earnings is the share price divided by profit per share, which shows how many years of current profit the price pays for.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Surya's strengths are real, but they are the strengths of a good manufacturer, not of a business that rivals cannot copy. It is the largest maker of ERW GI pipe and the largest ERW pipe exporter in India, according to its own filings[1]. It has a pipe brand, Prakash Surya, that has been sold for about 50 years through 21,000 retailers[8]. Its managing director says it is the only Indian company making mild steel pipe in thicknesses from 0.6 mm to 25 mm and sizes from half an inch to 140 inches[1]. In 2025-26 it made API 5CT casing pipe by ERW technology for the first time in India and won an order from ONGC[6]. These are useful edges because they let the company shift output between products as demand moves.

The balance sheet is the strongest part of the case. Borrowings were ₹65 crore at 31 March 2026 against net worth of ₹2,634 crore, and net cash was ₹337 crore[8][6]. CARE reaffirmed AA with a stable outlook in July 2026 and noted an interest cover of 17.67 times and a total debt to operating profit of 0.16 times[11][12]. That rating was an upgrade from AA- in July 2025, and in September 2024 CARE had moved the outlook on AA- to positive[15][16]. ICRA called liquidity strong, with free cash and bank balances of about ₹420 crore[13]. Promoters hold 63.02 percent and have pledged none of it[34]. A company in this shape does not need lenders, so it can fund capacity from its own cash.

The weaknesses show up in the returns. ICRA describes Surya as a steel converter in a business with limited value addition, low entry barriers and many unorganised rivals, and it says that limits pricing power[13]. Revenue was ₹7,731 crore in FY22 and ₹7,540 crore in FY26, so five years of capacity and brand spending have produced no growth in sales[8]. Profit after tax fell from ₹347 crore to ₹286 crore in FY26, and operating margin fell from 7.8 percent to 6.5 percent[8][13]. Return on net worth was 11.21 percent in FY26 against 15.06 percent in FY25[6].

Exports are still a modest part of the group: 13.90 percent of FY26 turnover according to the sustainability report, though the steel segment aims for 25 percent of its own volume[18][2]. ICRA's 2025 review put FY25 operating margin at 7.8 percent and lighting margin at about 9 percent[14]. Compare the steel spread with the closest listed rival. APL Apollo Tubes earned ₹5,522 per tonne in Q1 FY27 on 7.45 lakh tonnes, while Surya earned ₹4,006 per tonne on 2.28 lakh tonnes[21][1]. APL Apollo's return on capital employed is 31.8 percent against Surya's 15.6 percent[30][29]. Surya is a mid-pack converter. That is why its shares trade at about 14.7 times trailing earnings on the 28 October close, while APL Apollo trades at 46.5 times on its 29 October quote[29][30].

The steel price spread: what we checked

Pipe makers do not own the steel mill, so the spread between coil cost and pipe price is what matters. We checked four facts. First, Surya's own profit per tonne is the cleanest measure of that spread, and it fell from ₹5,392 in FY25 to ₹4,553 in FY26 and ₹4,006 in Q1 FY27[8][1]. Second, in the June 2026 quarter coil prices rose ₹3,000 to ₹4,000 in April and then fell in May and June, so the quarter had no inventory gain or loss, and management said steel prices were stable within ₹1,000 either way[2]. Third, the spread is sensitive to coil prices: in the December 2024 quarter average hot-rolled coil prices were ₹10,500 a tonne lower than a year earlier, and steel pipe revenue fell 8 percent even though volume rose 8 percent[17]. Fourth, by early October 2026 Indian steel prices were reported 21 to 28.8 percent above a year earlier, and the Steel Ministry declined to seek more import curbs[28].

A rising coil price helps a pipe maker with stock on hand and hurts when buyers hold back. APL Apollo said distributors turned cautious about stocking because of the steep rise in hot-rolled coil prices[27]. Surya had the opposite experience in October to December 2025, when a sharp price fall caused an inventory loss of about ₹12 crore, which is about ₹500 per tonne[10]. The safeguard duty on flat steel imports, which are Surya's raw material, was 12 percent in the first year and 11.5 percent in the second, and it supports domestic coil prices and so raises Surya's input cost[25]. We could not read a daily coil quote because the BigMint price page refused access, so we state the spread through Surya's own profit per tonne and the Ministry price comment only.

The lighting and consumer durables business

Lighting grew more steadily but earned a thinner margin than rivals report. Segment margin was 8.6 percent in FY26 against 9.6 percent in FY25, and 7.9 percent in Q1 FY27 against 7.7 percent a year earlier[8][1]. Management passed on an input cost rise of about 10 percent gross and 7 percent net in Q1, with no big profit hit, and said fans were the one flat category because copper and steel costs made distributors careful about stock[1]. Crompton, a listed rival, reported lighting sales up 14.3 percent in Q2 FY27 but a lighting profit margin down 2.6 points to 12.9 percent, partly because of fixed-price business orders[23]. The two companies measure margin differently, so we use the trend, not the level: costs are rising for everyone and pricing power is limited.

The group is also paying to build new lines. Marketing spending for the steel brand is planned at ₹20 crore in FY27 against ₹7 crore last year, and the wires business is in its first full year[1]. These are growth costs that should show up as sales later. They are also costs that can push margin down first, which is why we score lighting margin as a watch item in chapter 2B.

What this means for an owner

An owner gets a debt-free, family-controlled manufacturer with a good brand, a growing export order book and a cheap valuation. The owner also gets a business whose sales have not grown for five years, whose profit per tonne has slipped, and whose managers have missed their own volume and profit forecasts several times in the last 12 months. Chapter 2B puts numbers on that record. Chapter 3 then shows whether the accounts back up the strength we see in the balance sheet.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A company that explains its misses well can still disappoint year after year, so we compared what management promised with what happened over the last 12 months. The record is mixed. On balance sheet and capital allocation, management did what it said. It paid down debt from ₹580 crore in FY22 to ₹3 crore in FY25 and says the group is debt-free[8]. It paid ₹5 per share of dividends for FY26, about 38 percent of profit, and it described a split of one-third of profit to dividends, one-third to working capital and growth, and one-third to capital spending[9][29].

On operating forecasts, the record is weaker. In February 2026 management said it would sell 9.35 to 9.40 lakh tonnes in FY26 and 2.9 to 3.0 lakh tonnes in Q4; the actual was 9.04 and about 2.6[10][9]. It said profit per tonne would be ₹4,800 to ₹4,900 for FY26 and ₹5,600 to ₹5,700 in Q4; the actual was ₹4,553 and ₹5,121[10][8]. In May 2026 it said Q1 FY27 volume would be 2.65 lakh tonnes and in August it reported about 2.28 lakh tonnes, citing 7,000 tonnes stuck at a port, weaker Middle East orders and a shortfall in spiral pipe[9][2][1]. One analyst on the May call said this was the third quarter in a row that a target had been missed[9].

The FY27 profit forecast has come down in steps. In February management told analysts to expect group EBITDA of ₹750 crore in FY27 with steel profit per tonne of at least ₹5,000. In May it said ₹680 to ₹700 crore with steel at about ₹4,700 per tonne. In August it said ₹670 to ₹680 crore with steel at ₹4,600 to ₹4,700[10][9][2]. That is a 10 percent cut in six months. The August figure needs ₹400 crore of EBITDA in the second half, against ₹120 crore in Q1 and about ₹150 crore guided for Q2[2].

Two items are still open. The company has said for three calls running that it is working on a buyback and has discussed a demerger of lighting from steel. In August the managing director said work was going on and a decision would come very soon, with nothing to disclose yet[10][9][2]. Until a board decision is filed, we treat both as ideas, not plans. The one clean recent delivery is Q2 volume: management guided 2.6 to 2.65 lakh tonnes and the company reported 2.67 lakh tonnes[2][3].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter plays the part of a skeptical colleague whose job is to find the strongest honest case against our own numbers. Each estimate in chapters 3 and 5 rests on one assumption that is weaker than the rest. We name it, say what evidence would show it wrong, and say when that evidence arrives. The bear case that follows is not a prediction. It is the story under which an owner of this stock would lose money even though every number in the earlier chapters was correct as reported.

The simplest bear story is this. Surya's Q1 profit growth of 77 percent came from a weak starting point, because management says Q1 FY26 volume was hurt by a software (SAP) changeover[2]. Once that base effect fades, the company has a flat-sales history, a spread that depends on coil prices that just hit a multi-year high, and managers who have missed their own targets. The price would then drift toward ₹160, which is 1.3 times book value of about ₹121 a share[29].

Flaw 1: the steel spread is hostage to coil prices

Indian steel prices were reported 21 to 28.8 percent above year-ago levels in early October 2026[28]. A converter does well when coil prices rise slowly and badly when they fall fast. In Q3 FY26 a price fall cost about ₹12 crore, or ₹500 per tonne[10]. A fall from today's levels would hit stock bought earlier at higher prices. APL Apollo saw distributors delay stocking because prices were rising, and its volume fell 6 percent in Q1 and rose 13 percent in Q2, for a gain of only 4 percent in the first half[27].

Evidence that would prove us wrong in the optimistic direction is a Q2 profit per tonne of ₹4,400 or more. Evidence that confirms the flaw is a profit per tonne below ₹4,200 with commentary about stock losses. Both arrive on 7 November.

Flaw 2: guidance has been wrong more often than right

Four of the five dated volume and profit targets in chapter 2B were missed, and the FY27 EBITDA target was cut from ₹750 crore to ₹670 to ₹680 crore[10][9][2]. The August plan needs ₹400 crore of EBITDA in the second half, 2.8 to 2.9 lakh tonnes in Q3 and about 3.2 lakh tonnes in Q4[2]. The Q2 record of 2.67 lakh tonnes is encouraging, but Q4 needs another 20 percent on top of that[3]. If management misses by the same 10 percent it has missed by before, EPS lands near ₹15, which is our middle case, not the guided ₹17.3.

Flaw 3: exports depend on freight, tariffs and one new market

Management said ocean freight on exports cost ₹3,800 a tonne in Q1 and took about ₹800 a tonne off group profit per tonne, and that API and spiral pipe profit per tonne was ₹3,420 in Q1 FY27 according to management, against ₹5,600 in FY26 and ₹9,136 in FY22[2][8]. It said the US market opened in 2026, and US orders were 78,000 tonnes on 1 July[1]. The United States raised its Section 232 tariff on steel to 50 percent in June 2025[26]. The filings we read do not say how Surya's API pipe is treated under that regime. We list that as unknown, and a change in US trade policy is a risk to a part of the FY27 plan that management puts at 1.2 to 1.25 lakh tonnes[2].

Flaw 4: domestic demand depends on slow government payments

Management said that galvanised pipe stayed soft in Q1 because state-led projects were waiting for funds[1]. In May it said the Jal Jeevan Mission budget was about ₹55,000 crore but only about ₹3,000 crore had been paid out[9]. The parliamentary research service noted that no central funds were released to states in 2025-26 as of 3 February 2026 and that the 2026-27 allocation is ₹67,670 crore[24]. A faster release would help Surya's galvanised and spiral lines. A slow one keeps volumes dependent on trade and exports.

Flaw 5: the cash cushion is shrinking while spending rises

Net cash was ₹337 crore on 31 March and ₹154 crore on 30 June, a fall of ₹183 crore in one quarter[6][1]. The working capital cycle rose from 58 days at the end of Q4 to 72 days[9][1]. The group plans ₹500 crore of plant spending over three years, pays about ₹109 crore a year in dividends at ₹5 a share, and has said a buyback is under consideration[6][2]. If steel prices keep rising, FY27 free cash flow could fall well below the ₹241 crore of FY26. That would weaken the reverse DCF base and leave less room for a buyback, which is one of the ideas bulls cite.

Flaw 6: lighting profit margin has slipped

Lighting EBITDA margin fell from 9.6 percent in FY25 to 8.6 percent in FY26. It was 7.9 percent in Q1 FY27 even as management raised prices by about 7 percent net[8][1]. The FY27 target of about ₹200 crore on ₹2,200 crore of sales needs a margin near 9.1 percent for the year, and Q1 delivered ₹36 crore, so the remaining three quarters need about ₹164 crore[2]. Crompton's lighting margin also fell 2.6 points in Q2 FY27[23]. The two companies share the same cost problem, which suggests the pressure is industry-wide and not specific to Surya.
