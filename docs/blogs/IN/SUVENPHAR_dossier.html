How the verdict was reached

The underwriting rulebook sorts every candidate through three layers, in order. Layer 1 is a set of hard gates. Each gate is either a pass or a fail, and one failure is enough to reject the stock. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five measures that matter for the business, and Layer 3 checks whether the price is close enough to a support level to justify a purchase. Layers 2 and 3 are only run when every Layer 1 gate passes, so a rejected stock never receives a business scorecard or an entry price.

Layer 1 has three parts. The financial part asks whether the company earns a good return on its capital, whether its borrowing is moderate, and whether its reported profit turns into cash. The technical part asks whether the price is in a clean uptrend. Asymmetry means how much a stock could gain compared with how much it could lose from the entry price. The asymmetry part asks whether the upside to a verified target is at least three times the downside to a stop-loss, for a core holding. The financial and technical tests use the company's reported figures and the price ledger, and every number in this report carries a citation or comes from the ledger for the session of 12 October 2026.

The verdict here is REJECTED because the return test fails at 5.81 to 10.8 percent depending on the measure, and the technical test fails because the price sits below a 50-day average that is still rising, while the 200-day average is falling. A third failure, the asymmetry test, cannot be scored, because no target can be anchored to a verifiable forward earnings figure multiplied by a verifiable historical multiple. Rejection means the stock goes to the archive. It does not mean the business is worthless. The rulebook keeps the company question and the entry question apart on purpose, so a company can be a sound business and still be a poor buy at a given price.

What the company does, and what the merger changed

Cohance is a contract development and manufacturing organisation, often shortened to CDMO. A CDMO is a company that makes medicines or their parts for other drug companies, which prefer not to build their own plants for every product. The company describes itself as an innovator-focused CRDMO, meaning it also does the early research and development work that comes before manufacture [2]. Its activities fall into three groups: pharma CDMO work on finished-drug intermediates, active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) manufacturing, and specialty chemicals. Within those, it works on antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), which are cancer drugs that carry a toxic payload to a target cell, and on oligonucleotides, which are short strands of genetic material used as drugs [5].

The merger explains why the company now looks very different from the Suven that was listed before. The scheme of amalgamation folded Cohance Lifesciences Limited into Suven Pharmaceuticals Limited, with Suven as the surviving company. The National Company Law Tribunal sanctioned the scheme on 27 March 2025 [4]. The merger took effect from 1 May 2025, after the Department of Pharmaceuticals gave its final approval for foreign investment, and the merged company was renamed Cohance Lifesciences Limited [3]. The scheme sets the appointed date equal to the effective date, so the merger was not backdated to an earlier accounting date [4].

The ticker change follows from the name change. The April 2025 release used the symbol SUVENPHAR [3], and the August 2026 filing uses COHANCE for both exchanges [2]. The sources read do not give the exact date the NSE symbol changed, so this report does not state one. The BSE scrip code, 543064, is the same in both releases [3] [2].

The merger changes the financial history, and this matters for every trend in this report. The company's own annual report states that FY25 and FY26 figures reflect the consolidated performance of the merged entity, while FY24 figures reflect erstwhile Suven Pharmaceuticals Limited only, and that the two are not comparable [5]. In practice, the jump in consolidated sales from ₹1,051 crore in FY24 to ₹2,608 crore in FY25 is mostly the addition of the Cohance business, not organic growth. Any three-year growth rate, and any three-year cash ratio, therefore joins years reported on different bases. The numbers chapter shows the figures both ways so that the reader can see whether the verdict depends on the choice. It does not.

Ownership also moved. The promoter group held 66.41 percent of the shares in June 2025 and 57.49 percent in June 2026 [9]. The sources read do not explain the fall in the promoter stake, so this report records the change without a cause. The business chapter explains why the promoter stake matters more than the percentage alone suggests.

Why the business is strong or weak

The business has two sides. The first is a deep pipeline: management says the company has 10 Phase III molecules and that requests for quotes (RFQs) doubled during FY26 [5]. An RFQ is a customer's formal request to price a project, so a rise in RFQs is an early sign of future work. The second side is execution. Earnings fell sharply in FY26, and management said the first quarter of FY27 would be its lowest quarter ever [2].

FY26 consolidated revenue from operations was ₹2,268.55 crore, down 13.0 percent from ₹2,608.50 crore. Profit for the year fell to ₹150.12 crore from ₹484.24 crore [5]. Working out the change in profit: 150.12 ÷ 484.24 = 0.31, so profit fell by about 69 percent. The company attributes the revenue fall mainly to customer destocking, which means customers used up stock they already held and ordered less, in two large commercial CDMO products worth nearly ₹260 crore. It also cites product-specific softness in its API business and a regulatory disruption at its Nacharam formulations site [5].

The first quarter of FY27 was weaker still. The company's investor information page lists an investor presentation for this quarter dated 5 August 2026 [21]. Revenue from operations was ₹4,223 million, down 23.1 percent year on year, and gross margin, which means the share of sales left after the direct cost of making the product, slipped to 71.5 percent from 73.0 percent [2]. Adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, after removing one-off items) was ₹92 million, a margin of 2.2 percent [6]. The company recorded a net loss of ₹45 crore for the quarter, against a profit of ₹46 crore a year earlier [1]. Management blamed shipment timing and said deliveries had moved into the second quarter, so the weakness is partly a matter of when work is delivered rather than lost work. The sources read do not yet show that the timing story holds.

The drag has a clear source. The NJ Bio unit, which the company bought for its ADC technology, recorded an adjusted EBITDA loss of ₹328 million in the quarter, and management said it will keep weighing on consolidated profit for some time [6]. The Sapala unit, which makes oligonucleotide building blocks, grew its revenue about 2.5 times [6]. Management says the standalone business and Sapala are in a materially strong position, and that operating leverage, the effect of fixed costs being spread over more revenue, will return margins as revenue rises. That is a plausible argument, but no margin target was given, and the sources do not allow a figure to be estimated.

Two quality markers matter for a manufacturer. The Nacharam formulations facility received an observation classified as Official Action Indicated (OAI) from the US Food and Drug Administration, which then moved to a Warning Letter and caused temporary shipment deferrals [5]. The company reports that its customer audits across the CDMO and API sites in the first quarter were completed without any critical observation [2]. On cash, the company reports free cash flow of ₹173 crore for FY26, which means the cash left after capital expenditure, meaning money spent on plants and equipment, and that spending was ₹215.4 crore. Its working capital, which means the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, stood at 154 days in FY26 [5]. Working capital days measure how long cash is tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, so 154 days is a heavy load for a manufacturer. Debtor days, the time taken to collect payment from customers, rose from 108 days in March 2025 to 110 days in March 2026 [1].

The balance sheet is the company's main protection. Management reports consolidated net cash of about ₹2,512 million, roughly ₹251 crore, at 30 June 2026 [2]. At 31 March 2026 the annual report gives total borrowings of ₹170 crore against cash of ₹323 crore, a net cash position of ₹153 crore [5]. The sources do not reconcile the two net cash figures, but both show a company with more cash than debt. That is why the leverage test passes, and why the weak returns are a problem of profitability rather than of solvency.

The credit rating has moved the wrong way. CRISIL's August 2025 letter, written after the merger took effect, recorded the merged company's rating as AA-/Positive on the long-term scale [8]. On 12 August 2026, CRISIL migrated the long-term rating from AA-/Positive to BB/Stable under its "issuer not cooperating" category, and then withdrew the ratings at the company's request [7]. The company says India Ratings still rates its higher-limit facilities and that the withdrawal does not affect its borrowing [11]. A rating moved to "not cooperating" is a warning about the flow of information to lenders, even when the loans themselves remain covered.

Ownership is the next risk, and the reader should know one more share-count detail. Employee option grants approved in October 2026 carry exercise prices of ₹325 for the newer plan and ₹495 for the older one, according to a summary of the filing, so any options exercised would add new shares [20]. The promoter group holds 57.49 percent of the company, which is 219,930,170 shares. Of those promoter shares, 207,974,582 are pledged, which is 94.56 percent of the promoter holding [9]. A pledge means shares are used as security for a loan, so if the loan is not repaid, the lender can sell them. Trendlyne records the pledge at 0.0 percent in March 2026 and at 94.56 percent in June 2026, which means it arrived in one quarter [9]. Working out the share of the whole company: 207,974,582 ÷ 382.57 million shares is about 54 percent of all shares outstanding [18].

The pledge sits at holding-company level. The two promoter entities, Behryanda Limited and Jusmiral Holdings Limited, have pledged 100.00 percent and 87.06 percent of their own holdings [9]. A summary of the company's disclosure gives the entity name as Berhyanda, and describes a facility agreement dated 11 June 2026 for term loans of USD 285 million, of which USD 233.5 million goes to Behryanda and USD 51.5 million to Jusmiral. It says the share pledges took effect on 16 June 2026 [10]. The sources read do not say what the loans were used for. The practical point is that the pledged shares are a claim on a large part of the stock, so a fall in the share price can force selling by lenders in a way that the company's own cash cannot prevent. Screener shows the same 94.6 percent figure [1], so two sources agree on the number even though neither explains the loan.

Taken together, the business is shrinking in profit terms while its order pipeline is growing, and its balance sheet is sound while its returns are not. The next chapter tests those facts against the hard rules.

The number

On the simple basis used here, the company would need about ₹396 crore more operating profit before interest and tax, with the same capital employed, to reach a return on capital employed of 15 percent. The required profit of about ₹647 crore is roughly 2.6 times the ₹250 crore the reported return implies, so the shortfall of ₹396 crore is about 1.6 times current profit.

How it was calculated

Capital employed is taken as shareholders' money plus borrowings: ₹3,911 crore plus ₹400 crore gives ₹4,311 crore. A 15 percent return on that capital needs about ₹647 crore of operating profit before interest and tax. The reported 5.81 percent implies about ₹250 crore (0.0581 × ₹4,311 crore), and the difference is ₹396 crore. Each step holds the rest of the balance sheet fixed.

The inputs

Equity capital of ₹38 crore and reserves of ₹3,873 crore, and borrowings of ₹400 crore, all at March 2026 [1]. The 5.81 percent return is the Screener header figure for the same date [1]. The sources read do not state the exact operating-profit definition behind that return, so the estimate is an approximation of the scale of the gap, not a restatement of it.

The range

On the annual report's borrowing figure of ₹170 crore, capital employed is ₹4,081 crore. A 15 percent return then needs about ₹612 crore, against about ₹237 crore implied by the reported return, so the gap is about ₹375 crore. The gap therefore lies between about ₹375 crore and ₹396 crore, which is roughly 1.6 times the current profit on either basis, while the required profit of ₹612 crore to ₹647 crore is about 2.6 times the current figure [5].

If the number is off

The estimate moves with the balance sheet, not with the share price. A profit recovery of the size management describes would narrow the gap only if capital employed stays flat, because new plant adds to capital employed as well as to profit. The estimate should be redone with each new balance sheet.

What could happen next, and the case against the verdict

The outcome depends on three things: whether the second half of FY27 brings the revenue back, whether the returns on capital recover towards 15 percent, and whether the promoter shares stay in the hands of their owners. Management is confident about the first and says little about the second and third. The company says it expects operating leverage to return margins to improve year on year, which means that as sales recover, the fixed costs of its plants are spread over more output [6]. When an analyst asked whether margins could reach 35 percent, management said that was difficult to articulate at that point [6]. No margin target was given, so no figure in this report depends on one.

Before accepting the verdict, the strongest case against it should be stated. It is this. The company has ₹251 crore of net cash, a pipeline of Phase III molecules, RFQs that doubled during the year, and customer audits in the first quarter that found no critical observations. A short period of weak sales could be followed by a normal rebound, and a return on capital could recover with it. Under that case, the rule would clear in a year or two, and the file would be reopened. The stock's relative strength over the quarter (+6.45 percent against the Nifty 50) also shows that buyers have not abandoned it. The analysts' consensus earnings path, from ₹5.19 a share in FY27 to ₹9.81 in FY28, is a bet on exactly that recovery [19].

The case has a weak point, and it is the one the rulebook is designed to test. A recovery in revenue does not by itself lift the return on capital. The company's capital employed rises every time it spends on plant, and it spent ₹598 million in the first quarter alone [2]. Capital spending that has not yet produced revenue lowers the return in the meantime. The return therefore has to rise on both the profit side and the capital side at once. The sources do not show how long that takes.

The single hinge claim. The verdict depends on one claim: that profit recovers in FY27 as revenue returns in the second half, and that the return on capital rebuilds towards 15 percent within the next two annual reports. The evidence for it is management's statement, not a filed figure. The evidence against it is the first-quarter net loss of ₹45 crore, the ₹328 million adjusted EBITDA loss at NJ Bio, and the reported ROCE of 5.81 to 10.8 percent for FY26 [1] [6]. If the second-quarter results, due around the start of November 2026 on a third-party estimate [18], show revenue growing and a positive profit, the claim is on its way to being tested. If they show another loss, the claim is not supported.

Fatal flaw one: the returns do not recover. If the return on capital stays below 15 percent through FY27, the verdict is correct and the stock stays in the archive. The return would stay low if the NJ Bio losses continue, if the Nacharam regulatory work delays shipments, or if the destocking in two large CDMO products continues. Management says two commercial molecules are scheduled for delivery in the second and third quarters [2]. Those deliveries are the first test of the claim.

Fatal flaw two: a forced sale of promoter shares. The pledged shares equal 94.56 percent of the promoter stake, which is about 54 percent of all shares outstanding [9] [18]. The loans sit with the promoter holding entities, and the disclosed facility is USD 285 million [10]. If the loans came under strain, lenders could sell the shares, and that selling would hit a stock whose 200-day average is already falling. The loan covenants, the trigger prices and the repayment schedule are not in the sources read, so the size of this risk cannot be measured. The pledge was 0 percent at March 2026 and 94.56 percent at June 2026, so the change was recent and the sources give no sign of an earlier warning.

Fatal flaw three: a quality or disclosure problem. The Nacharam site has a Warning Letter from the US FDA, and CRISIL has moved the company to "issuer not cooperating" before withdrawing its ratings [5] [7]. A rating agency uses that label when it lacks the information it needs. The company says the withdrawal does not affect its borrowing, and India Ratings still rates its larger facilities [11]. The sources read do not say what information CRISIL lacked, so this report cannot judge whether the label reflects a serious problem or a dispute.

Monitoring triggers

  • Return trigger: a filed ROCE of 15.0 or more on the rule's basis. Until then the return test fails, whatever management says.
  • Price trigger: a close above the 50-day average of 448.26, with the 200-day average turning up from 414.22. A close below the 63-session low of 411.0 would confirm a breakdown from the base.
  • Pledge trigger (context only, does not change the verdict on its own): a fall in the pledged share of the promoter holding from 94.56 percent in the next shareholding pattern. A rise, or any sale by lenders, would strengthen the rejection.
  • Rating trigger: any action by India Ratings on the facilities it still covers, or a new rating from another agency.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in this report comes from a source, but several of the sources disagree, and the reader should know where the disagreements are and which way they could move the answer. The first is the definition of debt. The Screener figure of ₹400 crore for March 2026 is well above the ₹170 crore total borrowings the company reports [1] [5]. The sources do not reconcile the gap. It does not change the verdict, because debt-to-equity is 0.10 on the higher figure and 0.04 on the lower one, both well inside the 0.70 limit.

The second is the return. Three sources give three figures for the same year: 5.81 percent in the Screener header, 6.12 percent in a ratio table, and 10.8 percent as the company's own adjusted measure [1] [13] [5]. The verdict uses the reported figures and does not depend on which one is right. The company's adjusted figure would need to be 4.2 points higher than its own number before the return test would pass. The sources do not explain the basis of the adjustment, so the gap cannot be closed from here.

The third is the earnings base for the multiple. The price-to-earnings ratio is 158 on Screener's basis, 158.82 on another, and 288 on a trailing basis that uses only the last twelve months of earnings [1] [18] [14]. Whichever is used, the multiple is high. The three-year median multiple, which the target rule needs, could not be found in any source reached, so no target can be built on it.

The fourth is the history itself. The three-year growth rates join two companies, and the FY25 third-party figures conflict with the company's accounts. ETMoney and Alphave both show FY25 sales near ₹1,198 crore and profit near ₹265 crore, while the annual report gives ₹2,608.50 crore and ₹484.24 crore [16] [15] [5]. This report uses the annual report. If the third-party figures were right, the FY25 peak would be much lower, and the decline in FY26 would look less steep.

The fifth is ownership. The Economic Times shareholding page lists the promoter holding as 0 percent [17], which is a classification gap in that page, not a fact about the company. Trendlyne shows both the 57.49 percent promoter holding and the 94.56 percent pledge [9], a second data service shows the same 57.49 percent holding [12], and Screener shows the 94.6 percent pledge [1]. The pledge number is therefore well supported, while the reason for it is not.

The sixth is the price itself. The ledger closes at ₹444.45, while the Screener quote at its last update showed ₹443 [1]. The difference is a matter of timing. A move of a few rupees changes the distances in ATR by a fraction and does not change any gate result.

What we would do, and when

We would not buy this stock now. The verdict is REJECTED, so the company goes to the archive, where its audit trail is kept for a later review. The action today is therefore no position, no order and no protective stop, because there is no position to protect. The file is reviewed again only when a trigger in the What could happen next chapter is met, or when a new filing changes the numbers. The ledger lists the stock's role as watchlist only, so no existing holding of it is recorded for this run.

The file leaves the archive only when the three reopen conditions in the layer-1 section all hold, and until then the stock stays in the archive.

The September 2026 shareholding pattern will show whether the pledged share has moved from 94.56 percent.
