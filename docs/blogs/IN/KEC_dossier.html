1. What the company does

KEC International is the flagship company of the RPG Group and is run from Mumbai. It is an EPC company, which means it handles engineering, procurement and construction for a client from design to hand-over. Its biggest business is power transmission and distribution (T&D): steel lattice towers, power lines and substations that carry electricity from power plants to cities.[3][8] It also builds factories, homes, metros, water pipelines and data centres, lays railway track and signalling, builds solar and wind plants, lays oil and gas pipelines, and makes power cables and conductors. It says it has a footprint in more than 110 countries and a tower-making capacity of 483,800 tonnes a year.[3][5]

Who pays? Mostly utilities and governments. Power Grid Corporation of India, a state-owned transmission company rated AAA by CRISIL, is the largest Indian customer; state utilities, railways and water missions follow, and private developers are a growing group.[8] Overseas, the main buyers are grid operators in Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Africa, plus tower buyers in the Americas through its US unit, SAE Towers.[6][17] About 25% of the order book, roughly ₹10,000 Cr, sits in the Middle East, split about evenly between Saudi Arabia and the UAE.[17]

How does it make money? KEC wins a contract, builds the work over one to two years and bills the client in stages. Its profit is a thin slice of each contract. In FY26 revenue was ₹23,506 Cr, and earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) — the profit before financing costs and accounting charges — was ₹1,659 Cr, or 7.1% of revenue.[4] After interest, depreciation and tax, profit after tax was ₹606 Cr, about 2.6% of revenue.[1] About half the order book is priced at a fixed cost, so a rise in steel, labour or freight costs lands on KEC rather than on the client.[22]

The second thing to know is working capital, which is the cash tied up in the day-to-day running of the business: money owed by clients, minus money owed to suppliers. EPC firms carry a lot of it. Clients pay in stages and hold back a share (retention money) until the work is accepted, and KEC pays suppliers late. CRISIL estimates that current assets stood at 320 to 330 days of sales on 31 March 2026, with creditors at 290 to 300 days.[8] KEC's own measure, net working capital, was 137 days at that date.[4] When this number rises, profit can grow while cash goes out of the door, which is exactly what happened in FY26 and is the heart of this report.

Part of that supplier credit is formal. Banks pay KEC's suppliers up front and KEC repays the banks later, with interest. The accounts call these trade acceptances and show them as a liability of ₹2,120.74 Cr at 31 March 2026, up from ₹1,507.27 Cr a year earlier.[7] The company counts them as debt when it reports net debt, which is borrowings minus the cash held: ₹4,601 Cr of ordinary net debt plus ₹2,121 Cr of acceptances made ₹6,722 Cr.[6] We follow the same rule, because the bank charges interest on them and they must be repaid in cash.[8]

The order book and what it means

An order book is the value of signed work not yet built. KEC's stood at ₹37,697 Cr on 30 June 2026, and the company adds another ₹3,000 Cr or so in contracts where it is the lowest bidder (called L1) but not yet awarded.[6] That is about 1.6 times a year of revenue, which gives roughly six to seven quarters of work. Of the book, 62% is T&D and 26% is civil, and 54% is Indian against 46% foreign.[6] Order intake in FY26 was ₹25,280 Cr, so the company booked ₹1.08 of new work for each ₹1 of revenue, a ratio called book-to-bill, meaning new orders divided by revenue; above 1 means the book is growing.[4]

Orders are still arriving. On 24 October 2026 KEC announced ₹2,872 Cr of 765 kV line orders from Power Grid, its largest ever India T&D order, which took intake for the year to above ₹13,500 Cr.[32][33] That order also tells us that the nine-month Power Grid bidding ban, which ran from 18 November 2025 to about August 2026, no longer blocks new awards.[25] Management's target for FY27 is ₹30,000 Cr of intake and 12% to 15% revenue growth.[34] Intake of ₹13,500 Cr in seven months means ₹16,500 Cr must come in the last five, which is possible only if the large Middle East and HVDC tenders convert.[19][32]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

KEC's strength is its transmission franchise. CRISIL calls it one of the largest global players in transmission towers, with over five decades of experience and long relations with Power Grid and the state utilities.[8] T&D revenue grew 24% to ₹15,883 Cr in FY26 and rose from 59% to 68% of group revenue, because the company chose to chase T&D orders over lower-margin work.[4][22] The group also has staying power: CRISIL notes cash of ₹522 Cr and unused bank lines of ₹2,686 Cr, and ICRA and CARE rate the long-term debt A+ with a stable outlook.[8][9][10][11] Return on capital is acceptable: return on capital employed (ROCE) — the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses — was 16.5%.[1]

The weakness is that those strengths sit on top of a thin and unstable margin. EBITDA margin was 7.1% in FY26 against the 8% to 8.5% management had promised, and fell to 5.8% in the June 2026 quarter from 7.0% a year earlier.[4][5][18] The drag is in the standalone company, where legacy railway, metro and water contracts lose money: standalone profit after tax in Q1 FY27 was ₹1 Cr on revenue of ₹3,898 Cr.[6] Four finished metro projects, two for Delhi Metro and two for Chennai Metro, wait for the client to take them over, and KEC says it spends close to ₹10 Cr a month on each while it waits.[17]

The second weakness is cash. In FY26 operations used ₹414.12 Cr of cash, even after ₹613.47 Cr of extra supplier credit through trade acceptances was counted as an operating inflow.[7] The big moves were a ₹1,150.38 Cr rise in trade receivables and a ₹1,022.11 Cr rise in contract assets, which are work done but not yet billed.[7] The company still paid a dividend of ₹146.42 Cr, about 24% of profit, and short-term borrowings rose ₹969.19 Cr in the year.[7] A dividend funded by borrowing is not a crime, but it shows that the board treats the cash squeeze as temporary.

The third issue is governance, and we treat it as a real flag. In March 2025 the CBI arrested a senior Power Grid manager and a KEC executive over a ₹2.5 lakh bribe, and its first information report named KEC International itself and five individuals as accused.[29] On 18 November 2025 Power Grid barred KEC from new tenders for nine months, citing contract provisions linked to that matter.[25] The Delhi High Court put the order on hold on 17 December, then on 8 January 2026 upheld it, adding that Power Grid could have banned KEC for two years.[27][28] No accounting fraud or promoter pledge has been reported, and Power Grid was only about 4% of FY26 order intake against 27% a year earlier, so the money effect was small.[12][25] The reputational effect was larger, and the share price fell 9.08% on the day to ₹710.20.[25]

Why the shares fell about 60%

The 52-week high in our price ledger is ₹868.40 and the close on 27 October 2026 is ₹331.65, a fall of 61.8%.[41] We can split that fall into two parts. Earnings per share (EPS) means a company's profit divided by its number of shares. EPS over the last four quarters is ₹20.80, down 19% from ₹25.67 a year earlier.[1] Meanwhile the price-to-earnings ratio, which is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit, fell from about 34 to about 16.[1][2] In log terms about a fifth of the fall comes from lower profit and about four fifths from the market paying a lower multiple. Investors lost faith in the profit, not just in the amount of it.

Three themes run through it: guidance that kept being cut, a balance sheet that kept getting heavier, and a handful of one-off shocks (the Power Grid ban and the West Asia war). The share price fell after each one, and each lower price came with fewer foreign owners and more retail owners.

Was it the whole sector?

Was this a sector fall? Mostly not. The Sensex fell about 15% in calendar 2026.[30] Over twelve months Kalpataru Projects (the closest peer) rose 6%, Skipper fell 2% and Larsen & Toubro fell 7%.[37][39][40] Only Transrail Lighting, which also builds transmission towers, fell much, down 41%.[38] That tells us the market punished KEC for its own record on margins, cash and conduct rather than for any weakness in transmission demand.

What this means for an owner: the fall has real causes that are still visible in the latest quarter, so a cheap-looking share price is not yet a reason to buy. What it means for the next chapter: the company's promises can now be checked against what it delivered, and the operating measures that matter most can be scored.

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on the future, and a reverse discounted cash flow (DCF) asks what bet the price implies. We take today's market value, ₹8,830 Cr, and solve for the yearly growth in cash flow that, over ten years, with a 12% discount rate and a final-year value of 20 times that year's cash flow, gives exactly that value.[1] The formula is: market value = sum over years 1 to 10 of C(1+g)^t / 1.12^t, plus 20 x C(1+g)^10 / 1.12^10, where C is the starting cash flow and g is the unknown growth.

KEC's free cash flow is negative (-₹691 Cr in FY26, and -₹343 Cr cumulative over FY24 to FY26), so the formula has no answer on cash.[1] We therefore run it on trailing profit after tax of ₹554 Cr, which is generous because the profit has not turned into cash. The answer is 3.67% growth a year. On FY26 profit of ₹606 Cr it is 2.5%, and on the three-year average profit of ₹508 Cr it is 4.8%.[1] These are very low hurdles for a company whose order book grows and whose revenue growth guidance is 12% to 15%.[34] The market is not saying growth is missing. It is saying the profit is not worth its face value.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each number, the evidence that would prove it wrong, and the date we expect to see that evidence. It then gives the strongest case against our own conclusion, which is a case that a REJECTED verdict is too cautious, and picks the one claim on which everything hangs.

Weakest assumption behind the FY28 EPS of ₹28

The assumption is that EBITDA margin rises from 5.8% in Q1 FY27 to 6.2% by FY28. Management says high single-digit margins are possible in FY28, but it also says the four old metro projects cost close to ₹10 Cr a month each, which is about ₹480 Cr a year across the four, or 1.7% of FY28 revenue.[17] If handover slips again, the 6.2% will not appear. Evidence that would show we are wrong: standalone EBITDA margin staying at 4% or below in Q2 and Q3 FY27, against 4.0% in Q1.[6] We will know on 2 November and again around the end of January 2027.

Weakest assumption behind the ₹420 central value

The assumption is that the market will pay 15 times earnings for KEC in a year. That multiple is paid only when profit turns to cash. If the half-year cash flow statement shows operating cash flow below zero and net debt including acceptances above ₹6,500 Cr, the market is likely to keep pricing the stock at 11 to 12 times, which gives ₹308 to ₹336 on our own ₹28 EPS; at 11 times the central value is 7.13% below the close, and with the other scenarios unchanged the probability-weighted upside falls from 15.2% to about 1.67%. The evidence will be in the September balance sheet on 2 November and in the full-year cash flow in May 2027.

Weakest assumption behind the reverse DCF

The reverse DCF uses profit as a stand-in for cash. That is generous, and it hides the fact that free cash flow has been negative. If the market is right that profit is overstated, for instance through allowances for expected credit losses (₹164.09 Cr in FY26 and ₹216.70 Cr in FY25) that prove too small, the true hurdle is higher than 3.67% and the stock is not cheap.[7] Evidence of this would be further write-offs on the ₹800 to ₹900 Cr of water and Afghanistan receivables, of which ₹400 to ₹500 Cr is overdue.[17][23]

The red-team case against our conclusion

The strongest argument that REJECTED is too cautious is simple. The order book is a record, a ₹2,872 Cr Power Grid order has just arrived, the ban is over, the stock trades at 1.4 times book value and 15.9 times trailing profit, its credit rating is still A+ stable from two agencies, and management has named specific cash sources that add up to more than its debt target.[1][6][11][17][32] A stock that has fallen 62% on poor cash conversion can rise 90% when that conversion turns, and a rule that waits for the chart to turn will enter higher: about 8% higher at the 20-day average (₹357.11 against ₹331.65) or about 18% higher at the 50-day average (₹390.85). We accept that cost.

The argument for caution is stronger because of what has already happened. Management said on 30 January 2026 that it would "definitely" reach ₹5,500 Cr of net debt by March, and it was ₹1,222 Cr above that figure.[4][19] The ratings agencies have written down their own tripwires: CARE downgrades if margin stays below 7.5% and CRISIL if interest cover stays below 2.5 times, and both conditions are met today.[8][10] A downgrade would raise borrowing costs in a business that pays ₹663.60 Cr of finance costs a year, and the company also depends on ₹16,800 Cr of non-fund-based bank limits for guarantees.[7][9] The stock has also gapped; a stop is a plan, not a guarantee, and against our bear value of ₹220 the reward-to-risk ratio from today is only 0.79 to 1.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, a verdict for this watchlist name that means we do not buy and do not set a live order. The rule behind it is the hard red line set out in chapter 3: the close of ₹331.65 is below a falling 50-day average of ₹390.85, which is below a falling 200-day average of ₹512.76, and Test C fails on two of four conditions.[41] Two other red lines also fail, cash conversion at 0.21 against a floor of 0.25 and gross debt-to-equity at 1.22 against 1.20.[1][7] The decision changes only when the price and the cash both turn, and the trigger paragraphs below name the levels. This is a judgment on timing and proof, not on the business alone.

For readers who ask whether the price is low enough to ignore the red lines, we ran the numbers. Our central value is ₹420, which gives a reward-to-risk ratio of 3.26 to 1 at the current price when measured to a stop of ₹304.59, and 3.21 to 1 at the top of the reference zone. A stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong and the position is cut, and ours is ₹304.59, one ATR below the 63-day low of ₹317.25, as the stop rule for turnaround bases requires.[41] Measured to our bear value of ₹220 the same trade offers 0.79 to 1, which is below the 2.5 floor, because a gap such as the 9.08% one-day fall on 19 November 2025 can carry the price straight through a stop.[25]

The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned, and a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better. If the name reopens, it would move to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, our label for a name that passes the business tests but is bought only through a limit order at a planned level. The reference entry zone would be ₹318.00 to ₹332.00, placed as a limit order, which runs from just above the 63-day low to about today's close. Support is 1.14 ATR below the price, which falls in the 0.6 to 1.2 ATR "standard" band of our distance ladder, so the ladder alone would allow a standard-sized position.[41] The Stage 4 line overrides the ladder, so the size today is zero and no order is live.

If the name reopened, we would size it from risk, not from conviction. Our tiers rank conviction: TIER_0 means no position, and TIER_3 is the third tier, with a position of 2% to 3.5% of the portfolio. Each tier has a risk budget in basis points (bps), where 100 bps equal 1%, and TIER_3's budget is 50 bps, meaning the portfolio would lose 0.50% if the stop is hit. The stop sits 8.3% below ₹332, so 0.50% divided by 8.3% allows 6.0%, which is above the 3.5% tier cap, so the position would be capped at 3.5%. The let-winners-run rule would then keep the position while the price stays above a rising 50-day simple moving average (SMA, the plain average of closing prices) or a 21-day exponential moving average (EMA, an average that gives recent days more weight) and the fundamentals hold.
