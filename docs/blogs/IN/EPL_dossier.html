1. What the company does

In plain English: EPL makes the squeezable tubes that hold toothpaste and face cream. Big consumer brands buy them. A pending merger would add a second business that makes plastic bottles for drinks makers.

EPL Limited, formerly Essel Propack, was incorporated in 1982 and is listed on the NSE as EPL and on the BSE as 500135[23]. It makes laminated plastic tubes: a flat laminate sheet is rolled into a tube, printed, fitted with a shoulder and a cap, and filled by the customer with toothpaste, face cream, ointment or food paste[23]. The company runs 21 factories in 11 countries and sells more than 9 billion tubes a year[1]. It says it holds about 20 percent of the global laminated-tube market and about 35 to 37 percent of the global oral-care segment[1].

The customers are global consumer-goods and healthcare companies. CARE lists Procter and Gamble, Colgate-Palmolive, Unilever, Johnson and Johnson, Dabur India and Zydus Lifesciences among the long-standing relationships[23]. FY26 revenue from operations was ₹4,763.1 crore, up 13.0 percent from ₹4,213.3 crore[23]. The June 2026 quarter brought ₹1,387.9 crore, up 25.3 percent, of which about 5 points came from higher selling prices passed through from polymer costs; the company calls the remaining growth of 20 percent underlying[3][4]. Beauty, pharma and other non-oral products, which the company calls Personal Care and beyond, were 54 percent of tube revenue in the June 2026 quarter and oral care 46 percent.

Money comes from selling tubes at a price built from the cost of polymer, freight and power plus a conversion margin. Polymer, the plastic granule, follows crude oil prices. Pricing arrangements that pass these costs on cover about 50 to 60 percent of the business, and the rest is covered by negotiated or order-based pricing[23]. That matters in 2026: management says a sharp rise in polymer prices after the Middle East crisis was recovered in full through pricing in the June quarter, but the pass-through raises revenue without adding profit, so the reported EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) margin fell to 18.8 percent against an underlying 19.6 percent[4][5].

The pending Indovida merger, in one page

The listing has an unusual owner story. Blackstone, a US investment firm, bought control of EPL in 2019. In May 2025 Indorama Ventures (IVL), a Thai chemicals and packaging group, bought about 25 percent of EPL from Blackstone[26][11]. On 29 March 2026 EPL's board approved a merger in which Indovida India Private Limited, a company that will hold IVL's rigid PET (clear plastic bottle) packaging business, merges into EPL[9]. Indovida makes preforms, bottles and closures for drinks makers; its CY25 turnover was ₹3,809 crore against EPL's ₹4,568 crore, and the company says its EBITDA margin was 21.3 percent[9][12]. EPL issues new shares to IVL's Dutch holding company, Indorama Netherlands, at 286 EPL shares for every 10,000 Indovida shares[9].

Blackstone then left. On 1 September 2026 its vehicle Epsilon Bidco sold all 8,44,79,781 shares, 26.38 percent of EPL, at an average ₹240.57 for ₹2,032.33 crore, in an open-market block[24][25]. On 21 October 2026 the board approved Epsilon's request to be reclassified from promoter to public[20].

Approvals so far are the Competition Commission of India on 26 May 2026, the European Commission on 31 July 2026, and no-adverse-observation letters from BSE and NSE on 19 October 2026[13][15][16][17]. 2. Why the business is strong or weak

In plain English: the business is large, profitable and hard to copy. Its weak spots are thin cash after spending, one large buyer whose parent group we cannot size, and a change of owner.

The case for the business is that it is large, profitable and hard to replace. It makes one tube in three of the toothpaste tubes sold worldwide, its operating margin has widened from 16.2 percent in FY23 to 20.4 percent in FY26 on the company's own basis, and net debt was about 0.58 times a year of EBITDA at June 2026[23][4]. The case against is that the margin is protected by pass-through clauses, the capital spending is running above depreciation, and cash after that spending is thin. Chapter 2B scores the operating measures. This chapter gives the evidence.

Position and growth: a mature core with a faster edge

Oral care revenue was ₹2,106 crore in FY26, up 3.4 percent on FY25, and ₹598.8 crore in the June 2026 quarter, up 23.9 percent[4]. The faster part is Personal Care and beyond at ₹2,360.8 crore in FY26 and ₹700.4 crore in the June 2026 quarter, up 25.1 percent. Management says Beauty and Cosmetics added more than 80 new customers[4]. CARE adds that cosmetics were over 55 percent of the global tube market in CY25, a crowded segment with rivals such as Amcor, Albéa and Hoffmann Neopac[23].

The merger would add a second format, rigid bottles and preforms for soft-drink and food makers, a customer set EPL does not serve today. Unilever, L'Oreal and Procter and Gamble buy from both businesses[12].

Customers and concentration, on a parent basis

The audited note gives the single largest customer, counted as one buyer in the books, as ₹3,255 million in FY26, which is 6.8 percent of consolidated revenue, against 8.0 percent in FY25[1]. The same ₹3,255 million is the standalone figure for the Indian company, where it is 23.5 percent of ₹13,829 million of revenue, down from 25.5 percent. The top three there were 35.2 percent, against 37.5 percent[1][2]. Because the consolidated and the standalone amounts match, the largest billed buyer appears to be served by the Indian parent company; the notes do not name it.

The notes do not say whether the affiliates of one global parent are added together. That is the figure that matters, because Colgate-Palmolive, Procter and Gamble or Unilever may buy through several EPL plants in several countries. We searched the annual reports, the rating releases and the call transcripts and found no parent-level share; the research record shows the lookup as not found after three queries. The 6.8 percent consolidated and 23.5 percent standalone shares use different denominators, and neither is a parent-group share. The 46 percent oral-care share of tube revenue is a category exposure, not a customer share[23]. We therefore treat the parent-basis concentration as undocumented and grade it WATCH in Chapter 2B, not PASS. The annual report lists customer concentration among its operating risks[1].

Raw materials, currency and the Middle East shock

The June 2026 quarter shows the pass-through working, and its cost. Revenue grew 25.3 percent and EBITDA grew 15.2 percent, so EBITDA margin was 18.8 percent against 20.5 percent a year earlier. Profit after tax fell 1.4 percent although pre-tax profit rose about 10 percent, because the tax rate was low a year ago[4][5]. Net debt rose to ₹580.6 crore from ₹389.8 crore, which the finance chief put down to dearer stock, safety stock and capital spending made ahead of demand[4][5]. It is the same pattern as FY26, when inventories grew faster than sales. CARE notes that recovery lags can hurt reported margins and that a currency fall, such as the Egyptian pound in FY24, can cut profit[23].

Governance and ownership: no promoter and a thinner board

At 30 September 2026 EPL has no promoter, and the filing declares no pledged or otherwise encumbered promoter shares because there are none to declare[18]. On 9 October 2026 three non-executive, non-independent directors resigned, each giving 'other professional commitments' as the reason; the filing does not link them to a shareholder[21]. On 13 October 2026 the company reported that a designated person, a senior manager, sold 6,686 shares on 19 August 2026 and bought 1,000 on 1 September 2026 without the required pre-clearance; the company issued a caution and said it was inadvertent[22].

The ownership change cuts both ways. A merger that makes IVL the 51.8 percent promoter brings in a parent whose own net debt is 6.77 times its EBITDA in the IVL deck[11]. Management says it does not expect the combined company to take on extra debt or pay large dividends[12]. Neither statement can be tested until the merger closes.
