1. What the company does

Ather Energy makes electric scooters and sells the things that surround them. It designs the scooter, the battery pack, the charging network and the software, and it assembles vehicles and packs in its own plant at Hosur in Tamil Nadu.[5][1] A second plant, called Factory 3.0, is being built at AURIC in Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar, Maharashtra.[1] Three scooter lines are on sale: the performance-minded 450 series, the family scooter Rizta, and Konarc, a lower-cost scooter on a new platform that launched on 29 August 2026.[1][4] Rizta was almost three-quarters of sales by the March 2026 quarter.[9]

The first customer is the dealer. Ather sells scooters in bulk to dealers, who sell on to riders, so the company records revenue at the wholesale step. In the September 2026 quarter wholesale volume was 93,211 scooters and riders registered 92,589 of them, so the two counts are close and dealers are not piling up stock.[1][2] The stores are run by partners who pay for them, which is why management says store growth needs no capital from Ather.[5][8] There were 700 experience centres, the company's word for its stores, at the end of March 2026, against 351 a year earlier.[5]

Revenue from operations was ₹1,464.24 crore in the quarter. Vehicles were 85 percent of it, and what Ather calls non-vehicle revenue was the other 15 percent, up from 12 percent a year earlier.[2] Non-vehicle revenue is software subscriptions (AtherStack Pro), the Ather Grid charging network, accessories, spares and service. Management says more than half of it is AtherStack Pro, which earns a gross margin (what is left of each rupee of sales after the direct cost of what is sold) of about 80 to 90 percent, against 30 to 40 percent on accessories and 40 to 45 percent on spares.[10] In the quarter 94 percent of customers chose AtherStack Pro.[1]

Two government programmes shape the price. The PM E-DRIVE scheme pays up to ₹5,000 on each eligible electric two-wheeler (₹2,500 for each kilowatt-hour of battery, capped at ₹5,000, for scooters priced under about ₹1.5 lakh), and on 10 August 2026 it was extended to 31 March 2028.[24][23][22] Ather does not receive the production-linked incentive, a separate subsidy for large makers, because the scheme sets entry tests such as a turnover of ₹10,000 crore that Ather does not meet; management says this has made it more careful about pricing.[26][25]

Demand is larger than supply. The Hosur plant ran at 98 percent of capacity at its peak in the quarter, pre-orders reached 1.80 lakh (180,000), and management put unmet retail demand at about 20,000 scooters a month.[1][10] To fix this, Ather will lift Hosur from 4.2 lakh to 4.95 lakh scooters a year by December 2026, start Factory 3.0 in phases from December 2026, and grow installed capacity to about 11.4 lakh a year by the September 2027 quarter, spending about ₹122 crore more.[1]

Hero MotoCorp, one of India's largest two-wheeler makers, held 30.07 percent of Ather at 31 March 2026 and is listed as a promoter, together with the two founders, Tarun Mehta and Swapnil Jain.[14][13] Promoters in total held 42.33 percent at 30 September 2026.[15] On 27 August 2026 Hero approved buying up to ₹1,758 crore of shares from an existing holder, which would lift its stake to about 32.8 percent on a fully diluted basis; no money from that deal goes to Ather.[36] Hero also sells a rival electric scooter, Vida, so the same company is Ather's largest owner and one of its competitors.[16]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Adjusted gross margin (AGM) — total income minus the cost of parts and goods sold, as a share of total income; it is the company's own measure of what each scooter earns before fixed costs.
  • Registrations and wholesale — registrations are scooters riders have registered with the transport authority (from the Vahan portal); wholesale is scooters Ather has sold to dealers.
  • AtherStack Pro — the paid software pack sold with most scooters; its share of buyers is called the attach rate.
  • PM E-DRIVE and PLI — two government schemes: the first pays buyers of eligible electric scooters up to ₹5,000, the second pays selected large makers for vehicles built with local content.
  • Interest cover — profit before interest and tax divided by interest paid; below 2.0 times is one of our hard red lines.
  • QIP, preferential issue and warrants — three ways of selling new shares: to large institutions, to chosen investors at a set price, and as rights to buy shares later at a fixed price after paying part now.
  • Lakh and crore — 1 lakh is 100,000 and 1 crore is 10 million; ₹1,000 crore is ₹10 billion.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit from running the business before those four items; Ather also reports it with other income included.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided by its number of shares.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is the cash left after running the business and buying plant and equipment; it is negative at Ather today.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business; it is negative at Ather today.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has been rising; above 70 is called overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the average daily price swing in rupees; we measure distance from support in these swings.
  • Stage 2 and Stage 4 — the Weinstein stage scale: Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • A base is a sideways stretch of trading before a price move, and the pivot is the top of that base, the level a breakout must clear.
  • Entry zone and limit order — the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned, and a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better and does not chase.
  • Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the gain from the entry price to the target divided by the loss from the entry price to the stop.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, and price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share.
  • Guidance is the company's own published forecast, and working capital is the cash tied up in stock and customer bills minus the credit suppliers give.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Ather is the third-largest electric scooter maker in India by registrations, behind TVS Motor and Bajaj Auto. In September 2026 it registered 30,578 scooters, against 54,105 for TVS, 48,482 for Bajaj, 24,341 for Hero's Vida and 13,452 for Ola Electric, out of 207,542 across India.[19] That is 14.7 percent of the month, against 26.1 percent for TVS and 23.4 percent for Bajaj.[19] For the September quarter Ather's share was 15.1 percent, down from 17.2 percent a year earlier and well below the 18.6 percent of the March 2026 quarter.[2][9]

The slide is mostly a supply problem, not a demand problem. The whole market grew 82 percent in the quarter, to about 5.98 lakh registrations, and Ather's registrations grew 60 percent, so Ather grew more slowly than the market it sells into. The company's 15.1 percent share comes from its own Vahan cut and does not divide exactly into the figures shown (92,589 over about 5.98 lakh is 15.5 percent).[1][2] It ran its plant flat out and sold what it built. Pre-orders more than doubled, and management estimated unmet retail demand of about 20,000 scooters a month.[1][10] If that is right, new capacity should help win share back. The test is the next two quarters of registrations, and we return to it in Chapter 6.

Three strengths stand out. The first is the software: the AtherStack Pro attach rate stayed between 89 and 94 percent over ten quarters while registrations rose more than fivefold.[2] The second is distribution: stores doubled from 351 to 700 in FY26, and management said new-format stores reach operating break-even in one to two quarters, far faster than the two years it had told partners to expect.[5][9] The third is engineering control. Ather owns the electronics design, so when China limited exports of heavy rare earth magnets in the September 2025 quarter, its engineers requalified parts within the quarter, at a one-time revenue cost of ₹24.52 crore.[5]

Four weaknesses matter just as much. Ather has no production-linked incentive, while Bajaj and Ola have received theirs, so some rivals can price lower against the same costs.[26] Ather buys most of its inputs on world markets: aluminium, copper, lithium, crude-linked plastics and memory chips all rose, and management put the commodity hit to gross margin at 9.4 percentage points in the quarter, with no relief in sight.[1][10] Until Factory 3.0 runs at scale, Ather cannot serve all the demand it has, which hands share to rivals with spare capacity. And profit is still thin: ₹1.26 crore on ₹1,464.24 crore of revenue.[2]

How the four large rivals compare

TVS Motor sold 186K electric two-wheelers in the September 2026 quarter, up 133 percent, and 65,799 in September alone.[18] Bajaj Auto said Chetak volumes more than doubled, revenue crossed ₹2,000 crore and profitability continued to improve, even though supply was tight.[17] Hero's Vida sold 66,531 scooters at retail, up 76 percent.[16] TVS, Bajaj and Vida together held about 62 percent of registrations in late September, up from 59.5 percent in August.[20] Ola Electric registered 13,452 scooters in September, 3.0 percent fewer than a year before, and reported a net loss of ₹336 crore for the June 2026 quarter.[19][21] Ather therefore sits between the profitable giants that can fund losses on electric scooters from other businesses, and a weaker pure-play rival.

What this means for an owner: Ather is the leading independent electric scooter company, but the market is turning into a contest among large groups with factories, dealers and government incentives that Ather lacks. That raises the bar for how much of the future profit Ather can keep, which Chapter 5 turns into numbers.

Control, conflicts and compliance history

Hero MotoCorp's stake is a strength and a conflict. It gives Ather a large, stable owner, and Hero said on 27 August 2026 that it was raising its stake.[36] But Hero also sells Vida, which grew 76 percent in the quarter, and it is classed as a promoter of Ather.[16][13] Promoter pledges are zero: none of the promoters' shares were pledged at any quarter-end from May 2025 to September 2026.[14] On compliance, a show-cause notice of 29 March 2023 from the heavy industries ministry about the earlier FAME II scheme led Ather to refund the price of off-board chargers to customers, ₹147.97 crore in total by March 2026.[5] In FY26 magnet-supply changes also forced Ather to defer claims and revenue of ₹24.52 crore under PM E-DRIVE until the rules are clarified.[5]

What this means for an owner: neither point, on what we read, signals financial distress, but both are reasons to read subsidy income and related-party arrangements with care.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 has a weakest assumption. This chapter names it, gives the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, and says when we would know. A report that only argues one side is an advertisement, so we also set out the case for buying.

Our base multiple could be too low

Our base value uses 85 times FY29 earnings. The street's mean target of ₹1,776.17 and Nomura's ₹2,070 imply that analysts pay much more, or look further out.[33][31] At 100 times our base earnings the value would be about ₹1,560, still below the close, and at 130 times it would be about ₹2,030. A multiple alone would need about 142 times to reach the ₹2,212 the floor requires at ₹1,565. The valuation therefore needs higher earnings, or higher earnings together with a richer multiple (for example about ₹21 at 105 times). When we would know: the street's FY28 earnings-per-share revisions after the February 2027 results.

The margin gain may be less permanent than it looks

Of the 8.0 points of gross margin gained since March, management says 5.3 points are structural (price rises, redesign savings, cost cuts) and 2.7 points are a lever that can be reversed, and commodities took away 9.4 points.[10] Management says it will not raise prices further in the next few quarters.[10] If commodity prices fall, margin could rise without help; if they rise again, the 2.7 temporary points are the first to go. When we would know: the gross margin in the December 2026 and March 2027 quarters.

Capacity and subsidy are single points of failure

Factory 3.0 must start in December 2026 and reach 42,000 a month by the end of Q4 FY27, with two quarters to ramp; delay would leave demand unserved and keep share falling.[1][10] On policy, PM E-DRIVE runs to 31 March 2028 but is fund-limited, so it could end early; the cap of ₹5,000 a vehicle is worth at most about ₹46 crore a quarter, which is larger than the quarter's profit.[22][24] Industry has asked for the magnet localisation deadline to move to April 2027, and Ather has already deferred ₹24.52 crore of claims over a magnet-rule deviation.[27][5] When we would know: monthly Vahan registrations (published each first week), the December 2026 start-up, and any change to scheme rules.

Hero MotoCorp's dual role

Hero held 30.07 percent of Ather in March 2026 and runs Vida, which sold 66,531 scooters in the quarter, up 76 percent.[14][16] Nothing in the filings we read shows a conflict, but if Hero's strategy favoured Vida, Ather would feel it in dealer sharing, technology and capital. When we would know: any change in Hero's board seats, supply or charging agreements; none was disclosed in what we read.

The strongest case against our own rejection

A buyer would argue three things. Electric scooters went from 7.8 to 11.2 percent of two-wheeler sales in a year, and Nomura thinks the real figure is about 20 percent higher because supply is short.[1][30] Ather's pre-orders doubled while it ran at 98 percent of capacity, so its next two years are about supply, not demand. Our rejection rests on the price needing about 28.9 percent growth a year for ten years, and on the written interest-cover rule.
