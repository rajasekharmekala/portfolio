Let’s walk into the two operating worlds rather than leave them as labels. In a shipyard, the integration challenge is compact, harsh and safety-critical. A naval vessel has limited space, demanding environmental requirements and multiple systems that must work together. In a data centre, the challenge is uptime, load density, redundancy and scheduled delivery so electrical fit-out does not delay commissioning. The products share electrical engineering, but the buyer’s acceptance tests and failure modes differ. This is why customer examples teach us more than a generic “power solutions” description.

For FY26, Marine supplied 52.9% of consolidated revenue and Industry 47.1%. Segment results before tax and interest were approximately ₹40 crore in each. But in the first half of FY27, Industry supplied 63.1% of revenue and Marine 36.9%. The shift is important. The Industry business is taking a larger share of the current growth pulse, especially through data-centre and infrastructure work. If we compare only full-year FY26, we miss the direction of change; if we extrapolate one half-year, we risk mistaking a project cluster for a stable new baseline.

Imagine the marine buyer’s workflow. A shipyard’s electrical design team sends load schedules and interface requirements. The vendor’s engineers develop equipment layouts, protection coordination and drawings. The shipyard reviews and freezes interfaces; components are procured; panels and systems are assembled; testing records are prepared; delivery is synchronized with hull progress. At installation, cable routes, access and commissioning windows matter. Payment may follow drawing approval, production, dispatch, installation or acceptance. Any delay between those stages can leave inventory and receivables on the supplier’s balance sheet.

Now take the data-centre example. The operator and EPC need reliable distribution through a hall, with capacity staged against construction. A switchboard or bus-duct package must match fault ratings, physical routes, redundancy and equipment interfaces. A PTU or other critical-power assembly arrives as part of an installation schedule. Factory testing reduces risk before delivery, but the site still has inspections and energization. The commercial relationship may create repeat orders when one design performs well, yet repeat work still depends on the next phase being funded and the supplier remaining competitive.

You may ask whether the business earns money from equipment, engineering or service. Public filings describe integrated solutions, products and projects, but they do not provide enough unit economics to isolate each revenue stream. We can say the customer buys a package and that engineering coordination is part of value; we cannot claim a specific service attachment rate, aftermarket margin or standard price. That boundary is not pedantry. A company with project integration can earn better margins through expertise, or it can carry more risk by owning schedule and interface obligations.

Here is the difference between a strong product and a strong segment. A product can win a customer’s confidence; the segment must earn attractive returns after procurement, labour, warranty, overhead, finance cost and working capital. FY26’s segment results look balanced, while the H1 FY27 mix leans toward Industry. We need to see whether contribution margins remain steady as larger data-centre orders are executed. We also need to watch if Indian naval work continues to grow steadily enough to protect the Marine platform while Industry expands.

In the US, Vertiv supplies critical digital-infrastructure equipment and gives us a public example of the economic value buyers place on continuity. In India, L&T’s data-centre and heavy-engineering work shows the coordinating role of an EPC contractor. In Europe, Schneider Electric’s switchgear and power-management ecosystem shows how standards, installed equipment and service can reinforce customer relationships. These parallels help you see the range of monetization models. Marine Electricals sells more project-linked systems, so we must be especially careful about acceptance timing and cash collection.

As we finish this chapter, remember the two worked examples. The shipyard buys systems that allow a vessel to generate, distribute, monitor and use power safely; the data-centre operator buys power distribution that helps a facility come online and remain available. In each case, identify who specifies, who approves, who installs, who accepts and who pays. If any one party is different, the commercial risk changes. That end-to-end map is our foundation for the ₹100 waterfall in the next chapter.

Hold the segment numbers in two frames. FY26 was almost evenly divided between Marine and Industry, while H1 FY27 tilted strongly toward Industry. The shift helps explain the current growth acceleration but also makes the latest mix less representative of the long history. A half-year can be affected by a few large deliveries. We should watch the full year and compare the next primary segment-result disclosure, not infer stable economics from one mix. The question is whether the company has built an industrial platform or is temporarily executing a cluster of large projects.

Let me walk you through a vessel order slowly. The shipyard starts with drawings, loads and required systems. The supplier’s engineers design how power is distributed, where boards fit, how protection works and how monitoring connects. The shipyard approves the interfaces. Components are purchased, assemblies are built and factory tests are recorded. Delivery depends on hull readiness. Installation and commissioning depend on cable routes and other contractors. Acceptance then triggers a contractual payment event. A vendor that understands the vessel can be valuable; a vendor that cannot manage these dependencies can lose money while the project still appears successful on the surface.

Now follow the data-centre package. The operator plans a hall and needs a power architecture that can support high density and maintenance. Equipment must match ratings, redundancy, site layout and energization dates. A supplier may repeat a design at another facility, but every site still has interfaces and sequencing. Repeat orders are a useful signal of customer confidence. They are not proof that prices are high or margins are recurring. You and I would want to know the amount of follow-on revenue, number of distinct buyers, the installation scope and how quickly invoices are collected.

The products solve different problems even though both involve electricity. At sea, compactness, environmental durability, safety and integration across a vessel are critical. In a data centre, uptime, fault isolation, redundancy and predictable installation are central. That technical difference shapes approvals and competition. Public disclosures show product families and customer categories, but not product margins or cash by segment. So our segment assessment is careful: each has a credible customer problem, and both can create value; the faster-growing segment’s quality still needs more reporting.

The two segments speak different commercial languages. Marine’s customer may care most about qualification, integration and acceptance aboard a vessel; an Industry customer may care about redundancy, schedule and repeatability across facilities. FY26 segment results were nearly evenly split, while H1 revenue shifted toward Industry. But the H1 profit split is not verified in the primary extract we reviewed. This is where you and I should stop rather than infer that revenue share equals economic share. A segment can grow quickly while consuming more engineering, inventory or receivables. The next disclosure that would sharpen our view is a comparable segment result and working-capital bridge, with the same definitions each period.

Revenue mix tells us where the work was recognized; it does not tell us which work created more cash. Marine and Industry have different buyers, installation environments and acceptance steps. Until disclosures line up segment result with working capital, treat the mix shift as a change in exposure rather than a confirmed improvement in quality. That restraint keeps our analysis honest.

A customer example becomes more useful when we trace the handoffs. The designer defines the interface, procurement checks the approved vendor, the factory records its tests, the site team accepts commissioning and finance releases the milestone. A failure at any handoff can delay revenue or payment. That chain is where execution capability meets cash conversion.

CHAPTER III

How It Is Sold & Delivered — Distribution & Unit Economics

Let’s trace one order together, because “distribution” sounds too simple for this business. A buyer defines a design, approves vendors and issues a tender. The company’s technical team qualifies the scope, coordinates interfaces and submits a commercial offer. If selected, it may have to freeze drawings before it can release components. Procurement begins; manufacturing and testing follow; the site decides when deliveries can be received; installation and commissioning create acceptance milestones. Only after the relevant documents are accepted does the invoice become collectible under the contract. Every pause can tie up cash.

In a naval program, the customer’s approval chain may be long and formal. In a data centre, construction phasing can make delivery windows just as demanding. The supplier does not control the entire project schedule. A customer can delay site readiness, a component can arrive late, and a design change can require rework. Good project management turns these risks into manageable milestones; weak management turns a growing order book into aging inventory and receivables. That is why we want data on advances and milestone billing, even though the company does not publish a standard contract template.

Now let’s make the ₹100 waterfall concrete without pretending the business has a fixed cost recipe. FY26 reported sales were ₹877 crore, operating profit ₹94 crore, PAT ₹59 crore and operating cash flow negative ₹16 crore. Per ₹100 of sales, that is about ₹10.7 of operating profit, ₹6.7 of profit after tax and negative ₹1.8 of cash from operations. Free cash flow in the market-data series was negative ₹43 crore, or about negative ₹4.9 per ₹100 sales. The numbers do not mean every individual order loses cash. They show that, in aggregate, growth and working capital overwhelmed the period’s cash earnings.

The company presentation gives another lens. It reports FY26 EBITDA margin of 12.4% against total income, but working capital changes used ₹100.3 crore while pre-working-capital operating cash flow was ₹106 crore. So almost all of the pre-working-capital cash generation was consumed by the change in working capital during that year. The presentation’s H1/H2 analysis argues that outflows are seasonal and second-half weighted. We should test that claim against subsequent full-year collections, not dismiss it and not accept it as a permanent excuse.

Here is the mental exercise I want you to practice on future filings. For each ₹100 of sales, ask: how much was gross profit, how much did operating expenses consume, how much did finance cost and tax consume, and how much cash remained after receivables, inventory, payables and capital expenditure? We know the reported profit layers, but public filings do not isolate a clean recurring free-cash-flow margin for a typical contract. The correct answer for now is that owner cash is not stable enough to be capitalized confidently.

Debtor days are the clearest red flag and the clearest monitoring signal. The series shows a long history of high receivable intensity, a sharp improvement from the FY20 peak, then a rise from 157 to 178 in FY26. A business may have good reasons for this—government approvals, project billing, seasonality—but those reasons must eventually resolve into collections. The latest H1 investor presentation reported debtors at 176 days for H1 FY26 and 158 for H2 FY26; historical basis and timing matter. Compare like periods, and use cash flow as the final arbiter.

Across the US, Rockwell Automation’s project and control systems show how qualification and service relationships can support pricing. In India, L&T’s EPC model illustrates why milestone schedules and subcontractor coordination determine working capital. In Europe, Siemens’ industrial automation business shows how product standards and installed systems can create customer continuity. These are larger businesses with different capital structures, but the same questions apply: who holds inventory, who owns installation risk, what triggers an invoice, and who finances the gap until collection?

When you hear “strong order book,” add three follow-up questions aloud: what margin is expected, what cash must be spent before delivery, and what exact event triggers payment? If the company reports those answers with improving consistency, our confidence rises. Until then, ₹100 of revenue produces reported profit but not reliably positive operating cash. That is the unit-economics tension around which the rest of our valuation must turn.

Let’s pretend we are watching one order from tender to cash. The customer’s engineers define what is required. A vendor list screens out suppliers that do not meet the standards. Marine Electricals’ bid team prices the equipment and scope, and design staff answer clarifications. If selected, the company waits for approved drawings, then buys components and schedules production. Quality control records the test. Delivery must line up with the site. Commissioning and acceptance close the loop. Until each commercial milestone occurs, cash may be tied up in stock, work in progress or receivables.

The ₹100 waterfall is sobering because it does not stop at PAT. Roughly ₹6.7 of FY26 sales appeared as PAT, but operating cash was negative around ₹1.8 and free cash flow around negative ₹4.9 per ₹100. That does not mean each order loses cash. It means that, in the aggregate, the company’s working-capital needs consumed more than the period’s operating cash earnings. If management is right that working capital is seasonal, then subsequent periods should return cash. We will look for that return rather than argue about the wording of the presentation.

Think of project working capital as a bridge. The supplier pays for materials, labour and manufacturing first. The customer may pay an advance or a milestone later. If the bridge is short and well-secured, growth can be funded safely. If it is long, and the supplier must borrow while waiting for approval or collection, the bridge gets expensive. Terms are not disclosed by contract, so we cannot calculate the bridge for each order. We can observe its aggregate footprint through debtor days, contract assets, cash flow and borrowing. Those metrics are the guardrails around the growth story.
