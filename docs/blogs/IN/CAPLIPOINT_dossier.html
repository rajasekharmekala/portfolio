Terms used in this report

This report uses a handful of finance and chart terms. Each one is explained here once, with an everyday comparison, so that the later chapters can use them without stopping to define them again. A reader who already knows these terms can skip to the first chapter about the framework.

The financial terms come from the company's own accounts. ROCE, or return on capital employed, measures how much operating profit the business earns on all the money tied up in it. It is like the rent a landlord earns on a building, measured against the cost of buying the building. The Indian floor is 15 percent, and 18 percent or more is preferred for Core names. Debt-to-equity compares borrowed money with the owners' own money. A ratio below 0.70 is the floor, which means the company owes less than seventy paise for every rupee the owners put in. CFO to PAT compares cash from operations with reported profit after tax over three years. A ratio of 0.70 or more means the reported profit is backed by real cash coming in, rather than by sales that have not yet been paid for.

The price terms describe how the share has traded. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of sessions, a bit like a smoothed trend line through a noisy chart. The 20-day, 50-day and 200-day averages are the three the framework reads. Weinstein staging divides a stock's life into four phases. Stage 1 is a flat base where buyers and sellers are balanced. Stage 2 is the markup, a sustained uptrend. Stage 3 is the topping phase, where the trend loses momentum. Stage 4 is the decline. A breakout is a close above the top of a base, and the pivot is the price level that marks that top. A retest is a later return to the broken level to see whether it now holds. RSI, the relative strength index, runs on a 0 to 100 scale and acts like a speedometer for recent price moves. Readings below 30 are often called oversold, which describes a fast fall rather than a promise of a bounce. ATR, the average true range over 14 sessions, is the usual daily swing of the price, in rupees. It is the unit the stop-loss and the support bands are measured in.

The valuation terms come next. P/E, or price-to-earnings, is the share price divided by earnings per share. A P/E of 27 means investors pay 27 rupees for each rupee of annual profit per share. Trailing EPS is the profit per share over the last four reported quarters. Forward EPS is the profit per share expected over the next year, and the house rule needs a sourced figure for it. The three-year median P/E is the middle value of the multiple over the last three years, which removes the effect of one unusually high or low month. Asymmetry is the ratio of the upside to the target against the downside to the stop. A ratio of 3.0 means the potential gain is three times the potential loss.

The last group covers the rules themselves. The stop-loss is the price at which the position would be sold to cap the loss, and the framework sets it from the swing low and the moving averages, minus a multiple of ATR. The momentum gate is a set of tests that a stock must pass to earn an allocation at all. The ledger is the frozen snapshot of prices and indicators for 13 October 2026, which every figure in the technical chapters comes from.

How the framework works for this company

The framework is a sequence of gates, and a company must pass each one before the next is read. Layer 1 is a set of binary floors: financial health, technical posture and asymmetry. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five operating measures for the specific business. Layer 3 is the entry-timing check, which asks how far the price sits above the nearest believable support. The sequence matters because a weak chart can make a strong business a poor purchase today. The framework is built so that a good business does not get a pass on a bad entry, and a cheap chart does not get a pass on a weak business.

Caplin Point is a manufacturer, not a lender, so the debt-to-equity floor applies in full. Some Indian financial companies are excluded from that floor because borrowing is their raw material. That exclusion does not apply here, and the company's debt is small in any case. The company is evaluated under the Core bucket, which is the long-horizon book with a three-to-five-year view, and the Tactical bucket is also tested because the stop-loss and asymmetry rules differ between them. Both buckets fail, so the choice does not change the verdict.

The house rules also set how the price is read. The framework does not use a single moving average for a Stage call; it reads the 50-day and 200-day averages together with the price. It does not allow a target to be drawn from a hope, so the target must be built from forward earnings and a multiple, or from a measured move, which is the distance a base's height projects upward from its breakout. A target that cannot be built this way counts as a fail, even if the business looks cheap. The sections that follow apply these rules one at a time.

What the company does and how it earns

Caplin Point makes finished medicines, meaning the vials, tablets, eye drops and syringes that patients use, and it also makes some of the raw active ingredients that go into them. The company says it is possibly the only mid-sized Indian pharmaceutical company that combines research, manufacture of finished formulations and active ingredients, clinical research and a USFDA and EU-GMP approved injectable facility [22]. A simple comparison helps. Think of a company that bakes its own flour, makes its own bread and sells it through its own shops in several countries. The more steps it controls, the more of the price it keeps.

Most of the money comes from the Latin American and African markets. The company's website says that generic and branded generic products in Latin America and Francophone Africa made up 79 percent of FY26 revenue, and that the US business made up 21 percent [5]. The Q1 FY27 press release gives the same split for the quarter: 78 percent from emerging markets and 22 percent from the US [8]. A large share of the emerging-market business is sold through tenders, where a buyer invites bids and the winning bidder supplies the order, with the rest sold in private markets. Tenders bring large single orders, and they also bring lumpiness, because the next order is not guaranteed.

The US business runs through a subsidiary, Caplin Steriles. Between December 2025 and January 2026 the subsidiary received final USFDA approval for a linezolid infusion product and for a methylprednisolone acetate injectable suspension [28][29]. In January 2026 the group also bought ten approved generic applications for injectable and eye products, which it said had an addressable market of $473.2 million over the twelve months to August 2025 [30]. These approvals matter because a US generic earns its revenue only after the regulator has signed off, and each approval opens a new product line.

How the earnings are made

The earnings engine is the gross margin, which is what is left after the cost of making the product. For the June 2026 quarter it was 59.8 percent, down from 61.7 percent a year earlier [8]. The fall is small but it is the kind of trend that the framework watches. The company reports an EBITDA margin, which is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, of 38.4 percent for the quarter, against 37.7 percent a year earlier, so cost control has more than offset the gross-margin slip [8]. Consolidated revenue from operations was ₹610.36 crore in the quarter, and profit after tax was ₹179.1 crore [8]. For FY26, the annual report gives revenue from operations of ₹2,187.19 crore and profit after tax of ₹649.73 crore [11].

Cash is the other half of the story. Cash from operations in the June quarter was ₹95 crore, against ₹118 crore a year earlier, and management tied the drop to a deliberate build of warehouse stock, which rose to ₹505 crore from ₹429 crore in March, along with higher government receivables [8][10]. Receivables stood at 128 days of sales, which means it takes about four months to collect the average bill [8]. The company reports free cash reserves of ₹1,502 crore and liquid assets of ₹2,875 crore at 30 June 2026 [8]. It has also planned capex of about ₹1,000 crore over the next two to three years, which it says it will fund solely from internal accruals [9].

The promoters hold 70.57 percent of the shares as of June 2026 [1]. That is a high promoter stake, which usually means the founding family's interests and the public's are closely aligned, but it also leaves a smaller free float. For the framework, the point is that the business is well capitalised and its profits are real, but the cash it earns is partly tied up in stock and tender receivables. That is the reason the cash-conversion lines matter later in the red team.

Asymmetry and target anchoring

Asymmetry is the test of whether the reward is large enough to justify the risk. The formula is the target less the close, divided by the close less the stop. A Core name must reach 3.0 to 1, and a Tactical name must reach 3.5 to 1. The formula is simple, but the input that makes it honest is the target, and the house rule is strict about where the target comes from. The target must be anchored to a sourced forward earnings figure multiplied by the three-year median P/E, or to a measured move on a base. An arbitrary target fails at once.

The company's three-year median trailing P/E is 27.2, and the series used runs from October 2023 to October 2026 [2]. That number is sourced. The forward earnings figure is the problem. No consensus forward EPS was found from three attempts, two on MarketScreener and one on the NSE quote page, and the NSE page loaded without figures. Management does not give numeric guidance, which means a published, quantified forecast. On the May 2026 call, a senior executive said the company could see another 25 to 30 percent growth next year on the overall Caplin Steriles business, but that was about the subsidiary, not the group [14]. A subsidiary growth remark cannot be turned into a group earnings figure without guessing, so the target stays unanchored.

The trailing EPS is ₹87.53, and it is sourced. It is the FY26 basic EPS of ₹84.36, plus the June 2026 quarter's ₹23.27, minus the June 2025 quarter's ₹20.10 [8][2]. Multiplying that trailing figure by the median P/E gives ₹2,380.82, only 0.8 percent above the close. This is not a target. It is a sensitivity, set out so the reader can see how far the price is from the floor even if trailing earnings were treated as forward earnings. On that basis the Core ratio would be 0.06 and the Tactical ratio 0.20, far below the floors of 3.0 and 3.5.

A red-team review asks what would make the decision wrong. The verdict here is REJECTED, so the test is whether a reader who wants to buy has a strong case that the framework has misjudged. Three flaws are tested, and each names the evidence and the trigger that would confirm it.

Flaw one: the multiple, not the earnings, is the risk

The strongest case for buying is that earnings are growing and the stock is well below its three-year high multiple of 40.0. The bull case is real, and the figures are sourced. Against its median, however, the stock is level: the current trailing P/E of 27.0 sits 0.2 turns below the 27.2 median, so it is not cheap on the anchor the house rules use. Trailing EPS has risen from ₹55.04 in November 2023 to ₹87.53 today, and the three-year high multiple of 40.0 would put the price near ₹3,501 [2]. But the same series shows the multiple falling from that high to 27.0. The fall is the risk. If the multiple went back to the three-year low of 19.3, the price would be about ₹1,689, which is 28.5 percent below the close. The framework's answer is that the median, not the peak, is the anchor, and that the chart is still, on our reading, in a topping phase. The trigger that would weaken this flaw is a close above ₹2,631.19 that holds.

Flaw two: cash conversion is slipping at the margin

The second case against the company is cash. Three-year CFO to PAT is 0.77 and passes the floor, but FY26 alone is 0.81, and the June quarter fell to ₹95 crore of operating cash flow. Receivables are 128 days, and inventory rose by about ₹76 crore in one quarter. Management blamed these on a deliberate stock build and on government receivables that it expects to collect by the third quarter [10]. That may prove right, but the cash rule is only met on a three-year basis, and a bear could argue that working capital, which is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills, is growing faster than profit. The trigger that would confirm it is a fall of CFO to PAT below 0.70 on the next three-year window, or receivable days rising further.

Flaw three: concentration in tenders and margin drift

The third case is concentration. Emerging markets made up 78 percent of revenue in the June quarter, and much of it comes from government tenders. On the call, management said it expected a decision on a repeat supplementary tender within a couple of months, and that it was not committing to the outcome until then [10]. On the Q1 FY27 call an analyst put the rest-of-world profit margin at about 37.5 percent a year earlier and about 33 percent in the latest quarter. That figure is the analyst's, not one this report checked, and management argued the change is small and that the business is not a commodity [10]. A bear would say a tender business can lose its margin when prices are reset, and that the next tender is the risk. The trigger is a further fall in the rest-of-world margin, or a lost large tender.

Other risks the framework notes

Two smaller risks need mention. The US tariff policy is uncertain, and management said it would cross that bridge when it reached it [10]. The capex plan of about ₹1,000 crore, spread over two to three years, equals between about a third and two-thirds of the ₹2,875 crore of liquid assets and the ₹1,502 crore of free cash reserves respectively. Although management says it can fund it from internal accruals, that cash is committed to the plants while they are built [9]. Neither is large enough to decide the verdict, but both belong in a complete picture of the risks.

Invalidation levels and what we would do

An invalidation level is a price or a fact that would show the current reading is wrong. For this verdict, there are three levels to watch, and each one has a clear action. The reader should not treat any of them as a forecast of direction. They are the lines at which the framework changes its mind, and the action at each line is set in advance so that no one has to decide it under pressure.

The first level is ₹2,631.19, the 50-day average. A close above it that holds, with the average still rising and the stage read as Stage 2, would clear the technical floor. That would not by itself change the verdict, because the momentum gate and the asymmetry floor would still need to pass. It would, however, move the company from the technical reject to the open questions list, and the framework would then ask for a sourced forward earnings figure. The second level is ₹2,134.82, the 200-day average. A close below it would confirm that the long trend has broken, which means Stage 4 is a live risk, and the stock would stay rejected under the technical floor with no change in the reading.

The third level is the Core stop of ₹2,018.95. A close below it would breach the Core rule. For a name on the watchlist with no position, the action is to keep it rejected and not to allocate. For a name that had been bought under a different view, the rule would require a sale. What we would do now is keep CAPLIPOINT on the active watchlist with no allocation. We would rerun the underwriting when one of three things happens: the next quarterly results add to the cash and margin picture, a sourced forward earnings figure becomes available, or the momentum gate passes. Any of those would reopen the question, and none of them is a trigger to buy on its own.

Trigger to reopen the case: a close above ₹2,631.19 that holds on the following sessions, with the 50-day average still rising, a momentum test that passes, and a sourced forward EPS that lifts the Core target to at least ₹3,389.55. Until all of these hold, the verdict stays REJECTED.
