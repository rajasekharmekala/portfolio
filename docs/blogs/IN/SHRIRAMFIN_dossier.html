Before you read

MUFG calls Shriram Finance India’s second-largest retail non-bank lender, one that mostly finances used trucks, buses and cars for small transport operators.[1][14] We reject the stock for now at ₹832.55 because its chart is in a confirmed downtrend with no floor yet, even though the business passed the tests we applied to a lender.[41] The company earned ₹3,677 crore in the September 2026 quarter, up 59 percent, but it also cut its loan-growth target and said bad-loan costs will stay higher for a quarter or two.[1][4] If the chart were not in a downtrend, our numbers would support waiting for a pullback to about ₹794 to ₹818 with a 12-month target near ₹965.[41] We would reopen the idea when the chart passes our recovery test and the December-quarter results show credit costs back near 2 percent.

1. What the company does

Shriram Finance is a non-bank finance company (NBFC), which means it is a lender regulated by the Reserve Bank of India that does not run cheque accounts like a bank. It funds itself by taking fixed deposits from households and by borrowing from banks, bond investors and foreign lenders. It then lends that money on at a higher rate and keeps the difference.[1] It was set up in 1979, began by financing used trucks, and is now the flagship of the Shriram group, with 3,225 branches, 82,292 employees and about 10.57 million customers.[1] In the 2022 amalgamation, Shriram City Union Finance and Shriram Capital merged into Shriram Transport Finance, which was renamed Shriram Finance. The accounts treat the merger as effective from 1 April 2022, which is why the 2022 numbers in Chapter 3 do not compare with later years.[9]

Who pays, and for what? Mostly small road-transport operators and small-business owners, who borrow to buy a second-hand truck, a bus, a car, a tractor or a two-wheeler, or to fund their working needs. At 30 September 2026 the loan book, called assets under management (AUM) (the total loans the company has made and still manages), was ₹329,022 crore. Commercial vehicles were 46.51 percent of it, passenger vehicles 21.74 percent, loans to micro, small and medium businesses 14.04 percent, two-wheelers 5.46 percent, construction equipment 3.63 percent, farm equipment 2.12 percent, personal loans 3.77 percent and gold loans 2.73 percent.[1] Crisil, a rating agency, reported in April 2026 that about 83 percent of the company’s vehicle loans were against used vehicles and only 17 percent against new ones. Organised lenders and banks largely avoid used-vehicle loans because the borrowers are hard to assess.[11]

How does a lender make money? It earns interest on loans and pays interest on its own borrowings. The September 2026 quarter shows where each rupee went. Interest earned was ₹13,669 crore and interest paid was ₹5,008 crore, leaving net interest income of ₹8,661 crore.[1] Add other income of ₹128 crore and the company had ₹8,789 crore to work with. Running costs took ₹1,980 crore (22.5 percent), expected loan losses took ₹1,877 crore (21.4 percent) and tax took ₹1,255 crore (14.3 percent). The remaining ₹3,677 crore (41.8 percent) was profit after tax.[1] The four slices add to 100 percent. The point to hold on to is that loan losses are the swing factor: a one-point move in credit cost changes profit by far more than a one-point move in running costs.

Two measures describe the margin. The net interest margin (NIM) is the interest the company keeps after paying for its own funding, measured against its loans. It was 9.52 percent in the September 2026 quarter, against 8.19 percent a year earlier and 9.04 percent in June.[1] Management says the jump is temporary, because the new equity from MUFG costs nothing, and it expects the margin to settle near 9 percent in the medium term and 8.5 percent in the long term.[4] Bad loans are reported as gross non-performing assets (GNPA), which the company calls gross Stage 3 loans: loans more than 90 days overdue, as a share of all loans. Net non-performing assets (NNPA) is the same figure after setting aside provisions. The gross figure was 4.65 percent and the net figure 2.31 percent at 30 September 2026.[1]

A lender also carries far more borrowing than a factory does, and that is normal because borrowed money is its raw material. Borrowings were ₹236,271 crore at 30 September 2026. Public deposits were 31.15 percent of them, term loans from banks 21.05 percent, bonds (non-convertible debentures) 15.71 percent, securitisation (loans sold on to investors) 13.92 percent and foreign-currency loans 13.22 percent.[1] The company’s own leverage ratio, which is borrowings divided by shareholders’ money, fell from 3.82 times in March 2026 to 2.16 times in September 2026.[4] The reason is the largest single event in the company’s recent history: on 8 April 2026 MUFG Bank of Japan paid ₹39,618 crore for 47.11 crore new shares at ₹840.93 each, taking 20 percent of the company.[15][2]

The latest results were announced on 23 October 2026, so they are known and in the price. The board declared an interim dividend of ₹6.50 a share with a record date of 29 October 2026, to be paid before 20 November 2026.[2] The next quarterly results, for the December quarter, have not been scheduled. Last year’s December-quarter call was held on 12 February 2026, so a date between late January and mid-February 2027 is plausible, but that is our inference from a past date and not a filing.[4] The rest of this report asks whether the business behind these results is worth owning at today’s price, and whether the chart allows it. Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPS stands for earnings per share (EPS): the company’s profit divided across all its shares, stated in rupees a share.
  • ROCE stands for return on capital employed (ROCE): profit earned on the capital a business uses. It suits factories and is not used for a lender, whose interest cost is the main cost of doing business. We use return on equity and return on assets instead.
  • guidance is the company’s own published forecast or target for a coming period.
  • consensus estimate means the average of analysts’ forecasts for a number such as profit per share.
  • dilution is the fall in each existing shareholder’s slice of the company when new shares are issued, as happened with the MUFG shares.
  • debt-to-equity is a company’s borrowings divided by shareholders’ money. For a lender it is normally above one and is judged against the regulator’s capital ratio, not against a factory rule.
  • price-to-earnings is the share price divided by a year’s profit per share, and it tells you how many years of profit you pay for; price-to-book is the share price divided by the net worth per share.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash a business generates from its own activities. A growing lender’s figure is sharply negative because every new loan counts as cash going out. FCF stands for free cash flow (FCF), the cash left after buying assets, and it is therefore not a useful test for a lender.
  • RSI stands for relative strength index (RSI): a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast the price has been moving. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR stands for average true range (ATR): the typical daily price swing in rupees, used to measure distances on the chart.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 sessions; the 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the same idea over 50 and 200 sessions. Each smooths the price and shows the direction of the trend.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend, where the price sits under a falling 50-day average that is itself under the 200-day average.
  • A base is a sideways stretch of trading where a falling price builds a floor; its top edge is the pivot. A breakout is a close above the pivot on heavy volume, and a retest is a pullback to a level the price has just left.
  • A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut; a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better; the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the possible gain divided by the possible loss: (target price minus entry price) divided by (entry price minus stop loss).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Shriram Finance is a strong lender in a hard corner of the market, with a visible edge in used-vehicle credit and a balance sheet that the MUFG money has made much safer. It is not a risk-free franchise. This chapter lays out what the evidence supports, then where the weak points are, because the stock’s fall in October 2026 is about exactly those weak points.

The used-vehicle edge

The first source of strength is knowledge. A used truck has no list price, and its value depends on its age, mileage, repair history and the route it runs. Shriram has financed these vehicles for more than four decades, and its staff value the vehicle and judge the owner’s cash flow in the same visit.[1] Crisil describes this as a “strong and sustainable competitive advantage” and says the company faces limited competition from banks and other organised lenders in this segment because the borrowers are risky and hard to assess.[11] CARE Ratings, a second agency, points to the same franchise: leadership in used-vehicle finance, a branch network of over 3,225 and a multi-product, pan-India retail book.[12]

The second source of strength is reach. Of 3,225 branches, 2,043 are in semi-urban and rural places, 819 in towns and 363 in metros, and Tamil Nadu alone has 772.[1] That network feeds a book where no single borrower matters: the company has about 10.57 million customers, so customer concentration is not a risk here. The risk is concentration by product: commercial vehicles are 46.51 percent of AUM, and the whole book depends on how truck owners earn.[1]

The third source is that the book has become broader since the 2022 merger. Passenger vehicles, small-business loans, two-wheelers, personal loans and gold loans together are now about 48 percent of AUM. CARE says this diversification has reduced the cyclicality of the older truck-heavy book.[12][1] The cost is a lower average yield: management says a new-vehicle loan earns 200 to 400 basis points less than a used-vehicle loan, and it plans to keep growing new-vehicle lending to keep its customers loyal.[4] A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the gap is 2 to 4 points of yield.

Funding, rating and the MUFG effect

A lender lives or dies on its funding. Shriram’s mix is wide: deposits are 31.15 percent of borrowings, up from 27.99 percent a year earlier, term loans 21.05 percent, bonds 15.71 percent, securitisation 13.92 percent and foreign-currency loans 13.22 percent.[1] Retail deposits are the stickiest of these. The company’s liquidity coverage ratio, a regulator’s test of whether liquid assets cover a month of stress outflows, was 303.84 percent at 30 September 2026, and its repayment schedule shows more money coming in than going out in every bucket up to five years.[1]

Ratings agencies have followed the balance sheet upward. Crisil upgraded the company’s long-term rating to AAA with a stable outlook on 9 April 2026 after the MUFG money arrived, and CARE and ICRA also rate it AAA.[11][12][13] AAA means lenders treat Shriram almost as a top-grade borrower, and Crisil says the MUFG link should lower funding costs further. It has already seen the incremental cost of borrowing fall.[11] The company’s cost of liabilities fell from 8.56 percent in June 2026 to 8.41 percent in September 2026, and the new borrowing in the quarter cost 7.3 percent on average.[4] The annual report sets a target of lowering borrowing costs by about 100 basis points over three years.[9]

The MUFG deal itself deserves precise reading. MUFG Bank bought 471,121,055 new shares at ₹840.93, which is 20.02 percent of the company, for about ₹39,618 crore. It gets two board seats and a strategic alliance agreement.[1][14][15][17] The money was fully utilised by the September 2026 quarter, and the monitoring statement reports no deviation from the stated use for the ₹39,617.98 crore.[2] The promoters’ stake fell from 25.38 percent to 20.30 percent only because the share count rose; their holding of about 477.6 million shares was essentially unchanged, and none of it is pledged.[21][22] The filings we read describe a 20 percent stake and a strategic alliance and nothing beyond that, so we do not price in a larger holding.[14][16]

Group structure and corporate actions

The company has changed shape three times, and each change affects how you read the numbers. The first was the 2022 merger of Shriram City Union Finance and Shriram Capital into Shriram Transport, which was renamed Shriram Finance and treated as effective from 1 April 2022. That is why revenue jumps between FY22 and FY23 in Chapter 3.[9] The second was the sale of the housing-finance subsidiary in FY25, which added a one-time gain of about ₹1,490 crore to that year’s profit.[9][10] The third is the MUFG stake of April 2026. A smaller change is in the treasury arm. Shriram Overseas Investments sold its foreign subsidiary in March 2026, received in-principle Reserve Bank approval in April 2026 to start primary-dealer business, and was renamed Shriram Gilts Limited on 15 October 2026.[1]

A reader may remember talk of a Piramal and Shriram merger. The record is old. Headlines on the Economic Times topic page show Piramal’s chairman discussing a combination of the two groups’ finance businesses in 2016, an IDFC and Shriram talks story in 2017, and a 2019 comment that merging Shriram group companies would create value, with Piramal’s stake in the group described as worth about ₹9,000 crore.[20] No Piramal and Shriram combination appears in the Q2 FY27 filings. In the FY26 annual report the only mention of Piramal is a Piramal Foundation education project in the corporate-social-responsibility schedule.[9][1] The latest event runs the other way. On 19 December 2025 Piramal Finance agreed to sell its entire 14.72 percent stake in Shriram Life Insurance, a separate group insurer, to Sanlam for about ₹600 crore, with completion expected by March 2026.[18][19] Sanlam, a South African insurer, also sits in the lender’s promoter group with 0.33 percent.[1] We treat the Piramal link as history and price in no merger.

Today’s ownership is simple. Shriram Capital Private Limited holds 14.27 percent and Shriram Value Services 5.68 percent, so the promoter group holds 20.30 percent. MUFG Bank holds 20.02 percent as a strategic investor and the Government of Singapore 3.75 percent. Foreign investors in total hold 55.65 percent at 30 September 2026.[1][21] Foreign investors can sell quickly, which is one reason the share price moved so sharply on the results.

Where the business is weak

The first weakness is asset quality. The company’s borrowers are self-employed people, small fleet owners and rural households, who are, in CARE’s words, “inherently vulnerable to economic and income shocks”.[12] Gross Stage 3 loans were 4.65 percent at 30 September 2026, against 4.57 percent a year earlier. They rose 19.44 percent in rupees over the year. Gross Stage 2 loans, which are overdue 31 to 90 days and feed future bad loans, rose to 7.04 percent of loans from 6.92 percent.[1] Stage 3 was worst in construction equipment (7.28 percent), farm equipment (5.93 percent) and the small-business book (5.10 percent). The net figure, after provisions, is 2.31 percent, and Crisil’s downgrade trigger is a sustained return on managed assets below 2.5 percent, which is far from today’s 3.95 percent.[1][11]

The second weakness is that the margin is flattered by a one-off. The margin rose 1.33 points in a year, from 8.19 to 9.52 percent, helped by MUFG’s equity, which carries no interest cost, and by cheaper borrowing. As that capital is lent out, the margin falls, and management says so.[4] The company lends at fixed rates and borrows mostly at fixed rates too. At the time of the October 2025 call, 87 percent of its liabilities were fixed and 13 percent floating. A rate rise therefore reaches the margin slowly, but it cannot be passed on to existing borrowers.[8][4] The Reserve Bank of India raised its policy rate by 0.25 points to 5.50 percent on 7 October 2026, a change of stance from neutral to calibrated tightening.[30][31] The chief executive said the incremental cost of borrowing could climb from 8 percent to about 8.35 percent if capital-market rates rise, though the book’s overall cost may hold.[4]

The third weakness is dependence on freight and rural incomes. Demand is strong today. The car makers’ association reports passenger-vehicle sales of 1.36 million units in the September quarter, up 30.7 percent, and management cites commercial-vehicle sales up 28.7 percent to 3.09 lakh units.[32][4] The 2026 southwest monsoon ended at 87 percent of the long-period average, its lowest in more than a decade, and management says stress rose in drought-hit Maharashtra, Karnataka and Telangana.[4] Used-vehicle prices have held up so far, which protects recoveries because the loans are secured, but they are the thing to watch. A fourth, smaller weakness is conduct. In August 2026 Kerala newspapers reported customers who found loans in their names on their credit reports. The company reportedly called it a technical glitch, and management deferred the question on the 23 October call.[38][39][4] We found no regulator action on this and treat it as an open question, not a finding.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 shows revenue, profit, borrowing and cash over five years, then applies the Layer 1 test. Lenders need a different reading from factories, and four standard measures do not carry over. Return on capital employed is replaced by return on equity and return on assets. The debt-to-equity red line is replaced by the capital ratio and the repayment schedule. Interest cover is replaced by the cover that profit before loan losses gives to credit cost. The cash-flow-to-profit test is not applicable. This follows scoring.json, which allows return on equity for financials in the compounder standard, and BOOK_STANDARD section 3, which names the margin, bad-loan ratios and capital ratio as the measures for lenders.[42]

The cash-flow point needs one more sentence. When a lender makes a loan, the cash goes out through operating cash flow. A fast-growing lender therefore always shows large negative operating cash flow even when it is very profitable, so we mark that test N/A and say why instead of reading it as a failure.

The number

Our estimate of the return on equity now is about 13.4 percent a year, close to the company’s own 13.19 percent.

How it was calculated

We multiplied the September 2026 quarter’s profit after tax by four and divided it by the average of shareholders’ funds at 30 June and 30 September 2026.

The inputs

Profit after tax ₹3,677.03 crore, so ₹14,708 crore a year. Net worth ₹108,297 crore at 30 June and ₹110,563 crore at 30 September, an average of ₹109,430 crore. ₹14,708 ÷ ₹109,430 = 13.44 percent.[1]

The range

If profit grows to ₹3,900 crore a quarter while net worth grows by ₹2,500 crore a quarter, the return would be about 13.6 percent in the March 2027 quarter. If credit cost rose a further 40 basis points above our forecast, profit would be about ₹262 crore lower a quarter (see the next estimate) and the return near 12.5 percent.

If the number is off

If the true underlying return is nearer 12 percent than 13.5 percent, the stock at 1.76 times book value pays a high price for a return that is below the 15 percent compounder floor, which is the central valuation risk in Chapter 5.

The number

Each extra 10 basis points of credit cost removes about ₹262 crore of yearly profit, or ₹1.11 a share. Management’s 30 to 40 basis points, if it applied for a full quarter, is ₹196 to ₹262 crore of profit for that quarter.

How it was calculated

We took average total assets in the September 2026 quarter and multiplied by 0.10 percent for each 10 basis points to get the extra yearly loss before tax. We took off tax at the quarter’s rate of 25.44 percent and divided by the share count. A quarter is one quarter of the yearly figure.

The inputs

Total assets of ₹347,169 crore in June and ₹354,532 crore in September, an average of ₹350,850 crore. 0.10 percent of that is ₹351 crore a year before tax. The tax rate is 25.44 percent, from ₹1,255 crore of tax on ₹4,932 crore of profit before tax in the quarter. After tax that is ₹262 crore a year for each 10 basis points, and ₹262 crore ÷ 235.3 crore shares = ₹1.11. So 30 basis points is ₹785 crore a year, or ₹196 crore a quarter, and 40 basis points is ₹1,046 crore a year, or ₹262 crore a quarter.[1]

The range

Over the one or two quarters management describes, 30 to 40 basis points costs ₹196 to ₹262 crore of profit after tax a quarter, ₹392 to ₹523 crore after tax over two quarters, or ₹1.67 to ₹2.22 a share. If the higher cost lasted a full year it would be ₹785 to ₹1,046 crore after tax, or ₹3.34 to ₹4.45 a share. A fall back to the 1.68 percent seen a year ago, 35 basis points lower, would add about ₹3.9 a share a year. How this fits our forecast, on one basis. For September that is ₹1,877 crore × 4 ÷ ₹350,850 crore = 2.14 percent, which is the 2.03 percent management reports; it uses a slightly different denominator. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 attacks our own conclusions. We state the strongest case against each estimate, say what evidence would show we are wrong, and say when that evidence will arrive. The first flaw argues against our rejection and the others argue against the base case.

Flaw 1: the rejection may be too strict

The case against rejecting the stock is strong on paper. The company passed every business test. Test C passes two of four conditions. The 200-day average is still rising. The price is 0.68 ATR from a three-month low and 27.84 percent below its high, and the stock’s own valuation is lower than its history. MUFG paid ₹840.93 a few months ago, 1.0 percent above the close, and 38 of 41 analysts rate it a buy.[41][27][15] A reader who follows the scoring text literally would route the stock to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, with a zone of ₹793.61 to ₹818.35 and a reward-to-risk of 3.56 at the top of it.

We still reject it, for three reasons. The ledger’s rule is a clean one and we do not bend a rule that is working. A stock below its 50- and 200-day averages with a falling 50-day average and no floor is exactly where support levels fail, and the swing low of ₹810.10 was set only days before the ledger date, in the 26 October sell-off. The reward-to-risk of 2.39 at the close passes only if the multiple stays at 14 times. The evidence that would show we were too harsh is a bounce that holds a rising low above ₹810 for two weeks, which Chapter 7 treats as the reopening test. It becomes visible in the November price data.

Flaw 2: credit cost may not be temporary

Our base case assumes credit cost edges up to about 2.1 percent on management’s basis for two quarters and then falls. Management guides 2.3 to 2.4 percent, so our base carries only part of the guided rise, and Chapter 3 shows the rest as a profit effect of ₹0.81 to ₹1.37 a share. The weak point is that this is management’s forecast about a risk it cannot control, the monsoon. Bad loans grew 19.44 percent in rupees over the year while the book grew 16.96 percent. Stage 3 loans in gold rose to 3.12 percent, in construction equipment to 7.28 percent and in the small-business book to 5.10 percent.[1] One brokerage noted that slippages rose about 50 percent over the year, and another that management will miss its credit-cost guidance for the year.[4][27]

The evidence that would prove us wrong is a December-quarter credit cost above 2.5 percent of total assets, gross Stage 3 above 5.0 percent or a second cut to the growth target. Management’s explanation, that gold borrowers are learning to pay monthly interest and that rural stress is seasonal, is plausible, but gold loans are fully secured and still show 3.12 percent in Stage 3, which is why we do not accept it on trust. The results are due in late January to mid-February 2027, and the date is not yet announced.

Flaw 3: margin and return on equity may fall further than we assume

Management says the margin will fall from 9.52 percent to about 9 percent and then 8.5 percent. We assume 9 percent in FY28. If new-vehicle lending grows faster, the average yield falls by 200 to 400 basis points on that part of the book, and the company lends at fixed rates while part of its funding reprices after a rate rise.[4] Citi’s target cut reflects a lower normalised return on equity, and Jefferies expects 13.3 percent for FY27 and 14 percent for FY28, below the 15 percent compounder floor and below the 16.4 percent the company earned in FY26.[25][26]

A margin of 8.5 percent in FY28 instead of 9 percent would cut net interest income by about ₹2,100 crore on our book. After tax that is about ₹1,600 crore, or 10 percent of profit, and about ₹6.8 a share. That is within the range between our base case (₹68.97) and bear case (₹54). The evidence to watch is the margin in the December quarter and the cost of new borrowing, which management puts at 7.3 percent last quarter and up to 8.35 percent if rates keep rising.[4]
