Alicon Castalloy casts aluminium engine parts, mostly cylinder heads, for motorcycles, cars and trucks.[3][6] We reject it for now: sales rose 38 percent in June 2026, but margins are falling, returns on capital are only 10.6 percent, and plant use and spending missed their targets.[1][8][6] The share price also jumped 20 percent in one day on 30 September, and the company says it does not know why.[18][19] Even at ₹870.35 our expected gain is 9.5 percent, below our 12 percent hurdle, and we would need new results before any buy near ₹735 to ₹762.[40][42] We would be wrong if the 12 November 2026 results show a margin of 10 percent or more, or plant use of 85 percent or more[11][19].

1. What the company does

Alicon Castalloy Limited makes aluminium alloy castings. A casting is a metal part formed by pouring molten metal into a hollow mould, called a die, and letting it set. Alicon's best-known part is the cylinder head, the cap on top of an engine where fuel burns. It also makes intake manifolds, engine brackets, crankshafts, and newer parts for electric vehicles such as motor housings and battery enclosures.[6][37] The company is based near Pune, runs four plants (three in India and one in Slovakia), and served 91 customers with more than 800 live parts in FY26 (the year to March 2026).[6][8]

Two casting methods matter. Low-pressure die casting (LPDC) pushes molten aluminium up into the die with air pressure. Gravity die casting (GDC) lets the metal flow in under its own weight. Alicon says it pioneered both in India, and after casting it machines parts to final size: machined parts are 65 to 70 percent of the tonnage it ships.[6][11] The company's own website says it makes 42 percent of India's cylinder heads and that 2.2 million bikes use them. We treat that as a company claim, not a verified market share, because the page is undated.[35]

Who pays Alicon? Vehicle makers and their first-tier suppliers. In FY26, two-wheelers were 42 percent of revenue, passenger cars 35 percent, commercial vehicles 18 percent and non-automotive uses 5 percent.[6] Crisil lists customers such as Hero MotoCorp, Bajaj Auto, Maruti Suzuki, Daimler India Commercial Vehicles, Mahindra, Honda, Toyota Industries Engine India and Ashok Leyland, and says no single customer is a significant share of revenue.[3] Exports and the Slovak plant gave about 20 percent of FY26 revenue and 12 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[3][8]

How does it make money? It sells parts by the piece, priced from the weight of aluminium plus a charge for the work. Aluminium is the biggest cost, about 61 percent of net revenue in the June 2026 quarter, and customers repay it through contract price adjustments.[8][11] That has a strange effect on the numbers. When aluminium prices jump, revenue jumps with them, but profit does not, so margins shrink as a share of sales. In that quarter revenue rose 38 percent. The company says growth after removing the effect of aluminium prices was 17.5 percent (22 percent for the parent company alone). That is a measure of value, not tonnes: standalone tonnage rose only about 1.4 percent.[8][10]

The owners are the Rai family, who hold 53.79 percent through Nastic Trading LLP (41.21 percent) and other holders, with none of it pledged as loan security.[1][21][39] Sumit Bhatnagar became chief executive on 1 April 2026, succeeding Rajeev Sikand, and has named the year's theme 'Reset, Refocus and Rebuild'.[6] In July 2026 the board approved a ₹125.5 crore plant in a leased building near the Shikrapur site, to be paid for with bank loans and the company's own cash.[17]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before finance costs and the accounting charge for wearing-out of machines. It is a rough measure of cash profit from running the plants.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit earned each year on the money tied up in the business, as a percent. Alicon's is 10.6 percent.[1]
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit after tax divided by the number of shares. Alicon's TTM figure is ₹22.36.[1]
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 meter of how fast a price has risen or fallen recently. Above 70 is called hot and below 30 is called weak.
  • ATR (average true range) is the average daily price swing in rupees. Alicon's is ₹41.53, so a normal day moves the price by about 5 percent.[40]
  • LPDC and GDC are low-pressure and gravity die casting, the two ways Alicon fills a metal mould.
  • A limit order is an order to buy only at your price or lower, so it never chases the market.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when customers keep coming back and it earns more than the cost of its money. Alicon has the first part and not yet the second. It has long customer ties and hard-to-copy process skills, but its return on capital is 10.6 percent, which is below the 15 percent we ask of a quality compounder.[1][41] Our reading is a narrow moat, built on engineering approval rather than on pricing power.

What protects the business

A new supplier must pass a customer's approval process for each part, and electric-vehicle parts can take two to three years to develop, and Alicon already holds approved parts across 91 customers.[6][10] The company says it keeps a 100 percent share of the cylinder head for a Japanese maker's hybrid car platform and ramped up cylinder heads for another Japanese maker's 1.5-litre engine.[6] Its website claims 70 percent of Honda's cylinder heads and all of KTM's 390 and 590 cc heads, and lists 72 gravity and 85 low-pressure die casting machines.[35][36] These ties are real but cannot be checked against filed share data.

Crisil rates the company Crisil A with a Stable outlook and calls its market position in aluminium castings established, with strong engineering and long relationships with major vehicle makers.[3] The outlook was cut from Positive in August 2026 because FY26 growth and profit came in below its expectations.[3][4]

What weakens it

Return on capital shows the weakness most clearly. ROCE was 19 percent in FY19, fell to 6 percent in FY21, recovered to 15 percent in FY24, and was 11 percent in FY26.[1] Crisil puts FY26 at 10.7 percent and blames slow ramp-up of new capacity and exports, plus lower profit margins.[3] Depreciation of ₹106 crore in FY26 took more than half of the ₹200 crore operating profit, so every plant that sits half-idle is costly.[1] The mix also moved the wrong way: passenger and commercial vehicles were 60 percent of sales in FY25 and 53 percent in FY26, while two-wheelers rose to 42 percent, and plant use was 75 percent in FY25 against 78 percent in FY26.[7][6]

Pricing power is thin. Aluminium costs are passed on by contract, and management says Indian customers moved to near real-time pass-through from 1 April 2026. But freight, energy and other cost increases are only partly recovered. The CFO told analysts that some customers are still discussing price corrections, with closures expected in the second and third quarters.[10][11] Endurance Technologies, a large listed aluminium die-caster, shows the same effect: its standalone EBITDA margin was 11.2 percent as reported and 13.33 percent excluding the cost pass-through.[27] Alicon's own adjusted margin was 11.4 percent against 9.5 percent reported.[11]

What the industry is doing

Demand is strong. Passenger vehicle sales grew 30.7 percent in July to September 2026, with utility vehicles up 34.2 percent, and two-wheeler sales grew 17.6 percent in the first half of FY27, after 28.4 percent in April.[23][24][26] Part of this is a weak year-ago comparison and the goods and services tax (GST) cut of September 2025, which lifted affordability.[25][23] A year of fast vehicle growth helps volume, but it does not fix the returns problem inside Alicon, because the cost of aluminium and of new plants rises at the same time.

Electric vehicles are a threat and an opening. A cylinder head does not exist in an electric car, but motor housings, e-axle housings and battery enclosures do, and Alicon has won such parts, including an e-axle housing for a German maker.[6][37] Electric vehicles are about 16 percent of the order book, hybrids about 12 percent, and the rest is petrol, diesel and two-wheeler work.[10] Management says slow electric adoption and late start dates, such as at JLR, held back the old order book.[10]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 compares static levels, the numbers as they stand today, with trajectory, the direction they are moving. The tests use the Weinstein stage scale, where Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend. Two ratios decide the reward side: asymmetry means how much we could gain compared with how much we could lose, and reward-to-risk is the same idea written as gain divided by loss. The debt-to-equity ratio is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. A name must clear five hard red lines first: debt-to-equity, interest cover, promoter pledge, three-year cash conversion and a Stage 4 breakdown.[41] Then it must fit one of three archetypes. A compounder needs a return on capital of 15 percent or more. A turnaround needs two improving signals and a price that has not run ahead of them. A tactical breakout is a move of the price above a flat trading range, and it needs a Stage 2 leader with a dated catalyst.[41]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate rests on assumptions that can be wrong, and the honest test is to name the weakest one and say when we will know. We also give the strongest case against our own verdict (a red-team view), then the single claim that decides the outcome.[42]

Weakest assumption: the margin recovery

Our FY27 and FY28 EPS rest on an EBITDA margin of 10.0 and 10.4 percent, against 9.5 percent reported in Q1 FY27 and 9.3 percent in Q4 FY26.[8][42] The path needs customers to agree price recoveries in Q2 and Q3, one-time costs (₹15 crore in Q4 FY26) not to repeat, and the plant to run fuller.[13][10] One point of margin is worth ₹10.1 of EPS and about ₹240 of price at 24 times, so a margin that stays at 9.5 percent takes FY27 EPS to about ₹30.9 and FY28 EPS to about ₹30.6, which is 24 percent below our central ₹40.3. At 24 times that is about ₹735, which is 3.6 percent below ₹762, so a margin stuck at 9.5 percent leaves the reference zone with no positive reward-to-risk.[42]

Red-team bear case: the gap was trading, not news

The strongest case against us is that the 20 percent jump on 30 September was not information. The volume was 14.8 times normal, the company said it knows no reason, and later days show thin volume of 9,000 to 131,000 shares.[18][1] If the gap was a trading event, the stock can return to the ₹725 to ₹745 area where it traded before, and Q2 results that show margins stuck near 9.5 percent put FY28 EPS at about ₹30.6, or about ₹735 at 24 times. Reaching ₹600 would need a multiple of about 19.6 times. Reaching ₹513 needs lower sales (₹2,150 crore) as well. At the three-year low P/E of 18.3 times, the bear EPS of ₹21.4 gives about ₹391, which is 55 percent below today's price, so ₹513 is not a floor. We keep 24 times in the bear case to isolate the effect of earnings.[18][42][22] That is why the verdict is a rejection now, and why any later order that is not yet filled lapses if the price closes below ₹702.05.

Reported margin versus adjusted margin

The adjusted margin of 11.4 percent is management's own number and is not audited. If aluminium prices fall, revenue falls and the reported margin rises without any change in profit; if aluminium rises again, the reverse happens. Volume, order book and tonnage

Standalone tonnage rose by 124 tonnes, to 9,124, in Q1 FY27 even though management speaks of 22 percent underlying growth for the parent (17.5 percent for the group). Both are value measures after removing aluminium prices, so the growth is in value added and machined content, not in metal shipped.[11] That may be good (higher-value parts) but is harder to verify. The order book is quoted at ₹7,600 crore (March), ₹8,094 crore (deck) and ₹8,450 crore (call), and was ₹9,100 crore a year earlier.[6][8][10][16] Crisil says conversion is slower than it expected.[3]

Debt, capex and the interest-cover line

The Shikrapur plant costs ₹125.5 crore and is to be paid for with loans and internal cash, on top of other capex of about ₹150 crore this year.[17][10] Borrowings may rise toward ₹400 crore.
