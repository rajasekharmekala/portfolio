1. What the company does

Sanghvi Movers owns cranes and rents them out, with drivers and technicians, to companies that need to lift something too heavy or too awkward to move any other way. A wind-turbine tower section, a refinery reactor and a steel-mill furnace shell are typical loads. The fleet runs from 20-tonne to 1,600-tonne lifting capacity, and the company calls itself the largest crane rental firm in India and Asia and among the five largest in the world.[5][23] Think of it as a taxi service for very large machines: the customer pays only while the machine is on the job, and the owner earns its return from keeping each machine working as many months of the year as possible.

Two numbers explain the economics. The first is utilisation, the share of the fleet that is out earning rent; it was 79.02 percent for the year to March 2026 and about 86 percent in the June 2026 quarter in India and Botswana.[5][15] The second is yield, the monthly rent as a share of what the crane cost. A yield of 2.12 percent a month, the FY26 figure, means each ₹100 of crane value earned about ₹2.12 of rent every month, or roughly ₹25 a year before running costs.[5] Rent in Saudi Arabia is much higher: the Saudi fleet earned a yield of 4.10 percent a month in the June 2026 quarter, against 2.29 percent in India.[15]

Who pays? Mostly large industrial and infrastructure projects. ICRA, the rating agency, estimated that wind energy supplied about 53 percent of group revenue in FY25 when crane rental and wind construction are added together, with the rest coming from thermal and nuclear power, refineries, steel, cement and general infrastructure.[29] India installed a record 6,057 megawatts of wind capacity in FY26, up from 4,151 in FY25 and 3,253 in FY24, and each turbine needs a heavy lift.[34] Customers place orders before the work starts. The company reported an order book of ₹1,253 crore at 24 July 2026, up 19 percent from ₹1,053 crore ten weeks earlier, and an enquiry pipeline of about ₹5,600 crore.[14][16]

The group now has three engines. Crane rental is the core: ₹693 crore of revenue in FY26, 65 percent of the total, and about 60 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[5][15] Sangreen Future Renewables, a wholly owned subsidiary carved out in 2024, builds and installs wind farms for owners who supply the turbines. It earned ₹333 crore in FY26 (31 percent of revenue), needs little equipment of its own, and keeps a lower margin: management expects 12 to 15 percent.[5][16][19] A small project engineering unit made ₹45 crore. A Saudi subsidiary started its first year of operation in FY26 and had added 36 cranes by March 2026, and the company has also won work in Botswana and Qatar.[5][14][22]

Owning cranes is capital hungry. The gross block, meaning the cost of the equipment, is about ₹3,300 crore, annual depreciation was ₹131.7 crore, and the company plans ₹652 crore of new spending in FY27.[15][2] The founding Sanghvi family, through the promoter group, owns 47.25 percent of the shares, and none of those shares is pledged to lenders.[31] History matters here: the company lost money in each of FY18 to FY21 and then earned ₹188 crore in FY24 when demand and rents recovered.[36] A business with this profile can look excellent at a cycle peak and poor at a trough, which is why this report spends so much time on rents, collections and the price of the shares.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A crane-rental business is strong when four things hold at once: customers keep the fleet busy, the rent covers the cost of the machine, customers pay on time, and the owner does not borrow so much that a slow year threatens it. This chapter tests the first two and part of the third and fourth with evidence; chapter 2B scores them one by one, and chapter 3 checks the accounts.

What protects the business

The first protection is scale in a field where the machines are expensive. ICRA notes that the high cost of high-capacity cranes keeps smaller players out of the largest lifts, and that the company's 17 depots, 13 of them operating when the agency last counted in 2024, cut the time and cost of moving a crane to a site.[30] The company now lists 15 depots across India and 175 acres of freehold land for parking cranes.[23] The investor presentation puts the share of cranes above 100 tonnes at 80 percent, the segment where a customer has few alternatives.[23]

The second protection is repeat business. The annual report says 40 to 45 percent of revenue comes from repeat customers, and about half of each year's revenue is already booked when the year begins; the opening order book for FY26 was ₹820 crore.[6] The June 2026 order book of ₹1,253 crore is described by management as fully executable inside FY27.[16] Order books of this kind give a rental company warning of slow quarters, though not immunity from them.

The third is credit quality. ICRA rates the company [ICRA]A+ (Stable) for bank facilities of ₹1,179.99 crore, and in September 2025 it reaffirmed the rating after the debt limits were raised.[28][29] Three details in the rating rationale matter: the agency calls the capital structure comfortable, it measured interest cover at 12.8 times in FY25, and its stated trigger for a downgrade is total debt above 2.5 times operating profit on a sustained basis.[29] On our arithmetic from the FY26 accounts, total debt is about 1.6 times EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, the profit from running the business before financing and accounting charges), so there is room, but the capex plan will use some of it.[1][2]

What makes the business fragile

The first weakness is that rent is flat across customers and competition is real. Management said in May 2025 that yield is the same across industries, and it blamed a prolonged monsoon, an election-year slowdown in government and private spending, and fragmented competition for pulling FY25 utilisation down to 73 percent from 84 percent and yield down to 2.05 percent from 2.2 percent.[21][22] ICRA says the company still faces competition from smaller and mid-sized players that limits its pricing freedom.[29] The 2025-26 annual report lists competitive intensity first among the threats.[8] The recovery in FY26 and the June 2026 quarter shows the pressure eased for now, but the pressure has returned in the past.

The second weakness is concentration in one end market. ICRA puts the wind share of revenue at about 53 percent in FY25 and 52 percent in FY24, against 36 percent in FY21 and 70 percent in FY17.[29][30] The group has therefore bet more on wind than at almost any point in the last decade, at the moment India's wind installations are at a record. That is a tailwind today. It also means a slowdown in turbine installations would hit both crane rent and construction fees at once.

The third weakness is the return on the money invested. The agency recorded a return on capital of about 23 percent in FY24 and 16.5 percent for the core business in FY25, while the company's own ratio table shows 13.43 percent for FY26 on the standalone accounts and the Screener page shows 15.6 percent on the group accounts.[30][29][7][35] The company is guiding to 16.25 to 16.5 percent for FY27, which would still be well below the FY24 level.[15] In plain words, the group is spending a great deal on cranes and construction capacity and earning less on each rupee than it did two years ago.

The fourth weakness is the newest business line. Saudi Arabia earns a much higher rent, but its debtors take about 201 days to pay, its overall margin was 23 percent in the June 2026 quarter (management also quotes about 47 percent for the rental activity alone, before shared costs), and about ₹324 crore of the ₹652 crore FY27 capex is planned there while West Asia is in conflict.[15][16] Management says supply of equipment is facing a temporary disruption and expects the new Saudi cranes to start earning between the third and fourth quarters of FY27.[16] If that slips, ₹324 crore sits idle on the balance sheet.

Construction fees carry their own risk. ICRA points out that the construction orders have no price-escalation clause, so a jump in costs falls on the company, and that revenue follows the stage of completion, which makes it lumpy.[29] Management in turn warns that 10 to 15 percent of the order book can slip into the next year because customer sites or equipment are not ready.[8][17]

Governance and disclosure

On the points that decide whether a promoter-run company is safe to hold, the record is clean but not spotless. The promoter group holds 47.25 percent, the June 2026 shareholding filing states that no promoter share is pledged, and the auditors issued an unmodified opinion on the FY26 accounts.[31][27] Two events in the last six weeks deserve notice. One independent director, Mr. Amitabha Mukhopadhyay, resigned on 7 September 2026, and the filing gives 'other professional commitments' in the cover letter and 'personal reasons' in the annexure.[32] On 6 October 2026 the promoter group moved 1,05,53,614 shares between two promoters as a gift, and the filing says the combined holding did not change.[36][42] Neither event changes the investment case, but a resignation from a board committee in the weeks before a results date is the sort of thing to watch.

Tax disputes are small beside the balance sheet. Goods and services tax demands rose to ₹5.53 crore from ₹1.15 crore, and the long-running sales-tax claims for FY08 to FY10 were decided in the company's favour, with the Supreme Court's January 2024 ruling cited in support.[10] The filings we read disclose no regulatory order and no qualified audit opinion. The standalone accounts also show inter-corporate loans to the company's own subsidiaries (₹25.4 crore net in FY25), which is normal for a group in build-out but means the standalone and group figures should be read side by side.[4]

What this means for an owner

An owner today holds a market leader with real operating momentum, a good credit rating and no pledged shares, in a business whose profit per rupee of capital has slipped and whose collections are slow. The strength is in the fleet and the order book. The weakness shows up in the receivables and in how little of the group's growth now comes from high-margin rent. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means comparing what management promised a year or two ago with what happened. We took the outlook given on the May 2025 call, when FY26 was about to begin, and the margin and working-capital promises of February 2026, and set each beside the filed result.[21][20] The record is good on growth and on the fleet, mixed on capex and on construction growth, and only partly kept on the two promises most relevant to today's share price: margins and collections. The core crane margin beat its target, but the group margin has not improved in FY27 and debtor days are back at 116.

On growth, management beat its own forecast by a wide margin. It said group revenue would grow 25 to 30 percent in FY26; it grew 36.9 percent to ₹1,070 crore.[21][18] It forecast crane revenue up 10 to 15 percent; the segment grew from ₹515 crore to ₹693 crore, about 35 percent.[22][5] It forecast utilisation of 75 to 80 percent at a yield of about 2 percent; it delivered 79.02 percent and 2.12 percent.[5]

On margins, the promise was to improve them. The February 2026 call said the company expected margin normalisation as the second half progressed and further improvement in FY27.[20] The second half did improve, from a 38.6 percent EBITDA margin in the December quarter to 40.6 percent in the March quarter, by our calculation from the filed profit and loss. The June 2026 quarter then fell to 36.6 percent.[23][2][12] Management explains the fall with four items, and we list them in the next table. The explanation may be right, but the promise of improvement in FY27 has not been met in its first quarter.

On collections, the February 2026 call said receivables had slowed because of billing dates and that there was no structural stress.[20] Debtor days did fall to 100 in December, but they were back at 116 in June, and the provision for expected credit losses was a visible part of the margin fall.[24][15] That is the weakest part of the record.

Management gives four reasons for the margin drop in the core crane business, from 53 percent in FY26 to 47 percent in the June 2026 quarter: about two points from a higher provision for bad debts (₹6.2 crore), one point from a non-cash currency revaluation of a foreign-currency loan (₹1.4 crore), one point from a one-off staff bonus for passing ₹1,000 crore of revenue, and about two points from taking on more equipment-hire and gross-rental work that earns a lower margin but uses no new capital.[15] Excluding the currency and bonus items, it puts the underlying margin near 49 percent, rising toward 51 percent if the bad-debt provision eases.[15] We accept the explanation as plausible. It is also exactly what a company would say if margins were falling for lasting reasons, so the September-quarter figures, due by 14 November 2026, are the test.

Fleet and capex are the third thing to track, because the cranes are bought before the rent arrives. Management reported a fleet of 370 cranes at a cost of ₹2,680 crore at March 2025 and ₹235 crore of capex in FY25.[22] It spent ₹474 crore in FY26, ₹373 crore in India and ₹101 crore in Saudi Arabia. That is management's figure for capex incurred in the year; the consolidated cash-flow statement shows ₹408 crore actually paid for equipment, which is the figure used in chapter 3. The ₹66 crore gap is not itemised in the statements we read, and may be equipment bought on credit or still to be paid, so we use the cash figure for cash left after equipment and the management figure for fleet growth. By mid-2026 it put the gross block, the cost of all equipment, at about ₹3,300 crore, which is a rise of roughly 23 percent in 15 months.[5][15] The plan for FY27 is ₹652 crore, of which ₹92 crore was spent in the June quarter and ₹324 crore is for Saudi Arabia and the Middle East.[15][16] That is more than twice the ₹278 crore of cash the group made from operations in FY26, so borrowing has to rise, and management has guided to gross debt of up to 0.72 times equity.[3][15] Management expects about 15 percent more revenue from the new cranes within FY27, and says the new Indian cranes are underwritten at 80 percent utilisation and a 2 percent yield.[15][16] Fleet counts differ across the company's documents, from 485 or more cranes to over 500 including Saudi Arabia to over 550, so we track the cost of the fleet and not the count.[23][26][6]

What this means for an owner: the operating promises that depend on cranes and customers have been kept, and those that depend on collections and cost control have not yet been. That pattern supports keeping the position while refusing to add to it until the next results show the receivable days coming down.

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on the future. A reverse discounted-cash-flow model works backwards from today's price and asks what yearly growth in profit, sustained for ten years, would justify it. The ledger solves this with a 12 percent discount rate, meaning future profit is worth less than profit today at that rate, and a terminal value of 20 times profit at the end of year ten. On those settings the price of ₹344.95 implies profit growth of only 2.85 percent a year from a starting profit of ₹199.4 crore, the trailing twelve months.[43] In plain words, the market is pricing the company as if the current profit is close to its peak. The model starts from accounting profit, not from FCF, which is short for free cash flow — the cash left after running the business and buying equipment — and the table below reports that cash figure as not available from the ledger.

The second input to expectations is what analysts forecast, and here there is nothing to read. The ledger records no consensus coverage, so no revision trend, forecast or price target exists. That has two effects. It means no outside forecast anchors the price, so moves can be larger in both directions. It also means this report's earnings estimate is the only forward estimate in the file, and chapter 6 explains why it should be held loosely.[43]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in the previous chapter rests on an assumption that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, gives the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, picks the single claim the thesis hangs on, and sets out when each question will be answered.

The weakest assumption behind each estimate

Earnings per share of ₹25.7. The weakest assumption is that EBITDA reaches at least ₹525 crore. Management has guided to it, but margins are the area where management's record is weakest, and the guidance has to be met in a year where the first quarter produced ₹139 crore and the next quarter is seasonally weak.[15][20] Evidence that this is wrong would be a September-quarter EBITDA below ₹105 crore, less than 20 percent above last year's ₹88 crore.

Target of ₹475. The weakest assumption is the multiple of 18.5 times. It is a haircut to a three-year median that was struck when most revenue was high-margin rent. If the market decides that a third of revenue earning 12 to 15 percent deserves the multiple of a contractor, the right multiple is lower than 18.5, and the first evidence would be a share price that stays below ₹400 after good results.[37][16]

Probability weights of 20, 45 and 35 percent. The weakest assumption is that the bear case is only 35 percent likely. The stock is already falling faster than a weak market, which can mean informed selling. If the next results show debtor days above 120, we would move the bear weight to 50 percent, and the weighted value would fall to about ₹405.[43]

The red-team bear case

The strongest honest argument against holding the shares runs as follows. Profit has not grown for two years: ₹188 crore in FY24 and ₹184 crore in FY26 while revenue rose 79 percent, because the added revenue is low-margin construction work with no price-escalation clause, which ICRA flags as a cost risk.[36][35][29] The profit is also less cash-rich than it looks. Receivables and unbilled work rose ₹117 crore in FY26, equal to 63 percent of the year's profit; unbilled work more than doubled; the provision for bad debts rose 41 percent; cash left after buying equipment was negative ₹129 crore; and borrowing rose 54 percent.[1][9][3] The credit-risk signs sit in the Saudi business, which is the destination for half the new capex, and 201 days of debtors there were recorded while the region is at war.[15][16]

Next, the economics are thin at the margin. The company borrows at 8.12 percent and earns about 15.6 percent on capital; depreciation was ₹132 crore in FY26, is already running at about ₹154 crore a year (₹38.59 crore in the June 2026 quarter) and is modelled at ₹161 crore for FY27. It rises with the ₹652 crore capex whatever utilisation does, so if utilisation fell back to FY25's 73 percent from 86 percent, profit would drop faster than revenue.[18][2][21] Management said in February 2026 that margins would normalise and working capital would ease; the first quarter of FY27 went the other way.[20][15]

Finally, the chart and the corporate record both carry warnings. The shares fell 19.7 percent in two weeks with no company filing found through 8 October (filings from 9 to 16 October were not available to us), below every moving average, on six heavy-volume down days; a director resigned in September; and the promoter group moved about 12.2 percent of the company's shares from one member to another in October.[43][42][32][36] None of these is conclusive. All of them are reasons not to add before the September results.

The hinge claim

The single claim on which the thesis hangs is this: the rise in unpaid bills is a timing effect that reverses, not a loss that builds. If group debtor days fall back toward 100, as they did in December 2025, and the provision for bad debts stops rising, then reported profit is real, the ₹652 crore capex is funded, and the stock at 13 to 14 times forward earnings is cheap.[24][15] If debtor days stay above 120, which is the top of management's own 110 to 120 range, a growing share of profit sits in other people's accounts, and the cheapness of the shares is an artefact of an accounting number.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is KEEP_COMPOUNDING, used here in its narrow meaning of hold the shares you own and do not add. The four holding verdicts available are keep, accumulate on a dip, trim and replace, and none fits perfectly. Keep is the closest, because each of the other three requires a condition that is absent. Replace needs a close below the stop, a Stage 4 breakdown, two failed operating measures or a broken thesis; the close of ₹344.95 is above the stop of ₹317, only one of four measures failed, and no red line is breached. Trim needs an extended price, an RSI above 75, a bull target reached or a weight above the hard cap of 15 percent; the stock is oversold, down 24 percent from your cost, and at 6.71 percent of net worth.[43][44]

The verdict cannot be accumulate on a dip, even though the ledger maps the distance to support of 0.31 ATR to the FULL_SIZE band, the closest band to support. Three facts block it. The Layer 1 gate is partial, because the technical floor is not met. The momentum gate says NO_MOMENTUM and the RSI of 24.5 is below the 35 that Test C needs. And the support level of ₹338.45 is today's low, which the price has touched but not held. The ladder tells you where to place a limit order, an order that fills only at your price or better and does not chase the market, once a floor is shown. It does not by itself justify buying into a fall.[43] The weight also matters: 890 shares are already 6.71 percent of net worth, just above the 6.5 percent upper limit of the TIER_2 band, so an addition would need the conviction tier to be raised first.

The reason to hold, not sell, is the evidence in chapters 2B and 5: the rental business is busy, rents are rising, the order book equals 84 to 90 percent of full-year guided revenue, and the shares are priced for almost no growth. The reason not to add is that the same chapters show the unpaid-bill problem has not been solved. A holder with a 24 percent loss is tempted to either average down or give up; the rule here is the same as for any other position. Hold the size, define the exit in rupees, and let the September results decide.

Entry zone. For any new money, whether yours or a new buyer's, the zone is ₹335 to ₹352, which is the range from just under the swing low to about the session high of ₹352.75. No order is placed in that zone until the retest sequence in chapter 4 appears; a retest is a pullback to a level the price has just left, to see whether it holds, and here it means an RSI back above 35 and a higher low held above ₹338.45. A limit order is the only order type we would use. Stop. The stop is ₹317, one ATR below the swing low, which is what the Core rule specifies; it is judged on a closing basis so that one intraday dip does not end the position. Targets. ₹475 is the base target, ₹655 the bull target and ₹280 the bear value, with the working in chapter 5.[43]
