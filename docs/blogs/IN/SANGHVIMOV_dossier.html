1. What the company does

Sanghvi Movers Limited, based in Pune, rents cranes. It was founded in 1989 and lists on the NSE under the symbol SANGHVIMOV.[1][3] The cranes range from 20 tonnes to 1,000 tonnes of lifting capacity, and most are imported from Germany, the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, China and Singapore.[1][8] At 24 July 2026 the group owned 492 cranes with a purchase cost (gross block) of about ₹3,300 crore, of which 49 cranes sit in Saudi Arabia and Qatar.[3] A customer who needs a 600-tonne crane for a few weeks or months does not buy one. It pays Sanghvi a daily or monthly rent, and Sanghvi also supplies the trailers, operators and lifting plan.[3]

The money is made on two dials. The first is utilisation, the share of the year a crane is out on rent and earning. The second is yield, which management describes as the return earned on the asset each month.[3][4] In the 2026 financial year (FY26, April 2025 to March 2026) India utilisation was 79% and the yield was 2.12% a month. In the June 2026 quarter they were 86% and 2.29%.[3][5] The cost side is fixed once the crane is bought. Depreciation was ₹132 crore in FY26 and interest was ₹37 crore, so every extra rented day adds profit at a high rate, and every idle day costs the same.[1] The profit measure used in this book is EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), which is profit before financing and accounting charges and is the figure the company guides on.[3]

That fixed cost is why the business is capital hungry. The company spent capital expenditure (cash spent on long-term plant and equipment, called capex) of ₹474 crore in FY26 and has a pool of ₹652 crore approved for FY27.[5][3] Of the FY27 pool, ₹327 crore is for India and ₹324 crore is for Saudi Arabia.[3] Cash from operations did not cover that spending in either of the last two years, so free cash flow (the cash left after running the business and paying for new cranes, also written FCF) was negative and debt rose.[1][7]

Over the last two years the company added two businesses beside the rental core. Sangreen Future Renewables, a subsidiary, builds the foundations, roads and crane platforms for wind farms and installs the turbines, using customers' turbines and its own cranes.[3][4] Management calls it asset-light. It needs little capital for equipment, although customers still pay it after 98 days on average, and it earns a thinner margin: 14% in the June 2026 quarter, against 47% for core crane rental in India.[3][4] Management calls its work engineering and construction (E&C) and says it is not EPC, because the turbine itself is supplied free by the customer.[4]

Why margin and growth now pull in different directions

Sales have roughly tripled since FY22, from ₹372 crore of total income to ₹1,100 crore, but EBITDA margin has gone from 47% in FY22 to 63% in FY24 and down to 39% in FY26.[3] EBITDA fell as a share of sales because the low-margin E&C business grew from almost nothing in FY24 to a third of revenue.[3][6] The group margin was never supposed to stay at 60%. The useful question is whether the rental margin itself is holding, and the first quarter of FY27 raises doubt: core rental EBITDA margin was 47%, down from 53% in FY26.[4]

Management gave four reasons: a ₹6.2 crore provision for receivables that are aging, a ₹1.4 crore non-cash charge on a foreign currency loan, one-off staff incentives for passing ₹1,000 crore of sales, and a change in mix toward rented-in equipment that earns less per rupee but needs no capital.[4] Excluding the currency and incentive items, the underlying core margin was about 49%, and management expects it to rise toward 51% if the provisions fall.[4] That is a forecast. The test comes with the results for the September 2026 quarter, which the company has not yet scheduled.[21]

What the company says it will earn

In its July 2026 presentation the company repeated its guidance (its own published forecast) for FY27: revenue of ₹1,400 to ₹1,500 crore, EBITDA of ₹525 to ₹575 crore, a blended ROCE (return on capital employed, the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses) of 16.25% to 16.5%, and a gross debt-to-equity — total borrowings divided by shareholder equity — ratio (borrowings divided by shareholders' money) ceiling of 0.72.[3][4] For FY28 it points to EBITDA of ₹650 to ₹700 crore.[3] The order book on 24 July 2026 was ₹1,253 crore, all due for execution in FY27, plus an enquiry pipeline of ₹5,641 crore.[3][9] A sceptic should note two things. Most of the capex that supports the second-half jump has not been spent yet (₹560 crore of ₹652 crore), and about 15% of the E&C order book may slip into the next year by management's own warning.[3][4]

Where the earnings go matters for owners. Profit after tax per share, the company's EPS (earnings per share), was ₹23.03 over the last twelve months, and a dividend of ₹2 a share was paid with a record date of 14 August 2026.[1][19] That is a payout of about 9% of FY26 consolidated profit, because the company keeps cash for cranes.[6][1] Promoters hold 47.25% of the shares and none of it is pledged to lenders.[13]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Utilisation: the share of time cranes are on rent. It is a company-reported figure for India and Botswana, and separately for Saudi Arabia and Qatar.[3]
  • Yield: the monthly return earned on the crane fleet, which the company reports as a percentage per month. It is not the same as margin.[4]
  • Order book: signed work not yet billed. For this company it is mainly wind construction work and shorter crane hire jobs.[3]
  • DSO (days sales outstanding): how many days of sales are still unpaid. Group DSO was 116 days in the June 2026 quarter.[4]
  • ECL (expected credit loss): a provision the company books today for customers who may pay late or not at all.[4]
  • E&C: engineering and construction, the company's wind-farm civil and installation work through Sangreen.[4]
  • GCC: the Gulf Cooperation Council region. The company's Saudi Arabia and Qatar units are reported together as GCC.[3]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A crane rental company wins on three things: owning the right cranes in the right place, keeping them busy, and being paid on time. On the first, Sanghvi is clearly the largest in India and in Asia. Its credit rater, ICRA, says the cost of high-capacity cranes is an entry barrier for smaller players, and that the company holds a dominant position in the segment.[8] The company also says it ranked third among the world's crane renters in the 2026 industry table from International Cranes, behind Mammoet and Sarens, a ranking based on total lifting capacity.[4] We treat the ranking as a company statement, because we could not open the industry table itself.

On keeping cranes busy, the record is mixed but improving. Utilisation was 84% in FY24, fell to 73% in FY25, recovered to 79% in FY26 and was 86% in the June 2026 quarter.[3] Management blamed the FY25 dip on a long monsoon, a slowdown in government and private capex during the general election, and more competition from smaller operators.[6] That list tells us the demand cycle is real and competitors do take work at the margin. ICRA says smaller and mid-size players still limit the company's ability to set prices.[8] Management argues the opposite, that yields in India are not capped by competition, and points to a yield that rose to 2.29% a month in the June 2026 quarter.[4][3] Over five years the yield has stayed between 1.71% and 2.29%, which is a narrow range for a business with a dominant position.[3]

On being paid, the picture is weaker. Group days sales outstanding were 116 days in the June 2026 quarter, with 124 days for crane rental and 201 days for the Saudi and Qatar units.[4] In May 2025 management said the average would stay between 110 and 120 days.[6] The group number sits inside that range, but crane rental sits above it, and a ₹6.2 crore provision in one quarter shows that older bills are aging.[4] In plain terms, the company is a good operator whose customers pay slowly, and slow payment is the cost of selling to large industrial and wind projects.

Returns on capital show the same pattern. The company's own ROCE was 6% in FY22, 16% in FY23, 21% in FY24, 17% in FY25 and 15% in FY26.[3] The consolidated figure printed by Screener, a data service, is 15.6% today.[1] ICRA says it would consider an upgrade only if core ROCE stays above 20% with strong debt cover, and a note on our holdings file credits the company with ROCE above 18%.[8][31] The filings support that claim only for FY24. For FY26 the number is 15%, so we do not count a high-return moat in the underwriting.

What the wind and Saudi bets do to the moat

Wind is both the biggest demand driver and the biggest concentration. ICRA estimated that wind work, through both rental and E&C, made up about 53% of FY25 revenue and 83% of the order book in April 2025.[8] India added a record 6.05 GW of wind capacity in FY26, up 46% on the 4.15 GW added the year before, according to the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy.[33][28] The Ministry is reported to expect 7.5 to 8 GW in FY27.[29] April to August 2026 added 2.42 GW, so the FY27 target needs a faster second half, which is the same shape as the company's own FY27 plan.[29] The company's own presentation flags that the transmission-charge waiver for wind projects applies only to projects commissioned by June 2028, a timeline risk.[3]

Saudi Arabia is the growth option with the most uncertainty. The Saudi unit now earns a yield of 4.10% a month on 86% utilisation, almost twice the India yield, and it turned cumulatively EBITDA positive in its first year.[3][4] But the same unit has a return on capital of minus 6%, a 23% EBITDA margin against 47% in India, and customers who take 201 days to pay.[3] On 22 October 2026 reports described attacks on targets in Saudi Arabia, and Brent crude traded above $110 a barrel.[26][27] Management says it sees a temporary supply-chain disruption and has placed all of the ₹324 crore of Saudi capex orders with manufacturers, who must deliver the cranes.[4] A moat built on cranes that cannot reach the customer does not earn money, so this is the first thing we watch.

Balance sheet strength as a competitive asset

Strong credit is a real advantage in this trade, because a renter who can fund new cranes wins the long contracts. ICRA rated the company [ICRA]A+ with a stable outlook in September 2025 and the company repeated that rating in July 2026.[8][3] Gross debt was ₹673.6 crore against equity of ₹1,310.4 crore at 31 March 2026, a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.51.[7] Management quotes 0.54 for June 2026 and guides a ceiling of 0.72 for the year, with treasury of more than ₹300 crore.[4] Management's own measure, net debt (borrowings less cash and linked deposits) to equity, was 0.47 at March 2026.[5] The average borrowing cost was 8.12% a year in FY26, and the company says new rupee loans cost about 8%, while loans in dollars cost the benchmark rate plus 5.5% to 6%.[5][4] Rising rates would raise the cost of the FY27 plan. India Today reported on 22 October that some members of the Reserve Bank's rate panel saw a need for further rate hikes.[26]

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A rental business is judged on a few operating numbers that sit between the filings and the share price. We scored five for Sanghvi Movers, and for each one we say why it was picked. The score is 1.0 for PASS, 0.5 for WATCH and 0 for FAIL. A company qualifies when the total is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 and at most one KPI fails. Each rule is set before we look at the reading, and the rules are written into the table so you can check them.

The five are: fleet utilisation and yield, because rent is fleet size times those two; order-book cover, because it shows how much of the year is already sold; receivable days, because a crane company is paid after the work; core rental margin, because it is the profit engine; and return on capital with debt headroom, because the business must keep buying cranes. We do not use a book-to-bill ratio (new orders divided by revenue) of 2.0 times, the usual rule for capital goods makers, because a crane job lasts weeks or months and the order book is rebuilt each year. We use cover of the next year's guided revenue instead.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A forecast is only useful if the people who make it hit it. We went back to the call held on 22 May 2025, when management told investors what FY26 would look like, and compared each promise with the reported result a year later.[6] We also checked what was said in May 2026 and whether the first quarter of FY27 delivered it.[5][4] The record is better than average, with one clear miss and one slip in cost control.

Management beat its sales promises. It said group revenue would grow 25% to 30% and crane revenue 10% to 15%, and it delivered 36.9% and about 35%.[6][5][3] It said core crane margin would be 50% to 52% and utilisation 75% to 80%, and it delivered about 53% and 79%.[6][4][5] It said the Saudi unit would reach cumulative EBITDA break-even by the end of the first half of FY27, and it reported that milestone in the June 2026 quarter, earlier than promised.[5][4]

The miss is the renewables business. In May 2025 management said it would double E&C revenue in FY26 from ₹229 crore.[6] The segment table in the July 2026 presentation adds up to ₹332 crore for FY26, an increase of 45%.[3] Management later said the business had almost doubled. The segment table does not show doubling, though the claim may include a logistics subsidiary that the table does not separate.[5] The cost-control slip is capex. The board approved a plan of ₹246 crore for India in May 2025, and the company spent ₹373 crore in India plus ₹101 crore in Saudi Arabia, after the board raised the FY26 plan to ₹639 crore.[6][5][32] Spending more than first planned is fine when returns are good, but it means the capex figure in any single call is a floor, not a ceiling.

This chapter does that for the three estimates we made, then gives the strongest case against owning the shares, and finally names the one claim on which the whole verdict hinges. The key dates are the September 2026 quarter results, which the company has not scheduled but which in previous years came on 7 November 2025, 14 November 2024 and 7 November 2023.[21]

Cash conversion: the weakest assumption is that receivables will not keep growing

The 1.47 times ratio rests on three years in which cash from operations was larger than profit. But consolidated receivables grew by ₹12,418 lakh in FY26 and by ₹13,286 lakh in FY25, and the company says its crane rental receivable days are 124.[7][4] A ratio above 1 can also be helped by payables, which grew ₹48.6 crore in FY26.[7] The evidence that would show us wrong is the cash flow statement for the half year to September 2026. The company files it with its results, because the house scoring rules note that SEBI requires cash flow statements only at the half-year and the year-end. If cash from operations is under 0.70 of profit for that half, the compounder standard fails.

The reverse DCF: the weakest assumption is that profit stands in for cash

The 2.2% implied growth treats each rupee of profit as if it could be paid out. Over FY25 and FY26 the group spent ₹644 crore on plant and equipment against cash from operations of ₹440 crore, so it could not.[7] The number is therefore a floor for what the price assumes. It is a floor, not a forecast, and a reader should treat it as a way to measure how little the price asks for. The fixed 12% discount rate and 20 times terminal multiple are house settings, and a different pair would give a different answer. The evidence that would show this estimate misleading is a free cash flow turning positive: if capex falls below cash from operations in FY27 or FY28, the profit proxy becomes fairer. Management's plan points the other way, with ₹652 crore of capex in FY27.[3]

The earnings model: the weakest assumption is that second-half capex arrives on time

Our base FY27 EBITDA of ₹510 crore already sits below the company's range. But it still needs a strong second half. The June quarter gave ₹139 crore, so the remaining three quarters must add ₹371 crore, an average of ₹124 crore a quarter. The same three quarters of last year gave ₹322 crore, which is ₹429 crore for FY26 less ₹107 crore in the first quarter.[3] That depends on ₹560 crore of capex being spent in the second half, with ₹324 crore of Saudi orders that need cranes shipped through ports the company itself calls congested and costly.[3][5][4] It also depends on E&C work not slipping: management says about 15% of the E&C order book may move into FY28.[4] The evidence that would show us wrong is September quarter operating profit at or below the ₹81 crore reported a year earlier. That would mean no growth and would move us to the bear path.[1]

The strongest case against owning it

The bear case starts with where the money goes. About half of the FY27 capex pool (₹324 crore of ₹652 crore) is for Saudi Arabia. That unit earns a minus 6% return on capital today and has 201 receivable days. It sits in a region where attacks were reported on 22 October.[3][4][26] It continues with quality of earnings. Interest cost rose 73% against revenue growth of 39% in the June quarter. Profit fell from March, provisions rose, and free cash flow has been negative for two years.[11][3][7] It adds two governance flags that are small but real. An independent director resigned in September with no reason in the filing we found. Promoters moved a large block of shares between themselves in October.[20][1] On the price, the stock is only 16% below its two-year median multiple, not 40% below, so the cheapness argument is more modest than a 38% fall from the high suggests.[14][30] On the chart, the stock fell 28% in about three weeks, from ₹456.05 on 30 September to ₹328.55. No company filing explains it. The 23 October print is below the floor we call a base.[16][17][30] If all of that is right, a price near ₹218 is possible, which is a third lower than today.
