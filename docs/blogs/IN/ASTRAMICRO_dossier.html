1. What the company does

Astra Microwave Products is a Hyderabad company, started in 1991, that builds the radio-frequency and microwave electronics that sit inside radars, electronic-warfare systems, missile telemetry, satellites and weather radars.[4][6] Think of a radar as a very fast, very exact flashlight and camera for radio waves. Astra makes the parts that send the beam, receive the echo and steer it: filters, transmitters, receivers, antennas and the small transmit-and-receive modules at the heart of modern radars.[5] By its own count it has built 35 radars, 25 satellites and 15 strategic programmes, and it says it is one of the few private firms in India able to design and supply chips for these systems that work up to 40 GHz.[5]

Almost every rupee comes from the Indian state. In the year to March 2026 the Defence Research and Development Organisation, known as DRDO, supplied 28.2 percent of standalone revenue, Bharat Electronics and other defence public-sector companies 22.7 percent, the India Meteorological Department 18.0 percent, and the space agencies ISRO and SAC 6.4 percent. Exports, mostly through a joint venture and a Singapore chip-design arm, were 11.9 percent, and private customers only 3.0 percent.[5] The government is a very safe borrower but a slow payer, and that slowness shapes the cash story told in chapter 3.

Revenue arrives in two stages. First a defence laboratory pays Astra to develop a part to a specification. If the part qualifies, a production order follows. In July 2026 Hindustan Aeronautics ordered the antenna units for the Uttam radar, and in the June quarter Bharat Electronics ordered ₹45 crore of Astra's chips.[4][6] At 30 June 2026 the standalone order book was ₹2,156 crore, made up of 65.7 percent production, 14.3 percent development, 8.0 percent export and 12.0 percent service contracts.[4][5] Management says most orders are built to Astra's own specification rather than to a customer's drawing, and that the first kind earns more per unit.[12]

Revenue is lumpy because it is booked when a customer accepts a delivery. In FY26 the March quarter alone brought ₹488 crore of the year's ₹1,163 crore, which is 42 percent, while the June 2026 quarter brought ₹177 crore.[3] That pattern is normal for this industry. It explains why one weak quarter says less than it seems to, and why this report gives more weight to the order book and the half-year totals than to any single quarter.

The companies around Astra

Besides the parent, the group has three subsidiaries and two joint ventures. Astra Defence Technologies is a fully owned subsidiary that mostly supports the group's own needs.[5][8] Aelius Semiconductors is a chip-design house in Singapore that designs gallium-arsenide and gallium-nitride chips for defence and space.[5] Astra Space Technologies was incorporated on 17 February 2024 to build satellite equipment, and it is also the company that will receive the space and weather business in the planned split.[5][19]

The two joint ventures are Astra Rafael Comsys, formed in August 2019 with Rafael Advanced Defense Systems of Israel to make software-defined radios and electronic-warfare equipment, and a 50-50 venture with Manjeera Digital Systems formed on 6 November 2024 for India's NavIC satellite-navigation chips.[5] The profit of the joint ventures shows up in the group accounts as a single line, ₹8 crore in FY26. Because only that profit share is shown, the ventures' own sales are not added to group revenue.[5] So when management talks about the Rafael venture booking ₹600 crore or more of sales, none of that lifts the consolidated sales figure used in this report.[8]

The plan to split off space and weather

On 10 June 2026 the board approved a scheme to move the space, meteorology and hydrology business, which had FY26 turnover of about ₹157 crore or 13.58 percent of the total, into Astra Space Technologies. Each shareholder of the parent would receive one new share for every share held, so the new company would mirror the existing shareholding, and no cash changes hands.[19]

Management says it expects the new company to work on its own from 1 April 2027, and that S. G. Reddy, the managing director who announced he would step down on 30 September 2026, has accepted the task of building it.[6][5] The scheme still needs approval from the company tribunal, the market regulator SEBI and the stock exchanges, so the date is an expectation, expected and not confirmed, and not a fact.[19] For you as an owner the split changes the price chart. By our inference, the share price should fall by about the value of the new shares on the day the scheme takes effect. We address that mechanical effect in the decision rules in chapter 7, where every level is restated on adjusted prices.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • RSI (relative strength index) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been moving. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in rupees. It is the ruler we use to measure how far the price sits from support.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit from running the business before financing costs, tax and the accounting write-down of equipment.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is the yearly profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses, shown as a percentage.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio is the share price divided by the profit per share. It tells you how many years of today's profit you pay for up front.
  • capital expenditure is cash spent on plants, equipment and other long-lived assets.
  • free cash flow (FCF, which stands for free cash flow) is the cash left after running the business and paying for capital expenditure.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself collects and pays out in its day-to-day work, before any borrowing or investing.
  • working capital is the money tied up in unpaid customer bills and stored inventory, less the credit suppliers give. A business with a lot of it needs funding to grow.
  • debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. A figure below 1 means the owners fund more than the lenders do.
  • net debt is borrowings minus the cash the company holds.
  • guidance is the company's own published forecast for a coming period.
  • book-to-bill is new orders received divided by revenue billed in the same period. Above 1 means the order book is growing.
  • dilution is the rise in the number of shares when new ones are issued, which shrinks each existing share's slice of profit.
  • consensus estimate is the average of professional analysts' forecasts for a number such as next year's profit per share.
  • base is a sideways stretch of trading that a price builds before its next move, like a platform. The low of that stretch is the floor traders watch.
  • pivot is the top of a base, the price a rise must clear to count as a real move.
  • breakout is a move above a pivot or other resistance level, ideally on heavier trading volume.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend in the Weinstein scheme, with the price above a rising 50-day average that itself sits above the 200-day average.
  • Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend in the same scheme, with the price below falling 50-day and 200-day averages.
  • stop loss is the price at which the idea is judged wrong and the position is sold.
  • limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, so it does not chase a rising market.
  • entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned, usually by limit order.
  • asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop loss, measured from a chosen entry price; 2.5 means the target is 2.5 times as far away as the stop.
  • These terms keep the same meaning throughout, and the next chapter turns to what makes the business strong or weak.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Astra's main strength is that it is hard to replace once it has been qualified into a programme. Defence electronics are not bought off a shelf. A new supplier has to be tested, certified and trusted by laboratories such as DRDO's, and that takes years. Astra has done this work: it makes the airborne antenna unit for the Uttam radar planned for the Tejas fighter, takes part in the electronic-warfare upgrade of the Su-30, supplies telemetry to BrahMos missiles and has QRSAM air-defence subsystems due before March.[4][6] In weather radar, management says that 13 of the 14 Doppler weather radars installed in India are Astra's.[12]

The second strength is depth in engineering. R&D spending was ₹58 crore in FY26 against ₹23 crore in FY21, and 701 people work in R&D.[5] Management says that two radars of its own, an electromagnetic wall and a vehicle-mounted anti-drone system are due around Diwali, and that none of the revenue from these is in its numbers.[6] We treat them as unproven upside and give them no value in the base case.

The third strength is visibility. The July 2026 order from Hindustan Aeronautics is worth ₹2,205 crore and covers 122 airborne antenna units and 121 interface frames. It is larger than the whole standalone order book of ₹2,156 crore at 30 June, and it runs for about five years.[4][6] Management says the first 12 units are due by September 2027 and that deliveries then run at 25 or more units a year.[6] One detail matters for arithmetic: the ₹2,205 crore includes GST, and management puts the value without tax at about ₹1,870 crore.[6]

The weak points are as clear as the strengths. Customers are almost all state bodies, so a delay in one programme can empty a quarter, as the June 2026 quarter showed: revenue fell 11.5 percent from a year earlier after inspection and supply-chain delays.[4][6] Customers were also slow to pay, taking 216 days in FY26, an improvement from 273 days a year earlier.[5] The promoter group owns only 6.54 percent through two holders, none of it pledged, and the company announced that the managing director's role would pass to M. V. Reddy on 1 October 2026.[14][5]

How Astra compares with its rivals

Bharat Electronics is both a major customer, grouped with other defence public-sector firms at 22.7 percent of revenue, and in some programmes a rival.[5] On the active antenna unit for the Mk1A fighter radar, management says Astra and Bharat Electronics emerged as the first and second lowest bidders, so the two firms compete and share work in the same programme.[6] In size the gap is large: Bharat Electronics had FY26 sales of ₹27,610 crore against Astra's ₹1,163 crore, about 24 times as much.[24][3]

Among listed peers of similar size, Apollo Micro Systems, Paras Defence and Space Technologies and Zen Technologies each sell different products, but all three trade on high earnings multiples, as Astra does, and all depend on the same government budgets.[20][21][22] Management argues that Astra has outgrown the label of a second-tier subsystem supplier and now builds complete systems.[8] That claim is partly visible in the order book, where development and system work now sits beside production, and chapter 3 sets the peer figures side by side so you can judge it on numbers.

What moves the business through a year

The first driver is the defence budget. The Union Budget for 2026-27 gives the Ministry of Defence ₹7.85 lakh crore, 15.19 percent above the previous budget estimate, and earmarks ₹1.39 lakh crore for buying from Indian defence industry, about 75 percent of the capital acquisition budget.[18] That supports demand for Indian-made electronics in general, though it does not decide which company wins which order.

The second driver is the delivery calendar. Defence customers accept equipment in batches, and Astra's revenue bunches in the last quarter of the financial year. In the June 2026 quarter, defence was 80.4 percent of revenue and space 7.7 percent, while in the March 2026 quarter space had been 19.5 percent and defence only 58.4 percent.[5] The third driver is supply: management itself names stretched overseas supply chains as a risk while every country restocks defence inventory.[6] These three drivers carry forward into the next chapter, where we score the specific operating measures that show whether the business is delivering.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Astra is an order-driven business, which means the numbers that matter are not the ones a software or retail company would use. Revenue is booked when equipment is accepted, so the order book tells you what is coming. Cash is slow to arrive, so cash conversion tells you whether profit is real. And margins swing with the mix between development work and production, so margin tells you whether growth is paid for. The scoring follows the house rule: a pass is worth 1.0, a watch 0.5 and a fail 0.0. A name qualifies when the weighted score is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 and no more than one measure fails. Capital allocation has been consistent with the stated plan. On the information available, management gets a qualified pass for walking the talk: reliable on annual headline numbers, late and loose on timing. That feeds directly into the confidence we place on the FY27 and FY28 revenue targets used in the scenario work in chapter 5.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Sales rose from ₹750 crore in FY22 to ₹1,163 crore in FY26, a pace of 12.5 percent a year over the last three years, FY23 to FY26. Profit after tax rose from ₹38 crore to ₹193 crore over the five years, and by 40.2 percent a year over those same three years, from ₹70 crore in FY23.[3] The gap between the two growth rates is the main feature of the period. EBITDA margin widened from 11.6 percent to 28.7 percent, which means each rupee of sales left far more profit. Management credits a favourable revenue mix and higher value-add work.[7] Part of this is operating leverage, where fixed costs spread over more revenue, and part is a shift toward production and system orders that price higher.

Cash tells a different story. Cash flow from operations was negative in FY23, FY24 and FY25, at minus ₹25 crore, minus ₹182 crore and minus ₹90 crore, and then positive ₹387 crore in FY26.[3] Management says the turn came when long-standing customer bills, mostly tied up in missile programmes, were finally collected.[11] Free cash flow, which also deducts spending on plant and equipment, was minus ₹229 crore and minus ₹169 crore in FY24 and FY25 and positive ₹304 crore in FY26, a swing that rests on one year of collections.[3]

The balance sheet is lightly geared for a business with this much cash tied up. Borrowings rose to ₹424 crore in FY25 and fell to ₹288 crore in FY26, a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.22, and interest cost has been covered 5.7 times by pre-tax profit.[3] CRISIL lifted its long-term rating to A+ with a stable outlook in August 2026.[15] The three tables that follow show five years of profit, balance sheet and cash, and the last five quarters.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 leans on a few assumptions that could be wrong. It also states the red-team bear case, which is the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion, and the single claim on which the whole report hinges.

The red-team case runs like this. The stock trades at 77.5 times trailing profit and 61 times the analysts' FY27 number, in a market the ledger calls a downtrend.[1] June 2026 revenue fell 11.5 percent against management's own full-year target of 15 to 20 percent growth, the managing director's role was due to change on 1 October, a corporate split is under way, and the order that excited the market is largely a five-year, production-priced contract whose first 12 units are due only in September 2027.[4][5][6] A margin that slips to 24 percent while the multiple falls to its 25th percentile gives ₹1,088, 29.5 percent below today's price. A return to 42.9 times is an assumption for illustration and not a forecast, it is not one of our three cases, and it shows why the exit rule exists.

The margin assumption is the weakest link

Our base case uses an EBITDA margin of 27.0 percent for FY28. The analysts' numbers need about 31.6 percent, and FY26 achieved 28.7 percent, of which the fourth quarter alone was 33.3 percent.[7][1] A margin is not one choice that management makes. It depends on product mix, on how much of the revenue is development work paid at higher rates, and on whether the production order carries a thinner starting margin. Management would not commit to a figure: on the August call a director said the company did not want to commit on margins because incremental products will change the mix, and added that there might be a trade-off between order size and initial margin.[6]

The evidence that would show our margin too low is a September 2026 quarter at 25 percent or above, which would compare with 22 percent a year earlier, and a December quarter near 30 percent.[3] The evidence that would show it too high is a second quarter below 20 percent. These margin readings are watch items only. The sale rules in chapter 7 use revenue and price, and no margin reading triggers a sale on its own. We expect to know by mid-November 2026 for the September quarter, though no date has been announced and the 45-day rule gives 14 November as the outer limit, and by mid-February 2027 for the December quarter, both expected and not confirmed.[27]

The multiple could fall further

The base value uses a multiple of 59.3 times, the median of the last 29 months, but the stock now trades at 77.5 times.
