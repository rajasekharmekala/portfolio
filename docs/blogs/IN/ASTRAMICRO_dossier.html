Chapter 1

Astra Microwave Products Limited (AMPL) was founded on September 13, 1991, in Hyderabad [6]. Its founders were three scientists from India's defence and space labs, not an industrial group [6]. B. Malla Reddy came from ISRO and DRDL/DRDO. P.A. Chitrakar came from DLRL/DRDO, and C. Prameelamma came from ERDE and DLRL/DRDO [6]. They left public-sector research as India's economic reforms began opening small cracks in state defence monopolies [6]. Their bet was clear. Labs like DLRL and LRDE had strong system designs on paper [6], [7]. Yet India lacked private partners that could build, test, and qualify RF and microwave parts up to 40 GHz [6], [7].

In 1994, Astra won DSIR recognition for its in-house R&D centre [7]. For twenty years, it operated as a specialised subsystem supplier [6], [7]. It built filters, power amplifiers, low-noise amplifiers, mixers, and T/R modules for DRDO, ISRO, and BEL [6], [7]. Unlike contract assemblers that only populate circuit boards, Astra built Class 10,000 and Class 100,000 cleanrooms [7]. It also invested in chip-and-wire hybrid electronics, a private Near Field Antenna Test Range (NFTR), and NABL-accredited EMI/EMC chambers [7], [8], [9]. That test base helped Astra move from single parts to full RF front-ends and Active Antenna Array Units (AAAUs) [17].

A major shift arrived in the late 2010s. Astra formed Astra Rafael Comsys Private Limited (ARC), a 50:50 joint venture with Israel's Rafael Advanced Defense Systems Ltd [5], [11]. Astra brought local factories, cleanroom assembly, and Indian MoD offset eligibility [5], [11]. Rafael brought combat-tested Software Defined Radio (SDR) waveforms, electronic warfare (EW) designs, and electro-optical systems [5], [11]. Through ARC, Astra moved up from bare RF modules to full tactical radio and EW suites for the Indian Air Force, Navy, and Army [11], [13].

Between FY15 and FY26, Astra's growth was far from smooth. Twelve years of consolidated results show three clear phases [1]. From FY15 to FY19, Astra went through a steep five-year slump [1]. Sales dropped from ₹622 Cr in FY15 to ₹406 Cr in FY16, ₹390 Cr in FY17, and ₹362 Cr in FY18 [1]. Revenue hit a floor of ₹293 Cr in FY19, a 53% fall, as telecom exports ended and domestic orders stalled [1]. Operating margin (OPM) fell from 31% in FY18 to 10% in FY19 [1]. Net profit shrank to ₹10 Cr, and ROCE dropped from 29% in FY15 to 3% in FY19 [1].

In the second phase (FY20–FY22), Astra pivoted toward global defence OEM exports like Rafael, Elbit, and Thales [1], [12]. This kept its Hyderabad lines busy while Indian MoD production orders stayed in trials [1], [12]. Consolidated revenue bounced back from ₹293 Cr in FY19 to ₹467 Cr in FY20, ₹641 Cr in FY21, and ₹750 Cr in FY22 [1]. Most of that export work was lower-margin build-to-print assembly using imported MMIC kits [1], [12]. As a result, OPM stayed at 12% in both FY21 and FY22, and ROCE hovered at 10%–11% [1], [12].

The third phase (FY23–FY26) marked a real structural turn under India's Atmanirbhar Bharat push [12], [13]. DRDO-designed radars and EW systems moved from field trials into serial production. Revenue climbed from ₹816 Cr in FY23 to ₹1,163 Cr in FY26 [1]. Over that period, OPM jumped by over 1,100 basis points—from 18% in FY23 to 21% in FY24, 26% in FY25, and 29% in FY26 [1], [14]. Quarterly OPM peaked at 33% in Q4 FY26 and Q2 FY27 [1], [14]. Net profit compounded at 46.2% CAGR over five years, reaching ₹193 Cr in FY26 and ₹216 Cr TTM through September 2026 [1].

Governance & Ownership Structure: The 6.54% Promoter Footprint

A defining structural quirk of Astra Microwave is its unusually low promoter shareholding of just 6.54%, which has remained flat since at least December 2023 [1]. Unlike family-controlled Indian industrials where founders retain 50%–70% equity across generations, Astra's scientist-founders gradually pared down their stakes over three decades, leaving the company effectively institutionally and publicly owned (FIIs 10.48%, DIIs 16.52%, Public 66.47% as of September 2026) [1].

In October 2026, Astra executed a major generational and structural handoff: long-serving Managing Director S. Gurunatha Reddy stepped down on September 30, 2026 to lead the planned demerger of the Space, Meteorology, and Hydrology verticals into Astra Space Technologies Limited, while former Joint MD Dr. M.V. Reddy assumed charge as Managing Director on October 1, 2026 [15].

From 1994 to 2014, Astra grew alongside India's push for homegrown radar and missile hardware [6], [7]. Rather than fighting BEL for prime MoD contracts, Astra worked as the specialist RF subsystem partner for DRDO [6], [17]. It supplied receivers, exciters, and T/R modules for Akash, Rohini, and naval radars [6], [17]. This partner role kept Astra out of prime-contracting battles while locking its parts into multi-decade platforms [12], [17]. At the same time, Astra expanded into Space (ISRO) and Meteorology (IMD Doppler Weather Radars) [12], [13]. All three government verticals shared the same core RF physics [12], [13].

The 2026 demerger plan opens a fourth chapter in this 35-year arc [15]. Astra will spin out Space, Meteorology, and Hydrology into Astra Space Technologies Limited under S. Gurunatha Reddy [15]. Dr. M.V. Reddy will lead the core defence unit [15]. Defence serial production (AESA radar arrays and EW suites) runs on multi-year MoD and HAL batch schedules [12], [15]. By contrast, Space and Meteorology rely on custom, lower-volume R&D and tender cycles [12], [15]. Splitting them lets each business manage its own balance sheet, cleanrooms, and capital [15].

Chapter 2

Every radar, missile seeker, EW jammer, satellite transponder, and weather station needs an RF front-end. That front-end generates, amplifies, filters, phase-shifts, and receives radio signals. Astra does not build the fighter airframe, rocket motor, or satellite bus [12], [17]. Instead, it builds the high-margin electromagnetic 'eyes and ears' inside them [12], [17].

Domestic defence production now dominates Astra's standalone backlog (99.2% as of Q2 FY27), while the Space & Meteorology verticals are being carved out into Astra Space Technologies Ltd [3], [15].

1. Defence (~54%–67% of revenue; 99.2% of Q2 FY27 standalone backlog): Defence is Astra's core growth engine. It covers airborne and ground radars, EW suites, and missile seekers [3], [12]. Concrete Real-World Example: HAL and DRDO's LRDE needed an indigenous AESA fire-control radar—the Uttam AESA Radar—for the Tejas LCA Mk-1A fighter [17]. They also needed a heavier GaN-based derivative—the Virupaksha AESA Radar—for the Indian Air Force's Su-30MKI Super Sukhoi upgrade [17]. A mechanical dish cannot track multiple agile targets at once or resist modern jamming. Astra partnered with LRDE as a Development-cum-Production Partner (DcPP) to build the plank-based Active Antenna Array Unit (AAAU) [17]. Each unit packs hundreds of GaAs/GaN T/R modules, cold plates, and RF manifolds [17]. Teams assemble each unit in Class 10,000 cleanrooms in Hyderabad and stress-test it to military thermal and vibration specs [7], [8]. Astra then calibrates the array inside its 17m x 15m x 11m Near Field Antenna Test Range (NFTR) [7], [8]. Finally, Astra ships units to HAL under multi-year fixed-price contracts, including the Q2 FY27 HAL radar order that lifted quarterly inflows to ₹2,036 Cr [3], [17].

2. Space Electronics (~9%–11% of FY26 revenue; +88% YoY growth): Astra is one of the few private Indian firms qualified by ISRO for space-grade RF hardware [7], [13], [21]. In orbit, radiation hardness, zero-defect solder joints, and extreme thermal swings leave zero room for repair [7], [13], [21]. Concrete Real-World Example: ISRO's Space Applications Centre (SAC) builds earth-observation and defence Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) satellites like the RISAT and EOS series [12], [13], [21]. Those payloads need high-power Solid State Power Amplifiers (SSPAs), frequency synthesizers, and multi-channel radar receivers that survive launch and vacuum [12], [13], [21]. Astra's space unit builds these sealed hybrid modules with automated die-attach, gold wire-bonding, and 3D X-ray checks [7]. Each module passes burn-in testing under strict MIL and ISRO standards [7]. Contracts are milestone-based, low-volume, high-IP awards where qualification barriers block uncertified rivals [12], [13]. To sharpen capital focus, Astra's board approved an in-principle demerger of Space, Meteorology, and Hydrology into Astra Space Technologies Limited under outgoing MD S. Gurunatha Reddy [15].

3. Meteorology & Hydrology (~2%–16% of revenue): This unit applies Astra's radar physics to civilian weather and water tracking [12], [13]. Concrete Real-World Example: The India Meteorological Department (IMD) places S-band and X-band Doppler Weather Radars (DWRs) along India's cyclone coastlines and Himalayan ridges [12], [13]. These radars measure storm wind speeds and rain density in real time [12], [13]. Astra designs the RF subsystem, pedestal control, and signal processor, installs the radome on-site, and runs multi-year Annual Maintenance Contracts (AMCs) [12], [13]. Government weather tenders arrive in lumpy batches, so this unit swings between 2% and 16% of annual sales [12], [13].

4. Exports & Deemed Exports (~11.9% direct FY26 revenue; historically up to 33%): Astra supplies global defence primes like Rafael, Elbit, and Thales as an offset and subsystem partner [12], [13]. Concrete Real-World Example: When a foreign prime wins an international airborne radar or EW deal, it outsources T/R modules or wideband receivers to Astra's Hyderabad lines [12], [13]. These contracts use build-to-print or build-to-spec terms [12], [13]. Export orders bring faster dollar cash than domestic B2G deals. However, they yield only 12%–16% operating margins because the foreign OEM owns the IP and supplies key MMIC kits [12], [13].

5. Astra Rafael Comsys (ARC) 50:50 Joint Venture: ARC is accounted for via the equity method rather than line-by-line revenue consolidation [5], [11], [13]. Its revenue grew from ₹245.2 Cr (₹18.7 Cr PAT) in FY25 to roughly ₹360 Cr in FY26, with a ₹622–636 Cr year-end order book [5], [11], [13]. Concrete Real-World Example: The Indian Air Force needed jam-resistant Software Defined Radios (SDRs) for fighter and transport aircraft to run encrypted voice and data links. ARC licenses Rafael's waveform IP and builds, integrates, and tests the airborne SDRs in Hyderabad [5], [11], [13]. That structure wins 'Buy Indian (IDDM)' slots that neither partner could capture alone [5], [11], [13].

Chapter 3

Astra Microwave has no retail network, no dealers, and zero consumer ad spend. Its sales channel is direct B2G and B2B defence OEM qualification [4], [12]. Every rupee of domestic revenue flows through a strict institutional funnel. Buyers include DRDO labs (LRDE for radars, DLRL for EW, DRDL for missiles), defence PSUs (BEL and HAL), ISRO centres (SAC and URSC), and the IMD [6], [12], [17]. Once Astra qualifies as a Development-cum-Production Partner (DcPP), switching suppliers mid-programme is costly [7], [9], [17]. A buyer would have to repeat years of environmental, EMI/EMC, and flight-clearance tests, creating high switching barriers [7], [9], [17].

In exports, Astra skips end-government sales and plugs into global Tier-1 supply chains [12], [13]. It serves Israeli and European primes like Rafael, Elbit, and Thales, often driven by Indian MoD offset rules [12], [13]. That split creates two very different margin profiles. Export build-to-print orders use customer-supplied or imported MMIC kits and earn 12%–16% operating margins [12], [13]. By contrast, domestic IP-led production—where Astra owns the RF design and localises assembly—earns 28%–33% operating margins [12], [13], [14].

Semiconductor and RF part lead times are the main supply chain bottleneck [4]. High-power GaN and GaAs MMIC dies, space-grade FPGAs, precision connectors, and RF laminates come mostly from US, European, and Taiwanese foundries. Defence contracts impose stiff fines for late delivery. To avoid delays, Astra buys long-lead imported chips 6 to 12 months before customer delivery dates [1], [4]. That buffer explains why inventory days stretched to 389 days in FY25 and 394 days in FY26 [1], [4].

Manufacturing and testing sit across two southern Indian aerospace hubs [6], [7]. In Hyderabad (Telangana), Astra runs five units across Bollaram (Units 1 & 2) and Maheshwaram Hardware Park / E-City (Units 3, 4 & 7) [6], [7]. These sites house Class 10,000 and Class 100,000 cleanrooms, SMT lines, chip-and-wire bonding, 3D X-ray checks, and stress chambers [6], [7]. In Bengaluru (Karnataka), Astra runs an R&D and integration hub at the Aerospace Park near the airport, right next to HAL and LRDE [6]. Astra also owns Near Field and Far Field Antenna Test Ranges (NFTR/FFTR) and an NABL-accredited EMI/EMC chamber up to 40 GHz [8], [9]. Having those labs in-house removes external test queues that slow down smaller peers [8], [9].

Billing and cash conversion are the hardest parts of Astra's unit economics [4]. Domestic B2G buyers (BEL, HAL, DRDO, IMD) inspect hardware in batches and pay only after milestone sign-offs. As a result, sales and cash collections bunch heavily in Q4. March-quarter sales hit ₹488 Cr in FY26—42% of full-year revenue—versus just ₹177 Cr in Q1 FY27 [1]. With 394 inventory days and 216 debtor days (down from 273 in FY25), Astra's Cash Conversion Cycle reached 537 days in FY26 [1]. That heavy working-capital load forces Astra to pay ₹56 Cr in annual finance costs (4.8% of sales) despite earning 28.7% EBITDA margins [1].

A typical domestic AESA radar or EW contract shows why gross margins stay high while cash stays tied up [4], [12]. Contracts are fixed-price or capped-escalation, won after competitive or single-vendor DcPP qualification [12], [17]. Raw materials—chiefly imported GaN/GaAs MMICs, RF circuit boards, metal housings, and connectors—take roughly 50.5% of sales (₹587.3 Cr net of stock changes in FY26) [1], [2]. After cleanroom assembly in Hyderabad, every unit goes through RF calibration, thermal cycling (-40°C to +85°C), vibration checks, and NFTR verification [7], [8], [9]. Customer teams (DGAQA, BEL, or HAL) must then sign off before dispatch [7], [8], [9]. Only after delivery and acceptance testing can Astra issue the final milestone bill [4].

Government defence budgets expire every March 31, so public-sector buyers cluster inspections and fund releases into January–March [1], [4]. That calendar forces Astra to hold peak raw materials and work-in-progress through Q1–Q3. Borrowings rose to ₹487 Cr by September 2026 before converting into Q4 sales (₹488 Cr in Q4 FY26) and cash [1], [14]. Investors should read this seasonal swing with care. Negative cash flow in H1 is built into the contract model, provided Q4 collections clear the receivable bulge [1], [4], [14].

Chapter 4

India's defence electronics sector runs as a tiered oligopoly. It is shaped by MoD buying rules, DRDO tech transfers, and long platform cycles [12], [17]. At the top sit public-sector lead integrators: Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL) for radars, EW, and naval systems, and Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) for military aircraft [17]. Below them sits a small group of qualified private RF, microwave, and avionics firms. These peers include Astra Microwave, Data Patterns (India), Centum Electronics, Avantel, Paras Defence, and MTAR Technologies [19], [20].

Astra's competitive moat rests on three pillars. First is Qualification & Switching-Cost Lock-in: earning AS9100, MIL-STD-461/704, and ISRO space-grade approvals takes 5–10 years of audits [7], [9]. Once Astra's AAAUs are certified on the Uttam or Virupaksha AESA radar, replacing Astra forces a full radar re-certification [17]. Second is Proprietary Test Infrastructure: Astra owns a 17m x 15m x 11m Near Field Antenna Test Range (NFTR) and NABL-accredited EMI/EMC chambers up to 40 GHz [8], [9]. Smaller private rivals cannot match that sunk capital quickly [8], [9]. Third is the Rafael JV Channel (ARC), which pairs Israeli combat-tested SDR and EW IP with Indian local manufacturing [5], [11].
