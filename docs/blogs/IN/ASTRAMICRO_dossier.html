1. What the company does

A modern fighter-jet radar has a flat panel made of hundreds of small transmit-and-receive modules. Each module is a tiny radio that sends out a pulse and listens for the echo. Astra builds those modules, the panel they sit on, and the units that feed them. The panel is called an active antenna array unit (AAAU). The radar for the Tejas Mk1A fighter, named Uttam, is the biggest example. In July 2026 HAL ordered 122 of these panels and 121 interface frames for ₹2,205 crore including tax.[2][24] Astra says it has contributed to 35 radars, 25 satellites and about 15 strategic programmes, and it makes chips (MMICs, monolithic microwave integrated circuits) for the same systems.[3]

Almost every rupee comes from the state. In FY26 the standalone company earned ₹1,156 crore, and the investor deck splits it by customer (the deck states the split on ₹1,155 crore, a one-crore rounding difference): the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) 28.2 percent, Bharat Electronics and other defence public-sector companies 22.7 percent, the India Meteorological Department 18.0 percent, exports routed through its Israeli joint venture and a Singapore subsidiary 11.9 percent, the space agencies 6.4 percent, other buyers 9.5 percent and private customers 3.0 percent.[3] Roughly three-quarters of sales therefore depend on a short list of Indian government bodies. That makes non-payment unlikely but delay common. The buyer decides when equipment is accepted, and acceptance is the moment the company can send a bill.

There are three kinds of contract. A development order pays Astra to design and prove a new unit with a government laboratory; it was 14.3 percent of the June 2026 order book. A production order follows when the design is chosen and the buyer wants many copies; it was 65.7 percent. The rest was servicing contracts (12.0 percent) and exports (8.0 percent).[3] Management calls its new role a development-cum-production partner, meaning it helps design the unit and then becomes the approved maker.[5] This is why margins rose. The gross margin, the share of sales left after materials, went from 39.9 percent in FY24 to 50.9 percent in FY26. Production of government-specified units replaced low-value assembly for foreign customers.[3]

The group is wider than the listed company. Astra Rafael Comsys (ARC) is a joint venture with Rafael of Israel that builds software-defined military radios; management reported its order book at ₹836 crore and planned FY27 sales of ₹360 crore.[4] Aelius Semiconductors in Singapore designs chips, Astra Defence Technologies is a captive electronics maker, and Astra Space Technologies is the shell that will receive the space business.[3] The listed company has given corporate guarantees of ₹43 crore for Astra Defence Technologies and ₹80 crore for ARC. That is small next to equity of ₹1,315 crore, but it is a real obligation.[10]

Sales arrive in lumps. In FY26 the fourth quarter alone brought ₹488 crore, 42 percent of the year's ₹1,163 crore, while the first quarter brought ₹200 crore.[1] Management says repeatedly that it runs the business on yearly targets, and that is fair. The lumpiness cuts both ways. One weak quarter proves little, yet it becomes a real warning when the full-year target needs a catch-up.[4]

The space and weather arm, and why it is being split off

The board approved a scheme on 10 June 2026 to move the space, meteorology and hydrology business into a wholly owned subsidiary and list it separately. That business had turnover of about ₹157 crore in FY26, 13.58 percent of the total, and each Astra shareholder would receive one share of the new company for every share held.[14] The scheme still needs approval from the company tribunal (NCLT), the market regulator SEBI and both stock exchanges.[14] Management expects the new company to operate independently from 1 April 2027; the rating agency CRISIL writes fiscal 2028, which is the same date.[4][12]

For an owner of Astra today, the split changes what you own rather than what you pay. After the split, the listed defence company will have lost about 14 percent of revenue, and CRISIL expects the growing defence order book to cover that gap.[12] Management has said the space entity should start with sales above ₹300 crore and a profit-before-tax margin of at least 18 to 20 percent; that is a statement of intent, not audited data, and the figures for the new entity are not published.[4]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong where it is hardest to copy and weak where it is hardest to fund. The strength is engineering. A private company that can design and qualify radar modules, jammers and chips that DRDO labs and HAL will fly is rare. The investor deck says it is one of the few private firms able to supply gallium-nitride and gallium-arsenide chips up to 40 GHz.[3] CRISIL lists it among the partners on the Uttam, Virupaksha, Angad, QRSAM, AMCA and AEW&C programmes.[12] Each programme took years to qualify, so a buyer cannot switch suppliers in a quarter. The weakness is cash. The same long approvals leave Astra waiting for payment and holding stock for a very long time.

Evidence for the strength sits in the margins. Consolidated operating margin, the share of sales left after running costs and before depreciation and interest, rose from 21.1 percent in FY24 to 25.6 percent in FY25 and 28.7 percent in FY26. Analysts also call it the EBITDA margin, where EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. CRISIL expects 28 to 30 percent to hold.[3][12] The rise came from mix: more domestic production of government-specified units, less assembly to foreign drawings, and backward integration through the chip and electronics subsidiaries.[12] It is also fragile. In the June 2026 quarter the margin dipped to 18.7 percent from 20.5 percent a year earlier, because costs fell by less than sales did.[2]

Competitive position: customer, supplier and rival at once

Astra's main buyers are also rivals. Bharat Electronics (BEL) is a prime contractor that buys subsystems from Astra, and it also bids against Astra for work. On the AMCA fifth-generation fighter radar, Astra emerged as the lowest bidder for the antenna array units on four prototypes and BEL was second.[25][4] The same two firms compete for the work and may then share the supply chain. Among listed peers, Data Patterns, Apollo Micro Systems and Paras Defence sell comparable electronics. The peer table in chapter 3 shows Astra's operating margin among the highest of the group, but its three-year sales growth is the lowest of the companies with data.

Pricing power is limited by the buyer. Government price-negotiation committees settle the rates on orders.[7] Management says it will trade some initial margin for larger orders, because service and upgrade contracts follow over the life of a system.[4] That is a sensible long-run bet. It also means the 28 to 29 percent margin of FY26 is a good-mix result and not a guaranteed floor.[12]

Cash and working capital: the weak spot

Working capital means cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, minus credit from suppliers. For FY26 the Screener table shows debtor days of 216, inventory days of 394 and a cash conversion cycle of 537 days.[1] CRISIL, using its own adjustments, puts debtors at about 218 days and stock plus receivables net of cash at about 429 days. A year earlier those were 273 days and more than 500 days.[12] In plain terms, a rupee spent on parts comes back in roughly a year and a half. That is normal for long-gestation defence work. It is also the reason the company borrowed ₹424 crore at March 2025.

The auditor flagged the provision against trade receivables as a key audit matter. Gross standalone receivables were ₹708 crore at March 2026, against a provision of about ₹14.8 crore.[10] CRISIL names the working-capital requirement as the first weakness. It says it would downgrade if stock plus receivables stretch back above 500 days.[12]

Cyclicality: the lesson of 2015 to 2019

This business has shrunk before. Consolidated sales fell from ₹622 crore in FY15 to ₹293 crore in FY19, and operating margin sank to 10 percent in FY19. The ten-year sales growth rate is only 11 percent a year.[1] Management describes the earlier years as an offset-obligation business whose exports were 'deemed exports', and says it has moved away from it.[4] The filings we fetched give no cause for the FY15 to FY19 decline, so we do not assign one. The present backlog is far larger and more domestic, so a repeat is less likely. The history still shows what happens when orders pause while a fixed engineering team and plant remain.

Today's exposure is concentrated in a few programmes instead. The Uttam radar alone is about half of the August 2026 order book. A delay in that programme, a design change, or a slower Tejas Mk1A delivery schedule would show up first in Astra's quarterly sales.[4][2]

Governance and capital allocation

Pledged promoter shares are zero, the rating is CRISIL A+/Stable after an upgrade on 28 August 2026, and the auditor's opinion is unqualified.[16][12][13][10] Two points need watching. First, management's messages are not always consistent. The August press release gave guidance of 10 to 15 percent growth, which is management's own forecast, while the slides said 15 to 20 percent. Management corrected this on the call to 15 to 20 percent.[2][4] Second, the call quoted first-quarter revenue of ₹182 crore and last year's revenue as ₹1,057 crore. The filed results show ₹177 crore and ₹1,163 crore, so this report uses the filed figures throughout.[2][4]

There is also a pending dilution, which is the increase in share count that shrinks each holder's slice. 20,13,885 convertible warrants issued at ₹864 must convert by 29 December 2026. That adds about 2.1 percent to the share count and ₹130.5 crore of cash.[10][12] The holders pay 25 percent now and 75 percent on conversion. With the shares near ₹1,625, conversion is almost certain.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on a weakest assumption. This chapter names each one, states the evidence that would show it wrong, and gives the date when we will know. It also sets out the strongest case against our own verdict. A position that was sold and bought back within a day deserves an honest second look.

The central price rests on a multiple that may not hold

The 64.7 times median comes from the last three years. That was a period when defence stocks were re-rated and Astra's profit base was small. A multiple measured on small profits can shrink when profit grows, because buyers then compare the stock with larger, slower names such as BEL at 44.8 times.[20][21] If the multiple settles at 55, the central price falls to about ₹1,470 on the same profit. If it stays at 81.6, the price is about ₹2,180. The estimate is therefore more sensitive to the multiple than to any single quarter of earnings. We would learn which way it is going at each quarterly result, since the market usually revalues defence names within a week of a miss or a beat.

The profit path depends on a second half that has not yet been shown
