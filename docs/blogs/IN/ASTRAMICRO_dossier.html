Chapter 1

Astra Microwave Products Limited (AMPL) was founded on September 13, 1991, in Hyderabad [6]. Its founders were three scientists from India's defence and space labs, not an industrial group [6]. B. Malla Reddy came from ISRO and DRDL/DRDO. P.A. Chitrakar came from DLRL/DRDO, and C. Prameelamma came from ERDE and DLRL/DRDO [6]. They left public-sector research as India's economic reforms began opening small cracks in state defence monopolies [6]. Their bet was clear. Labs like DLRL and LRDE had strong system designs on paper [6], [7]. Yet India lacked private partners that could build, test, and qualify RF and microwave parts up to 40 GHz [6], [7].

In 1994, Astra won DSIR recognition for its in-house R&D centre [7]. For twenty years, it operated as a specialised subsystem supplier [6], [7]. It built filters, power amplifiers, low-noise amplifiers, mixers, and T/R modules for DRDO, ISRO, and BEL [6], [7]. Unlike contract assemblers that only populate circuit boards, Astra built Class 10,000 and Class 100,000 cleanrooms [7]. It also invested in chip-and-wire hybrid electronics, a private Near Field Antenna Test Range (NFTR), and NABL-accredited EMI/EMC chambers [7], [8], [9]. That test base helped Astra move from single parts to full RF front-ends and Active Antenna Array Units (AAAUs) [17].

A major shift arrived in the late 2010s. Astra formed Astra Rafael Comsys Private Limited (ARC), a 50:50 joint venture with Israel's Rafael Advanced Defense Systems Ltd [5], [11]. Astra brought local factories, cleanroom assembly, and Indian MoD offset eligibility [5], [11]. Rafael brought combat-tested Software Defined Radio (SDR) waveforms, electronic warfare (EW) designs, and electro-optical systems [5], [11]. Through ARC, Astra moved up from bare RF modules to full tactical radio and EW suites for the Indian Air Force, Navy, and Army [11], [13].

Between FY15 and FY26, Astra's growth was far from smooth. Twelve years of consolidated results show three clear phases [1]. From FY15 to FY19, Astra went through a steep five-year slump [1]. Sales dropped from ₹622 Cr in FY15 to ₹406 Cr in FY16, ₹390 Cr in FY17, and ₹362 Cr in FY18 [1]. Revenue hit a floor of ₹293 Cr in FY19, a 53% fall, as telecom exports ended and domestic orders stalled [1]. Operating margin (OPM) fell from 31% in FY18 to 10% in FY19 [1]. Net profit shrank to ₹10 Cr, and ROCE dropped from 29% in FY15 to 3% in FY19 [1].

In the second phase (FY20–FY22), Astra pivoted toward global defence OEM exports like Rafael, Elbit, and Thales [1], [12]. This kept its Hyderabad lines busy while Indian MoD production orders stayed in trials [1], [12]. Consolidated revenue bounced back from ₹293 Cr in FY19 to ₹467 Cr in FY20, ₹641 Cr in FY21, and ₹750 Cr in FY22 [1]. Most of that export work was lower-margin build-to-print assembly using imported MMIC kits [1], [12]. As a result, OPM stayed at 12% in both FY21 and FY22, and ROCE hovered at 10%–11% [1], [12].

The third phase (FY23–FY26) marked a real structural turn under India's Atmanirbhar Bharat push [12], [13]. DRDO-designed radars and EW systems moved from field trials into serial production. Revenue climbed from ₹816 Cr in FY23 to ₹1,163 Cr in FY26 [1]. Over that period, OPM jumped by over 1,100 basis points—from 18% in FY23 to 21% in FY24, 26% in FY25, and 29% in FY26 [1], [14]. Quarterly OPM peaked at 33% in Q4 FY26 and Q2 FY27 [1], [14]. Net profit compounded at 46.2% CAGR over five years, reaching ₹193 Cr in FY26 and ₹216 Cr TTM through September 2026 [1].

Governance & Ownership Structure: The 6.54% Promoter Footprint

A defining structural quirk of Astra Microwave is its unusually low promoter shareholding of just 6.54%, which has remained flat since at least December 2023 [1]. Unlike family-controlled Indian industrials where founders retain 50%–70% equity across generations, Astra's scientist-founders gradually pared down their stakes over three decades, leaving the company effectively institutionally and publicly owned (FIIs 10.48%, DIIs 16.52%, Public 66.47% as of September 2026) [1]. Notably, Whole-Time Director (Strategy & Business Development) Atim Kabra—the largest individual executive shareholder—reduced his personal holding by 0.41 percentage points during the September 2026 quarter to 3.12% (29.64 lakh shares), a governance datapoint that warrants ongoing tracking alongside the low promoter base.

In October 2026, Astra executed a major generational and structural handoff: long-serving Managing Director S. Gurunatha Reddy stepped down on September 30, 2026 (with a ₹1.36 Cr cash payout for his unexpired seven-month tenure) to lead the planned demerger of the Space, Meteorology, and Hydrology verticals into Astra Space Technologies Limited (ASTL), while former Joint MD Dr. M.V. Reddy assumed charge as Managing Director on October 1, 2026 [15]. Both BSE ('no adverse observations') and NSE ('no objection') cleared the draft Scheme of Arrangement on October 29, 2026, advancing the 1:1 mirror-shareholding demerger to NCLT petition and SEBI listing relaxation.

From 1994 to 2014, Astra grew alongside India's push for homegrown radar and missile hardware [6], [7]. Rather than fighting BEL for prime MoD contracts, Astra worked as the specialist RF subsystem partner for DRDO [6], [17]. It supplied receivers, exciters, and T/R modules for Akash, Rohini, and naval radars [6], [17]. This partner role kept Astra out of prime-contracting battles while locking its parts into multi-decade platforms [12], [17]. At the same time, Astra expanded into Space (ISRO) and Meteorology (IMD Doppler Weather Radars) [12], [13]. All three government verticals shared the same core RF physics [12], [13].

The 2026 demerger plan opens a fourth chapter in this 35-year arc [15]. Following BSE and NSE no-objection letters on October 29, 2026, Astra is proceeding toward NCLT approval to spin out Space, Meteorology, and Hydrology into Astra Space Technologies Limited (ASTL) on a 1:1 mirror share ratio under S. Gurunatha Reddy, with management projecting ₹2,250–₹2,500 Cr in cumulative ASTL revenue over the next four years [15]. Dr. M.V. Reddy leads the core defence unit [15]. Defence serial production (AESA radar arrays and EW suites) runs on multi-year MoD and HAL batch schedules [12], [15]. By contrast, Space and Meteorology rely on custom, lower-volume R&D and tender cycles [12], [15]. Splitting them lets each business manage its own balance sheet, cleanrooms, and capital [15].

Chapter 2

Astra Microwave designs, builds, tests, and ships RF, microwave, and millimeter-wave subsystems from 30 MHz to 40 GHz [7], [8]. Every radar, missile seeker, EW jammer, satellite transponder, and weather station needs an RF front-end. That front-end generates, amplifies, filters, phase-shifts, and receives radio signals. Astra does not build the fighter airframe, rocket motor, or satellite bus [12], [17]. Instead, it builds the high-margin electromagnetic 'eyes and ears' inside them [12], [17].

In FY26, Astra posted ₹1,163 Cr in consolidated revenue and a ₹2,141 Cr standalone order book [1], [13]. By September 30, 2026, the backlog surged to ₹3,937 Cr standalone and ₹4,708 Cr consolidated [3]. Revenue spans four end-market verticals plus the 50:50 Astra Rafael Comsys (ARC) joint venture [1], [12], [13]. By contract type in FY26, standalone sales came from serial production (51.0%), development contracts (27.6%), exports (11.9%), and maintenance and services (9.4%) [13].

Domestic defence production dominates Astra's standalone backlog (99.2% as of Q2 FY27, with a single ₹1,844 Cr ex-GST HAL Uttam AESA radar contract representing ~47% of the ₹3,937 Cr standalone book), while ARC scaled to ₹334 Cr H1 FY27 revenue and a ₹913 Cr backlog [3], [15].

1. Defence (~54%–67% of revenue; 99.2% of Q2 FY27 standalone backlog): Defence is Astra's core growth engine. It covers airborne and ground radars, EW suites, and missile seekers [3], [12]. Concrete Real-World Example: HAL and DRDO's LRDE needed an indigenous AESA fire-control radar—the Uttam AESA Radar—for the Tejas LCA Mk-1A fighter [17]. They also needed a heavier GaN-based derivative—the Virupaksha AESA Radar—for the Indian Air Force's Su-30MKI Super Sukhoi upgrade [17]. A mechanical dish cannot track multiple agile targets at once or resist modern jamming. Astra partnered with LRDE as a Development-cum-Production Partner (DcPP) to build the plank-based Active Antenna Array Unit (AAAU) [17]. Each unit packs hundreds of GaAs/GaN T/R modules, cold plates, and RF manifolds [17]. Teams assemble each unit in Class 10,000 cleanrooms in Hyderabad and stress-test it to military thermal and vibration specs [7], [8]. Astra then calibrates the array inside its 17m x 15m x 11m Near Field Antenna Test Range (NFTR) [7], [8]. Finally, Astra ships units to HAL under multi-year fixed-price contracts, anchored by the July 30, 2026 HAL contract for 122 Uttam AAAUs and 121 Interface Frames worth ₹2,205.23 Cr inclusive of GST (~₹1,844 Cr ex-GST over 5 years)—a landmark win that lifted Q2 FY27 inflows to ₹2,036 Cr, while also concentrating roughly 47% of Astra's ₹3,937 Cr standalone backlog into a single HAL fighter-radar programme [3], [17].

2. Space Electronics (~9%–11% of FY26 revenue; +88% YoY growth): Astra is one of the few private Indian firms qualified by ISRO for space-grade RF hardware [7], [13], [21]. In orbit, radiation hardness, zero-defect solder joints, and extreme thermal swings leave zero room for repair [7], [13], [21]. Concrete Real-World Example: ISRO's Space Applications Centre (SAC) builds earth-observation and defence Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) satellites like the RISAT and EOS series [12], [13], [21]. Those payloads need high-power Solid State Power Amplifiers (SSPAs), frequency synthesizers, and multi-channel radar receivers that survive launch and vacuum [12], [13], [21]. Astra's space unit builds these sealed hybrid modules with automated die-attach, gold wire-bonding, and 3D X-ray checks [7]. Each module passes burn-in testing under strict MIL and ISRO standards [7]. Contracts are milestone-based, low-volume, high-IP awards where qualification barriers block uncertified rivals [12], [13]. To sharpen capital focus, Astra's board approved a demerger of Space, Meteorology, and Hydrology into Astra Space Technologies Limited under outgoing MD S. Gurunatha Reddy, which received BSE/NSE no-objection clearance on October 29, 2026 [15].

3. Meteorology & Hydrology (~2%–16% of revenue): This unit applies Astra's radar physics to civilian weather and water tracking [12], [13]. Concrete Real-World Example: The India Meteorological Department (IMD) places S-band and X-band Doppler Weather Radars (DWRs) along India's cyclone coastlines and Himalayan ridges [12], [13]. These radars measure storm wind speeds and rain density in real time [12], [13]. Astra designs the RF subsystem, pedestal control, and signal processor, installs the radome on-site, and runs multi-year Annual Maintenance Contracts (AMCs) [12], [13]. Government weather tenders arrive in lumpy batches, so this unit swings between 2% and 16% of annual sales [12], [13].

4. Exports & Deemed Exports (~11.9% FY26 revenue; historically up to 33%): Historically, Astra supplied global defence primes like Rafael, Elbit, and Thales as a build-to-print offset partner [12], [13]. Concrete Real-World Example: When a foreign prime wins an international airborne radar or EW deal, it outsources T/R modules or wideband receivers to Astra's Hyderabad lines [12], [13]. Crucially, however, true direct foreign-OEM build-to-print exports shrank near zero by FY26 as Astra intentionally exited low-margin conversion work (12%–16% OPM); today, the 11.9% FY26 'Exports' line consists predominantly of deemed exports billed to the domestic Astra Rafael Comsys (ARC) JV, meaning genuine non-Indian sovereign customer diversification is minimal [12], [13].

5. Astra Rafael Comsys (ARC) 50:50 Joint Venture: ARC is accounted for via the equity method rather than line-by-line revenue consolidation [5], [11], [13]. Its execution accelerated sharply in FY26–FY27: revenue rose from ₹244–245 Cr (₹18.7 Cr PAT) in FY25 to ₹361 Cr in FY26, and then surged to ₹334 Cr in H1 FY27 alone—nearly matching all of FY26 in six months—while the JV order book expanded from ₹622 Cr at end-FY26 to ₹913 Cr as of September 30, 2026 (with ₹413 Cr of H1 inflows and >₹500 Cr in near-term pipeline) [5], [11], [13]. Concrete Real-World Example: The Indian Air Force needed jam-resistant Software Defined Radios (SDRs) for fighter and transport aircraft to run encrypted voice and data links. ARC licenses Rafael's waveform IP and builds, integrates, and tests the airborne SDRs in Hyderabad [5], [11], [13]. That structure wins 'Buy Indian (IDDM)' slots that neither partner could capture alone [5], [11], [13].

Chapter 3

Astra Microwave has no retail network, no dealers, and zero consumer ad spend. Its sales channel is direct B2G and B2B defence OEM qualification [4], [12]. Every rupee of domestic revenue flows through a highly concentrated institutional funnel: in FY26, DRDO accounted for 28.2% of revenue, BEL and other DPSUs for 22.7%, IMD and meteorological agencies for 18.0%, deemed exports via ARC/Aelius for 11.9%, and ISRO/SAC for 6.4%—meaning three sovereign customer buckets generate over half of annual sales [6], [12], [17]. Once Astra qualifies as a Development-cum-Production Partner (DcPP), switching suppliers mid-programme is costly [7], [9], [17]. A buyer would have to repeat years of environmental, EMI/EMC, and flight-clearance tests, creating high switching barriers—yet concentrating receivables and milestone sign-off power in a handful of state agencies [7], [9], [17].

In exports, Astra historically plugged into global Tier-1 supply chains [12], [13]. It served Israeli and European primes like Rafael, Elbit, and Thales, often driven by Indian MoD offset rules [12], [13]. That split created two very different margin profiles. Export build-to-print orders use customer-supplied or imported MMIC kits and earn 12%–16% operating margins [12], [13]. By contrast, domestic IP-led production—where Astra owns the RF design and localises assembly—earns 28%–33% operating margins [12], [13], [14].

Semiconductor and RF part lead times are the main supply chain bottleneck [4]. High-power GaN and GaAs MMIC dies, space-grade FPGAs, precision connectors, and RF laminates come mostly from US, European, and Taiwanese foundries. Defence contracts impose stiff fines for late delivery. To avoid delays, Astra buys long-lead imported chips 6 to 12 months before customer delivery dates [1], [4]. That buffer explains why inventory days stretched to 389 days in FY25 and 394 days in FY26 [1], [4].

Manufacturing and testing sit across two southern Indian aerospace hubs [6], [7]. In Hyderabad (Telangana), Astra runs five units across Bollaram (Units 1 & 2) and Maheshwaram Hardware Park / E-City (Units 3, 4 & 7) [6], [7]. These sites house Class 10,000 and Class 100,000 cleanrooms, SMT lines, chip-and-wire bonding, 3D X-ray checks, and stress chambers [6], [7]. In Bengaluru (Karnataka), Astra runs an R&D and integration hub at the Aerospace Park near the airport, right next to HAL and LRDE [6]. Astra also owns Near Field and Far Field Antenna Test Ranges (NFTR/FFTR) and an NABL-accredited EMI/EMC chamber up to 40 GHz [8], [9]. Having those labs in-house removes external test queues that slow down smaller peers [8], [9].
