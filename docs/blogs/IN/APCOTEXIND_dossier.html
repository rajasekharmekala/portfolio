1. What the company does

Apcotex Industries makes emulsion polymers. An emulsion polymer is a plastic or rubber that is made in water and sold either as a milky liquid, called latex, or as a dry crumb or powder. The liquid grades are used as glue, binder and coating. The dry grades are rubber that customers shape into hoses, rollers and shoe soles. The company began in 1980 as the latex division of Asian Paints and became a separate company in 1991 under Atul Choksey.[1] The promoter group holds 58.23% of the shares, and none of those shares is pledged.[12]

The company has two plants. Taloja in Maharashtra makes 103,000 tonnes a year of synthetic latex, wet weight, and 7,000 tonnes of high styrene rubber.[29] Valia in Gujarat makes 21,000 tonnes a year of nitrile rubber and 50,000 tonnes of nitrile latex, the liquid used to dip gloves.[29][1] ICRA, the rating agency, says Apcotex is the only domestic maker of nitrile rubber, nitrile latex and high styrene rubber in India.[1] The Taloja plant sits 36 km from the JNPT port and Valia is 40 km from the Hajira and Dahej ports, so shipping out is short.[30]

Customers are makers of other things. Paper mills use the latex to coat paperboard. Tyre-cord dippers use vinyl pyridine latex to bond fabric to rubber. Construction chemical firms use it in waterproofing and bonding agents, carpet makers use it as backing, and glove makers dip formers in nitrile latex.[2][3] Named customers include ITC, Asian Paints, UltraTech Cement, Pidilite, MRF and SRF.[2] No single customer gave 10% or more of revenue in FY26 or FY25.[3] Exports were ₹452.60 crore out of ₹1,426.84 crore of product sales in FY26, which is 31.7%, and the company ships to more than 45 countries.[3][30] Our calculation: 45,259.94 divided by 142,684.00 lakh.

The money works like a toll on a chemical chain. The company buys styrene, butadiene and acrylonitrile, which come from oil and gas, turns them into polymers, and sells them at prices set by formula against those inputs.[1] The cost of materials was ₹988.97 crore on ₹1,441.50 crore of sales in FY26, or 68.6% (our calculation from the audited statement).[3] What the company keeps is the gap between the price it sells at and the price it paid. That gap is called the spread. When raw material prices jump, Apcotex passes the rise on with a short lag, and an unsold stock bought earlier at lower prices becomes worth more. That is an inventory gain. When prices fall, the same stock loses value.[1][10] This is why the margin, measured before interest, tax and depreciation, has ranged from 9.0% to 22.3% in five years. It is also why the next chapters spend so much time on what is cycle and what is company.

How the glove plant changed the story

In FY23 the company spent ₹186.99 crore on plant and equipment, its largest outlay in five years.[5] Management has put the Valia glove-latex plant at ₹150 crore to ₹160 crore and a Taloja expansion at about ₹50 crore.[11] The glove plant was planned in the boom after 2020. By the time it was running, makers elsewhere had added capacity too, and prices fell.[7] In July 2024 management said the plant ran at about 50% of capacity and was about break-even at the operating line.[11] By May 2026 it ran at almost full capacity, but management still described the market as oversupplied.[8]

For a reader, the point is simple. The company’s growth since 2022 has been bought with capital, and the return on that capital depends on a market it does not control. Chapter 3 shows what that did to returns, and Chapter 2B tests whether it has recovered.

  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash from operations less the cash spent on plant and equipment.
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and customer bills, less what is owed to suppliers.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The moat is real in one place and thin in the rest. Apcotex is the only Indian maker of nitrile rubber, and the trade-remedies authority records that Apcotex told it so and that no one disputed the claim.[1][25] That sounds like a monopoly. It is not, because the company supplies only about 30% of Indian demand for that product range and the other 70% arrives by import.[7] The plant is full, so the protection is about the lack of a local rival, not about pricing power. If imports get cheaper, the local price follows them down.

The company tried to turn that position into a duty on imports. It filed an anti-dumping case against nitrile rubber from China, the European Union, Korea and Russia in 2024, and the authority issued final findings on 25 September 2025 in its favour.[25][26] ICRA reports that the Finance Ministry did not implement the recommendation.[1] This has happened before. The authority’s own record shows importers arguing that the ministry rejected earlier recommendations on the same product.[25] On the July 2026 call management said it has no anti-dumping protection today and competes without it.[7] Investors should treat a duty as a possible bonus, not as part of the case.

Where the business is stronger is in the plain operating choices. The plants can burn coal or gas, so management says that when gas ran short in 2026 Apcotex kept running while rivals with one fuel did not. Each raw material has several suppliers, so when the Strait of Hormuz closed it says it bought from other regions.[7] Buying fast in a shock is a skill, and it shows in the result. ICRA says the company’s formula pricing lets it pass on cost increases with a limited lag, and its AA- rating with a stable outlook was reaffirmed on 26 August 2026.[1] The customer list is also wide: no buyer reached 10% of revenue in the last two years.[3]

The weaknesses are the ones every spread business has. The business buys oil-linked chemicals and sells to price-sensitive industries, so profit moves with the spread more than with volume. In three of the five fiscal years from FY22 the EBITDA margin was 12.3% or lower, and it was 9.0% in FY25.[2][5] The company has a record of capital spent into a downturn. The glove plant cost about ₹150 crore to ₹160 crore and was at about break-even at the operating line in mid-2024.[11] ICRA lists raw material swings, imports from Korea, China and Russia, and export exposure as the main constraints on the rating.[1]

How much of the June 2026 quarter was the company and how much was the market

The record quarter had a clear outside cause. The conflict that began in late February 2026 effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, and styrene prices rose sharply in Asia as naphtha and benzene costs jumped.[23] Korean producers cut rates and declared force majeure, which tightened supply.[23] Apcotex stayed supplied while others did not, and its operating margin went from 10.3% a year earlier to 22.3%.[2] The same pattern was already easing by May 2026. ICIS reported sustained upstream losses and Asian buyers of styrene-butadiene rubber waiting for butadiene prices to bottom before committing.[24]

Four listed peers show the same jump. Supreme Petrochem’s operating margin was 19% in the June 2026 quarter, after 16% in March 2026 and 6% to 8% in the three quarters before that. Styrenix reached 22% after 14% in March and 5% to 11% before. Tinna Rubber reached 22% after 16% to 18%. BASF India reached 10% after 3% to 6%.[18][19][20][21] Styrenix’s profit rose 166.37% over the year even though its revenue rose only 7.14%.[22] So some of the margin is the market. Management agrees: it says it would not annualise this level of benefit, and that about 2 points of margin were inventory gains.[7] What belongs to the company is the supply record. What belongs to the market will fade when the war ends.

3. The numbers, in plain words

The numbers tell one story over five years: a company whose profit moves with a spread, with a plant-building phase in the middle and a clean balance sheet at the end. Sales grew from ₹956.89 crore in FY22 to ₹1,441.50 crore in FY26, a rise of 10.8% a year.[5][3] Profit after tax did not grow at all in that time, going from ₹98.81 crore to ₹101.41 crore.[5][3] The reason is the margin. In FY22 and FY23 the company earned 14.6% and 14.7% at the EBITDA line, after the post-2020 boom, and in FY24 and FY25 it earned 10.1% and 9.0% as industry capacity caught up and the glove plant started.[2][5][11]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a weak joint. This chapter names the weakest assumption in each one, builds the strongest case against our own view, and then picks the single claim that matters most. Reading it is the cheapest way to see what would make us wrong.[7]

Weakest assumption in the FY28 EPS estimate of ₹29.0

The weakest assumption is the 14.0% margin. It is above the five-year average of 12.1% and above FY26’s 12.3%, and it is built on plants running full and nitrile latex staying profitable.[2][7] The evidence that would show it wrong is a September 2026 quarter below 15% with no inventory gain, or any quarter where nitrile latex falls back below 15% at the operating line. We will know on the September 2026 results, due by 14 November 2026, and again on the December 2026 and March 2027 results.[27] The error can go both ways, which is why the range in Chapter 5 is wide.

Weakest assumption in the ₹675 target

The weakest assumption is that the market will pay 23.31 times FY28 profit in October 2027. The ten-year median is a fair guess for an average year, but the multiple is a judgement and it moves a lot. The year-end multiple was 43.02 in FY24 and 16.06 in FY26, the lowest in ten years, in a year when profit was rising.[16] If the market pays 20 times, the target is ₹580 and the stock is not worth buying. If it pays 26 times, the target is ₹754 and the gate is cleared. The multiple will be visible each quarter as results arrive.

Weakest assumption in the 2.06 asymmetry

The weakest assumption is the stop. It is only ₹37.65, or 6.3%, below the price, and a typical day moves the stock 3.6%. A stop that close is hit by ordinary noise, and a wider stop lowers the ratio further. A stop at ₹540 would make the ratio 1.35.[15] The stop is based on a swing low of ₹570.60, which is a 20-day low, so it is a short-term level and not an old floor. The 63-day base low of ₹526.00 is a stronger floor but is 12% below the price.

The red-team case against owning the stock

The strongest bear case runs like this. The June 2026 quarter was a once-in-a-decade spread, and peers all printed the same jump.
