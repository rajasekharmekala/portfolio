Terms used in this report

This chapter defines each technical and financial term once, with an everyday comparison, so the later chapters can use the words without stopping to explain them. The terms appear in the order the framework needs them.

ROCE stands for return on capital employed. It asks how much operating profit a business makes for each rupee tied up in factories, machines and working stock, much as a shopkeeper asks how much a stall earns on the stock sitting on its shelves. A figure of 20 percent means about 20 paise a year on each rupee used[1].

ROE stands for return on equity, the profit a company earns on the money its shareholders own. For the year to March 2026 it was 17 percent[1].

Debt-to-equity is the company's borrowings divided by the capital its owners have put in. A value of 0.15 means borrowings are about a seventh of owners' capital. The framework's ceiling is 0.70, which keeps the company from being carried by its lenders[1].

CFO to PAT is cash from operations divided by profit after tax, summed over three years. It asks whether reported profit has arrived as cash. A ratio of 1.57 means the company collected ₹1.57 of operating cash for every ₹1 of profit[1].

EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is the profit from running the plants before financing and accounting charges are taken out. OPM (operating profit margin) is the same profit as a share of sales, and it shows how much of each sales rupee the core business keeps[1].

P/E is short for the price-to-earnings ratio, the share price divided by earnings per share. A P/E of 19.4 means investors pay about ₹19 for each ₹1 of a year's profit per share[1].

RSI stands for relative strength index, a gauge from 0 to 100 of how quickly a price has recently risen or fallen. Readings below 30 are called oversold and readings above 70 overbought, and the reading of 50.85 for this stock is neither.

ATR stands for average true range, the typical daily price swing over the last 14 sessions. It works as a ruler: a distance of 1.2 ATR means the price is about one and a fifth of a normal day's swing away from the reference level. For Apcotex one ATR is ₹22.84 in the ledger.

Stage 2 is the rising phase of a stock's cycle, when the price holds above averages that are themselves rising. Stage 3 is the topping phase that often follows, when the rise stalls and the averages flatten. Stage 4 is the falling phase, when the price breaks below averages that are heading down.

base is a sideways stretch, often a few weeks to a few months, in which a stock trades inside a narrow range before its next move. It is like a car idling at a junction: the stock is waiting for a direction to be chosen. The 63-session base low for this stock is ₹512.80.

breakout is the moment the price closes above the top of a base, ideally on heavier trading than usual. It signals that the waiting period has ended, although some breakouts fail.

retest is the return of the price to a level it has just broken through. If the level holds as support on the way back, the breakout is confirmed. If it fails, the breakout was false.

pivot is the price level a stock has to clear to confirm a breakout, usually the top of its base. The ledger does not record a top-of-base price, so the momentum gate measures distance from the 63-session base low instead.

stop loss is the price at which a holding is sold to cap the loss. It is set below the level at which the trend would count as failed, so the sale happens before the loss grows.

asymmetry is the ratio of expected upside to possible downside: how many rupees of gain the rules expect for each rupee that could be lost. A ratio of 3.0 means three rupees of expected gain for every rupee at risk.

measured move is a price target found by adding the height of a base to its breakout point. Chart readers use it to estimate how far a move might run once a base has resolved.

guidance is the company's own forecast of what it expects to achieve in the coming period. It is useful because it is the management's stated view, and it is judged against what the company later delivers.

Momentum gate is a code check on the frozen ledger snapshot. Test A asks whether the stock is already in momentum, and Test B asks whether it is entering momentum. A candidate has to pass one test, and failing both means no allocation[26].

Anchored target is a target built from a checkable input: a forward earnings figure multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings multiple, or a measured move from a verified base. A broker's average target is not accepted, because it cannot be recomputed from stated inputs.

How the framework works for this company

The framework is a set of hurdles that a company must clear in a fixed order. The framework never lets a good business excuse a weak chart, and it never lets a strong chart excuse a weak business. Apcotex passes the business tests and fails the chart tests. The verdict follows the chart, which is why the first numbers a reader should look at are the technical ones.

Fatal-flaw red team

Before a verdict is accepted, the strongest honest case against it should be made. The three flaws below are the reasons a careful investor might still see this dossier as too cautious, or the reasons the rejection could be wrong. Each is tested against the record.

Flaw 1: the earnings jump may not last

The June quarter's profit of about ₹79 crore is more than double the ₹35 crore of the March quarter[1][11], and it is up from ₹19 crore a year earlier[10]. Management attributed the gain to price realisation rather than volume, and volumes were lower[10]. Price gains tied to raw material costs can reverse when those costs fall. Set against that, full-year profit was ₹101 crore in FY26, below the ₹108 crore of FY23, and the three-year profit growth rate is −2.2 percent[1]. The stock fell 6.48 percent on the results day, to ₹637.45[9], which suggests the market did not treat the quarter as a new level. The test that would settle the question is the September 2026 quarter, which is not yet in the record.

Flaw 2: the export engine is under strain

Management described the West Asia situation as causing volatility in raw material prices and some moderation in export demand in the March quarter[11]. In June it said export volumes were hit by logistics disruption and higher ocean freight[10]. Exports are a part of the business that the company cannot fully control. If the disruption continues, growth in the domestic market has to carry more of the load. The record does not show the export share of revenue, so the size of the risk cannot be measured here.

Flaw 3: the chart can keep falling even with good numbers

The stock sits below its 50-day average, has lost relative strength over the last 28 sessions, and trades on a fraction of its normal volume. Those are the conditions under which a stock drifts. Even a strong quarter can fail to lift a stock that buyers are no longer supporting, as the weak 28-session relative strength and low volume show. The verdict stands until the chart proves otherwise, which is the framework's point. The cost of that discipline is that waiting may mean missing a move, and the framework accepts that cost in exchange for not buying a falling stock.
