1. What the company does

Apcotex Industries, listed in India as APCOTEXIND, makes synthetic latex and synthetic rubber. Latex here means a milky, water-based polymer that is sprayed, dipped or spread; rubber is the solid form. Customers use the latex to coat paper, back carpets, bind cement mixes, dip tyre cord and make nitrile gloves. The rubber goes into hoses, seals, rice-dehusking rolls and footwear[4][1]. The business began in 1980 as a division of Asian Paints and became a separate company in 1991 under Atul Choksey. Abhiraj Choksey is vice chairman and managing director[4].

Taloja in Maharashtra makes 1,03,000 tonnes a year of synthetic latex and 7,000 tonnes of high styrene rubber. Valia in Gujarat makes 21,000 tonnes of nitrile rubber (NBR) and 50,000 tonnes of nitrile latex[4]. Nitrile is an oil-resistant polymer made from two petrochemicals, butadiene and acrylonitrile. The company calls itself the only Indian maker of NBR, high styrene rubber and nitrile latex[11], and says it holds about 30 percent of the Indian market for the product range discussed, with the rest imported[5]. ICRA put its share of Indian NBR demand at 25 to 30 percent in 2024[33].

The sales mix is wide. In the September 2025 quarter about 70 percent was latex and 30 percent rubber. Inside latex, paper and construction made 16 to 18 percent, carpet and textiles 11 to 12 percent, nitrile latex 15 to 16 percent and tyre cord about 10 percent[8]. Management later put nitrile latex at 15 to 20 percent of sales[6] and gloves at 8 to 10 percent of the business[5]; the two figures overlap and we do not reconcile them. Named customers include ITC, JK Paper, Asian Paints, UltraTech, Pidilite, MRF, SRF and Top Glove[11]. The latest export share is not published; it was 31 to 32 percent in September 2025[32]. No product is above one fifth of sales[6], so earnings depend less on one customer or product than at a pure glove maker, and more on butadiene, acrylonitrile and styrene prices than on any end market. NBR has the least competition, since only imports compete with it, but it runs near full capacity and cannot grow without the new Valia lines.

The money comes from the gap between selling price and material cost. Materials were 68.6 percent of FY26 revenue (₹988.97 crore of ₹1,441.50 crore)[1], and the main inputs are butadiene, acrylonitrile and styrene, all tied to oil and gas chains[33]. When inputs rise, selling prices follow, so revenue swings with oil even when tonnes barely move, and profit depends on how wide the gap stays. Chapter 2 asks whether that gap belongs to the business or to the cycle.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of Apcotex is a short list of products that few others make in India, sold into many industries by a company with almost no net debt. FY26 revenue was ₹1,441.50 crore and profit after tax ₹101.41 crore, up from ₹54.06 crore a year earlier[1][2]. The rating firm ICRA reaffirmed its AA- rating with a stable outlook on ₹588.89 crore of bank limits on 26 August 2026[12]. The weakness is that the same business has earned very different margins in different years. The EBITDA margin was 10.1 percent in FY24, 9.0 percent in FY25, 12.3 percent in FY26 and 22.3 percent in the June 2026 quarter[4].

This chapter explains what holds the profit up, what moves it, and which moves belong to the cycle. The cycle matters most here, because the latest quarter is the best in the company's history and the market is already trying to price what follows.

What protects the profit: local supply and the NBR position

A moat is something that protects profit from competitors, as a moat protects a castle. Apcotex has three small ones. It is the only Indian maker of NBR, high styrene rubber and nitrile latex[11][1]. ICRA put its share of Indian NBR demand at 25 to 30 percent in 2024, with imports meeting the rest, and the managing director gave about 30 percent for the product range he discussed in July 2026, so customers who want local supply have one source[33][5]. Its plants can run on gas or coal, while many rivals have one fuel, and it buys each raw material from several sources[5].

The limits are plain. The protection against imports is cost, service and approvals, not law. The company asked for an anti-dumping duty, the trade-remedy body DGTR found in its favour in the September 2025 quarter, and the finance ministry never notified the duty[8][7]. On 30 July 2026 the managing director said none was in force[5]. ICRA found in 2024 that the top ten customers gave 20 to 30 percent of revenue, and no newer figure is published[33]. So the moat is real but narrow, and its test is whether margin holds when imports and new Asian capacity press on price. The next section turns to input costs.

Input costs: butadiene, acrylonitrile and how well prices follow them

Pass-through means the company charges customers for changes in its input cost. Butadiene is the key input, and the annual report admits it comes from limited domestic suppliers[1]. On the November 2025 call an analyst noted three straight quarters of falling raw material prices, and management said such runs had occurred three or four times in 20 years[8]. Apcotex still lifted its margin from 8.96 percent in FY25 to 12.1 percent in the September 2025 quarter, so volume and plant use mattered more than price in that run[4][32].

Then the West Asia war reversed the pattern. A trade source puts the butadiene price index in Japan up 36.5 percent in the June 2026 quarter, at about USD 1,921 a tonne, and the nitrile rubber index up 26.2 percent, with both easing in June on weak tyre, plastics and car demand[23][24]. A Malaysian glove association reported a severe shortage of nitrile latex on 26 March 2026 because of the Hormuz blockade[26]. By September acrylonitrile makers in China were losing about 1,155 yuan a tonne, with supply up and demand weak[25]. Apcotex bought raw material early and lost no plant day[6]. Our own arithmetic shows the effect: materials net of the stock change cost 64.5 percent of revenue in the June 2026 quarter, against 73.8 percent a year earlier, 68.7 percent in FY26 and 63.1 percent in the March 2026 quarter[3][1]. The gap was already wide before the quarter that made the headlines.

Demand: gloves, tyres, paper and construction

Domestic numbers were quite strong in July 2026, across paper, construction and rubber goods, and domestic volume rose about 10 percent in the June quarter[5]. SBR latex, used in paper coating and carpet backing, rose 9.6 percent in Thailand in the June quarter[28]. Exports fell 10 to 12 percent because the Strait of Hormuz was shut, and management calls that a short-term blip that reverses once the war ends, while admitting the fighting had flared up again[5].

Nitrile latex for gloves is the most cyclical line. After the pandemic boom the world added glove capacity, and the managing director says the glove chain is still in an over-capacity mode, though much better than before[5]. He also said the United States raised its duty on Chinese gloves from 50 to 100 percent[5]. A glove supplier's blog says Chinese glove prices nearly doubled from March to June 2026; we treat it as colour, not evidence[27]. Management points to added Malaysian capacity coming in July and August 2026 and will wait three to four months before deciding the second Valia stage[5]. For you, tyre-cord and paper demand look steady, while nitrile latex brings the biggest upside and the biggest reversal.

Margin cyclicality and the new capacity

The record shows a business that earns 15 percent in a good stretch and 9 percent in a poor one. Screener, a data site, lists the operating margin at 15 percent in FY22 and FY23, 10 percent in FY24, 9 percent in FY25, 12 percent in FY26 and 16 percent over the last twelve months, and return on capital employed at 35, 28, 13, 13 and 20 percent[16]. The managing director says every plant ran full after COVID, then rivals added capacity worldwide and margins fell below normal, especially in nitrile latex[5]. The stated range has crept up with the results: 12 to 15 percent in July 2025, 13 to 16 percent in January 2026 and 15 to 16 percent in July 2026[9][7][5].

Capacity is the second lever. NBR ran at about 95 percent in the September 2025 quarter, and the latex plants above 80 percent[8]. The board approved ₹210 crore on 5 November 2025 for 37,000 tonnes of latex and 14,600 tonnes of NBR, in phases by the June quarter of FY28, funded by cash and debt[10]. In July 2026 the figure was about ₹220 crore with 15 to 20 percent spent, and management says it adds about ₹600 crore of sales[5]; Axis Securities put the potential at ₹550 to 600 crore[32]. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on a weakest assumption. This chapter names each one, says what evidence would show it wrong, and when that evidence arrives. The key date is 28 October 2026, when the board meets on the September-quarter and half-year results, with an investor call on 29 October[16][20]. Weakest assumption of the margin: that spreads settle at about 15.5 percent and no lower

The central case needs the EBITDA margin to fall from 22.3 percent to about 17 percent in FY27 and 15.5 percent in FY28. Management says the June quarter included about 2 points of inventory gain, constrained supply and a timing benefit, and that it would not annualise it[5]. The March 2026 quarter was already near 18 percent with about ₹18 crore of one-offs added back[6][4]. Evidence against: an EBITDA margin under 15.0 percent in the September quarter or the December quarter; for context, the March 2026 quarter was 13.8 percent. A 14.0 percent FY28 margin takes the ratio at the zone top to 2.17, and 13.5 percent takes it to 0.5.

Weakest assumption of the volume: that exports return and domestic demand holds

Total volume fell 10 to 12 percent in the June quarter because exports fell, while domestic volume rose about 10 percent[5].
