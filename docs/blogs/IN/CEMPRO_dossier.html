Cemindia Projects builds ports, metro tunnels, airports and data centre shells, and the Adani Group has controlled it since May 2025[5][2]. The business looks strong on paper, with a ₹31,307 crore order book, small borrowings and no pledged promoter shares[4][14]. 1. What the company does

Cemindia Projects Limited is a heavy civil contractor. It does not own what it builds. A customer hires it to design a project, buy the materials and build it, and then pays it for the work done.[4] The company began in 1931 as the Indian branch office of The Cementation Company of the UK, was taken over by ITD of Thailand in 2005 and traded as ITD Cementation until an Adani Group company took control in 2025.[5]

Its skill is the awkward job. The fleet includes eight tunnel boring machines, 279 cranes, 46 hydraulic piling rigs and four jack-up barges, and four more tunnel boring machines are on order.[5] Few contractors can show that kit and the track record that goes with it. Management says maritime work has only three to five serious bidders in India.[6]

The order book is the work already won but not yet built. At 30 June 2026 it stood at ₹31,306.80 crore across eight sectors, and the table below shows how it splits and how fast it moved in one quarter.[4]

Who pays Cemindia

Customers split into government (10%), public sector companies (27%) and private companies (63%) by order book value.[5] The private share is flattering. The placement document says 52.53% of the order book comes from Adani Group companies and 47.47% from outside customers.[4] A year earlier the group share was about 20%, and by February 2026 it was 27%.[10][8] In FY26, Adani Group companies paid ₹2,834.21 crore, which is 28% of standalone revenue of ₹9,983 crore.[2]

How the company gets paid

Revenue is booked as work progresses, not when a project ends. Customers usually pay a mobilisation advance of 5% to 10%, then pay monthly for certified work, and hold back a retention sum until the project is handed over.[10] Advances from customers were ₹1,472.04 crore at 31 March 2026, and only about 10% of them carried interest.[4][7] Work done but not yet billed sits on the balance sheet as unbilled revenue, called contract assets, and stood at ₹2,263.49 crore.[2] That line is the one to watch, because cash only arrives once the bill is certified and paid.

Who controls the company, and what the October 2026 share sale did

Renew Exim DMCC, an Adani Group entity, bought 46.64% from Italian-Thai and took 20.83% more through an open offer, reaching 67.46% on 28 May 2025.[2][11] The promoter has not pledged any shares.[14][23] On 19 October 2026 the company sold 1,26,35,379 new shares to institutions at ₹1,108.00 each, raising ₹1,400.00 crore, which cut the promoter stake to 62.84% without any sale by the promoter.[18][1] Of the net proceeds, ₹400.00 crore is for equipment, ₹503.13 crore repays borrowings, ₹126.30 crore is for the cash that running projects need, and ₹343.15 crore is general.[4]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong in one narrow way and weak in two broad ways. It is strong in the complex jobs that few firms can win: ports in open sea, underground metro and large foundations. It is weak because the work is low in margin, and because its biggest new customer is also its owner. The evidence is below, and each claim carries a source.

Strength 1: credentials that are hard to copy

A tunnelling or marine contract usually requires proof of earlier projects of the same type, plus the machines to do them. Cemindia built the breakwater and jetty at Vizhinjam Port, the underground stations of Mumbai Metro Line 3, and the Udangudi offshore breakwater with a 9.1 km pipe conveyor.[11][5] Management puts the market for maritime work at three players, or five if newcomers count, and says its hit rate on bids is about 15%, down from 20% as it bids for more work.[6] Those credentials help explain why the order book doubled in four years, from ₹15,548 crore in FY22 to ₹31,307 crore.[5]

Strength 2: a stronger balance sheet and cheaper credit

Three rating agencies moved Cemindia up in 2026. ICRA rose from A+ to AA (Stable), CARE from A+ to AA- (Stable), and Crisil assigned AA/Stable with the top short-term grade A1+.[16][17][15] The parent's standing also helps with bank guarantees, which are the main cash cost of winning work. Some government buyers have cut the performance guarantee they demand from 10% to 5%, which frees credit lines.[6] Borrowings were ₹1,001 crore at 31 March 2026 against equity of ₹2,399 crore.[1][2]

Weakness 1: the margin is ordinary

Operating profit was 10.1% of sales in FY26 and has stayed between 9% and 10% since FY24.[1] Management itself says the normal range is 10% to 10.5%, and that the 11.9% reported for FY26 was lifted by about ₹150 crore of old claims paid by customers.[7] A contractor with a 10% margin has little room for mistakes. A 1 percentage point cost overrun on a ₹12,500 crore year is ₹125 crore, which is about a fifth of FY26 profit.

Weakness 2: the owner is the biggest customer

Adani Group companies were 52.53% of the order book at 30 June 2026, and ₹6,000 crore of the ₹8,500 crore won in Q1 FY27 came from the group.[4][6] Management says group work is won by competitive bidding with no fixed price, and the placement document states that no group company has to award work to Cemindia.[6][4] That may be so, but the share moved from about 20% to 53% in 15 months, and the company can no longer be judged as an independent contractor. The upside is that group customers pay advances: contract liabilities from them were ₹515.11 crore against ₹198.17 crore of unbilled work, so the group was net funding Cemindia at 31 March 2026.[2]

The industry backdrop

Crisil expects infrastructure spending to grow 8% to 10% a year over FY26 to FY28, and large construction firms to grow revenue 9% to 10% in FY27 and FY28, and 50 to 75 basis points of margin pressure in FY27 from fuel, freight and insurance costs.[28] Cemindia's own forecast of 25% growth is two and a half times that pace. That gap is the heart of the debate, and it is why Chapter 2B tests whether management has met its past forecasts.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has an excellent record on balance sheet discipline and a poor record on revenue forecasts. It said revenue would grow 25% in FY26 and delivered 8.8%. It then repeated 25% for FY27, and the first quarter grew 5.6%.[11][1][6] The explanation is credible: Vadhvan Port has produced no revenue for more than a year because of delays outside the company's control, and management says about ₹12,000 crore of orders, including Delhi Metro, Pune Metro, Munger and Morsagar, had no progress because design and mobilisation take six to seven months.[6] Credible is not proven, so we treat the forecast as a hypothesis to test on 5 November 2026.

The reversal on fundraising matters more than the revenue miss. On 30 April 2026 the managing director said there was no plan to raise funds.[7] On 29 July the board had an enabling resolution for ₹5,000 crore, and management said the current order book did not need it.[6] The money raised, ₹1,400.00 crore, will repay ₹503.13 crore of borrowings and buy equipment, mostly for large-diameter tunnelling.[4] We do not read bad faith into this, because the company only had to find buyers once it saw the pipeline, but we do read it as a sign that plans change faster than the calls suggest.
