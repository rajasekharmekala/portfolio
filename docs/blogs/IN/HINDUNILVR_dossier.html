Before you read

Hindustan Unilever (HUL) is India’s largest packaged consumer goods company, selling soaps, detergents, tea and health drinks through about 9 million shops[1]. We tested it against our three-layer checklist, and the verdict is REJECTED for now because the share price is in a falling trend[2]. The business is strong, with 7% volume growth in the September 2026 quarter and almost no debt[3][4]. The price assumes about 16% yearly profit growth for ten years, while continuing profit grew only 1.7% a year from FY23 to FY26[2][5]. The call would be wrong if margins hold without ad cuts and the price closes above ₹1,853, its 20-day average.

1. What the company does

Hindustan Unilever sells the everyday products that most Indian households buy every week. These include Surf Excel and Rin for washing clothes, Lifebuoy, Lux and Dove soaps, Pepsodent and Closeup toothpaste, Brooke Bond tea, Bru coffee, Horlicks and Boost health drinks, and Kissan sauces[3][1]. It has 21 brands that each sell more than ₹1,000 crore a year, ships about 85 billion packs a year and reaches roughly 9 million shops[1]. Turnover for the year to March 2026 was ₹63,763 crore[6]. Turnover means sales of products and services (₹63,636 crore plus ₹127 crore). Revenue from operations of ₹64,468 crore, which the tables in chapter 3 use, also includes ₹705 crore of other operating revenue[6]. The company’s 23.6% EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) margin divides by turnover, and the 23.3% operating margin in chapter 3 divides Screener’s operating profit by revenue from operations. The parent, Unilever, owns 61.9% of the shares and supplies brands, technology and central services in return for a fee[7].

The household is the final customer, but HUL sells in the first step to distributors and retailers. Goods move from HUL factories to distributors, then to general-trade shops, supermarkets, e-commerce sites and quick-commerce apps. Quick commerce kept growing at strong double digits in the June 2026 quarter, and general-trade reach grew from small towns and rural India[8]. The filings we read do not give a customer-concentration figure, so we cannot size the largest buyer. The spread across 9 million outlets suggests that no single retailer is critical, but we treat that as an inference and not a fact.

A rupee of sales works like this. In the September 2026 quarter, raw materials and bought-in goods cost about 52 paise of each rupee of revenue. That left a gross margin of about 47.6% on our own arithmetic from the profit and loss statement[4]. HUL then spent about 8.1 paise on advertising and promotion[4]. The rest went on people, freight, factories and fees to Unilever before the company reached an EBITDA margin of 22.6%[3]. The margin is the gap between what a pack sells for and what everything behind it costs, so small changes in input prices move profit quickly.

Cash arrives before it leaves. At 30 September 2026 HUL owed suppliers ₹16,052 crore, while customers owed HUL only ₹3,698 crore and inventory was ₹5,126 crore[4]. Screener shows a cash conversion cycle of minus 89 days for FY26[5], which means suppliers fund the business. That is why cash profit tracks reported profit closely and why borrowings are only ₹14 crore, apart from ₹1,658 crore of lease liabilities[4].

Demand is steady but not immune to shocks. Soap, detergent and tea volumes follow rural incomes and the monsoon. Costs follow palm oil, crude-linked chemicals, tea, sugar and milk. In April to June 2026, industry volumes fell 2%, rural volumes fell 5% and 68% of categories lost volume, according to NielsenIQ[9]. HUL’s own volumes grew 5% in the same quarter[10], so it gained ground against a shrinking market. Cost shocks are the larger recurring risk: Brent crude was near $115 a barrel on 28 October 2026, up about 90% in a year[11].

Reporting basis: what the ice-cream demerger changed

HUL moved its ice-cream business (Kwality Wall’s, Cornetto, Magnum) into a separate company, Kwality Wall’s (India) Limited (KWIL), with effect from 1 December 2025. Shareholders on the record date of 5 December 2025 received one KWIL share for each HUL share[12]. For tax purposes, 98.09% of a holder’s original cost stays with the HUL shares and 1.91% moves to the KWIL shares[12]. The court order sanctioning the scheme is dated 30 October and 6 November 2025[13]. Unilever holds 61.9% of KWIL[14]. We have not valued the KWIL shares that HUL holders received, so total return to a long-time holder is somewhat better than the HUL share price alone.

The demerger changes how every number must be read. Since December 2025, HUL shows ice cream as discontinued operations and restates the earlier columns. Revenue for FY25 is ₹63,121 crore as first reported[15] but ₹61,328 crore on the restated continuing basis[5], a gap of about ₹1,793 crore, or 2.8%, that is our arithmetic for the size of ice cream. FY22 to FY24 on the Screener page are as originally reported and still include ice cream. Any five-year growth rate therefore mixes two bases, and we say so wherever we quote one.

Profit needs more care. FY26 reported profit is ₹15,059 crore, but that includes ₹4,407 crore from discontinued operations, mostly a one-time gain booked when ice cream left the group[6]. Profit from continuing operations is ₹10,652 crore. Earnings per share (EPS) from continuing operations is ₹45.25, against ₹64.01 reported[4]. Screener’s headline three-year profit growth of 14% is lifted by that gain[5]; on the continuing basis the FY23 to FY26 rate is 1.7% a year, from ₹10,143 crore to ₹10,652 crore.

Our rule for this report is simple. Per-share figures and profit are on the continuing basis unless a cell says reported. Sales growth for FY25 to FY26 (5.1%) is like for like because both years are restated. Longer growth rates mix bases and are flagged. The price history in our ledger is not adjusted for the demerger. Because only 1.91% of cost moved to KWIL, unadjusted prices from before December 2025 overstate the old level by about 2%, which does not change the downtrend reading in chapter 4. The ledger and Screener also differ on the 52-week high (₹2,667.20 against ₹2,480[5]), most likely because one series is adjusted and the other is not, so we use the ledger for every technical figure.

Who gets paid: Unilever, shareholders and the brand owner

HUL pays Unilever a royalty for brands and technology, plus a fee for central services. In January 2023 the board agreed to lift the combined charge from about 2.65% of turnover to about 3.45%, in steps to January 2025, under a five-year contract that began on 1 February 2023[16]. In FY26 the annual report shows technology royalty of ₹751 crore, trademark royalty of ₹420 crore and central services fees of ₹870 crore[7]. That adds to about 3.2% of FY26 revenue on our arithmetic. The contract runs to about January 2028, so a renewal decision falls inside the window of our FY28 estimate, and we carry it as a risk in chapter 6.

Shareholders receive most of the cash. FY26 dividends were ₹41 a share, or ₹9,633 crore, about 90% of continuing profit[6]. The board declared another interim dividend of ₹19 a share on 28 October 2026, with a record date of 12 November[4]. Because Unilever owns 61.9%, most of that cash goes to the parent. For a minority holder, the dividend yield of about 2.3%[5] is real but it also means little profit is retained to fund growth, which is why capital spending has run near 2% of turnover[17].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA is profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a rough measure of operating profit in cash terms.
  • Underlying sales growth (USG) is growth in turnover that leaves out acquisitions and disposals. Underlying volume growth (UVG) is growth in the quantity sold, adjusted for mix.
  • Continuing operations are the businesses HUL still owns. Ice cream is now shown as discontinued operations.
  • ROCE means return on capital employed, the operating profit earned on each rupee of capital in the business.
  • EPS means earnings per share, which is profit divided by the number of shares.
  • Reverse DCF is a calculation that starts from today’s price and solves for the profit growth that the price assumes.
  • Stage 2 is the rising markup phase of a share price. Stage 4 is the falling markdown phase, in which the price is below a falling 50-day average that is below a falling 200-day average.
  • FCF means free cash flow, the cash left after capital spending.
  • ATR means average true range, the typical daily price swing, which we use to measure distances to support and stop levels.
  • A base is a flat, quiet trading range where a stock stops falling and builds a floor. A pivot is the price level a move must cross to count, and a breakout is a close above the top of the range, usually on higher volume.
  • Asymmetry is the possible gain divided by the possible loss from an entry price to the stop, also called reward-to-risk.
  • The entry zone is the range of prices where we would buy, set near verified support. A limit order is an order to buy only at or below a set price. A stop loss is the price at which we would sell to cap the loss.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

HUL’s strength is scale and reach. Crisil, the rating agency, notes that HUL brands hold the top two positions in most categories where they compete, that 19 of its brands had sales above ₹1,000 crore in FY25, and that its distribution network and advertising support have kept leadership for decades[18]. By September 2026 the number of ₹1,000-crore brands was 21[1]. Scale gives a cost advantage in media, procurement and shelf space, which smaller rivals find hard to copy. This is a moat of habit and distribution more than a patent or a licence, so it is durable but not permanent.

The best evidence of competitive strength in the last year is volume. Underlying sales growth stepped up from 3% in the first half of FY26 to 5%, 7%, 10% and then 15%[17][3]. Underlying volume growth was 7% in the September 2026 quarter and 6% for the half year[3], while NielsenIQ measured industry volumes at minus 2% for April to June[9]. Unilever’s own report says India grew 10% in its second quarter, as Home Care and hair care reached their highest ever market shares[19]. Market-share data comes from management and NielsenIQ, and we could not check the underlying panels ourselves.

The first weakness is the long pause in growth. HUL’s chief executive said growth was muted for two years[1]. Crisil recorded revenue growth of only about 2% in FY25, because urban mass demand was weak[18]. On the Screener figures, revenue grew 2.1% a year from FY23 to FY26 and continuing profit grew 1.7% a year[5]. A business that grows this slowly for three years is hard to value at 40 times earnings, even if the latest quarters are better.

The second weakness is margin quality. HUL’s gross margin fell from 51.5% in the September 2025 quarter to 47.6% in September 2026 on our arithmetic[4]. The company held its EBITDA margin at 22.6% partly by cutting advertising and promotion by 9.25% to ₹1,481 crore[11]. An analyst at LKP Securities said investors worry that profit was shielded by ad cuts[11]. Had advertising stayed at last year’s 10.25% of revenue, EBITDA would have been about ₹3,702 crore, a margin near 20.5%. That figure is our arithmetic, not a company number.

The third weakness is balance-sheet quality. Goodwill of ₹18,062 crore and other intangible assets of ₹31,173 crore, mostly from the 2020 merger with GSK Consumer Healthcare, make up about 59% of ₹82,907 crore of total assets[4]. Crisil itself amortises these assets over ten years in its analysis[18]. This is why consolidated ROCE is 28.4%[5] while the company’s own standalone formula shows 110.9%[7]. The business earns very high returns on tangible capital, but a shareholder owns the acquired intangibles at their purchase price, and the extra growth from new acquisitions has to earn a return on that price.

Where the moat is widest

Home Care and hair care are the clearest strengths. Home Care grew 22% in the September 2026 quarter, with double-digit growth in both fabric wash and household care, and with liquids growing at a double-digit pace[3]. The company took price increases in the segment of about 10% in the quarter[11], and still grew volumes in double digits, which is a sign of pricing power. Beauty & Wellbeing grew 16% on double-digit volume growth, led by premium hair care and skin care[3].

Foods adds stability. Lifestyle nutrition (Horlicks and Boost) grew at a double-digit pace, and Boost passed ₹1,000 crore of annual turnover[10]. Tea remains a commodity-driven business, with tea prices up about 9% over a year in the September quarter[20]. Coffee, packaged foods and health drinks are giving HUL a wider footprint than soap alone.

Where the moat is thinner than it looks

Personal Care is where cost pressure shows first. Segment profit fell to ₹449 crore from ₹492 crore a year ago on higher revenue[4], and the chief financial officer said the underlying margin is around 17%[11]. The segment depends on palm oil, which the company said has been inflating for a second year[10]. Home Care margin also fell. Comparing the same quarter, segment profit was 19.1% of segment revenue in Q2 FY26 and 16.2% in Q2 FY27 on our arithmetic, because detergent inputs are linked to crude oil[4].

Competition is changing shape. On the June 2026 call, analysts asked about value players in liquid detergents, hand wash and beauty[8]. NielsenIQ reported that small manufacturers kept outpacing larger players on volume growth in the October to December 2025 quarter[21]. HUL answers with premium products and market development in small towns, but premium mix takes years to show in margins. HUL bought a 90.5% stake in Minimalist[22] and paid ₹2,661 crore in cash for business combinations in the first half of FY26[4]. The documents we read do not report Minimalist’s profit separately. Some peers grew faster: Nestle India’s sales rose 20.45% in the September 2026 quarter[23], while Dabur guided to double-digit revenue growth[24], so HUL’s 15% is strong but not unique.

The last test of a moat is whether it protects profit when costs jump. In the September 2026 quarter, it protected volume and revenue but not gross margin. That is the main reason the next chapter on company-specific indicators scores margin as a failure and not a pass, even though the EBITDA margin sits inside the guided range. The next chapter turns from the general case to the five numbers that run this particular business.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management credibility matters more for HUL than for most companies because the thesis depends on a turnaround in growth that management itself called. We went back through the last twelve months of guidance and capital-allocation promises and compared each with what the filings show. Most were kept on time. The weak spots are the quality of profit growth and one change to a margin target.

The first promise was about timing. On the December 2025 call, the chief executive said the second half of FY26 would be better than the first half and FY27 better than FY26[25]. The second half delivered volume growth of 4% and then 6%[25][6], and the first half of FY27 delivered underlying sales growth of 12%[3]. On the July 2026 call she repeated that FY27 would be ahead of FY26[8], and the half-year numbers are tracking that statement.

The second promise was about margin. In December 2025 the chief financial officer said the guided range was 22% to 23%, with about 50 basis points of benefit from losing the ice-cream business, which implies 22.5% to 23.5%[25]. FY26 came in at 23.6%[6]. In September 2026 the company widened the medium-term range to 22% to 24%[20][17], which lowers the floor by half a point. The September quarter margin of 22.6% would have sat near the bottom of the old range and sits comfortably inside the new one. We do not think the widening is dishonest, because management tied it to freedom to invest, but we note that the bar moved just before a quarter in which margin fell 140 basis points[4].

5. What could happen next

Chapter 5 asks what the current price already assumes, how analysts are changing their numbers, where we differ from them, and what the next twelve months could look like. We work in three steps. First, a Reverse DCF solves for the profit growth that the price of ₹1,815.80 requires. Second, we compare that growth with what HUL has delivered and what analysts forecast. Third, we build bull, base and bear cases with a number, a driver and a probability for each. All per-share numbers are on the continuing basis described in chapter 1.

A reverse DCF works backwards from the price. A normal discounted cash flow (DCF) model forecasts profit and asks what it is worth today. A reverse DCF takes today’s price and asks what yearly profit growth would justify it. Two settings matter. The discount rate is the yearly return we demand from a share; we use 12%. The terminal multiple is the price-to-earnings ratio we assume at the end of year ten; we use 20 times. With those settings, HUL’s price needs about 16% profit growth every year for ten years. HUL’s continuing profit grew 1.7% a year over the last three years, so the price asks for far more growth than the company has delivered.

If advertising returned to last year’s intensity with no other offset, EBITDA would be about ₹387 crore lower per quarter, or about ₹1,550 crore a year. After tax at 26%, that is about ₹1,150 crore, or ₹4.9 a share, so FY28 EPS would be near ₹48 and not ₹53 (our arithmetic). At 37.9 times that gives about ₹1,825, a price about the same as today’s close.

The evidence that would show this assumption is wrong is the advertising line and the gross margin line in the December 2026 quarter. If advertising goes back above 9.5% of revenue (it was 9.6% in Q1 FY27 on our arithmetic) while EBITDA margin stays above 22.5%, the assumption holds. If EBITDA margin drops below 22% when advertising rises, it fails. We will know when HUL reports the third quarter, which we expect in late January or February 2027; the date is not yet announced, and last year’s call was in February[25].

Estimate 2: the multiple of 37.9 times stays put

The weakest assumption is that the multiple has stopped falling. The Screener price-to-earnings ratio fell from about 52 in January 2026 to 38.1 now[29], and the Reserve Bank raised its repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% on 7 October while retail inflation reached 5.54%[20]. Higher rates cut the value of far-off profits, and a defensive, high-multiple stock is the kind that suffers first. The Nifty FMCG index has fallen about 20% this year[11].

The evidence that would show this wrong is a further fall in the multiple while earnings estimates stay flat, or a close below ₹1,714.44, which breaks the floor. The multiple’s direction will be visible every day, and the next hard test is the third-quarter result in early 2027.

A multiple-only stress shows the risk. If FY28 EPS arrives at the consensus ₹53.10 but the multiple falls to 30 times, the price is ₹1,593, which is 12.3% below the close. Delivering consensus earnings does not by itself protect capital.

Estimate 3: the price needs about 16% yearly growth for ten years

The weakest assumption is the house setting of a 12% discount rate and a 20-times terminal multiple. A debt-free staples franchise rated AAA by Crisil[18] might justify a lower return requirement. At 11% the implied growth is about 14.9%, and at 10% it is about 13.8% (our arithmetic). Neither is a figure HUL has delivered over three years. The weakest point is therefore not the model but the profit record, because continuing profit has grown 1.7% a year since FY23.

The evidence that would show our reading wrong is two consecutive years of profit growth above 12%. HUL’s first half of FY27 shows profit before exceptional items up 9%[3], so the early signs point the right way but not yet far enough. We will know more after the full-year FY27 result in April or May 2027.

Estimate 4: scenario probabilities of 20%, 50% and 30%

The weakest assumption is the 30% weight on the bear case. It rests on crude oil near $115, retail inflation at 5.54% and rural volumes that fell 5% in April to June[11][20][9]. If crude falls back below $90 and the monsoon-driven rural volume recovers, the bear weight should fall to 20% and the bull weight should rise to 30%, which moves the expected price to ₹2,052, up 13.0% (our arithmetic), the top of the 2% to 13% span in chapter 5. The REJECTED verdict does not depend on the 30% bear weight, because it rests on the Stage 4 gate; the weight only moves the expected return within that span. There is also one slow-moving risk we have not priced: the royalty and central-services contract with Unilever runs for five years from 1 February 2023[16], so a renewal near January 2028 could raise the charge above 3.45% of turnover. Each extra half-point of turnover would cost about ₹320 crore a year before tax on FY26 revenue (our arithmetic).
