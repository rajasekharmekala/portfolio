1. What the company does

This chapter says what the company sells, to whom and how it earns money. It makes parts for car and bike makers, and much of its growth came from new plants and bought firms.

Lumax Auto Technologies Limited trades on the NSE as LUMAXTECH and on the BSE as 532796. It was incorporated in 1981 and belongs to the D.K. Jain group, which also owns the listed lighting maker Lumax Industries[15][7]. The company sells parts to vehicle makers, called original equipment manufacturers or OEMs. In FY26 it earned consolidated revenue of ₹4,870.33 crore from 28 plants in seven states[1][7]. A crore is ten million rupees.

The business has five product groups. Advanced plastics, which means cockpits, door panels, trims, air-intake parts and two-wheeler lamps, was ₹2,566 crore or 53 percent of FY26 revenue. Structures and control systems, mainly gear shifters and two-wheeler frames, was ₹816 crore, or 17 percent. Greenfuel, which makes CNG fittings, was ₹383 crore, and mechatronics, which is switches, sensors, antennas and telematics boxes, was ₹281 crore. Aftermarket, which is spare parts sold to repair shops and not to vehicle makers, was about 10 percent[8]. By vehicle type, passenger cars were 53 percent, two- and three-wheelers 24 percent, aftermarket 10 percent and commercial vehicles 9 percent[8]. In Q1 FY27 plastics grew 47 percent, mechatronics 56, structures 20, CNG fittings 17 and aftermarket 6, so growth is broad[7].

Most products are made by subsidiaries and joint ventures with foreign partners. Lumax Mannoh (Japan) makes gear shifters, Lumax Cornaglia (Italy) air-intake parts and urea tanks, and Lumax Alps Alpine, Yokowo, Ituran and FAE make electronic parts[7]. These joint ventures are consolidated, so the group reports all of their sales and profit and then deducts the share that belongs to the partners, called the minority interest.

Three deals shaped the balance sheet. In March 2023 the company bought 75 percent of IAC India, a maker of car interiors, with a cash outflow of ₹394.83 crore[3]. In November 2024 it bought 60 percent of Greenfuel, a CNG-fittings maker, for ₹153.09 crore, funded by debt and internal cash[14]. In FY26 it paid ₹220.96 crore for the remaining 25 percent of IAC India[1]. The chief financial officer says more than 90 percent of the long-term debt exists because of the inorganic deals[8].

How the company earns money, and what raw material prices do to it

A part is sold under a supply contract that covers the life of a vehicle model. The price is reset for resin, steel and electronics costs with a lag. Management says that for plastics it recovers 80 to 90 percent of cost rises in the same quarter, and for a few customers one quarter later[6]. In June 2026 it said recoveries can take three to six months[8]. CRISIL, the rating agency, says prices are revised annually for most customers, so the company cannot fully pass on a sudden rise[13]. The two statements differ, and the Q1 FY27 margin of 15.1 percent, up 190 basis points on a year earlier, shows the recovery working so far[7].

Electronics are the weak spot. Management says electronic part prices rose 30 to 50 percent and take longer to recover, but mechatronics is only 6 to 7 percent of sales, so the effect was small. The next paragraphs say whether the business is strong or weak.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • An OEM, or original equipment manufacturer, is a vehicle maker such as Mahindra or Maruti Suzuki that buys the part to fit into a new vehicle.
  • Minority interest is the part of a subsidiary's profit that belongs to outside owners. IAC India was 25 percent owned by outsiders until May 2025.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

This chapter asks if the business is strong. Demand and the credit rating are high, and a few large customers pay for much of the revenue. We test each point with evidence.

The case for the business is that it is growing faster than vehicle output, earns about 19 to 21 percent on its capital and holds a high credit rating. The case against it is that 30 percent of revenue comes from one customer, that its debt is larger than the headline ratio suggests, and that its share price has already run up. Chapter 2B scores these points against fixed cut-offs. This chapter gives the evidence.

Vehicle demand: strong now, and about to meet a higher base

The Society of Indian Automobile Manufacturers reported passenger vehicle sales of 1.36 million units in July to September 2026, up 30.7 percent (released 15 October), with utility vehicles 68 percent of the total[19]. Management says industry production grew 22 percent in the first quarter and warns that growth will slow sharply in the second half, because last year's second half was already lifted by a cut in the goods and services tax[6]. That base effect is the main reason we model slower growth after the second quarter. What this means for an owner: the 33 percent growth of the first quarter is a high-water mark for the year, not a run rate. The order book, not the quarter, should guide the forecast.

Competitive position: one market leader, several challengers

The deck calls the company one of the dominant players in gear shifters, interiors and CNG parts, and management says it wants to be among the top two in antennas and switches within three to five years[6]. CRISIL expects 15 to 20 percent revenue growth over the medium term[13]. In CNG fittings, management says only two or three large players compete, and its technical partnerships let it supply localised parts that were once imported. Market-share claims such as a 65 percent gear-shifter share come from a machine-generated summary on Screener, so we do not rely on them[15]. What this means for an owner: the position looks strong, but it rests on company claims and ratings, not on an audited market-share figure.

Contingent claims are ₹36.3 crore, about 2.5 percent of equity[1].

Earnings quality: a tax gain and a changing minority share

Reported profit for FY26 includes a one-time deferred tax gain. Management says the effective tax rate was 26 percent without it[8]. At 26 percent on the ₹405.92 crore profit before tax, tax would have been ₹105.54 crore, but the books show ₹68.78 crore, a gain of about ₹36.8 crore. Management says the gain arose in the 60 percent owned Greenfuel, so about 40 percent of it went to the minority; on that split the owners' share is about ₹22.1 crore, or ₹3.24 a share[9]. The split is our arithmetic, not a filed figure. The minority share of profit was 17 percent in FY26 and is guided to 11 to 13 percent, as IAC and FAE are now wholly owned[8][6].
