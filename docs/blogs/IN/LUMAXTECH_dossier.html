2 What the company does

A supplier sells to a few large buyers, and its fortunes follow those buyers. Lumax is an automotive component company, meaning it makes parts that go inside vehicles rather than the vehicles themselves. Its product list is broad. The company's products, as summarised by InvestyWise, on the Q1 FY27 results (as of 30 Jun 2026), cover advanced plastics, lighting for two- and three-wheelers, chassis, gear shifters, emission systems, seat frames, oxygen sensors and wiring harnesses. The company's profile on Screener (as of 12 Oct 2026) says it is a manufacturer of gear shifters and interior solutions in India, with a claimed 65%+ market share across its passenger vehicle customers. Treat that share figure as a company claim, not an independent measurement.

The business in plain terms

The company is organised around four broad product groups: advanced plastics, mechatronics (electronics that sense, switch and communicate), structures and control systems, and aftermarket spare parts. In the first quarter of FY27, advanced plastics was the largest group. Passenger vehicles made up 52 percent of revenue, down from 55 percent a year earlier. Two- and three-wheelers rose to 27 percent, according to InvestyWise (as of 30 Jun 2026). Mechatronics is still small at 6 percent of revenue, but it is the division management is pushing hardest.

The mix shift is what makes this useful for an investor. A plastic part for a petrol scooter is a volume product with thin margins and heavy competition. A sensor module for a connected car carries more engineering content per vehicle, and it tends to be sticky, because a car maker that designs a sensor into a platform does not switch it lightly. Management's strategy is to move the mix toward the second kind. Whether that move is fast enough is the question the Layer 2 scorecard would ask, if the company had passed Layer 1.

Corporate structure and the IAC merger

Much of the recent history is a set of internal mergers. The most significant was the amalgamation with IAC International Automotive India. The National Company Law Tribunal sanctioned the scheme on 8 May 2026, and the company's merger filings page lists the order. GlobalAutoInsight reports the scheme became effective on 18 May 2026, with an appointed date of 1 October 2025. The appointed date is the date from which the accounts treat the businesses as combined, so the merged business is in the reported numbers from that earlier point.

A merger like this makes year-on-year comparisons harder to read, because reported growth includes businesses that used to sit outside the company. It also changes the balance sheet. 4 Layer 1 gates

Layer 1 is the set of hurdles every candidate must pass. The idea is that a business which cannot earn a good return on capital, cannot turn profit into cash, or carries too much debt should not be bought however good the story sounds. The chart hurdles protect you from buying into a falling price. The asymmetry hurdle makes sure a buyer can state the upside in numbers. There are six hurdles. Three concern the business and balance sheet, two concern the price chart, and one concerns whether a target can be verified. A candidate must pass all six, and failing any one is enough for a rejection.

Some chart terms need defining before the table. A base is a stretch of sideways trading after a decline. Stage 1 is that base. Stage 2 means the rising phase, when the stock trades above a rising 50-day and 200-day average. Stage 4 means a sustained breakdown below the long-term average. A breakout is a move above the top of a base. The rulebook needs a Core candidate to be at a Stage 1 breakout or in Stage 2, and a Tactical candidate to be in Stage 2. No candidate may be in Stage 4.

Hurdle 1: return on capital employed

Return on capital employed, or ROCE, asks how much operating profit the company earns on all the money tied up in running it, both owners' money and borrowed money. It is the most important single business measure in this rulebook, because it separates businesses that earn well from businesses that merely grow. Screener's tables show 21 percent for FY26, up from 18 percent in FY24 and 19 percent in FY25, per Screener (as of 31 Mar 2026). The current header on the same site shows 21.2 percent (as of 12 Oct 2026).

The number clears the 15 percent floor by a wide margin and the 18 percent core preference too. Each rupee of capital in the business earns a healthy return before the cost of debt. The caveat is that ROCE counts borrowed money in its denominator, so a business that borrows more can look better on it. That is one reason the debt hurdle is read separately.

Result: PASS, at 21 percent for FY26.

Hurdle 2: debt to equity

The debt-to-equity ratio compares the money the company has borrowed with the money its owners have put in and kept in the business. A ratio of 1.0 means the two are equal. The rulebook asks for less than 0.70, so borrowings must sit well below the owners' cushion. Borrowed money has a fixed claim: interest and principal are owed in a good year or a bad one, so high debt makes a downturn more painful.

The audited figures for March 2026 are borrowings of 1,231 crore rupees, reserves of 1,197 crore and equity capital of 14 crore, per Screener (as of 31 Mar 2026). Net worth is therefore about 1,211 crore. Borrowings divided by net worth gives 1.02, which is the figure the rulebook uses. If the 266 crore rupee minority interest is added to the denominator, the ratio is about 0.83, per ETMoney (as of 31 Mar 2026). Both figures are above 0.70.

Management gives a different number. On the June 2026 call, the company said its long-term debt was 508 crore rupees and that its debt-to-equity ratio was 0.32, which it described as conservative, per inve.money (as of 30 Jun 2026). The sources do not say how the 0.32 was computed. The arithmetic does not reproduce it. Five hundred and eight crore divided by the audited net worth of about 1,211 crore is about 0.42. Dividing 508 by 0.32 implies a denominator of about 1,588 crore, and no source in the record gives that figure. So management's number does not reconcile with the audited balance sheet, and this report does not supply a reason for the gap.

To pass on the audited basis, total borrowings would need to fall to about 848 crore rupees at the March 2026 net worth. That is roughly 383 crore less, or net worth would have to rise substantially. Quarterly profit adds to net worth, so the ratio falls a little each quarter if borrowings stay flat, but not by enough to pass in one step.

Result: FAIL, at 1.02 on total borrowings and net worth. The management figure of 0.32 is disclosed and does not reconcile with the audited basis, so it does not change the result under the rulebook.

Hurdle 3: three-year cash from operations to profit

This hurdle asks whether reported profit turns into real cash. Profit is an accounting measure and can be shaped by timing and by sales recognised before cash arrives. Cash from operations is harder to shape. A ratio below 0.70 over three years warns that profit is running ahead of cash, which is a common sign of stress in suppliers that wait to be paid.

The figures for FY24 to FY26 are 265, 290 and 460 crore of cash from operations, and 167, 229 and 337 crore of net profit, per Screener (as of 31 Mar 2026). Summed, that is 1,015 crore of cash and 733 crore of profit. Dividing gives 1.38, well above the 0.70 floor. Each individual year also shows cash above profit, which is a stronger sign than one good total.

Result: PASS, at 1.38. The cash figure is consolidated, so it includes the cash of the minority-owned subsidiaries, and the profit figure includes their profit too. The ratio is consistent on that basis, which is why it is used.

Hurdle 5: the momentum test

The momentum test is a second, more mechanical check of the price. Relative strength means how a stock's return compares with the benchmark index over the same period. A pivot is the price level a stock must clear to resume its trend, and the test measures distance from a base pivot. The test has two versions. Test A asks whether the stock is in a clean uptrend, meaning the price is above a rising 50-day average that is above the 200-day average. It also asks whether relative strength over three months is positive, and whether the stock is not stretched from its base. Test B asks whether the stock is entering momentum: whether its 50-day average is holding, whether relative strength over 28 sessions is turning up, whether the stock is near its base pivot, and whether volume dries up and then expands. A candidate needs to pass one of the two versions. The code computes the test from the ledger, so no one can argue with it on the day.

The distance used in the extension and base-pivot conditions needs a precise definition. The ledger field called base_pivot_atr is not a distance to a pivot high. It equals the close minus the 63-day base low of 1,384.40 rupees, divided by the ATR. That is (1,827.40 minus 1,384.40) divided by 86.24, which is 5.14 ATR. The test therefore measures how far the close sits above the 63-day base low. The 0.74 ATR figure in chapter 6 is a different anchor: the close against the 20-day swing low of 1,763.60 rupees. The two numbers answer different questions and should not be read as one.

Test A passes two of four conditions and fails two. The 200-day average is not falling, and relative strength over 63 sessions is 29.5 percent above the benchmark, so those two pass. The structure condition fails because the close is below the 50-day average. The extension condition fails because the close sits 5.14 ATR above the 63-day base low, against a limit of 1.2. Hurdle 6: asymmetry and target anchoring

The asymmetry hurdle asks two questions. First, is the upside large compared with the downside? The rulebook expresses that as the target minus the price, divided by the price minus the stop. Core candidates need at least 3.0 and Tactical candidates at least 3.5. Second, and before the ratio can be computed, is the target verifiable? The rulebook allows two anchors. One is forward earnings per share multiplied by the company's three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E, which means the price paid for each rupee of earnings. The other is a measured move from a verified chart base. An arbitrary target is forbidden, and an unverified target is an automatic failure.

The support ladder in plain terms

The rulebook's support ladder groups distances into four bands. A full-size buy sits within half an ATR of a verified support. A standard buy sits within one ATR. Buying far from support means the stop has to be far away too, and a wide stop on a volatile stock produces a large loss when it is hit.
