Before you read

GE Vernova T&D India builds the heavy equipment that moves electricity over long distances, and its US parent owns 51%.[1][10] It has no debt, turned Rs 1.55 of operating cash into every Rs 1 of profit in three years, and grew sales 31% a year.[1][11] The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT because the price of Rs 3,980 assumes about 25% yearly profit growth for ten years.[30] The reward-to-risk ratio (what we could gain for each rupee we could lose) is only 0.78 to 1 now and 2.13 to 1 near Rs 3,720.[30] We would be wrong to wait if margins hold in the mid-20s and the large HVDC order lands on time; a close below Rs 3,378 ends the case.

1. What the company does

GE Vernova T&D India is the Indian arm of a company that has built electricity grid equipment for more than a century. Think of the power grid as a road network for electricity. Power stations and solar farms are the factories. Cities and industries are the shops. This company builds the heavy trucks and the junctions: the large transformers that raise or lower voltage, the switchgear that opens and closes circuits safely, the control systems that watch the grid, and, increasingly, the giant converter stations that push power down very long direct current lines.[1][11]

It earns money in three ways. First, it sells products, such as a transformer or a circuit breaker, and books the sale when the product ships. In the year to March 2026 that was Rs 1,855 crore, or 30% of sales. Second, it runs projects, such as building a full substation, and books revenue as work progresses over months or years. That was Rs 4,290 crore, or 69% of sales. Third, it sells services and software, a small slice of Rs 56 crore.[10] The customer is mostly a company that builds and operates power lines or solar parks, plus government utilities and, for exports, sister companies in the GE Vernova group.[3]

Orders come first, sales come later. The company reports an order backlog, which is the value of signed work not yet delivered. On 30 June 2026 that backlog was Rs 20,930 crore, about 3.4 times the full-year sales of Rs 6,206 crore. Because a large transformer takes 18 to 24 months from order to delivery, today's sales were mostly signed one to two years ago, and today's orders decide sales in 2028 and 2029.[2][1][3]

The company is also changing shape. Private customers now make up 77% of the backlog, central government utilities 21% and state utilities only 2%. ICRA says the shift toward private and export customers supports timely collections. Exports were 33% of sales in the year to March 2026, and about 80% to 90% of export orders come from other GE Vernova group companies, which is helpful for volume but means the group controls part of the order flow.[3][10]

Where the money comes from in one picture

A good way to see the business is to follow one rupee of a new order. Around 55 paise is spent on materials and bought-out parts, such as steel, copper and electronic components. About 7 paise pays employees and another 11 paise covers other costs. What is left, about 27 paise in the year to March 2026, is operating profit. That is the company's EBITDA margin (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, as a share of sales) of 27.1%. Screener's operating margin line, which means operating profit as a share of sales, shows 28% because it treats other items slightly differently. This report says which one it uses.

That 27 paise is unusually high for an equipment maker. Two years earlier it was only 10 paise. The jump came from better pricing on new orders, more exports and services, fewer low-margin turnkey projects and more output from the same factories.[2][4] Section 3 shows the five-year record, and Section 2B tests whether management delivered what it promised.

Why the HVDC part matters

High-voltage direct current, or HVDC, sends power as direct current over lines of 1,000 kilometres or more with far less loss than ordinary alternating current. India needs it to carry solar and wind power from Rajasthan and Gujarat to cities in the south and west. The company won the Khavda to South Olpad 2.5 gigawatt HVDC job and the Chandrapur HVDC refurbishment in the year to March 2026.[4][5]

On 7 September 2026 the national grid company told it that it was the lowest bidder for a second, larger HVDC job: 6,000 megawatts at 800 kilovolts between Barmer in Rajasthan and South Kalamb in Maharashtra. The company has not published the value. PL Capital estimates about Rs 12,500 crore, which would take the backlog from Rs 20,930 crore to about Rs 33,430 crore. Management says HVDC work is back-ended, so the big revenue starts in the year to March 2029.[14][13][3]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Order backlog: signed work not yet delivered or billed.
  • Transmission: carrying electricity at high voltage over long distances from power plants to cities.
  • HVDC: high-voltage direct current, a way to move very large amounts of power over very long lines.
  • TBCB: tariff-based competitive bidding, where private developers bid to build and run a power line and then order equipment.
  • RPT: related party transaction, a deal between the company and its parent group, which needs shareholder approval above set sizes.
  • Standalone: figures for this company alone; it reports no subsidiaries that change the picture.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The company has a strong competitive position, and the evidence is in three places. The first is price power. Operating margin (operating profit as a share of sales) went from 10% in the year to March 2024 to 28% in the year to March 2026, while sales rose 96% in two years. A business with weak pricing cannot lift margins like that on a bigger volume of work.[1] The second is the technology access. It uses designs from the parent for transformers up to 1,200 kilovolts, compact gas-insulated switchgear from 72.5 to 800 kilovolts, and both main HVDC types, which the company lists as its core offer.[4][11] The third is the credit rating. ICRA upgraded it to [ICRA]AA with a Stable outlook on 29 July 2026, citing the large backlog, the shift away from low-margin turnkey work, and zero debt.[11]

The moat is real but not unlimited. It rests on skill in the highest voltage classes, where only a few firms such as Siemens Energy and Hitachi Energy compete, and on the parent's technology licence. It does not rest on a patent that cannot be copied. Management names technology, lean production and local sourcing as its edge. ICRA lists intense competition as the first credit risk, and the company itself expects some price normalisation as transformer capacity grows across the industry.[3][11][12]

Competition is rising from a new direction. On 24 June 2026 the Finance Ministry allowed four China-linked makers with Indian factories to bid for public power projects for two years. On 3 July the shares fell up to 10% on the news, and the stock dropped for nine sessions in a row, down 17% in total. Brokers later called the effect small, because only one of the four has meaningful transformer capacity, local content of 50% to 60% is required, and the exemption lasts only two years.[21][22][25][23] Management also says capacity added by rivals will partly serve exports, not only India.

The demand backdrop is the strongest part of the case. The Central Electricity Authority has planned transmission to carry power from over 900 gigawatts of non-fossil capacity by 2035-36. For the new wind and solar links alone it estimates 1,37,500 circuit kilometres of lines and 8,27,600 MVA of substation capacity, costing Rs 7,93,300 crore, between 2026-27 and 2035-36.[24] That is a decade of orders, not a one-year spike. The risk is the timing of orders, not their existence, which is what the Q1 FY27 numbers show.

What the evidence says, claim by claim

Claim one: pricing is better than the cycle. Evidence: gross margin (sales minus the cost of materials, as a share of sales) of 45.3% in the year to March 2026, against 41.3% in the June 2026 quarter, where management blamed a lower export share, higher metal prices and a mix shift to lower-margin high-voltage work. Operating margin of 25.1% still met the mid-20s guidance, which is the profit range management says it expects.[3][2]

Claim two: balance-sheet strength is genuine. Evidence: borrowings of Rs 24 crore against net worth of about Rs 2,690 crore, interest cover of 114 times, and available cash of Rs 2,930 crore including money lent to a group cash pool that can be recalled on demand. The cash pool is a minor watch point, because the company's own presentation says the pool includes lending to a group company, LM Wind Power Blades (India), and ICRA notes a Rs 1,000 crore cap and a parent guarantee.[1][11][3][2]

Claim three: ownership is stable. The group owns 51% with none pledged, after selling down from 75% to 51% during 2024. Foreign funds hold 19.16% and domestic funds 22.18% on 30 September 2026. Fund flows are a risk on both sides: foreign holding rose from 0.69% in December 2023 to a peak of 22.89% in June 2026 and was 19.16% on 30 September 2026, so those holders can also sell.[1][26]

Where the moat is thinner than the margins suggest

Two customers made up about 21% and 11% of sales in the year to March 2026, so one delayed project can move a quarter. About 80% to 90% of export orders come from group companies, and two large group orders, one of Rs 3,000 crore and one of Rs 1,300 crore for a US data centre, have not been booked. The Rs 3,000 crore project is on hold for budget reasons, and the group is still negotiating the data-centre one.[10][3] These are not signs of weak business. They are signs that part of the order book depends on decisions made outside India.

What this means for an owner: the business is high quality and well protected for the next three years, but the very high return on capital of 77% invites competition and the margin is the thing to watch. The next section checks that view with five measures chosen for this business.[1]

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 of the underwriting asks whether the few numbers that drive this particular business are healthy. For a maker of grid equipment, sales do not show the health of the business by themselves. Orders come first and sales follow. Orders divided by sales is called book-to-bill, and working capital means the money tied up in unsold stock and unpaid bills. The useful measures are how much signed work is waiting, whether new orders keep arriving, whether the profit margin survives rising metal prices, whether profit turns into cash, and whether the balance sheet and customers are sound.[3]

Each measure is scored PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). The business qualifies when the total is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 with no more than one FAIL. The five measures were chosen because the company itself and its credit rating agency discuss these same five things when they explain the business.[11][2]

The second half of Layer 2 asks whether management did what it said it would do. This matters here because the valuation depends on belief in guidance. The record is good on margin and capital spending, and mixed on order timing. The table lists six promises, what happened and a verdict.[3]

3. The numbers, in plain words

What did revenue, profit, cash and debt do over five years, and what do the levels and the direction of travel say? The company reports standalone figures, because it has no subsidiaries that change the picture. All figures are in Rs crore unless stated. A crore is ten million.[1][10]

The five years split into two eras. In the year to March 2022 and the year to March 2023 the company lost money or broke even on sales of about Rs 3,000 crore, with high debtor days and negative or near-zero operating cash flow. From the year to March 2024 it changed: sales rose 96% in two years to Rs 6,206 crore, operating margin went from 10% to 28%, and profit after tax grew from Rs 181 crore to Rs 1,233 crore. Profit per share, or EPS (earnings per share), rose from Rs 7.07 to Rs 48.17. Three-year sales growth is 31% a year; three-year profit growth shows 490% in Screener only because the base year was a loss, so we read it as not meaningful.[1]

The most important single fact is that profit turned into cash. Operating cash flow was Rs 1,710 crore in FY26 against profit of Rs 1,233 crore, and free cash flow (FCF), which is cash from operations minus spending on plant, was Rs 1,521 crore. Over three years the company generated Rs 3,132 crore of cash from operations for Rs 2,022 crore of profit, a ratio of 1.55. A caveat applies: other liabilities, which include customer advances and payables, rose from Rs 2,853 crore to Rs 5,025 crore in FY26, and brokers expect that cushion to reverse as work is delivered.[1][12]

Debt is almost gone: borrowings fell from Rs 273 crore in FY23 to Rs 24 crore in FY26, and interest was Rs 35 crore against operating profit of Rs 1,706 crore. Return on capital employed, a measure of profit earned on every rupee put into the business, reached 77% in FY26 on Screener's count, and PL Capital puts it at 72.4% on its own method. The two sources differ because they define capital differently, and both are far above the 15% compounder floor.[1][12]

The number

Over the next three years, our expected ratio of operating cash to profit is about 0.58, down from 1.55 in the last three years.

How it was calculated

We took PL Capital's projected operating cash flow of Rs 1,682 crore, Rs 1,200 crore and Rs 737 crore for FY27, FY28 and FY29 and divided the sum, Rs 3,619 crore, by its projected profit of Rs 1,539 crore, Rs 1,934 crore and Rs 2,736 crore, which sums to Rs 6,209 crore.[12]

The inputs

PL's cash flow statement shows net cash from operating activities of 16,818, 11,996 and 7,371 million rupees, and profit after tax of 15,388, 19,342 and 27,363 million rupees for FY27 to FY29. The reason it falls is working capital: PL expects 8,438 million rupees of cash to be absorbed in FY28 and 21,238 million rupees in FY29 as receivables and inventory grow with sales.[12]

The range

In a bull case, customers keep paying advances on HVDC jobs and the ratio stays near 1.0. In a bear case, receivable days rise from 128 toward 200 on big projects, and the ratio falls below 0.4. Even PL's own path stays above the 0.25 red line over three years, but it falls below the 0.70 compounder bar, and FY29 on its own, at 0.27, would be close to the red line.

If the number is off

If cash conversion falls faster than PL expects, free cash flow yield, now about 1.5%, would shrink further and the reverse DCF test in Section 5 would become harder to pass. On PL's path the three-year ratio of 0.58 would be under the 0.70 bar for a COMPOUNDER, so the archetype test would no longer be met on cash conversion and we would re-underwrite the name. A trailing year below 0.5 would lower our conviction one tier.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Which assumption in the estimates is weakest, what does the strongest case against the idea look like, and when will we find out? The red-team view that follows is written as a bear analyst would write it, using only facts already cited.[3]

The bear case in one paragraph: the company's 77% return on capital and 28% margin were earned in an unusually favourable stretch with scarce high-voltage supply, and both are the type of number that fades when rivals add capacity and new Chinese-linked competitors bid. The June 2026 quarter already shows gross margin down 7 points year on year, orders down 30% and two large group orders stuck. Management itself says 20% is a reasonable baseline margin. At 75 times trailing earnings the share price leaves no room for any of this, and cash flow is expected by PL to fall as working capital builds.[2][3][12]

Weakest assumption one: the margin stays near 24%

Our base case uses consensus profit for FY28, which in PL's model rests on an operating margin of 24.3%. The evidence against it is the June 2026 gross margin of 41.3% against 45.3% for FY26, commodity cost rises that management says were not fully priced, and a plan to dilute the mix with lower-margin HVDC and high-voltage products. Evidence that would show the assumption wrong: an operating margin below 23% in the September or December 2026 quarters. We learn the first data point on 30 October 2026 and the next in late January 2027.[3][12][1]

Weakest assumption two: the HVDC award converts and executes on time

The company has a letter naming it the lowest bidder, not a signed contract, and has not published the value. PL's Rs 12,500 crore is an estimate that rests on a 50% share of the project's approved cost of Rs 24,970 crore. The right-of-way risk sits with the line, not the converter stations, but a delay in the line could still shift the schedule. Evidence that would show the assumption wrong: no contract signed by the end of the March 2027 quarter, or a scope smaller than half the project. The company has not given a date for the formal award.[14][13][12]

Weakest assumption three: orders recover and the group sends work

The bridge from a 0.62 book-to-bill to the base case needs base orders to reach Rs 7,000 crore to Rs 8,000 crore a year, and needs group export orders to restart. Both are controlled by customers and a parent. The Rs 3,000 crore export order is on hold, and the August 2026 call said its shareholder approval would expire at the September 2026 AGM (the AGM outcome is not in our records); the Rs 1,300 crore US data-centre order is still in negotiation, with tariffs named as a factor. Evidence that would show it wrong: second-quarter orders below Rs 1,500 crore, or no group export order by the March 2027 quarter. The second-quarter orders are out on 30 October.[3][12][5]

Weakest assumption four: the multiple holds near 55 times

The target price uses an average of two rivals' forward multiples, not a measured long-run median for this stock, because none is published. If the whole group de-rates, as it did on 3 July when the stock fell 17% in nine sessions, the multiple would fall even if profit is on plan. The cash-flow check is also weaker than the profit check: PL expects cash from operations to fall to 0.27 of profit in FY29. Evidence that would show it wrong: a price to earnings below 45 times FY28 earnings while estimates stay flat.[21][12]

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, which means wait for a lower price before buying. Do not buy at Rs 3,980. Do not place any order before the 30 October 2026 results. After the results, if the operating margin is at least 23%, place a limit order (an order to buy only at a stated price or lower) in the entry zone of Rs 3,533 to Rs 3,720.[30] The rule behind it is the technical routing rule in scoring.json. The name passes all red lines, qualifies as a COMPOUNDER (a high-quality steady grower) with a Layer 2 score of 4.0 out of 5.0, and is not in Stage 4, so it is not rejected. The reward-to-risk, which our rules also call asymmetry, is 0.78 to 1 at Rs 3,980 and fails the 2.0 floor for a core holding. At the zone top of Rs 3,720 it is 2.13 to 1 and passes. That mix is called PASSED_CONDITIONAL: the name passed, but only on condition that the entry price is lower.

The entry zone runs from the 20-day swing low and 63-day base low at Rs 3,533.40 up to that floor plus 1.2 average true ranges, which is 3,533.40 + 1.2 x 155.58 = Rs 3,720. The price of Rs 3,980 is 2.87 average true ranges above support, in the ledger's NO_BUY band, which means no purchase at market and starts above 1.6. The stop sits at the base low minus one average true range: Rs 3,533.40 - Rs 155.58 = Rs 3,378. That is the rule for a basing stock in the CORE_ALPHA bucket, our long-term quality holdings, and it is 9.19% below Rs 3,720.[30]

The targets are Rs 4,450 base, Rs 5,900 bull and Rs 2,600 bear, derived in Section 5. Size comes from risk. We rate the idea TIER_3, our third conviction tier, because the price is demanding, the chart is only basing and two Layer 2 items are on watch. TIER_3 has a 50 basis point risk budget and a 3.5% cap. The raw weight is 0.50% divided by the 9.19% stop distance, or 5.44%, so the 3.5% cap binds. If the stop is hit, the portfolio loses 3.5% x 9.19% = 0.32%.[30]

Timing matters. Results and the call are on 30 October 2026, three days after a 5.01% jump. We buy nothing before the result. A weak margin would probably push the price into the zone, but a weak margin also fails the 23% check, so no order is placed and we reassess after the next quarter. A strong margin could lift the price, and then the order may not fill. That is acceptable. Above Rs 4,337 the reward-to-risk at our base target is below 0.2 to 1, and chasing would need a target near Rs 6,250. If we do own it and it moves into Stage 2, the let-winners-run rule applies.
