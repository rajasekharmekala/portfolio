1. What the company does

A transformer changes the voltage of electricity: it raises it for long-distance lines and lowers it again near towns and factories. The current has to enter and leave the grounded steel tank of the transformer without leaking sideways. The part that makes this safe is the bushing, an insulated plug, a little like the rubber grommet that lets a cable pass through a metal wall. A bushing rarely fails, but when it does the transformer can catch fire, so buyers approve new suppliers slowly. The company says utilities take 8 to 10 years to qualify a new bushing supplier, and each transformer carries five to seven bushings.[6]

Yash Highvoltage was founded in 2002 and is run by Chairman and Managing Director Keyur Shah, who took control in 2008. It sells to transformer makers, to power utilities such as POWERGRID and NTPC, and to industrial users. Mr Shah described the sale this way on the October 2026 call: the bushing is installed on a transformer, so the company sells to the transformer maker, and the end use may be a transmission line, a renewable plant, a power station or a data center.[1][5] Transmission still dominates, he said, although renewable and data-center transformers are a growing share.[5]

The company makes three families of bushing. Oil-impregnated paper (OIP) bushings wrap paper around a conductor and soak it in oil. Resin-impregnated paper and synthetic (RIP and RIS) bushings are dry, use resin instead of oil, and are expected to last 30 to 35 years against 15 to 20 for OIP.[12] High-current bushings carry very large currents, up to 25,000 amperes, out of generator transformers.[1] By value, RIP bushings made 86% of revenue in the half year to September 2026, OIP 9%, high-current 2% and other items 3%.[6]

Unit economics explain why the numbers move so fast. A small 72.5 kV oil bushing sells for about ₹30,000, while a 420 kV bushing sells for ₹36 lakh to ₹44 lakh, so a shift to higher voltages lifts revenue per unit sharply.[5] In the half year to September 2026 the company sold about 4,000 bushings for ₹194.5 crore, against 2,950 for ₹99.6 crore a year earlier.[5] That is an average of about ₹4.9 lakh per bushing, up from about ₹3.4 lakh (our arithmetic), and it came with a gross margin (revenue less the cost of materials, as a share of revenue) of 48.3% against 42.4%.[5] Raw materials were ₹94.8 crore of the ₹194.5 crore of revenue, about 49%.[4] That sits about three points away from the 51.7% implied by the gross margin. Management’s margin figure also counts direct costs beyond the materials line, and the filings do not let us reconcile the gap exactly.[5] A bushing is only 2% to 6% of the value of a power transformer, so the buyer pays for reliability and speed of delivery more than for the lowest price.[12]

How the company gets paid and when

Orders are made to specification. The RHP says the order life cycle runs between three and twelve months, and that raw-material prices are tied to London Metal Exchange or Bursa Malaysia rates, so metal price moves can be passed on to customers.[3] On the May 2026 call, management added that customers accept price pass-through for commodity and currency moves.[7] Sales are heavier in the second half of the financial year: management put the split near 40% in the first half and 60% in the second, because utilities and transformer makers try to finish projects before March.[12] That pattern means the half-year results for September are not a clean guide to the full year, and it is why the October 2026 call was careful about H2.[5]

Where the cores come from, and why that matters

The key part inside a RIP bushing is the capacitive core. India imports about 95% of the cores it needs, the Chairman wrote, and Yash today assembles, tests and packs bushings around imported cores.[1][6] At the time of the 2024 prospectus one supplier provided between 47.9% and 55.3% of the company’s purchases in each period shown.[3] The company has not published a newer split, so we treat the dependence as unresolved. The new Jarod plant is meant to make cores in-house, which would save the import duty and freight that management puts at 18% to 20% of the core cost.[5] Until the type tests are passed and customers approve the local core, the plant earns money only from assembly and testing.[5]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong because the product is small, safety-critical and slow to qualify. The company says it is one of fewer than 12 independent bushing manufacturers in the world and the only independent one from India, with approvals across every Indian utility, including nuclear power utilities.[6] It reports a 96% repeat-order rate and more than 1,000 customers in over 60 countries.[6][1] Its test hall is accredited by the national testing body NABL and can test to 600 kV, and its research unit is recognised by the government’s science department.[1] In 2016 it was the first company to localise dry-type RIP bushing technology in India under a Swiss collaboration.[1] The Chairman says it holds close to a third of the Indian bushing market.[1] Other sources give lower or narrower figures: the October 2025 deck says more than 30% of the operating segment up to 245 kV, and Choice Institutional Equities estimates 18% of the domestic addressable market.[10][22] Those figures use different definitions, so we treat the leadership claim as credible but not precisely measured.

Demand is strong for reasons outside the company’s control. The National Electricity Plan for transmission adds 1,274 gigavolt-amperes of transformer capacity and 1,91,474 circuit kilometres of lines at 220 kV and above between 2022-23 and 2031-32.[24] Brokers cite aggregate transmission and distribution investment of over ₹9 lakh crore.[22] Management calls it a seller’s market for the next two to four years, with the transformer makers placing blanket orders for 24 to 36 months.[5][7] The order book rose from ₹150 crore in March 2025 to ₹400 crore in March 2026 and ₹760 crore in September 2026.[6] The order book is now 2.3 times trailing twelve-month revenue of about ₹330 crore, which is the best single piece of evidence that the demand is real.[26][5]

Where the position is thin

The first weakness is the import dependence described in Chapter 1. If the main core supplier cut volumes, raised prices or favoured another buyer, Yash would have no second source until its own cores pass type tests. Management says the first results from its local core are encouraging and that type tests should start within weeks, and it will keep importing cores for a couple of years even afterwards.[5] The second weakness is customer concentration. The top 10 customers made 85.8%, 86.8% and 80.5% of revenue in FY22, FY23 and FY24, and 84.0% in the three months to June 2024.[3] The company has not published a newer split. A single large transformer maker that moved volume away would hurt.[3] The third is competition. The prospectus names Hitachi Energy India, Crompton Greaves and GE T&D India as rivals in high-voltage bushings, and on the October 2026 call management said a couple of companies had announced plans to enter beyond the captive units of the large transformer makers.[3][5] Management also says Chinese suppliers have so far affected component makers less than transformer makers.[5]

Governance and control

The auditor, Shah Mehta & Bakshi, gave a clean opinion on internal financial controls for FY26.[1] Two items need attention. First, the company lost ₹2.10 crore to an email-impersonation fraud on a China-based supplier payment in March 2026 and booked it as an exceptional item; the Board says no employee was involved and recovery is being pursued.[4][1] Second, the promoter family has been selling in the market: Keyur Shah and Twinkle Shah sold 52,000 and 248,000 shares at about ₹947 on 7 September 2026, and earlier sold smaller blocks near ₹748 in May 2026.[15] The promoter stake fell from 57.94% in March 2026 to 53.79% in September 2026, partly because new shares were issued and partly because of these sales.[14][13] No promoter shares are pledged.[13] The sales are small against the family’s holding of 1,60,75,595 shares, yet they came while the price was rising, which an owner should note.[13][15]

Cyclicality and what could end the good run

Bushing demand follows transformer output, and transformer output follows grid spending, which has been strong for several years but is political and slow to reverse. Management argues that a niche component with high entry barriers will not see the price wars typical of commodity products, and that margins can hold for five or six years.[5] History in other component markets suggests caution: when new capacity arrives, price and margin pressure tend to follow, first at lower voltage classes. An analyst raised exactly this concern on the May 2026 call, and management replied that its mix, geography and own cores would protect margins.[7] We treat the current 28% EBITDA margin as a peak-like level that must be proven, not as a floor.[6]
