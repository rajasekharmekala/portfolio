Before you read

AWL Agri Business, formerly Adani Wilmar, sells Fortune cooking oil and packaged staples, and Singapore-based Wilmar owns 56.94 percent of it.[12][11] We reject the stock for now, because its chart is in a Stage 4 downtrend that fails the recovery test and its operating scorecard reaches only 2.5 of 5.0.[31] The business is large and safe on debt, with ₹82,318 crore of revenue and no pledge, but profit is thin and first-half cash flow turned negative.[1][13][22] The shares fell 6.16 percent on 28 October after quarterly profit slipped 2.3 percent, and no corporate action or share sale explains the drop.[21][15][14] The call is wrong if cash returns in the December quarter and the price closes above its 20-day average of ₹180.34[31].

1. What the company does

AWL Agri Business buys edible oils, wheat, rice and other farm products in bulk, processes them in its own plants, packs them under brands such as Fortune, Kohinoor and Kings, and sells them to shops, restaurants and factories. Think of a very large kitchen supplier. In the 12 months to September 2026 its sales were ₹82,318 crore, and the first half of FY27 alone brought ₹42,250 crore.[1][2] Fortune is sold to about 135 million households, which the company puts at one in every three Indian families, through 24 plants in 11 states and a network of more than 10,000 distributors.[2] Most of the money still comes from cooking oil. In the September 2026 quarter edible oil gave 77 percent of revenue, packaged food gave 10 percent, and industrial ingredients gave 13 percent.[3]

The business earns a small slice on a very large flow of goods. In the September quarter the operating profit, called EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), was ₹623 crore on revenue of ₹22,202 crore, a margin of 2.8 percent.[1][2] Put another way, the company keeps about ₹2.80 of every ₹100 it sells before paying for borrowing and equipment. A rupee-level move in the price of crude palm oil, which the company imports, can wipe out a large part of that margin, so how well it buys and hedges matters more than how well it sells.[4]

That small slice is repeated many times a year, which is why the company can earn a return at all on such thin margins. At 10 percent that return is below the portfolio's 15 percent floor, as Chapter 3 shows. Company data show revenue of 4.0 times the capital employed, and an operating return of 2.5 percent on revenue. Multiply the two and you get a return on capital employed (ROCE) of 10 percent over the last 12 months.[3] The ratio called ROCE tells you how many rupees of yearly operating profit the business earns for each 100 rupees it ties up in plants, stock and customer bills.

How crude oil becomes a bottle on a shelf

AWL imports crude palm, soybean and sunflower oil, mainly from Indonesia, Malaysia, Argentina, Brazil, Ukraine and Russia, and refines it at port plants such as the one at Mundra, which can process 5,000 tonnes a day.[4][2] The rest of the supply chain is local: mustard, groundnut, cottonseed and rice bran oils are crushed or refined inland, close to where the seeds grow.[4] Imported oil is usually bought on letters of credit, which are short-term promises from a bank to pay the supplier. The company said it had ₹8,500 crore of this supplier credit and does not count it as net debt, meaning borrowings minus cash, because the cash only leaves on the payment date.[22]

The refined oil is then sold in three ways. Branded packs go to households through general trade, e-commerce and quick-commerce apps. Bulk oil goes to restaurants, hotels and caterers (the HoReCa channel). Part goes to industrial buyers. In the September quarter, quick commerce grew more than 40 percent, branded exports grew 72 percent and HoReCa grew 22 percent.[2] Direct reach is now about 9.77 lakh outlets, and the company says its total reach is more than 26 lakh outlets in over 63,000 rural towns.[3]

The three businesses and what each one needs

Edible oil is the scale business. It sold 1.1 million tonnes in the quarter and earned ₹4,091 of EBITDA on each tonne. Management's guidance, which is its own published forecast, is ₹4,000 to ₹4,500 per tonne for FY27 and 5 to 6 percent volume growth. The quarter met the profit range and the low end of the volume range.[3][10] The business is working-capital heavy, meaning a lot of cash is tied up in oil in tanks and in transit before the sale is made, and its capital employed jumped to ₹12,365 crore from ₹7,926 crore at March 2026.[3]

Food and FMCG is the growth story. It covers Kohinoor basmati rice, wheat flour, pulses, besan, sugar, soya nuggets and the Tops range of sauces and noodles. Its revenue grew 32 percent in the quarter, but its profit fell 7 percent to ₹71 crore, and its return on capital employed is 6 percent, well below the 20 to 25 percent the company says it wants as the business matures.[3] Management has said it will put sales growth ahead of margin at least through FY27 and perhaps part of FY28.[9]

Industry Essentials makes oleochemicals (fatty acids, glycerine and soap noodles), castor derivatives and de-oiled cake. It is the best-earning of the three on a capital basis, at 17 percent ROCE, and it grew volumes 26 percent in the quarter. Management warned that 26 percent is not repeatable and guided to 10 to 11 percent growth in the coming quarters, because de-oiled cake profit depended on favourable prices this time.[3][22]

Who owns the company and why it matters

AWL was a joint venture between the Adani Group and Wilmar International of Singapore, set up in 1999 and listed in February 2022. Adani sold its whole 44 percent stake in stages during 2025 for about ₹15,707 crore, ending with a block sale of the last 7 percent at ₹275 per share on 20 November 2025.[4][17] Wilmar, through its subsidiary Lence Pte. Ltd., now holds 56.94 percent, is the only promoter, and has made AWL a foreign-owned and controlled company under India's rules.[16][11]

For you as an owner, this removes the group-governance question that used to hang over the stock, and it adds a different one. The controlling shareholder is also a supplier, a trading partner and a lender in a small way. Shareholders approved material related-party transactions with Wilmar Trading Pte. Ltd. and ADM International SARL, and the audit committee says these are at arm's length.[11] The management audit in the chapter on company specifics lists how management has behaved on these points, and Chapter 3 shows the pledge and debt tests.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is yearly operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. The company, the annual report, the rating agency and Screener each compute it differently, and Chapter 3 shows all four.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is operating profit before financing and equipment costs.
  • Operating margin is operating profit divided by revenue. AWL earns about 3 percent, which is normal for an edible-oil processor.
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and customer bills, minus the credit that suppliers give. It rises when the company holds more oil in storage.
  • Stage 4 is the downtrend phase of a share price, when the price sits below falling 50-day and 200-day averages.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has been moving. Below 30 is called oversold.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing, measured in rupees.
  • Limit order is an instruction to buy only at your price or lower, so it never chases a rising price.
  • Reverse DCF is a calculation that starts from today's share price and solves for the yearly growth the price already assumes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A good way to judge AWL is to ask what a rival would find hard to copy. The answer is three things: the Fortune name, the reach into small shops, and the buying power that comes from being part of Wilmar. Each is real and each has a number behind it. None of them has yet produced a high return on the money invested, and that gap is the main finding of this chapter.[2][3][4]

What is strong

The brand and the shelf space come first. CARE Ratings describes Fortune as the single largest brand in India's organised edible-oil market and says AWL leads in soybean, mustard and rice bran oil and sits among the top three in palm, groundnut and sunflower oil. In branded basmati rice, wheat flour and besan it is also among the top three.[4] Management said edible-oil market share rose 60 basis points in the March 2026 quarter, and that its share on e-commerce and quick-commerce platforms stays above 30 percent.[9][3]

The distribution network is the second strength, because a small shop will stock only a few oil brands. Direct reach grew from about 7.8 lakh outlets in September 2024 to 9.77 lakh in September 2026, and rural town coverage grew from 37,604 to more than 63,000 over the same period.[3] Management now says the build-out is largely done and the focus has moved to selling more through each outlet.[10]

The third strength is parentage and credit standing. CARE reaffirmed AA-, with a stable outlook, on ₹18,111 crore of bank limits in September 2026, citing the Wilmar link, procurement scale and risk-management policies.[4] The same AA- rating was in place a year earlier, when the rated limits were ₹15,711 crore.[5] Gearing, which compares borrowing with owners' money, was 0.65 times at March 2026, and the rating agency expects it to stay below 1.0 times.[4]

What is weak

The weak point is the return the business earns on the capital it uses. CARE puts ROCE at about 12.5 percent in FY26 and expects similar levels, because the new food assets have not yet matured.[4] The company's own slide shows total ROCE of 15 percent in FY25, 13 percent in FY26 and 10 percent in the last 12 months, as capital employed grew from ₹14,347 crore to ₹19,548 crore in six months.[3] Chapter 3 shows that a different data vendor reports 18.3 percent, and explains why that figure should be read with care.

The second weak point is that profit depends on forces the company does not control. Edible-oil prices, import duties and the rupee all move the margin, and CARE notes that duty changes between September 2024 and June 2025 hurt profit in FY25 and the first quarter of FY26.[4] On 24 September 2026 the government cut the basic customs duty on crude palm and soybean oil to 5 percent and scrapped the 10 percent duty on crude sunflower oil, which lowers landed costs and can pull down selling prices on stock the company already holds.[19] The CEO said the effect would be limited because of the brand, and that unbranded players are more exposed.[22]

The third weak point is operational and regulatory. The food regulator FSSAI penalised AWL in August 2026 after a sample of Fortune Fryola sunflower oil, taken from a Goa resort, showed Vitamin A and Vitamin D below the limits in the fortification rules. The amount of the penalty was not disclosed.[18] The company's Bangladesh subsidiary also created a tax drag in the latest quarter, because Bangladesh law taxes a minimum amount on revenue even when profit is thin.[2]

How the position compares with other listed food and edible-oil names

The market prices AWL like a processor and not like a brand owner. Its market value is ₹21,497 crore against revenue of ₹82,318 crore, or 0.26 times sales. Its price-to-earnings ratio, which is the share price divided by earnings per share, is 18.4. Patanjali Foods, which also sells edible oil and packaged food, trades at 0.95 times sales, and basmati rice maker KRBL at 1.3 times.[1][26][27] A fair reading is that the gap reflects margin, not neglect: AWL keeps about 3 percent of revenue as operating profit, while Patanjali Foods keeps 4.6 percent and KRBL keeps 17 percent.[26][27] Management argues that 70 percent of its revenue comes from brands, and the market has so far not paid a brand multiple for it.[9]

What this means for an owner: the franchise is hard to dislodge, which lowers the risk of a permanent loss from competition, but it does not guarantee a high return. The investment case rests on one question, which is whether the food and chemicals businesses can raise the return on capital, now that the capital they use has grown by more than a third in six months. The next chapter tests that with five operating measures.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management credibility matters more than usual here, because the business sells at a thin margin and the share price rests on promises of improving returns. The record is mixed. On the oil profit per tonne it has delivered, and on food margin it has stayed inside its range. On volume and on return on capital it has fallen short. In the first half of FY26, profit after tax had fallen 23 percent to ₹483 crore.[6][9][3][7]

In May 2025 the company said it expected edible-oil EBITDA of ₹3,500 to ₹3,600 per tonne in FY26 and was hopeful of 7 to 8 percent volume growth. Edible-oil volume grew 6 percent in FY26, and the profit per tonne came in near ₹3,500 on the company's count and ₹3,425 on CARE's.[6][9][4] In the same call, management said an overall ROCE of 15 percent would "look better" as food began to earn margin. The company's own figure is 13 percent for FY26 and 10 percent for the last 12 months.[6][3]

On profit the company is inside its own range. The FY27 guide for the edible-oil segment is ₹4,000 to ₹4,500 per tonne, and the September quarter delivered ₹4,091. The company states about ₹4,700 per tonne for the first half. That figure rests on a stronger June quarter, because first-half segment profit of ₹959 crore less ₹435 crore in September leaves ₹524 crore for June. The segment profit also includes derivative gains and losses, so the September figure is the cleaner reading. Management itself asks investors to look at two to four quarters together.[10][3][22]

On governance, the clean-up is real. The Adani exit finished in November 2025, and the promoter pledge is zero in all 12 quarters shown, from June 2024 to September 2026.[17][13] Two items still need watching. The chief financial officer was described as interim on both the April and July 2026 calls, and the Madhur sugar brand is licensed from Shree Renuka Sugars for a 0.5 percent royalty on sales, a company that shares a director with Wilmar International.[9][10][11]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks two things. Does the company clear the portfolio's hard red lines, and which of three archetypes does it fit? Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain you hope for divided by the loss you accept, and each archetype has a minimum. The red lines are debt-to-equity of 1.20 or more, interest cover below 2.0 times, a promoter pledge of 25 percent or more, three-year cash-to-profit below 0.25, and a Stage 4 chart that fails Test C. The first archetype is a steady compounder, with ROCE of 15 percent or more and asymmetry of at least 2.0. The second is a turnaround or value inflection, with two or more improving trends and asymmetry of at least 2.5. The third is a tactical breakout, which is a stock in Stage 2, the confirmed uptrend, that moves above a prior ceiling on heavy volume, with asymmetry of at least 2.5. The entry zone is the range of prices where a buyer plans to buy.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report rests on assumptions that could be wrong, and this chapter names the weakest one for each, what evidence would show it is wrong and when that evidence arrives. It also sets out the strongest case against our own view, which an adversary would make, and the one claim on which the whole verdict hinges.

The red-team bear case

The strongest case against the stock is that the first half shows AWL is a working-capital business that has used growth to hide a cash drain. Capital employed rose by ₹5,201 crore in six months, from ₹14,347 crore to ₹19,548 crore, while operating profit over the same half was ₹1,347 crore. The company counts supplier credit as capital but the cash must be paid on the due date, so any fall in oil prices after the 24 September duty cut turns high-cost stock into a margin loss.[3][23][19][2] The CEO expects only limited impact, but management also said the inventory rose from a slow September, which implies the sales that were meant to move the oil did not happen.[22]

The bear case adds three smaller points. The chart shows seven distribution days (down days on heavy volume) in the last 25 sessions. Foreign holders cut from 21.86 percent in March to 19.50 percent in September, while mutual funds rose from 8.17 percent to 10.03 percent. AustralianSuper, a foreign pension fund, bought 9,485,998 shares at ₹274.95 in November 2025 and sold 16,504,965 shares at ₹180.14 on 2 July 2026.[31][1][13][14] The controlling shareholder is a supplier, and a licence for the Madhur sugar brand comes from a company with a common director, so the related-party flows deserve a line-by-line read in the next annual report.[11][10] Finally, a food regulator order on fortified oil shows that quality control in a brand sold at scale is exposed to single-sample failures.[18]

Where the bear case could be wrong

A fair review also states the case for the stock. The rating agency calls liquidity strong, with ₹2,333 crore of unencumbered cash and AA- credit, and net debt had fallen to ₹4,425 crore at March 2026.[4] Operating profit was in line with a pessimistic estimate, edible-oil profit per tonne is inside the guided range, and the company says the added stock will correct in the October to December quarter.[24][3][22] Capitulation volume at a 52-week low can be followed by a floor, and if the cash recovers the stock could look cheap at 18 times earnings very quickly. We do not rule that out. The rules, and the evidence we have today, say wait for proof.

The decision is REJECTED, which means no new money. The rule is the portfolio's hard red line for a Stage 4 chart that fails Test C. A rejection is not a verdict that the business is poor. AWL is a large, solvent franchise with no pledge and a strong parent, and the price is about 18 times earnings.
