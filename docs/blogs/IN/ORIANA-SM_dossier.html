1. What the company does

Oriana Power Limited is a Noida company, founded in 2013, that has been listed on the NSE platform for small companies since August 2023.[1][9][36] It trades under the brand name TrueRE. Three founders run it: Parveen Kumar, Rupal Gupta and Anirudh Saraswat. They held 57.98% of the shares at March 2026.[1][9] The company works in three steps. It builds solar plants, it keeps part of the ownership through single-plant companies, and it sells those companies on to funds.

The main product is an engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) contract. EPC means the company designs a plant, buys the panels and equipment, builds it and hands over a working station. Sales of solar plants were INR 1,756 crore of the INR 1,814 crore of FY26 revenue, which is 96.8%. Power sales were INR 33 crore and other operating income INR 22 crore.[2] The company is not a panel maker. It buys panels, inverters and cables, so its margin depends on what it pays for those parts and on how well it hedges the price.[9]

The buyers fall into two groups. The first group is outside customers. The June 2025 presentation names Dalmia Bharat Cement (128 MWp), JK Cement (110 MWp), the Maharashtra power distributor MSEDCL (100 MWp) and Bharat Petroleum (71 MWp) as project wins, and a cement company placed a 50 MW order worth INR 214.80 crore in August 2026.[8][19] The second group is the company's own project companies. The annual report shows INR 1,085.10 crore of FY26 plant sales to promoter-group entities and project companies in which control is intended to be temporary, which is 59.8% of revenue. A year earlier it was INR 385.24 crore, or 39.0%.[2] This second group matters more than any other fact about the business, and Chapter 3 tests what it means for cash.

The model behind the second group is build, own, operate and transfer (BOOT). Oriana forms a special purpose vehicle (SPV), which is a company that owns one plant. It bills the SPV for building the plant, so the work appears as revenue and profit in Oriana's accounts. The SPV borrows from banks, with Oriana standing behind the loan as guarantor. Later Oriana sells its 74% stake. The buyer is Leo Energies, formerly Helioact Power India 1, a group company of the investor Actis GP LLP.[14][13] The first two sales closed in September 2026: Truere Guj (about 70 MWp with 30 MWh of battery) at an enterprise value of about INR 325 crore, and Truere Current (about 40 MWp with 20 MWh) at about INR 176 crore.[15][16] That is 110 MWp of the 238 MWp that was announced, so less than half the programme is done.[4][35]

The newest line is battery storage. On 21 September 2026 MSEDCL awarded the company two contracts for 900 MW and 1,800 MWh of battery storage systems in Maharashtra. The tariffs are INR 2,40,000 and INR 2,38,000 per MW per month, each for 15 years, with an estimated total value of INR 3,870 crore over the term and expected government grants (viability gap funding) of INR 324 crore.[18] The company's own words in June were that it had not won a battery project for months, because it refused to bid at the low prices on offer.[5] Management also said battery work would be 35% to 40% of FY27 revenue.[5] A battery plant that the company owns earns tariff over 15 years and does not create up-front EPC revenue, so that revenue claim only holds if the company sells the battery companies on, as it does for solar.[5] The project cost and the funding plan for the new awards are not published.

Green ammonia is the long-dated bet. The company signed a 10-year agreement with the Solar Energy Corporation of India for 60,000 tonnes a year, valued at about INR 3,135 crore.[4] Management expects no revenue from it in FY27 and about 10% of revenue in FY28.[5] Crisil lists the funding of that project's equity as a rating sensitivity.[9]

What would move the company to the main board

Companies on the NSE small-company platform can move to the main board after three years of listing, with at least INR 50 crore of net worth and 1,000 public shareholders.[34] Oriana listed in August 2023 and has 15,186 shareholders and net worth of about INR 763 crore, so it appears to meet those tests.[36][1][2] Management calls the move a key strategic objective but has set no date.[5] A move would widen the set of possible buyers, which matters because only about 185,000 shares traded on 16 October.[24]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPC (engineering, procurement and construction): a contract to design and build a plant for a customer.
  • IPP (independent power producer) and BOOT (build, own, operate, transfer): the company builds a plant, owns it for a time, sells the power, then transfers or sells it.
  • SPV (special purpose vehicle): a company that exists to own one project, so its debt stays with that project.
  • BESPA (battery energy storage purchase agreement): a long contract that pays the owner a fixed monthly tariff for each megawatt of battery capacity.
  • VGF (viability gap funding): a government grant that covers part of the cost of a project that would not otherwise pay.
  • TReDS: an exchange where a supplier's unpaid invoice from a large buyer is sold to a bank at a discount, so the supplier is paid early and the buyer pays the bank later.
  • ALMM (Approved List of Models and Manufacturers): the government list of solar panels and cells that projects may use; List-II covers cells.
  • Order book: signed work not yet delivered. Adjusted debt: debt plus items a rating agency treats as debt.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Oriana has real strengths. Sales rose from INR 124 crore in FY22 to INR 1,814 crore in FY26, and profit from INR 7 crore to INR 252 crore.[1] Return on capital employed (ROCE), meaning operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business, was 39.7%.[1] Crisil raised the credit rating from BBB to A-, and its January 2026 bulletin refers back to a rationale of 2 September 2025. It reaffirmed A- with a stable outlook in June 2026.[4][12][9] The order book was INR 6,612 crore at 31 March 2026, which is 3.6 times FY26 revenue, and management put it near INR 6,800 to 7,000 crore in June.[9][5][36] The company had delivered 835 MW of solar projects and was executing 700 MW more.[4] Promoters released every pledged share on 28 September 2026, so none of their shares are pledged now.[17]

The market around it is growing. India had 171.05 GW of installed solar capacity at 30 September 2026, and it added 20.79 GW in the first half of FY27.[32] Government cell rules keep changing, which raises the cost and the risk of imported parts: the first approved list of solar cells (List-II) was issued on 31 July 2025 and was revised for the twelfth time on 8 October 2026.[33] A company that hedges its inventory at the time of order, as Crisil says Oriana does, can pass part of that risk to the customer.[9]

The weaknesses are in how the profit is earned and funded. The advantage Oriana claims is skill in land, grid connection and fast execution, not a patent or a factory. Those advantages are real but copyable, and the bids for batteries show how quickly price competition arrives: management said bids had fallen to about INR 1.46 lakh per MWh per month and that it doubted such projects could survive.[5] The company then won 900 MW of capacity at about INR 2.4 lakh per MW per month three months later, which are different units and not comparable without the project cost, which has not been published.[18]

Weakness one: the customer is often the company itself

In FY26, INR 1,085.10 crore of the INR 1,813.67 crore of revenue came from plant sales to promoter-group entities and project companies in which control is intended to be temporary. INR 286.10 crore of receivables were due from the same group, which is 42.6% of the INR 671.14 crore of trade receivables on the balance sheet.[2] Those project companies are not consolidated while they are held for sale, so the profit on building them is booked in full, while the debt they carry sits outside the accounts.[2] The auditors made the classification of these holdings as assets held for sale a key audit matter, noting that the sales were not completed in the year and that the judgement turns on the likelihood and timing of completion.[2]

This is a legitimate model and Actis has now paid for 110 MWp. It still means the profit is real only when the sale closes. Until then the company has put cash into the project companies: INR 462.98 crore of equity and debentures, and INR 165.86 crore of loans, with INR 537.98 crore shown as assets held for sale.[2]

Weakness two: the balance sheet is bigger than it looks

The consolidated accounts show borrowings of INR 508.55 crore against net worth of INR 763.43 crore, a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.67.[2] Crisil's adjusted ratio is 1.23, up from 0.33 a year earlier.[9] The gap comes from items outside the borrowing line. Credit arrangement liabilities, which include invoices sold through TReDS and bank letters of credit, rose from INR 137.69 crore to INR 719.55 crore.[2] The company explained that it uses TReDS limits of 180 days.[5] In addition, the notes disclose guarantees of INR 1,384.77 crore for borrowings of the held-for-sale project companies, which are secured on plants carried at INR 1,825.03 crore that are not in the consolidated balance sheet.[2] Promoters have also given personal guarantees of INR 334 crore on term loans and INR 254 crore on working-capital lines.[2]

Weakness three: management's numbers have been too high

The company told investors in June 2025 that FY26 revenue would be INR 2,000 to 2,500 crore, and in December 2025 that it was trying to get over INR 2,000 crore. It reported INR 1,814 crore.[6][7][2] Management said earlier calls guided 100% profit growth for FY26 and FY27, and FY26 delivered 59%.[5] Crisil projects FY27 revenue growth of only 8% to 12%, while management speaks of 40% to 50% a year.[10][5] When two careful readers of the same order book differ by a factor of four, the lower number is the safer planning figure. Our central revenue figure of INR 2,100 crore sits just above Crisil's range, and Chapter 5 shows the value at Crisil's range too.

What the price says about all of this

At INR 268.05 the stock is valued at INR 2,723 crore, or 10.8 times FY26 profit, and the stock has fallen 56% from its 52-week high of INR 613 on a split-adjusted basis.[1] Three listed solar EPC peers trade at 14.2, 16.8 and 17.2 times profit.[28][30][29] The discount to peers is about 25% to 37%. Part of it is the small-company platform and thin trading. We think most of it is the market charging for the three weaknesses above, which is why Chapter 3 tests each one with the audited numbers.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

The management walk-the-talk audit compares what the company said one to two years ago with what it delivered. The record is good for FY25 and poor for FY26. In June 2025 management said it had committed to about INR 800 crore of FY25 revenue and delivered INR 987 crore, and it said the market expected INR 130 to 140 crore of profit and it delivered more than INR 150 crore.[6] Then the pattern turned. Revenue, capacity and profit targets for FY26 were all missed, and the Actis sale, which management had expected near the end of March 2026, closed in two parts on 25 and 30 September 2026.[7][15][16]

On capital allocation, FY26 spent INR 152.68 crore on plant and equipment and INR 259.50 crore on investments in project companies held for sale, placed INR 138.05 crore in longer deposits, and raised INR 75 crore of 11.65% debentures in January 2026.[2] It paid no dividend and raised no equity in FY26.[2][5] FY25 included a share-premium raise of INR 205.15 crore.[3] Capital has gone into building and financing plants for sale. That is consistent with the stated model, and it is the reason the cash comes back only on a sale.

One governance event is minor and worth noting. Crisil recorded that INR 67.02 lakh of interest on unlisted debentures due on 6 July 2026 was paid on 7 July because of a delay at the bank, and it judged the delay to be an operational one-off.[10]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate rests on a weakest assumption. This chapter names the assumption behind each value, says what evidence would show it wrong, and gives the date by which we will know. It then sets out the strongest honest case against our own sell call, and picks the single claim on which the position turns.

Base value: revenue of INR 2,100 crore at a 20% margin

The base value of INR 325 assumes the second-half run rate holds for a full year. The weakest part is the margin: the company's own measure fell from 24.86% to 23.45% over a year and the second half was 4 points lower than a year earlier.[2][1] Crisil says 18% to 20% is the range it expects, and the margin on battery projects is unproven.[9] The evidence will come in the half-year results to September 2026. The board met on 13 November 2024 and 14 November 2025 for the equivalent results, so we expect a date near 13 to 14 November, but no date has been announced.[27][26] If the half-year margin is under 19%, the base value falls to about INR 290.

Bull value: the Actis gain and the multiple

The bull value uses an assumed after-tax gain of INR 80 crore, which is our placeholder, because no gain has been disclosed. Management said the sale would add 6 to 8 points to profit margin in its December 2025 call, but it did not give the sum.[7] The deal terms changed in June: the enterprise value for two subsidiaries rose from INR 414.30 crore to INR 488.70 crore, while the third was still being negotiated.[14] The half-year results will show the gain. At a gain of INR 40 crore after tax the bull value is about INR 633, and with no gain it is about INR 562, so the bull value stays above INR 500 unless revenue or the multiple also disappoint.

Bear value: a 30% chance

The 30% weight on the bear case is a judgement. The bear case needs a trigger, and the triggers are visible. Crisil lists a delay in selling project companies and total outside liabilities to net worth above 2.5 as downgrade factors.[10] The company already paid debenture interest a day late in July 2026, which Crisil called a one-off.[10] A larger worry is the funding of 900 MW of new battery contracts, because the filings give no project cost and no funding plan.[18] A rating action or a delay in the next two tranches would be the sign. The weights are our best view and not a forecast.

The red-team bear case against selling

The strongest case against our own sell call is this. At 10.8 times profit, the stock is priced below every listed peer, and the company has just signed 900 MW of battery contracts, cleared its promoter pledges and closed two Actis sales for INR 501 crore of enterprise value.[1][18][17][35] Our own weighted value is 28% above the price. A holder with a 2.2% position and a stop 12.2% below the price is risking about 0.27% of net worth for the chance of a 160% gain in the bull case. On those numbers, many investors would hold.

We still sell because three framework rules fire at once. The adjusted debt ratio is above the red line, one of five KPIs passes, and reward-to-risk is 1.73 against 3.5. The positive expected value depends on a profit figure that the filings show is mostly billed to controlled project companies and funded by bank credit, and the stock has no analyst coverage to cross-check it. The sell call is also cheap to reverse, because the half-year results will arrive in about four weeks.

Three other limits apply. First, the price feed is inconsistent around the split. The decision is to sell all 375 shares. The verdict is REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER, and the rule behind it is the one for current holdings that fail two or more operating checks. Three facts trigger it. Reward-to-risk is 1.73 on a base target of 325 and a stop of 235.21, against a floor of 3.5. The instruction is a single order: sell 375 shares with a limit order at 268.05 or better. A limit order only fills at your price or better and does not chase. At 268.05 the order brings in INR 100,518.75, which is 2.2% of net worth. If it does not fill within five sessions, lower the limit in steps to 260. Selling everything is the right answer because the three rule failures are independent of the chart, and the price offers no margin for error: the stop is 12.2% below the 268.05 close and 11.2% below the 265.00 cost, and the bear value is 50% below the close. Holding is the wrong answer because the expected gain depends on a profit figure that is mostly billed to controlled project companies and funded by supplier credit. Adding is wrong for the same reason. The cheap price is a fact, and it explains why the loss from selling is small, but it does not cure the red line.

The ladder reading deserves a clear statement. The support-distance ladder maps 0.48 ATR from the ledger to the FULL_SIZE band, which would allow a full-size buy for a name that passes every gate. Corrected for the split, the distance to the 20-day swing low is 0.96 ATR (the STANDARD band), and to the 63-day low it is 1.99 ATR (the NO_BUY band). The ladder says how much to buy at what distance. It does not override the red line, so no purchase is made on any reading.

The let-winners-run rule does not apply. This is a 1.15% winner against cost, with the chart in a base, and the rule needs a Stage 2 chart with intact fundamentals and rising estimates. None of these holds. If the name later cures the red line and rebuilds a Stage 2 chart, the rule would apply again and allow the weight to rise toward 22% without mechanical trimming.

Here is why the size is zero and the tier is TIER_0. TIER_2 is for names that pass every gate. TIER_3 and TIER_4 are for names that pass the red lines but fail floors. This name fails a red line, so it gets TIER_0. If the half-year results cure the red line, the most we would hold is TIER_4, which allows 1% to 2% of net worth with a risk budget of 35 basis points.
