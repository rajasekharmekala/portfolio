1. What the company does

Oriana Power Limited is a solar and battery-storage contractor based in Noida, Uttar Pradesh.[2] The company was founded in 2013 and listed on the NSE SME platform after an IPO that opened on 1 August 2023.[34] Exchange filings use the symbol ORIANA, and your broker's log writes ORIANA-SM; the tail seems to be the series code for the SME board, which I infer from Screener's 'NSE - SM' label.[1] Most of its income comes from building solar plants for other people, a job called engineering, procurement and construction, or EPC. The rest comes from building plants it owns for a time and then selling a stake to a financial investor.

Three kinds of customer pay Oriana. Industrial companies pay for rooftop and ground-mounted plants that cut their power bills, and the company's own call in June 2025 named Dalmia Cement, JK Cement and BPCL among them.[23] Utilities and project developers pay for large plants, such as a 234 MW floating solar plant on the Maithon Dam in Jharkhand, priced at ₹1,180.53 crore across supply, EPC and maintenance contracts.[20] State power distributors pay for battery storage under long contracts, such as the two awards from Maharashtra's MSEDCL in September 2026 for 900 MW and 1,800 MWh, worth about ₹3,870 crore over 15 years, with expected government viability-gap funding of ₹324 crore.[19]

A BESS, short for battery energy storage system, is a large bank of batteries that charges when solar power is plentiful and releases it later. It matters to Oriana because the MSEDCL awards pay a fixed monthly tariff of ₹2.38 lakh to ₹2.40 lakh per megawatt for 15 years.[19] That is closer to owning an asset than to selling a service once, so the company must find the capital to build it. Management expects storage to supply about 40% of FY27 revenue, against an expected 10% in FY26.[4][22]

A second source of profit is selling stakes in the solar plants it builds, which management calls asset recycling. In May 2026 the board approved the sale of its 74% stakes in subsidiaries that own solar plants to Helioact Power India 1 Private Limited, now Leo Energies Private Limited, a group company of the investor Actis, at an estimated enterprise value of ₹954 crore.[9] The sale slipped out of FY26, and the first two parts were completed on 25 and 30 September 2026.[11][12] Until a sale completes, the stakes sit in the accounts as 'assets held for sale', which stood at ₹538 crore of investments and loans on 31 March 2026, while the plants' own bank loans, guaranteed by Oriana for ₹1,385 crore, stay outside the consolidated balance sheet.[5] Chapter 3 explains why that matters.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares; here it is on the post-split basis.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before finance costs and accounting charges.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is operating profit divided by the capital the business uses, a measure of how well each rupee is put to work.
  • debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money; the scoring rules fail a company at 1.20 or more.
  • working capital is the cash tied up in day-to-day operations, mainly money customers owe minus credit suppliers give.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before borrowing or investing.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is cash left after running the business and buying plant and equipment.
  • operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue, before interest and tax.
  • price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share, the price paid for each rupee of profit.
  • guidance is a company's own published forecast for a coming period.
  • consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for a number.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been moving; below 30 means it has fallen unusually fast.
  • ATR (average true range) is the average daily price swing in rupees, used here to measure distances on a chart.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 sessions, and the 50-day and 200-day averages work the same way over longer spans.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend phase of the Weinstein scale, as Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • pivot is the top of a base, the level a rising price must clear.
  • breakout is a move above a base or pivot on high volume, and a retest is the later pullback that tests that level from above.
  • stop loss is the price at which the thesis is judged wrong and the position is cut.
  • limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better.
  • entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry is the ratio of possible gain to possible loss from an entry, and reward-to-risk is another name for it: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).
  • net debt is borrowings minus the cash held.
  • capital expenditure is spending on plants, equipment and projects; the next chapter looks at where the business is strong and where it is weak.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business has real strengths and one structural weakness. The strengths are in the audited numbers. Consolidated revenue rose from ₹135 crore in FY23 to ₹1,814 crore in FY26, a growth rate of 138% a year over three years, and profit attributable to shareholders reached ₹252 crore.[1][2] Operating profit was ₹399 crore, a margin of 22%, and ROCE was 39.7%.[1] CRISIL, the rating agency, moved the company from BBB to A- and reaffirmed A-/Stable in July 2026, pointing to timely execution and an order book of ₹6,612 crore at 31 March 2026, which is 3.6 times FY26 revenue.[2][7]

Solar EPC has little moat. Panels, cells, batteries, copper, silver and steel are bought at market prices, tenders are won on price, and policy can change the rules, as the government's approved-list rules for solar cells and modules show.[7][4] Oriana's edge is narrower. It handles land, grid connection and construction as one package, it hedges material needs when it books an order, and it can now bid for bigger projects because its rating is A-.[7] Management says it stayed out of aggressive tenders for six to eight months because battery and lithium prices rose 40% to 80%, and that it won no battery projects in that period.[4] The September 2026 MSEDCL awards show the pause ended when the tariff worked.[19]

The weakness is how profit is earned and how growth is funded. A large part of the profit planned for FY26 was meant to come from selling plant stakes to Actis, the sale slipped, and the earnings release says reported results fell short of expectations for that reason.[2] Meanwhile growth is funded with other people's money. Other current liabilities rose from ₹329 crore to ₹1,020 crore in one year. Within that total, 'credit arrangement liabilities' rose by ₹582 crore to ₹720 crore, and the notes describe them as invoices financed through the TReDS platform (a market where suppliers sell unpaid invoices to financiers), bank letters of credit and deferred land payments. A further ₹259 crore of mobilisation deposits, received for project needs, appeared for the first time, while customer advances fell by ₹140 crore.[5] Customers owe ₹671 crore, or 135 days of sales.[5][1]

The share-price fall is not unique to Oriana. In the year to 22 October 2026 the shares lost 57.6% on a split-adjusted basis, while KPI Green Energy lost 41.4%, Waaree Renewable Technologies 43.1% and Sterling and Wilson 35.6%.[25][33][28] Oriana fell further than each of the three, so sector weakness may explain most of the decline but not all of it. No filing or broker note in the record explains the fall, so any link to the balance-sheet questions in this chapter and the next is my inference.

Guarantees and related-party signals

Oriana guarantees loans of subsidiaries and joint ventures. At 31 March 2026 the notes show ₹20.9 crore of such guarantees in the contingent-liability table and a further ₹1,384.8 crore for subsidiaries held for sale, whose plant assets of ₹1,825 crore are outside the consolidated balance sheet.[5] On 6 October 2026 the board widened a guarantee for a ₹460 crore working-capital loan to Oriana Usolar Joint Venture, the owner of the Maithon floating solar plant, from 74% to 100% of the loan.[15] Shareholders vote on related-party dealings with that venture until 8 November 2026.[16] The floating-solar purchase orders came from 'a renewable energy developer', and the guarantee filing names the venture as the developer of that project, which suggests, though no filing says so, that part of the ₹1,180.53 crore order is with a group company.[20][15]

The promoters have given personal guarantees of ₹254.5 crore to lenders.[5] Until 28 September 2026 each promoter had pledged 19.5% of his holding to two securities firms, and all pledges were released on that date.[13] In June 2026 the three promoters instead signed a non-disposal undertaking in favour of Catalyst Trusteeship Limited over 52,82,979 shares before the split, which is 26.00% of the share capital, and the company says it is not a pledge.[14] The filing does not give the reason. A trustee usually holds such comfort for lenders, so it may relate to the company's debentures, but that is my inference.

The auditor's report on the consolidated accounts (annual report pages 128 to 131) says the statements give a true and fair view, and lists three key audit matters: revenue recognition, capitalisation of project costs, and the classification of subsidiaries as assets held for sale.[5] Clause (e) of the same report refers to an 'Emphasis of Matter' paragraph that 'may have an adverse effect on the functioning of the Group', yet I found no such paragraph in the report's extracted text. It may be template carry-over, but I could not confirm that.[5] CRISIL also records that an interest payment of ₹67.02 lakh on unlisted debentures, due on 6 July 2026, was paid a day late because of a delay at the banker, which it treats as technical.[7]

What this means for an owner

An owner of this business gets fast growth, strong reported returns and a rating that has improved, in an industry where margins can be squeezed by input prices and policy. An owner also carries a balance sheet that depends on suppliers' and banks' short-term credit, on customers paying within about four and a half months, and on stake sales closing. If any of those three supports weakens, the growth story weakens with it, because the business has little slack. The good news is that most of the evidence needed to judge them arrives in one event, the half-year results. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in chapter 5 rests on an assumption that can fail. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each one, the evidence that would show it wrong, and when that evidence will be known. The next dated events are the postal-ballot vote on related-party dealings with Oriana Usolar, which closes on 8 November 2026, and the half-year results for the six months to 30 September 2026.[16] The company filed its half-year results on 13 and 14 November in 2024 and 2025, so I expect the next ones around mid-November, but no board-meeting date is announced, so that date is expected, not confirmed.[34]

The base revenue and margin

The base EPS of ₹23.31 assumes revenue of about ₹2,000 crore and an operating margin of 19%, the middle of CRISIL's ranges. Management says 40% to 50% growth is achievable, and CRISIL says 8% to 12%, so the assumption is the weakest where the two disagree most.[7][4] The evidence that would show the base wrong is the first-half revenue and margin: revenue well above ₹1,000 crore for the half (28% above the ₹781 crore of a year earlier) would show the base is too low, while revenue near ₹781 crore with a margin at or below 21% would support the bear case.[1] We will know when the half-year results are filed. The two sources differ because management plans from signed orders and the rating agency plans from a lower assumption about how fast orders turn into revenue. Neither has been tested against a full year yet. The first half of FY27 is the cleanest early test, because the order book was already large when it began.

The 14.0 times multiple

The multiple is the average of two peers, which makes it as weak as the peer group is thin. Over the past year those peers fell about 41% to 43%, and if the sector re-rated to 10 times, base value would be ₹233.1, almost exactly the stop, so the multiple carries most of the base case's upside.[33][28] The evidence is how peers trade on their own half-year results in November, and the evidence on Oriana is whether it trades at a discount to them that narrows once the funding questions are answered. A second risk is that the two peers are not truly alike. KPI Green earns a 36% margin because it owns plants, while Waaree earns about 19% as a contractor, so an average of the two blends different businesses. If a better comparison were available, the multiple could be higher or lower by several turns, and the base value would move by about ₹23 for each turn.

Cash quality and the funding mix

Reported cash from operations of ₹337 crore rests on liabilities that rose far faster than revenue. Credit arrangement liabilities went from ₹138 crore to ₹720 crore, mobilisation deposits of ₹259 crore appeared, and customers owed ₹671 crore against ₹394 crore a year earlier.[5][2] If the credit lines are ordinary supplier credit, the balance sheet is stronger than the ratio suggests. If they are bank or investor-financed invoices, they behave like debt, the adjusted debt-to-equity is 1.61, and CRISIL's adjusted figure of 1.23, with its standalone gearing of 1.2 times, is at or above the 1.20 line and would fail it if that basis applied.[7][28] CRISIL names a total-outside-liabilities ratio above 2.5 times as a downgrade trigger, and a delay in monetising the plant stakes as another.[7] The estimate cannot be settled from the annual report alone, because the note does not split the line by instrument. The half-year balance sheet and its notes are the earliest place the split could appear, and a request to the company's investor contact is the other route.

Governance and disclosure

Four items could change the view: guarantees of ₹20.9 crore on the face of the notes, a further ₹1,384.8 crore for held-for-sale subsidiaries, and a new ₹460 crore guarantee for the floating-solar joint venture; a related-party vote on that venture; an auditor's clause that refers to an emphasis paragraph I could not find; and a debenture interest payment made one day late.[5][15][16][7] The investor presentation also carried a capacity typo that management corrected on the June 2026 call, from 2 million tonnes to 2 lakh tonnes a year for green fuels by FY28.[4] None of these is proof of a problem. Each would be settled by the next annual report and by the vote result.

Red-team bear case against the business

The strongest honest bear case is that reported profit is partly a timing effect and the funding is fragile. The first two stake sales brought in ₹193.9 crore of consideration against ₹501 crore of enterprise value, about 39%, which suggests (an inference from the filings' wording about project financing) that the buyer takes over most of the plants' loans, and the guarantees Oriana gave on those loans fall away only as each sale completes.[10][11][12] If the third subsidiary's price is cut or delayed, if the 234 MW floating plant (valued at ₹1,180.53 crore) runs late, or if banks stop rolling the credit lines, a business with 135 receivable days and a ₹763 crore net worth would have little slack. The price may reflect that possibility, which is my inference, because no filing or broker note in the record explains the fall.[20][2]

The strongest case against selling

The opposing case is that the stock trades at 10.0 times FY26 profit, the promoters released every pledge on 28 September 2026, the company won 900 MW and 1,800 MWh of storage on 18 September, two stake sales completed in the last days of the half-year, and my own base case is 31% above the close at a reward-to-risk of 4.59 to 1.[13][19][11][12][28] Peers fell 36% to 43% in the same year, so part of the fall is the sector. The rule still says sell, because a falling chart without a base has no floor, and the evidence above is about value, not about trend.
