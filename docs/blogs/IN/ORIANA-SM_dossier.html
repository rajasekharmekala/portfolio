Primer: five terms

A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, like a trend line through daily noise. Stage analysis sorts a stock into four phases: Stage 1 is basing after a fall, Stage 2 is a rising markup, Stage 3 is topping near a peak, and Stage 4 is a falling markdown. RSI (relative strength index) is a momentum gauge from 0 to 100, where a reading below 30 means the stock has fallen unusually fast. ATR (average true range) is the stock's typical daily price swing in rupees, and we use it to measure distance from support. Asymmetry is the upside to the target divided by the downside to the stop, and the rulebook asks for at least 3.5 to 1.

Asymmetry and the target

Asymmetry is the ratio of upside to a target to downside to a stop. A ratio of 3.5 means you can expect to win three and a half times what you risk. The rulebook requires the target to be anchored to something you can verify: forward earnings times a three-year median multiple, or a measured move from a completed base. A measured move is a projection that adds the height of a base to the level it breaks out from, and it is valid only once the base is complete. An unanchored target fails the gate by definition.

The anchor used here is the earnings-times-median approach. FY26 earnings per share (EPS, profit divided by the number of shares) were ₹24.83 on the post-split count, as MarketScreener and StockAnalysis both report[19][18], and the two Screener half-year figures sum to the same[15]. The three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, the share price divided by earnings per share, is taken from MarketScreener's year-end figures for FY24, FY25 and FY26: 22.2, 16.5 and 12.4 times. The median of those three is 16.5[19]. Three points is a thin basis for a median, and the notes say so. Multiplying FY26 earnings by 16.5 gives a target of ₹409.70. No consensus forward earnings figure was available, so the target carries no growth. The full working, with low and high cases, is in the estimate section below.

On that target the ratio is 2.07 to 1, below the 3.5 floor for a tactical holding and below the 3.0 floor for a core holding. The line-by-line working is in the asymmetry section. The result does not depend on the target alone. On a measured move, which we do not use because no base has been completed, the ratio would be about 2.8, still below the floor. The gate fails.

Debtor days and cash tied up in bills

Debtor days measure how long, on average, the company waits to be paid after it bills a customer. Working capital is the cash tied up in customer bills, stock and supplier payments, and debtor days are its largest part for this business. A contractor that bills ₹1,000 and waits 135 days is financing its customer for four and a half months. On Screener's consolidated figures the debtor days were 135 at March 2026, down from 146 at March 2025 but still almost double the 75-day limit[15]. The word "underperformer" in the label refers to these rule tests, not to the stock trailing the market, which it has not done.

ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP requires gates to pass as well as support; the support condition is met, but the gates are not. KEEP_COMPOUNDING requires all gates to pass and at least three of four operating measures to pass; neither condition holds.

What would change this view:

  • If debtor days fall below 75 for a full year and operating margin holds near 22 percent, the two operating FAILs could become one WATCH, and the holding would no longer meet the replacement rule on its own.
  • If the Actis tranches complete and borrowings fall so that debt to equity stays below 0.70 on the broader definitions, the debt gate would pass cleanly rather than borderline.
  • If the stock closes above ₹342.72 with the 200-day average turning up, the technical gate moves toward a pass.

The target estimate: working, range and what would break it

The target is gathered into one place here, so a reader can see the number, the working behind it, the range of outcomes and the conditions under which it would be wrong. The estimate is a reference point for the asymmetry test. It is not a price forecast.

The number

The target is trailing earnings times that median, with no credit for growth.

Fatal flaws: the red team

A red team tries to break the case. The question is not whether the company is good but whether the verdict would survive a strong argument against it. The flaws are listed in the order a careful reader would worry about them, and each is tied to a number you can check.

Flaw one: the exit could be the wrong call on a cheap, strong stock

The strongest argument against the verdict is that the stock trades at about 11.6 times earnings, earns a 39.7 percent return on capital and has beaten Nifty over 63 and 28 sessions. A replacement would sell a cheap, high-return name that is also relatively strong. We accept the facts and do not accept that they settle the verdict. The label rests on the two operating FAILs, debtor days and the FY26 delivery shortfall. The Layer 1 failures on technical posture and asymmetry explain why no purchase or addition is allowed. Cheapness and return are real, but the rulebook does not count them as a pass for the trend or for the ratio. If the market re-rates the stock to 16.5 times, the holder gives up 41.7 percent, and that is the cost of the rule. No challenger has been verified, so the verdict is a conditional exit: keep the shares until a replacement passes the gates, then swap.

Flaw two: the monetisation stalls

The company plans to sell its entire 74 percent stake in three subsidiaries to a group entity of Actis GP. The combined enterprise value is about ₹950 crore[21]. The first tranche, covering a 40 MWp solar project with 20 MWh of battery storage, has an enterprise value of about ₹176 crore, and the company told the exchange on 30 September 2026 that it had received the consideration for that tranche[13]. The remaining tranches need shareholder approval and other clearances, and the final price is subject to closing adjustments[21][13]. If the rest slips, the company keeps the debt and working capital that the sale was meant to clear, and the debt gate tightens.

Flaw three: growth outruns cash

Revenue more than doubled in two years, and the business is funded partly by trade finance and letters of credit. Debtor days of 135 mean every new rupee billed waits a long time for collection. If a large customer delays payment, the working capital requirement rises and borrowing room falls. The cash gate passes on the three-year record, but that record includes the FY26 surge, and a year of slow collection would move it quickly.

Flaw four: the bidding war

Battery storage capacity is bought through viability gap funding tenders, such as a 125 MW / 500 MWh standalone auction whose results were announced in March 2026[26]. Management said battery tenders had become aggressive, that it had chosen to bid more carefully, and that some projects it had already won might struggle to earn an acceptable return at current costs[10]. The margin decline from 23.7 to 22.0 percent is the first sign of that pressure, not necessarily the last.

Flaw five: ownership and pledges

The promoters have pledged a small share of their holding, and their stake has fallen by about 3.4 percentage points over two years[15]. Neither is alarming on its own. Together with a share price that has fallen by more than half in a year, they could become a self-reinforcing problem if lenders act on the pledge. The pledge is a WATCH and the decline is a point to monitor.

Flaw six: the data itself

The sources disagree on figures that matter. FY26 profit is about ₹250 to ₹252 crore in four sources, but ₹158.6 crore in a summary of the audited results that repeats the FY26 profit figure as its FY25 comparative[28]. Debt to equity ranges from 0.67 to 1.59 depending on the definition. Return on capital ranges from 34.6 to 45.4 percent depending on the source. We use the figures that are internally consistent and that match most sources, and we record every conflict in the notes.

Flaw seven: the liquidity of the listing

The stock is listed on the NSE's SME platform, a smaller and less liquid market than the main board[22][8]. On 9 October about 1.66 lakh shares changed hands, a few crore rupees of trading. That is ample for 375 shares. The company had promised a move to the main board by August 2026[11]; the later Screener page still lists the stock on the SME platform[15], which is later information.

Invalidation levels and what would change the verdict

An invalidation level is a price or event that proves the case wrong. Writing these down in advance means you do not have to decide under pressure. The price levels are the stop, the warning level and the 200-day average. The events are the sale of the subsidiaries and the operating record.

Price levels that change the view:

  • A daily close below ₹230.85 on heavy volume. This is the stop. It breaks the structure, and the holder exits.
  • A daily close below ₹275 on heavy volume. This is a warning only. It is not the stop, but it would show that the recovery from ₹235 has stalled, and the holder should review the position that day.
  • A daily close above ₹342.72, the 200-day average, with that average rising over 21 sessions. This moves the stock toward Stage 2 and begins to repair the technical gate.
  • An anchored target of ₹493.20 or more would be needed for the asymmetry gate to pass at a 3.5 ratio, with the stop unchanged.

Events that change the view:

  • Termination or renegotiation of the Actis share purchase agreement, or failure of shareholder approval for the remaining tranches.
  • Order cancellations above 20 percent of the book, which was an invalidation condition in the September record[8].
  • Two consecutive quarters of negative operating cash flow.
  • A further rise in the promoter pledge, or a fall in the promoter stake below 55 percent.

Each has a clear reading. A price level says the market has changed its mind; an event says the business has changed its circumstances. The stop and the warning level are the two a holder can act on directly, and the stop is the only one that forces a decision.

What could make these estimates wrong

Every estimate here has a weak point, and a reader should know where. The stop and the target are computed. The operating record is reported. The outlook is management's. Each can be wrong in its own way.

The three-point median

The target uses a median of three year-end multiples, 22.2, 16.5 and 12.4 times[19]. Three points are too few for a reliable median. The range of outcomes is shown in the estimate section, and the target is one point inside it, not a precise value.

Trailing earnings against management's growth

The anchor uses FY26 earnings, which were earned before the Actis sale and before most of the battery projects were built. If management's guidance of 40 to 50 percent annual growth in FY27 and FY28 is met even in part, the earnings anchor and the target would rise[10]. We do not use that growth in the base case, because management has just missed its FY26 targets and the profit guidance is only medium confidence. The bull case is the reference for that outcome.

Mixing standalone and consolidated figures

Some figures come from the parent company alone and some from the group. The rulebook asks for consolidated figures, and we use them wherever available. Where only a standalone figure exists, we say so. The standalone figures give a similar picture on cash conversion and return on capital, but the debt figure changes a lot between the two bases.

Price moves after the session

The price fell to about ₹278.80 on 12 October and to about ₹273 to ₹275 on 13 October[20][18][15]. These are later information. They do not change the verdict, which rests on the two operating FAILs; the Layer 1 tests at the 9 October close explain why no addition is allowed. They show that the recovery did not continue into the next week. A reader who sees further falls should measure them against the ₹230.85 stop, not against the ₹275 warning level.

The outlook and the verdict itself

The verdict is strict. It rests on two operating FAILs, and one good year of collections could cure one of them. A reader who expects faster collection in FY27 may judge the holding more kindly than the rulebook does. That is a legitimate view, and the next annual filing can test it. The rules do not let that view override the gates, but they leave the holder free to decide whether to act now or wait for the next figures.

What we would do and when

The decision is simple to state and needs care to carry out. The rulebook gives REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER, which makes the holding a candidate for a swap. The rulebook does not name a replacement, and we have not yet found one that passes the gates. So the action is to hold, set the exit rule now and act on it when a replacement passes.

The single event that changes the decision is a daily close below ₹230.85 on the 9 October session basis, the rulebook stop. On that close, sell the whole holding without waiting for a replacement; ₹275 is a warning only and does not trigger a sale.

What we would do now

Hold the 375 shares and do not add to them. Record ₹230.85 as the stop and treat a close below it as the signal to sell the whole holding. Record ₹275 as a warning that prompts a review, not an exit. Decide in advance what a replacement must show: it must pass Layer 1, be in Stage 2 or confirmed by a close above its long average, and show an upside to downside ratio above 3.5 on an anchored target. Sell into a replacement only once it has been verified. Because the position is 2.31 percent of net worth and the money at risk to the stop is about 0.47 percent, there is no urgency that justifies selling into a weak market.

Glossary

Each term is defined in plain words. The first use in the body is marked where it appears.

  • ATR (average true range): the average daily price range over fourteen sessions, a measure of normal daily movement.
  • Asymmetry: the upside to the target divided by the downside to the stop.
  • BESS: battery energy storage system, a large battery installed at a grid connection.
  • Base: a sideways pattern a stock builds after a fall; a base target is the central case anchored to a checkable number.
  • Borrowings: money the company owes to banks and lenders.
  • Cash from operations: the cash a business generates from trading, before investment and financing.
  • Debtor days: the average number of days taken to collect payment from customers.
  • EBITDA: earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating cash earnings.
  • Entry zone: the band of prices in which buying would be sensible.
  • EPC: engineering, procurement and construction, building projects for a fee.
  • EPS (earnings per share): profit divided by the number of shares.
  • Guidance: the forecast management publishes about the coming year.
  • ISIN: the code that identifies a security.
  • Measured move: a projection that adds the height of a completed base to the level it breaks out from.
  • Moving average: the average closing price over a set number of sessions.
  • Net debt: borrowings less cash.
  • Operating margin: operating profit as a share of revenue.
  • Pledge: a promoter's shares offered as security for a loan.
  • Price-to-earnings: the share price divided by earnings per share.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed): operating profit as a share of the capital used to earn it.
  • RSI (relative strength index): a momentum gauge from 0 to 100.
  • SME platform: the NSE's market for smaller companies, with its own listing rules.
  • Split (sub-division): a change in face value that multiplies the number of shares and divides the price.
  • Stage: the four phases of a stock's trend described in the primer.
  • TReDS: an online platform through which suppliers and contractors can have their invoices financed.
  • Working capital: the cash tied up in receivables, stock and payables.
