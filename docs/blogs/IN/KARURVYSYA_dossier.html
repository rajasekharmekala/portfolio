1. What the company does

Karur Vysya Bank (KVB) was incorporated in 1916 in Karur, Tamil Nadu[7]. It now runs 921 branches, 2,192 ATMs and cash recyclers, and 349 business-correspondent outlets, which are small agent-run counters that do basic banking[1]. Think of a bank as a shop that buys money and resells it. The bank buys money from depositors by paying them interest, and it sells money to borrowers by charging them a higher rate. At 30 September 2026 it held deposits of ₹1,28,123 crore and had lent ₹1,11,589 crore[1]. In the September quarter the bank charged an average of 10.09% on its loans and paid an average of 5.49% on its deposits[1]. After paying staff, branches and bad-loan provisions, what is left is profit: ₹787.69 crore for the quarter, up from ₹573.96 crore a year earlier[2].

The seed note for this report quoted "revenue of ₹3,670.21 crore". That figure is correct, but it is a bank's total income, which adds interest earned (₹3,235.40 crore) and other income (₹434.81 crore) before any interest is paid out[2]. It grew 10.54% on the year, while net profit grew 37.24%[2]. The gap between the two growth rates is the most useful clue in the quarter: profit grew much faster than income because the bank kept more of each rupee it earned and set aside less for bad loans. Chapter 2B and Chapter 3 test whether that is lasting.

The bank's borrowers fall into four groups. Commercial loans to small and mid-sized businesses are 35% of the loan book, retail loans to households are 26%, agriculture loans are 24%, and corporate loans are 14%[1]. The first three together are called RAM (retail, agriculture and MSME, where MSME means micro, small and medium enterprises), and they are 86% of the book[3][23]. RAM loans are small and spread across many borrowers, which is why one bad borrower rarely hurts the bank. Corporate loans average about ₹42 crore each, which the chief executive describes as "literally the MSME of big banks"[3].

One product needs its own explanation: the gold loan, which the bank calls a jewel loan. A farmer or family pledges gold ornaments, the bank lends up to a fixed share of the gold's value, and the borrower pays interest. That share is called the loan-to-value (LTV) ratio. The bank's jewel book is ₹32,153 crore, or 29% of all loans, at an average LTV of 65% and with only 0.08% of the book 30 days or more overdue[1][3]. The bank charges 10.75% on agriculture gold loans and 14% on retail gold loans, and RBI rules cap the LTV at 85% for small loans and require the bank to maintain it throughout the loan[3][17]. Management credits the repricing of gold loans for part of the margin gain, so they are both a strength and the biggest concentration to watch.

How the bank is funded and what else it earns

A bank is only as good as its deposits. KVB's deposits are mostly term deposits (72.48%), where a customer locks money for a period, and the rest is CASA, short for current and savings accounts, which pay little or no interest. CASA is 27.52% of deposits, against 27.65% a year earlier[1]. A higher CASA share is better because that money is cheaper. KVB's is lower than Federal Bank's 32.07% (a December 2025 figure on Screener), so its funding advantage comes from granularity, not cheapness: 78% of term deposits are under ₹5 crore[1][30].

Besides interest, the bank earns fees (₹290 crore in the quarter, up 15%), including commission for selling insurance, which was about ₹165 crore in the last financial year[1][3]. It also earns a smaller amount from treasury, meaning trading and holding government bonds, and from recoveries on loans it had already written off. Management says treasury added 0.06% and recoveries 0.21% of assets to the return on assets in the quarter[3]. These are the less repeatable lines, and Chapter 2B watches them.

What the regulator and the rating agencies say about the bank

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) regulates the bank. Its rules set the minimum capital the bank must hold, how it must classify and provision for bad loans, and how it may lend against gold. The bank's long-term issuer rating is [ICRA]AA (Stable), which is the second-highest tier of investment grade for a bank issuer in India, and its certificate-of-deposit programme is rated A1+, the top short-term grade[10][9]. ICRA lifted the long-term rating to AA- in November 2023 and the bank's latest presentation shows AA (Stable) reaffirmed[11][1]. The bank's annual report records nil penalties, fines or settlements with regulators in the year to March 2026[6].

KVB has no single controlling owner. The promoter group holds 1.95% of the shares, mutual funds and insurers (grouped as domestic institutions) hold 42.10%, foreign portfolio investors hold 18.10%, and the public holds 37.85%, with 2,77,239 shareholders on the register at September 2026[29]. No pledge of shares by the promoter group is shown[20]. Because there is no large promoter to lean on for fresh capital, the bank has to fund its own growth from profit, and management says it does not need to raise capital in FY27[5].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Net interest income (NII): interest earned on loans and investments minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings. It was ₹1,497 crore in the quarter[1].
  • Net interest margin (NIM): NII as a percentage of the earning assets. It is the bank's equivalent of a shop's gross margin. It was 4.26% in the quarter[1].
  • Gross and net non-performing assets (GNPA (gross non-performing assets) and NNPA): loans where the borrower has stopped paying for 90 days. Gross is before the bank's provisions and net is after. They were 0.69% and 0.18% of loans[1].
  • Provision coverage ratio (PCR): how much of the bad loans the bank has already set aside money for. It was 96.13%[1].
  • Credit cost and slippages: credit cost is the yearly charge for loan losses as a share of loans. Slippages are loans that newly turned bad. They were 0.43% and 0.44% annualised in the quarter[1].
  • Capital adequacy ratio (CRAR) and CET1: the bank's own capital as a share of risk-weighted assets. CET1 is the purest layer. They were 17.97% and 17.32%, against a requirement of 11.50%[7].
  • Return on assets (ROA) and return on equity (ROE): profit as a share of the bank's assets and of its shareholders' money. They were 2.10% and 20.47% in the quarter, annualised[1].
  • Price-to-book (P/B): the share price divided by the bank's net worth per share. A bank that earns a high return on equity deserves a higher P/B.
  • Expected credit loss (ECL): a new RBI method that makes banks set aside money for losses they expect, not only those that have happened. It starts on 1 April 2027[15][16].
  • EBLR and MCLR: two ways a bank links its loan rates to a benchmark. EBLR loans move with the RBI repo rate, and 57% of KVB's loans are EBLR-linked[3].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

KVB's competitive strength is credit discipline in a book that regulators and rating agencies treat as granular. Gross non-performing assets fell from 1.73% in September 2023 to 0.69% in September 2026, and net non-performing assets fell from 0.47% to 0.18% over the same three years[29]. The bank has set aside money for 96.13% of its bad loans, and its stress book (net bad loans, restructured loans and security receipts) is 0.40% of total assets[1]. The bank's own explanation is a collection and monitoring system that flags every jewel loan whose value falls below the lending limit and asks for a top-up within a stated period, instead of leaving it to branches[3]. The evidence is the numbers, not the explanation: the bank grew its loans 20% in a year while its bad-loan ratios fell.

The second strength is earning power. Return on assets rose from 1.63% in FY24 to 1.72% in FY25, 1.93% in FY26 and 2.10% in the September 2026 quarter[1]. The chief executive's tenure shows the same shape: quarterly ROA moved from 0.59% in June 2020 to 2.05% in December 2025, and net NPA fell from 3.44% to 0.19% over that period[22]. Cost control helped: the cost-to-income ratio, the share of income eaten by running costs, fell from 48.26% in FY24 to 42.96% in FY26 and 39.85% in the latest quarter[1]. Fewer provisions helped too. This is a turnaround that has run for more than five years, not a one-quarter event, and that is the main reason we treat the bank as a quality candidate.

The weaknesses are about funding and concentration. CASA is 27.52%, flat on the year, so the bank pays market rates for most of its money[1]. Loans grew 20.4% while deposits grew 16.0%, and the net credit-to-deposit ratio rose to 86.6% from 84.9% three months earlier[20]. Gold loans are 29% of the book, and management's own ceiling is 35% of advances, so it has chosen to be concentrated[3]. Its branches are 26% metro, 20% urban, 39% semi-urban and 15% rural, and management says the expansion focus is the south first and then Maharashtra and Gujarat, which means new-market risk is coming[1][3]. The bank also competes on price in gold loans. The chief executive says some banks, "out of their anxiety" to grow, are offering a higher LTV and a lower rate[3].

Three outside forces bear on the story, and all three are dated. The RBI raised its policy repo rate by 25 basis points (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point) to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 and changed its stance to calibrated tightening[14]. The RBI's final expected-credit-loss rules start on 1 April 2027[15][16]. And the insurance regulator proposed on 23 September 2026 to cap the commissions banks earn from selling insurance, with first-year commission on loan-linked life cover proposed at 2% to 2.5% against effective payouts of about 45% today[19][18]. Management estimated in its call that bancassurance income could fall by 60% to 70% in some cases if the rules are not diluted, and said that lifting insurance penetration from 1.6% to nearly double would recover about half[3].

Moat check: what protects the profit, and what does not

A moat is a lasting advantage that competitors cannot copy quickly. KVB's advantages are knowledge of south-Indian small-business and farm borrowers, a granular deposit base, and a record of recovering loans: the bank recovered ₹82 crore from loans it had already written off in the September quarter alone, and management says about ₹2,500 crore of written-off assets remain to be chased[3]. Those are real, but they are skills, not structural protections. A rival with cheaper funds can offer a lower rate to a good borrower, and the bank's own call says it is already letting some housing and gold-loan customers go rather than cut prices[3]. We therefore score the moat as good but not wide, and we do not price the stock as a permanent compounder.

What this means for you: the franchise supports a high return on equity for as long as credit costs stay low and the margin holds, but it does not protect the margin if deposit costs rise faster than loan yields. Chapter 2B turns this into five measurable KPIs, and Chapter 3 tests them against five years of numbers.

3. The numbers, in plain words

A bank's accounts read differently from a factory's. A factory's "revenue" is what it sells. A bank's income is interest from loans and investments, plus fees and other income, and its biggest cost is the interest it pays to depositors. So we show interest earned, interest paid and the difference (net interest income), then add other income and subtract running costs and provisions. Profit is what is left.

Two standard tests in our scoring file do not fit a bank. Debt-to-equity, the ratio of borrowing to shareholders' money, is meaningless when deposits are the raw material: every bank looks highly indebted by that test. And cash flow from operations, the cash a business generates from its trading, swings with deposit and loan flows, so a bank's cash flow does not measure its earnings. Free cash flow (FCF), the cash left after a company pays for its own investment, has no useful meaning here either. The scoring file already lets financial companies be judged on return on equity instead of return on capital employed. We extend that logic: ROE and ROA replace the return test, and capital ratios and asset quality replace the debt and cash-conversion tests. Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks whether the company clears the hard floors and the entry tests. The scoring file's wording is for companies with factories and debt, so for a bank we apply the same intent with bank-specific measures. The return test uses ROE. The solvency test uses capital adequacy and asset quality. The debt-to-equity and cash-conversion lines are shown and marked not applicable. The promoter-pledge and Stage 4 lines apply as written. 5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on future profit. A reverse discounted cash flow (reverse DCF) works backwards from today's price and asks what growth the price already assumes. If the assumed growth is low, the market is not asking for much and good news can lift the price. If it is high, even good news may be in the price already. The ledger solves this with net profit as the cash flow, a 12% discount rate and a terminal multiple of 20 times profit in year ten, and it gets -0.47% a year[37].

We do not rely on that figure for a bank, for three reasons. First, a bank cannot pay out all its profit: it has to keep some to hold its capital ratio as loans grow, so profit is not the cash available to shareholders. Second, a terminal multiple of 20 times profit is about 3.6 times book value for a bank earning 18%, far above what that return justifies. Third, the ledger's market value of ₹34,510 crore uses about 98.5 crore shares, while the bank's filing shows paid-up capital of ₹193.34 crore at a ₹2 face value, which is 96.67 crore shares and a market value of ₹33,854 crore[37][2][25]. We weight three values by our probabilities: 25% on ₹450, 50% on ₹400 and 25% on ₹300. That is 0.25 × 450 + 0.50 × 400 + 0.25 × 300 = ₹387.50.

The inputs

The range

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption, and each assumption can be tested. This chapter names the weakest one under each estimate, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when we will know. It then lays out the strongest case against the stock, which we call the red-team bear case, and ends with the one claim the whole report hinges on.

Weakest assumption under the reverse DCF: the return on equity stays near 19%

Our reading that the price asks for only 5.2% growth assumes the return on equity stays at 19.2%. History says that is generous. Screener's ten-year average return on equity for the bank is 12%, the five-year average is 16% and the three-year average is 18%[29]. The current return rests on a net interest margin of 4.26% and a credit cost of 0.43%. Both are at the favourable end of the bank's own range: the credit cost was 0.77% in FY26, and the margin was 3.97% in FY26 after 4.20% in FY24[1]. The evidence that would show the assumption wrong is a margin below 3.95% or a credit cost above 0.8% in the December quarter. We will know when the December-quarter results come out, expected in mid-to-late January 2027; the bank has not yet published the date[12] (see Chapter 7).

Weakest assumption under the base value: the market keeps paying about 2.1 times book

The base case needs 2.11 times book value in a year. The bank's own history argues for less. SMIFS's chart of the stock's one-year forward price to adjusted book shows a five-year mean of 1.12 times and a +1 standard deviation level of 1.55 times, with the stock now at 1.83 times[20]. Adjusted book value takes out some items, so it is not identical to our book value, but the direction is clear: the stock is priced for a return that is above its history. If the multiple fell to 1.55 times on September 2027 book value of about ₹189, the value would be about ₹294, which is below our bear case. That is why we give the bear case a one-in-four probability. The evidence would be a fall in the bank's price-to-book while its earnings stay on track; we would see it in any session, so it is a risk we watch daily, not one we wait for a report to settle.

Weakest assumption under the earnings path: credit cost near 0.55% to 0.60%, margin near 4.1%

The FY28 profit per share of ₹34.84 needs loan growth of about 17%, a margin above 4%, and credit cost below 0.6%. Three outside events can break it. The RBI's expected-credit-loss rules start on 1 April 2027 and the bank says its buffer of 1.65% of loans, down from 1.77% in March, should cover the change, but it said the answer needs one to one and a half more months of work[15][3][5]. The insurance regulator's draft caps could cut commission income: the bank earned about ₹165 crore from insurance last year, and a cut of 60% to 70% would remove about ₹99 crore to ₹116 crore of pre-tax income, which is roughly ₹0.77 to ₹0.90 a share after tax, or about 2.2% to 2.6% of FY28 profit[3][18]. And RBI tightening raises deposit costs, which management already expects: "Funding costs are likely to remain under upward pressure"[3][14]. The evidence will arrive in two steps: the ECL answer within weeks, and the margin and credit cost in the December-quarter results.

The red-team bear case: why this could be a trap

The strongest honest case against the stock has five parts. First, the headline growth is flattered. Reported profit before tax rose ₹301 crore on the year, and ₹156 crore of that, 52%, came from lower provisions (₹118 crore against ₹274 crore). The base quarter also held a ₹139 crore one-off interest recovery, and the current quarter holds ₹28.35 crore[1]. Second, the margin driver is spent: management says the repricing of gold loans is over and expects yields to fall by 3 to 4 basis points[3]. Third, funding is tightening: loans are growing faster than deposits, the credit-to-deposit ratio is 86.6%, CASA is flat at 27.52%, and the RBI has started to raise rates[20][1][14]. Fourth, 29% of loans are gold-backed, and an analyst on the call said bureau data showed an uptick in gold-loan stress at some lenders; the bank said it had not seen it, but gold-price falls would test its 65% average LTV[3][1]. Fifth, the price: the stock fell 16.6% from the ₹390.80 high to the ₹326.10 low within three sessions after that high, which shows how quickly the market takes back a re-rating[36]. Sector peers had equally strong quarters, so KVB has no unique edge in the profit growth[26][28][27].

The counter-case is that the bank beat its own guidance in both of the years we checked, that it can earn about 2% on assets even with lower recoveries (management's own claim, to be tested), and that a ₹300 bear value is only 14% below the price. We weigh the bear case at 25% because several of its parts are already in motion, and we require a price that pays for it.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is to wait, and our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. We do not buy at ₹350.20. We place a limit order, an order that buys only at a stated price or lower, so that we pay no more than ₹331.50. The entry zone is the price range in which we would be willing to buy: ₹322.00 to ₹331.50. The stop loss, the price at which we accept the idea is wrong and sell, is ₹304.39. It is the 20-day swing low of ₹311.40 less half the typical daily swing (₹7.01), measured on closing prices, which is our rule for tactical positions[37]. The base target is ₹400, the bull target ₹450 and the bear value ₹300.

The rule behind the verdict is the reward-to-risk floor of 2.5 to 1 for tactical ideas. At ₹350.20 the ratio is 1.09 to 1, because the ₹400 base target gains ₹49.80 while the stop risks ₹45.81. At the top of the zone, ₹331.50, it is 2.53 to 1, with ₹68.50 to gain and ₹27.11 to lose. At the 20-day average (₹340.57) it is 1.64 to 1 and at the 50-day average (₹338.22) it is 1.83 to 1, so neither average gives a buy under the old stop. The ladder of distance to support puts the stock beyond 1.6 ATR from its swing low, in the NO_BUY band, and that alone says wait. The ₹331.50 top of the range depends on the ₹400 target holding: at the SMIFS-style 1.8 times book the target would be about ₹341 and no price in the zone would pass.

The mechanics move on 31 October. The ₹311.40 low leaves the 20-day window after the 30 October session, and the next lowest low is ₹320.55 on 5 October[36]. On the same rule the stop then becomes about ₹313.54 and the price at which the reward-to-risk is 2.5 to 1 becomes about ₹338, which is where the 50-day average sits. If an order has already been placed, we cancel it at that ledger and re-strike only if every entry condition below passes on the refreshed numbers. We do not move it automatically up to ₹338, because a stop that rises only because an old low aged out is not stronger support.

The order plan is as follows, and it applies only after the entry conditions in the first trigger below are met. Our risk budget for a tier-three idea is 50 basis points of the portfolio, and the stop distance is 8.18% from the ₹331.50 zone top, which is the upper bound, and 5.47% from the ₹322.00 order price ((322.00 - 304.39) divided by 322.00). That 3.5% is the tier cap shown in the head of this report and in the sizing table; the first order is 1.75%, half of it. In the current regime, a downtrend with a capitulation-bounce watch, the scoring file asks for a starter of up to half the standard size, so we start with 1.75% as a single limit order. The price-to-book check in the first trigger limits that order to about ₹322.70 on today's numbers, so on today's numbers the order goes at ₹322.00, not at the ₹331.50 top of the zone. The second half is bought at ₹322.00 or lower, never above the first-half price, and only after the December-quarter results pass the hinge test (margin at or above 4.0% and credit cost at or below 0.6%).
