1. What the company does

A transformer is a steel-and-copper machine that raises or lowers the voltage of electricity. Power stations send current out at very high voltage because less energy is lost on long lines, and each substation along the way steps the voltage down again until it reaches a factory or a home. Transformers and Rectifiers (India) Limited, known as TARIL, builds the whole range from its plants near Ahmedabad in Gujarat. The range runs from 5 kV to 1,200 kV and from 0.5 MVA to 500 MVA per unit. It includes power transformers and shunt reactors that steady voltage on long lines. It also makes distribution transformers, furnace transformers for steel mills, rectifier transformers for smelters and traction units for locomotives.[2][15] A megavolt-ampere (MVA) is the unit for a transformer's size. The company reports installed capacity of about 75,000 MVA a year, with customers in more than 40 countries, but a good part of that capacity is still being commissioned, as Chapter 2 explains.[2]

The buyers are mostly governments and the contractors who work for them. At 31 March 2026 the order book was ₹5,008 crore. State utilities were 31.4 percent and engineering contractors (known as EPC players) 31.9 percent. Central utilities were 13.2 percent, industrial customers 8.1 percent, exports 8.7 percent, renewable projects 5.4 percent and railways 0.6 percent.[15] Concentration inside those groups is high. The annual report says one customer gave ₹901.29 crore of FY26 revenue, which is 37.6 percent of standalone revenue of ₹2,395.49 crore, and it does not name that customer.[15][13] Orders in the last six months show the pattern. Power Grid Corporation of India placed an ultra-mega order above ₹1,000 crore, to be delivered over 30 months.[26][7] The Gujarat transmission utility, GETCO, ordered 80 and 125 MVAr reactors and 500 MVA auto transformers, delivered over 24 to 30 months.[27] Damodar Valley Corporation ordered 500 and 200 MVA auto transformers over 15 to 28 months.[28] The company also won its first nuclear order. It is for generator transformers for Kaiga units 5 and 6 of the Nuclear Power Corporation of India, placed through Megha Engineering and Infrastructures.[29]

Each machine is built to order and billed in stages, so revenue arrives long after the order is won. The company says the order book is executable over the next 18 to 24 months.[4] Raw materials are the largest cost. In Q2 FY27 the cost of materials consumed was ₹490.00 crore on standalone revenue of ₹709.04 crore, which is 69 percent. The chief financial officer puts the gross margin, meaning revenue less the cost of goods as a share of revenue, at 30 to 31 percent.[3][8] Most orders carry price-variation clauses, which pass copper, steel and oil price moves on to the customer, and price increases added 7 to 8 percent to Q2 revenue growth.[5] Management quotes average realisation of about ₹7 lakh per MVA, and the quarter's production was about 9,500 MVA.[5] Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) margin was 16.1 percent on a standalone basis and 17.4 percent on a consolidated basis.[2]

How the company grew and what changed in 2025 and 2026

The founder moved from repairing transformers to manufacturing them in 1981, and the company listed on the stock exchanges in 2008. Between 2024 and 2026 it raised ₹500 crore in a qualified institutional placement (QIP, a sale of new shares to large institutions only). It won a ₹740 crore GETCO order and bought a majority stake in a CRGO processing unit. It took a repair order for a high-voltage direct-current (HVDC) transformer from Power Grid. And it lifted capacity from 40,000 MVA to about 75,000 MVA.[15][2] CRGO is cold-rolled grain-oriented electrical steel, the core material of a transformer, and India imports most of what it uses.[35]

Four changes in the last twelve months matter for an owner. First, order inflow jumped: ₹2,114 crore in the June 2026 quarter and ₹1,169 crore in the September quarter, so ₹3,283 crore in the half-year against ₹2,374 crore for the whole of FY26.[7][5][8] Second, a UK subsidiary, Maxwell Grid Transformers (UK) Private Limited, was incorporated on 4 September 2026 with no capital yet paid in.[30][3] Third, leadership changed. Chief executive Mukul Srivastava joined on 8 July 2025 and resigned effective 7 January 2026. Satyen Mamtora, the managing director and a promoter, then took the chief executive role. Mehul Shah replaced Chanchal Rajora as chief financial officer on 5 March 2026.[25][15] Fourth, the World Bank's integrity unit pursued the company over a 2020 Nigerian supply order, which Chapter 2 sets out because it bears on the verdict.

Plants, capacity and the backward-integration plan

The investor presentation lists installed capacity of 35,000 MVA and 40,000 MVA a year at the two main plants and 1,200 MVA at a third small plant, with utilisation of 100 percent, 33 percent and 100 percent. The text extraction does not show which main plant is at 33 percent, but the call places the low utilisation at Changodar, where about 23,000 to 25,000 MVA of new capacity is still being commissioned.[2][5] Commercial production there is now expected around Diwali, which is later than the Q1 FY27 start the company gave in January 2026.[4][9] Production in FY26 was 33,763 MVA, which the annual report calls about 45 percent of the new total.[15]

Alongside the plants, the company is building its own supply of inputs: CTC conductors (continuously transposed conductor, the wire wound into coils), pressboard insulation, bushings, fabricated tanks and CRGO processing. Management says these will cover 80 to 85 percent of raw-material needs and add 200 to 300 basis points of margin from FY28. They cost ₹900 to ₹1,000 crore, on top of about ₹150 crore for the Changodar expansion.[2][5][7] A basis point is one hundredth of one percentage point. The commissioning target for all of it is the first quarter of FY28, after allowing a delay of up to one quarter.[2]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Transformer: a machine that changes the voltage of alternating current; a shunt reactor is a related device that absorbs excess reactive power on long lines.
  • MVA (megavolt-ampere): the size of a transformer; MVAr is the same unit for a reactor.
  • Order book (unexecuted order book): signed orders not yet delivered and billed. Order inflow: new orders signed in a period. Book-to-bill: order inflow divided by revenue; above 1 means the order book is growing.
  • EPC player: an engineering, procurement and construction contractor that builds a project for a utility and buys equipment such as transformers.
  • CRGO: cold-rolled grain-oriented electrical steel; CTC: continuously transposed conductor, a specialised copper wire for transformer windings.
  • Pledge: shares put up as security for a loan; if the borrower cannot repay, the lender can sell them.
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer invoices, less what suppliers are owed. Retention money: a share of an invoice (here 5 to 10 percent) that a utility holds back until the equipment has performed.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

TARIL is a real manufacturer in a market where demand is running ahead of supply, and its numbers show it. The annual report describes lead times of 18 to 24 months for transformers of 400 kV and above and says utilities are placing orders early because they cannot switch suppliers without restarting approvals.[15] The National Electricity Plan for transmission, released by the Central Electricity Authority, budgets over ₹9,15,000 crore to 2032 and adds about 1,270 GVA of transformation capacity at 220 kV and above between 2022-23 and 2031-32.[34] Against that, the company's order book is ₹7,237 crore, which is 3.0 times FY26 standalone revenue and 2.7 times trailing standalone revenue of ₹2,724.86 crore, our arithmetic from the filings.[2][3]

The order book is also getting more valuable. About 55 to 60 percent of it is in the 400 kV and above class, and 83 percent of it is power transformers, the higher-margin part of the range.[5][2] Q2 consolidated EBITDA margin was 17.4 percent against 12.8 percent a year earlier, and the quarter's operating profit was ₹128.77 crore against ₹58.89 crore.[2] The company completed dynamic short-circuit testing on three more transformers in Q2. It shows a 315 MVA, 400/220 kV auto transformer under short-circuit test at KEMA in the Netherlands. It also dispatched a 1,200 kV unit that it describes as the highest AC voltage in the world.[2] Its lenders agree on credit quality. India Ratings affirmed IND A+ with a stable outlook in July 2026. It assigned a further ₹500 crore of limits, after upgrading the rating from IND A- in June 2025.[16][17]

Where the competitive position is strong

The strength rests on approvals, not patents. A utility will not accept a 400 kV transformer until the maker has passed type tests and the buyer has qualified the factory, so a maker with a record of delivered units can charge for that record. The annual report names Hitachi Energy India, TARIL, BHEL, CG Power and Sterlite Power's Kalpataru transformer subsidiary as the limited group able to serve 400 kV and above. It says competition at 220 kV and lower is intense and cuts margins.[15] Management says it competed with Crompton and other large makers for the nuclear order, and that at 132 and 220 kV it also competes with a newly listed maker the transcript names as Kanohar.[5] TARIL's moves up the voltage ladder, into nuclear generator transformers and an HVDC repair order, are an attempt to sit in the part of the market with fewer rivals.

Win rates are modest but the pipeline is large. The company states a historical win ratio of 10 to 15 percent on inquiries under negotiation of about ₹21,000 crore, and says about 80 percent of the pipeline is domestic and 20 percent export.[4] It also says it limits the order book to what it can deliver within 24 to 28 months, which means it is turning down work it cannot ship.[5][8]

Where the position is thin

Hitachi Energy India reported an order backlog above ₹29,500 crore at March 2026, more than four times TARIL's order book, and is described in the annual report as the market leader by capability.[15] In the peer table in Chapter 3, TARIL earns a return on capital of 23 percent. Hitachi Energy India earns 29 percent and GE Vernova T&D India 77 percent. TARIL is the only one of the five that burned cash in its last audited year.[1][36][37][38][39] Receivables are the second weakness. Trade receivables in the standalone accounts were ₹1,057.71 crore at March 2026, which the auditors call 42.30 percent of total assets, and they flagged recoverability as a key audit matter.[15] Management estimates retention money, the part of invoices utilities hold back, at about ₹200 crore.[5]

CRGO steel is the third. A supplier-trade article puts India's yearly CRGO need at about 400,000 tonnes and says domestic mills cover only 10 to 12 percent, with the gap met by imports until at least FY2028.[35] TARIL has bought raw material to cover itself until its own plants run, and management says inventory is enough until Q3 FY27.[5][7] The price of that cover shows on the balance sheet: inventory rose from ₹615.52 crore to ₹723.26 crore in six months.[3]

Governance and funding flags that sit outside the numbers

A governance case came to the surface in November 2025. On 4 November 2025 the World Bank's sanctions office issued a Notice of Uncontested Sanctions Proceedings in Sanctions Case No. 788 about a 70-transformer supply contract for the Nigeria Electricity Transmission Project, an order of US$24.74 million received in FY20.[22][23] The notice records two accusations from the Bank's integrity unit: failing to disclose commission payments to two agents, and making improper payments to public officials. It lists the repeated incidents and the involvement of high-level management as aggravating factors, and the company's cooperation as a mitigating one. The recommended sanction was debarment with conditional release after at least three years and seven months.[22] The company told the exchanges that it had not received the July 2025 notice, that it would contest, and that the findings were not conclusive.[23]

By 14 November 2025 the World Bank had removed the company's name from the list of debarred firms and extended the deadline for its explanation to 12 January 2026.[24] Management then told investors the matter would be settled in two to three weeks in January and would close in 45 days in April.[9][8] We found no later filing, no line in the FY26 annual report and no remark on the Q1 or Q2 FY27 calls that reports an outcome. The World Bank's own list could not be read by our tools. So the status today is not verified.[15][7][5] An unresolved integrity finding that names senior management is a governance risk even if no World Bank-funded order is in the book.

Three further flags are factual and smaller. The promoters have pledged 4,22,00,000 shares, which is 21.84 percent of their holding and 14.06 percent of all shares. The same number of shares is pledged in the March 2025 and September 2026 filings, and the ratio was 22.26 percent in March 2024, so it has edged down, not up. The promoter group also declared that it created no new encumbrance during FY26.[18][19][20][21] The QIP monitoring agency reported a deviation from the stated use of proceeds. The board proposed re-allocating the money and extending the timeline to 31 March 2027. Shareholders approved it on 28 September 2026, with 99.9959 percent of the promoter group's shares voting in favour.[31][32][33] Finally, the chief executive and the chief financial officer both changed within eight months, and independent director Ajay Patil resigned on 12 November 2025, citing other commitments.[25][15][42]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Guidance, which is the company's own published forecast, tells you how far to trust the next forecast. Management beat the FY25 revenue target, with ₹2,051 crore of group revenue against about ₹2,000 crore. The Q2 FY27 margin met the 16 percent guide, though H1 was 14.6 percent. It missed on FY26 revenue, the FY26 order book, plant dates, net debt and the timing of the World Bank case. In August and November 2025 the company also said it would bring net debt, which is borrowings minus cash, to zero within 18 to 24 months.[12] The FY26 promises were not kept: in November 2025 and January 2026 the company said it would reach at least 25 percent growth, about ₹2,600 crore, and close the year with an order book near ₹8,000 crore; it reported standalone revenue of ₹2,395.49 crore, up 22.8 percent, and an order book of ₹5,005 crore.[10][9][13][2]

An analyst put it directly on the April 2026 call, noting that this was the second quarter in which guidance had been missed. Management replied that it was being selective and would not take orders beyond 24 months.[8] That is a fair reason for the order book, but it was not the reason given in January, when the company expected the order book to rise from about ₹5,500 crore to ₹8,000 crore by March.[9] On 13 October 2026 the company said it was confident of its FY27 guidance of 25 percent revenue growth, 16 percent EBITDA margin and a profit margin of 9 to 10 percent, while the chief executive also quoted a full-year target of ₹3,200 crore, which is more than 25 percent growth on standalone FY26 revenue of ₹2,395.49 crore.[4][5][13]

Three ideas help with the tables. Operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales. Debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share. Free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operations less capital spending. Cash flow from operations (CFO) is the cash the business generates from trading, after tax and after paying for stock and unpaid invoices. Profit after tax (PAT) is the accounting profit. A healthy company turns most of its PAT into CFO over a few years; when CFO falls far short, the profit is sitting in receivables and stock.

Layer 3 support-aware entry posture, risk-unit sizing, and let-winners-run rules

Layer 3 turns the verdict into an order. It says where buying could restart, where the exit line sits, and how large a position may be. Position size comes from a risk budget, the share of net worth you accept losing if the stop is hit, set by a conviction tier. TIER_0 is the lowest tier: a zero risk budget and a zero weight. Portfolio heat is the sum of those risk budgets across all holdings. Risk-unit sizing divides the budget by the distance to the stop, and the let-winners-run rule lets a rising Stage 2 winner grow instead of being trimmed by habit.
