1. What the company does

Transformers and Rectifiers (India) Limited, known as TARIL, builds the big transformers that raise and lower voltage on the electricity grid. Think of a transformer as a gearbox for power: a power station sends electricity at very high voltage because that wastes less energy over long wires, and transformers step it down in stages until it is safe for a factory or a home. TARIL makes power, furnace, rectifier, distribution and specialty transformers and shunt reactors, in sizes from 0.5 MVA to 500 MVA and voltages from 5 kV to 1,200 kV.[2] MVA, short for mega volt-ampere, is the unit transformer makers use for size. The company's press release counts 75,000 MVA of installed capacity across three Gujarat plants, but the finance chief said in April 2026 that the plants then ran about 40,000 MVA and would reach 75,000 MVA only when the Changodar and Moraiya expansions finish, so this report treats 40,000 MVA as the working figure.[2][7]

The customers are mostly state and central power utilities, large engineering contractors and industrial plants. The September 2026 quarter shows the mix: generator transformers for the Nuclear Power Corporation's Kaiga units 5 and 6, a large order from Megha Engineering and Infrastructures, a letter of intent from Andhra Pradesh's transmission company, and orders from Damodar Valley Corporation and Gujarat's GETCO.[3] Management describes its bidding pipeline as an equal mix of government and private customers, and says 80% of the ₹21,000 crore of inquiries under negotiation are domestic.[3] About 55% to 60% of the order book sits in the 400 kV-and-above class, exports were about 8% of Q2 revenue and about 15% of the order book.[3] Customer concentration (the share of sales from the top ten buyers) is not published in the documents we reviewed.

A customer places an order, usually at a fixed price with a clause that adjusts the price when copper, electrical steel or transformer oil costs move. The finance chief said price variation added roughly 7% to 8% to revenue growth in the quarter.[3] The company builds the unit over several months, tests it, ships it and bills in stages. Retention money, which is 5% to 10% of an order that the customer holds back until the project works, was about ₹200 crore when the call took place, and average realisation is about ₹7 lakh per MVA.[3] Because the work is slow and billing comes in stages, a transformer maker has to fund a great deal of stock and unpaid bills while it grows. That single fact explains most of what Chapter 3 shows about this company's cash.

The consolidated group includes six subsidiaries, among them Transpares, Transweld, TARIL Infrastructure, Savas Engineering, TARIL Switchgear and Triveni Transtech.[1] They add to profit: consolidated FY26 revenue was ₹2,509 crore against ₹2,395 crore for the parent alone.[7] Management is also building its own supply chain. A plant that processes grain-oriented electrical steel (CRGO) already operates, and plants for copper winding conductor (CTC), insulation pressboard, bushings and steel fabrication are under construction at a stated total cost of ₹900 crore to ₹1,000 crore.[5] When they are finished, management expects 80% to 85% of its raw-material needs to come from its own plants.[5]

A few terms carry the rest of this report, and each is defined here once. Profit before the accounting charges for interest, tax and wear on equipment is EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), and the operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales. EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided by its number of shares. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the yearly profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses. The price-to-earnings ratio is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit, and debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before buying assets or raising money, and free cash flow (FCF) is that cash less spending on plants. Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid bills, and the book-to-bill ratio is new orders divided by sales, so a reading above 1 means the order book is growing. Guidance is a company's own published forecast, a consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts, and capital expenditure is spending on plants and equipment.

On the price side, RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast a price has been moving, and ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in rupees. A base is a sideways stretch a price builds before a new move, and its pivot is the top of that stretch. A breakout is a close above that top, and a retest is the pullback that checks the same level from above. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut, a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain from the entry to the target divided by the loss from the entry to the stop, and a measured move is the height of a base projected upward as a target. Net debt is borrowings less cash.

How the order cycle works, and why it matters for cash

Order intake is lumpy. Order inflow was ₹2,374 crore in FY26, which is 0.99 times that year's standalone revenue, and only ₹244 crore of it arrived in the fourth quarter.[8] Then ₹3,283 crore arrived in the first half of FY27, which is 2.59 times first-half revenue.[2][1] Management says it has been choosing orders by price, payment terms and delivery date and does not want a book longer than 24 months.[7] The order book therefore tells you how busy the plants will be, but it says little about how quickly each order turns into cash.

A customer's payment follows the plant's work with a long lag. At 30 September 2026 standalone trade receivables were about ₹1,053 crore, of which ₹825 crore was current and the rest was classed as non-current, and net working capital was about 154 days.[3] That is the figure to keep in mind through the rest of the book: every extra rupee of sales ties up more than a rupee of money for about five months. The next chapter asks whether the business is strong enough to carry that burden.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • MVA and kV: MVA (mega volt-ampere) measures a transformer's size, and kV (kilovolt) measures the voltage it handles.
  • CRGO: cold-rolled grain-oriented electrical steel, the thin steel that forms the core of a transformer.
  • CTC: continuously transposed conductor, a copper strand bundle used for the windings.
  • HVDC: high-voltage direct current, a way of moving bulk power over very long distances that needs specialised converter transformers.
  • GETCO, PGCIL, NPCIL, DVC: Gujarat Energy Transmission Corporation, Power Grid Corporation of India, Nuclear Power Corporation of India and Damodar Valley Corporation, the state and central buyers named in the order list.
  • Unexecuted order book: the value of signed orders not yet delivered and billed.
  • Promoter pledge: shares held by a company insider with a large stake, given to a lender as collateral, so that a lender could sell them if a loan is not repaid.
  • QIP: qualified institutional placement, a share sale to large institutions; TARIL raised ₹500 crore this way in 2024.
  • Standalone and consolidated: standalone covers the parent company alone, while consolidated adds its subsidiaries.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The demand case is strong and well documented. Management's presentation, which cites the Central Electricity Authority's National Electricity Plan, points to ₹7.9 lakh crore of planned grid investment, and this is a company claim that we treat as secondary evidence.[4] The company's own order book is the firmer evidence: it grew from ₹1,499 crore at the end of FY23 to ₹2,582 crore, ₹5,132 crore, ₹5,005 crore and then ₹7,237 crore on 30 September 2026.[4] Management says that book is executable over 18 to 24 months, and that it sees no price war because competitors also hold full order books.[2][3] That last sentence is management's view, not a measured fact.

Evidence for a moat is narrower than the order book suggests. TARIL can build units up to 1,200 kV and has completed dynamic short-circuit testing on seven units, a test that proves a transformer survives a grid fault, and qualification for the highest voltage classes is hard to obtain.[4] It won the Kaiga nuclear order against Crompton and other large makers, and it says it competes with the newly listed Kanohar at 132 kV and 220 kV.[3] Component supply is the industry's bottleneck, which is why the company is integrating backward.[7] Against that, transformer orders are won in tenders where price decides, and the buyers are mostly governments, so a moat here is a matter of qualification and delivery record rather than a brand.

Margins show both the strength and the risk. Consolidated operating margin rose from 6% in FY22 to 10% in FY24 and 16% in FY25, then eased to 15% in FY26.[15] The standalone EBITDA margin was 8.7% in Q2 FY26 and 16.1% in Q2 FY27, a 736 basis point swing in a year.[2] The earlier low came when the company delivered the last of its low-margin orders and ran below capacity, so the margin depends on order mix and on how full the plants are.[10]

The weaknesses are about cash, governance and execution rather than demand. Standalone current borrowings of ₹370.92 crore at March 2026 included about ₹249.7 crore of vendor financing, a supplier-finance arrangement that carries recourse to the company.[13] The annual report also notes that stock and receivable statements given to banks did not agree with the books because of timing, stock at job workers and pending reconciliation entries.[13] The World Bank matter is unclear. In November 2025 management said the World Bank had 'debarred' it from participating in new tenders over a Nigerian project that was completed and paid.[10] In January 2026 it said there was 'no debarment' as of now and that the Bank had set 12 January as the deadline for a reply.[9] In April 2026 it said the reply was filed and a response was awaited.[7] The July and October 2026 documents we reviewed give no outcome, so the current status is not confirmed.

Shareholders have also been leaving. Foreign investors held 11.33% in March 2025 and 6.98% in September 2026, and domestic institutions fell from 7.22% to 1.19% over the same period, while the public (retail) share rose from 17.07% to 27.46%.[15] Taken together, TARIL has a strong demand story and good margins, and it also has weak cash conversion, a pledged promoter block, a slipping delivery record for new plants and a World Bank matter whose status is unconfirmed. The honest summary is a good business that currently fails two of the portfolio's own safety tests. The next chapter scores the operating numbers behind that summary.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 takes each estimate, states its weakest assumption, and says what evidence would prove it wrong and when we would know. Dates that come from our own recollection of reporting patterns are marked 'expected, not confirmed'.

Weakest assumption in the earnings estimate: the second half of FY27

Our FY27 base needs second-half consolidated revenue of about ₹1,738 crore, which is roughly ₹869 crore a quarter against ₹739.44 crore in Q2, and the operating plants already ran near 95% of capacity.[1][3] The step-up rests on the Changodar plant starting around Diwali and ramping within weeks. Management has moved that date at least four times since April 2025.[12][9][5][2] Proof of error would be a Q3 revenue below about ₹800 crore or a statement that Changodar commissioning has slipped again. We expect to see Q3 FY27 results around mid-January 2027, expected and not confirmed: last year's Q3 call was held on 8 January 2026 and no date has been announced.[9] In plain terms, this is the assumption that carries the most weight in the whole report. If the plant starts on time and the ramp is quick, FY27 earnings per share lands near our base. If it slips a quarter, the second half is closer to the first-half run rate, and FY27 earnings per share would be nearer ₹9.42, which is twice the first-half figure of ₹4.71. That would still be growth, but it would push the date at which cash conversion can recover further out, and it would make the pledge and cash tests harder to cure within the next year.

Weakest assumption in the target multiple: that 30x holds

A 30x multiple is below this stock's own 52-week median of 34.6x. Over that year trailing earnings per share moved only from ₹8.25 to ₹10.14, so the market paid more for each rupee of earnings while it waited for the order book to convert.[16] If growth normalises toward 20% and the market focuses on the pledge and the cash conversion, 24x is plausible. Proof of error would be a sustained trading range below 26x. The multiple is observable every day, so the test is continuous. The multiple is also tied to the same two failed tests. A company with a pledged promoter block and weak cash conversion usually trades at a discount to peers with clean records, so a re-rating toward Hitachi Energy India's multiple, which is above 100x, is not a reasonable base for this stock, and a de-rating toward the low end of its own range is a live risk. We would revisit the 30x assumption if the pledge fell below 20% and operating cash turned positive, because that would remove the main reasons for the discount.

Red-team bear case

The strongest case against the business is that reported profit is not turning into cash and the gap is being financed by lenders and suppliers and backed by a promoter director's pledged shares. Over FY24 to FY26 consolidated operating cash was ₹80.34 crore against profit of ₹535.61 crore.[13][14] Retention money of about ₹200 crore, non-current receivables of about ₹228 crore at the parent and vendor financing of about ₹250 crore with recourse all sit in the balance sheet.[3][1][13] About 7% to 8% of revenue growth in the latest quarter came from price variation, which inflates sales without adding tonnage.[3] The lender could invoke the pledged block of 42,200,000 shares, which is 14.06% of all shares, if the price fell sharply.[17][13] The World Bank matter has an unconfirmed status in the documents we hold: debarment was described in November 2025, denied 'as of now' in January 2026, and the reply was filed by April 2026.[10][9][7]

A second item is a data-quality warning, not a finding.
