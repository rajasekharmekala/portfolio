What the company does and why the order book matters

Transformers and Rectifiers (India) manufactures power, furnace and rectifier transformers, shunt reactors and related equipment, and sells them on a business-to-business basis to power generation, transmission and distribution utilities and to industrial customers [1]. A transformer is a long-lived piece of heavy equipment that a utility orders for a specific substation or line, so revenue is recognised over the months it takes to build, not on the day an order arrives. That is why the unexecuted order book, the value of signed work not yet delivered, is the best guide to the next two years of sales.

At 30 June 2026 the unexecuted order book stood at ₹6,630 crore, 26 percent higher than a year earlier, and order inflows in the June quarter reached ₹2,114 crore, up 218 percent [3]. Management reported an enquiry pipeline of about ₹23,000 crore [3]. Against trailing consolidated revenue of ₹2,552 crore, the order book covers about 2.6 times a year of sales, which means the company has signed work for roughly two and a half years at the current pace [1]. That is a strong position, and it is the reason the business case is not weak.

The order flow after the quarter shows the same pattern. On 2 October 2026 the company announced a Gujarat utility order for 80 MVAR and 125 MVAR shunt reactors and 500 MVA auto transformers, to be delivered in 24 to 30 months [1][14]. The same day it announced a domestic order from the Damodar Valley Corporation for 500 MVA and 200 MVA auto transformers, with delivery in 15 to 28 months [13][1]. An earlier award from the Nuclear Power Corporation's Kaiga units, placed through a contractor, is worth ₹166.74 crore [16], and its delivery is set at 35 months [15].

Capacity is the second half of the business story. The FY26 annual report, as summarised by a financial news service, puts installed capacity at nearly 75,000 MVA [19]. Output in FY26 was 33,000 MVA, up from 29,118 MVA in FY25 [1]. Output divided by installed capacity gives 44 percent utilisation, and management has publicly targeted 85 to 90 percent [20]. The gap between those numbers is the main operating question for the next year, and it is taken up in the Layer 2 section.

How to read the business evidence. The order book is a promise from customers, and it converts into revenue as factories build the equipment. A large book is good news only if the factory can build it at the planned pace and the company can finance the stock of parts and finished units while it does. The next sections test both.

Growth in the book and in the factory

The book grew faster than revenue in the June quarter, which means the business is winning more work than it is delivering. Management attributes the slower quarterly revenue growth to lower capacity utilisation at the Changodar plant, where an expansion is under way. The June-quarter release, issued in July, gave August 2026 as the expected completion month [3]; this dossier has no source confirming it. The expansion plan includes an investment of about ₹150 crore at Changodar, backward-integration projects worth ₹900 to 1,000 crore, and three greenfield facilities at Chiyada [3]. Backward integration means making the components the company now buys, such as press-board and insulation materials, in its own plants. If it works, it protects margin and supply; if it is late, the cash planned for it stays tied up in the existing supply chain.

The company's own schedule for these projects has moved. On the June-quarter call, the chief financial officer said the backward-integration facilities would come up in the first quarter of FY28, and that a margin benefit of 200 to 300 basis points would build from FY28 [5]. Management attributed the Changodar delay to extended monsoon conditions, construction labour constraints and engineering changes to product-specific lines [5]. Those are ordinary reasons, but they mean the cash-heavy investment phase runs into FY27, which matters for the gate.

A subsidiary is being set up to extend the business abroad. The board approved a GBP 10 million investment to create a 51 percent owned UK subsidiary, Maxwell Grid Transformers, on 27 August 2026 [25][26]. The consolidated results include the subsidiaries, which is one reason the consolidated and standalone figures differ, as the Layer 1 section shows.

The business has the demand and the order visibility that a long-term holder would want: a record book, strong inflows and a pipeline many times larger than the book. The weakness is conversion. Management is building capacity and backward integration at the same time it is carrying a larger stock of inventory and receivables, and the cash cost of both appears in the statements before the benefit appears in the profit line. What the numbers add up to is a company whose future earnings may be attractive but whose current cash does not yet support the price structure. That is the distinction the rest of the dossier tests.

Gate 1: return on capital passes

ROCE (return on capital employed, operating profit as a share of all capital used) measures how much operating profit the business earns on all the money invested in it, both borrowed and owned. A figure of 15 percent is the floor because a business that earns less than that on its capital rarely creates value for the owners after the cost of that capital is paid. TARIL's 23.3 percent is above the floor and above the 18 percent the core bucket prefers [1]. The gate passes, and it is not the reason for the verdict.

Gate 2: debt is moderate

Debt to equity compares the money borrowed with the owners' money. The house ceiling is 0.70, and a company above that carries enough debt that a downturn could make interest payments hard to meet. TARIL's consolidated borrowings were ₹457 crore against equity of about ₹1,515 crore, made up of share capital of ₹30 crore and reserves of ₹1,485 crore, giving 0.30 [1][10]. The borrowings rose by ₹174 crore during FY26, which is the figure to watch. The rating agency India Ratings affirmed IND A+ with a Stable outlook on the company's bank loan facilities, covering ₹1,050 crore of existing and ₹500 crore of additional facilities, in July 2026 [21]. The gate passes today, but the trend is in the direction of the ceiling, and the cash gate explains why the debt is rising.

Gate 3: cash conversion fails, and the basis is the deciding question

This is the gate that decides the result, so it needs the most explanation. The house rule asks whether a company's earnings are backed by real cash. It is measured as the sum of three years of operating cash flow divided by the sum of three years of profit after tax, and a result of 0.70 or more is required. The reason for the three-year window is that a single year can be distorted by one large receipt or one large payment. The reason for the 0.70 level is that a healthy manufacturing business usually converts most of its profit into cash over a cycle, and a conversion well below that means the profit is locked in stock or debtors.

The company publishes both consolidated and standalone statements, and the two give different answers. The consolidated statements cover the parent and its subsidiaries. Over FY24 to FY26 they show operating cash flow of ₹29 crore, ₹157 crore and minus ₹105 crore, which adds to ₹81 crore, against profit of ₹47 crore, ₹216 crore and ₹272 crore, which adds to ₹535 crore. The ratio is 81 divided by 535, which is 0.15 [1]. The standalone statements show operating cash flow of ₹28 crore, minus ₹34 crore and minus ₹59 crore, adding to minus ₹65 crore, against profit of ₹41 crore, ₹188 crore and ₹225 crore, adding to ₹454 crore. The ratio is minus 65 divided by 454, which is minus 0.14 [2]. An independent data page reports the same standalone operating cash flow for FY25 and FY26 [9].

The gate basis is consolidated, for three reasons. The consolidated statements are the group's economics: the subsidiaries that will carry much of the expansion are inside them, and an investor who owns the parent owns the group. The company's own guidance and headline figures are consolidated. And the consolidated result is the more favourable of the two, at 0.15 against minus 0.14, so using it is the generous choice. The gate fails on either basis, which means the choice does not change the outcome. The one caution is that the gap between consolidated and standalone cash in FY25 is large, about ₹191 crore, and the consolidated cash figure has not been confirmed against the audited statements in a second independent source. That is an open item, listed in the run notes and gate exceptions.

The reason the cash is missing is visible in the working-capital figures. Inventory and receivables consumed the cash that the profit produced, and the company's own description of the FY26 build is that it was deliberate. The gate does not ask why cash is missing; it asks whether it is there, and at this point it is not.

The ledger shows the opposite. On 9 October 2026 the close of ₹290.65 was below the 50-day average of ₹291.57, which had been ₹310.41 twenty-one sessions earlier, so it has fallen. The last gate asks for a reward-to-risk (the same idea as asymmetry, which means possible gain divided by possible loss) ratio of at least 3.5 for a tactical name, measured against a target that has been anchored to evidence. The house accepts two anchors: forward earnings per share multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings (the price divided by earnings per share, which means the years of earnings the price represents) ratio, or a measured move (a target equal to the height of a chart pattern, added to the level where the move starts, which means a chart-based target) of a technical chart pattern. Layer 3: entry posture and support

Layer 3 is the timing question. A business can pass every quality test and still be a poor purchase if the price has already run away from its support, because the buyer then has more to lose if the trend turns. The house measures the distance from the price to the nearest support level below it, in units of ATR, a unit that adjusts for each stock's normal daily movement. The result is placed on a single ladder: within half an ATR is a full-size band, within one ATR a standard band, within 1.2 ATR a reduced limit-order band, and beyond that a no-buy band.

How to read the entry posture. The support anchor is the level the price is measured against. The distance is the gap between the close and the anchor, divided by the 14-day ATR. The posture is the band the distance falls into. Layer 3 is informational for this holding because Layer 1 fails.

The close of ₹290.65 is ₹9.59 above it, and with an ATR of ₹11.33 the distance is 0.85 ATR, which places the price in the standard band and gives the posture NEAR_SUPPORT. The 50-day average is at ₹291.57, which is 0.08 ATR above the close, so the price is essentially on that average. The two readings differ because the price is below a falling 50-day average, and the 20-day average is a weaker anchor in a downtrend than it is in an uptrend.

Fatal flaws: a red-team review

The three flaws below are the ones most likely to break the conclusion. For each, the reader should weigh the evidence and decide whether the replacement rule still applies.

Flaw one: the cash cycle may turn faster than the statements suggest

The strongest case for keeping the stock is that the cash shortfall is temporary. Flaw two: the guidance may already be out of reach

The company guides to 25 percent revenue growth in FY27, and the first quarter showed 8 percent [3][5]. The remaining quarters would need about 29.5 percent growth against the prior year. Flaw three: debt and pledge make the balance sheet less forgiving

Borrowings rose by ₹174 crore in a year, and about 14 percent of the shares are pledged as security [1][11]. The rebuttal is that the debt-to-equity (borrowed money divided by owners' money, which means a measure of leverage) ratio is still 0.30, which leaves room. That is true today. Notes, glossary and sources

The terms below are the ones the dossier uses, each explained in plain words for a reader new to these measures. Each term is also explained in the section where it first matters.

Glossary

ATR (average true range) is the average daily price move in rupees, used to size stops. RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 momentum gauge. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, an operating profit measure. ROCE is operating profit as a share of all capital used. EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares. Stage 2 means a rising uptrend. Stage 4 means a falling trend below falling averages. Base is a sideways range where the price consolidates before a move. Breakout is a move above a resistance level. Entry zone is the price band where a buy would be considered. Asymmetry is the ratio of possible gain to possible loss. Reward-to-risk is the same idea as asymmetry. Operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales. Debt-to-equity is borrowed money divided by owners' money. Price-to-earnings (P/E) is the price divided by earnings per share. Guidance is management's published expectation for the year. Measured move is a target equal to the height of a base, added to its breakout point. Working capital is cash tied up in stock and debtors, net of creditors. Consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts. Moving average is the average closing price over a set number of sessions. Cash conversion is operating cash flow divided by profit over three years. CFO is cash from operations. PAT is profit after tax. Debtor days are the average days taken to collect after a sale. Order book is the value of signed contracts not yet delivered. Stop is a predetermined price at which a position is sold to limit a loss.

Method notes

  1. Stop: the lower of the 20-session swing low and the 20- and 50-day averages, less half an ATR. That gives 260.94 from 266.60 less 5.67.
  2. Cash gate basis: consolidated, because it is the group figure and the more favourable of the two bases. Standalone gives a negative ratio, so both fail.
  3. Asymmetry: (target minus close) divided by (close minus stop). With no verified target, the ratio is null, not low.
  4. Hurdle: the close plus 3.5 times the risk per share. It is about 394.6, or 39 times consensus earnings.
  5. Later information: prices, consensus and peer snapshots dated 12 and 13 October are labelled and do not change the verdict.
