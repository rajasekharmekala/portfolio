Before you read

Maharashtra Seamless makes seamless steel pipe, mostly for oil and gas wells, and is the largest maker in India with more than half of the market.[19][7] It also holds ₹3,848 crore of investments and almost no debt, which is about half of what the whole company is worth on the stock market today.[7][37] The business is solid and cheap but not yet a good buy at ₹557.90, so the verdict is to wait for ₹527 or lower, where the possible gain is at least 2.5 times the possible loss. The call would be wrong if the cash is never shared with owners, or if the government lets the duty on Chinese pipe lapse after 27 January 2027[21][15].

1. What the company does

Seamless pipe is steel tube made without a weld seam. That makes it strong enough to line an oil well or carry gas under high pressure, and power plants use it in boilers. Maharashtra Seamless Limited, called MSL in this report, was set up in 1988 and belongs to the D. P. Jindal group.[22] It runs three seamless mills, at Nagothane and Mangaon in Maharashtra and at Narketpally in Telangana, plus a mill for welded pipe at Nagothane.[7] ICRA, a credit rating agency, counts a total seamless capacity of 650,000 tonnes a year and says the company is strong in the large-diameter pipe that has fewer competitors.[19]

Most customers are state-owned oil companies. In the order book as on 31 July 2026, ONGC and Oil India made up ₹714 crore of ₹1,709 crore, or 42 percent, and the rest came from other buyers.[7] Exports to the United States and Canada were about 20 percent of the book, and exports were 22 percent of first-quarter dispatches against under 10 percent in FY2026.[16] Orders are short. The company says the book covers roughly three to four months of sales.[1] MSL buys the steel for each order at the time it books the order, which fixes the margin on that order and shields it from later swings in steel prices.[7]

MSL buys steel billets, which are solid steel rods, and rolls and pierces them into pipe. It sells by the tonne. In FY2026 it sold 419,000 tonnes of seamless pipe, earning about ₹12,591 of operating profit before depreciation on each tonne, which came to ₹527 crore of the group's ₹684 crore.[7][1] The remaining pieces are small. Welded pipe made ₹62 crore, wind and solar plants made ₹70 crore, and one offshore drilling rig made ₹25 crore.[7] The rig works under a three-year contract that runs to November 2028.[7]

A second source of profit is the pile of money the company keeps. On 30 June 2026 it held ₹3,848 crore in mutual funds, bonds and deposits against ₹10 crore of debt.[7] The return on that pile appeared as other income of ₹387 crore in FY2026, which was 41 percent of profit before tax.[2] A buyer of this stock therefore owns a pipe factory and a large investment fund at once, and the price has to be judged against both.

Why the pipe business moves with oil spending

Seamless pipe has few uses that can wait. A well must be lined before it produces, so orders follow what oil companies decide to drill. The company says its pipe sales depend on government and oil-sector spending, not on general consumer demand.[15] When oil companies slowed their tenders, MSL's order book fell to its low point in the first half of FY2026 and revenue fell 11 percent to ₹4,671 crore.[1]

Price matters as much as volume. Imports from China sold at low prices pushed down what MSL could charge, and operating profit per tonne of seamless pipe fell from ₹25,931 in FY2024 to ₹12,591 in FY2026, a drop of 51 percent, while tonnes sold slipped only 5 percent from FY2025.[8][7] The rest of this report keeps coming back to that gap between tonnes and price per tonne.

A restructuring that was announced, withdrawn and announced again

On 7 October 2026 the Board approved a scheme to split MSL into three listed companies. The Mangaon mill with a 10 MW solar plant would go to MSL Seamless Tubes, which had ₹793 crore of FY2026 turnover, or 17 percent of the total. The Narketpally mill, two Rajasthan solar plants and the rig would go to United Seamless, with ₹693 crore, or 15 percent. The Nagothane mills, with 68 percent of turnover, would stay in MSL.[17]

Shareholders would receive one share of each new company for every five MSL shares held, so each of the three would be owned in the same proportions as today. The Board calls this value neutral.[17] The filing does not say where the ₹3,848 crore of investments would sit, and that missing detail matters more to a buyer than the share ratio. An almost identical scheme was approved on 22 May 2026 and then withdrawn, as Chapter 2B shows.[20][19]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

MSL's strength is scale in a narrow field. It is estimated to hold more than 50 percent of India's seamless pipe market, and the company itself says 55 percent.[19][7] It is the only Indian maker that offers pipe up to 20 inches across, and it carries approvals as a registered supplier to the main domestic oil producers and refiners.[22][19] Those approvals take years to earn and are the main reason a new competitor cannot simply open a plant and win ONGC orders.

Policy adds a second layer. In April 2025 the Ministry of Steel revised its domestic-preference rules for five years and made seamless and ERW pipe subject to a 'melt and pour' test, so in government-company projects only pipe made from steel produced in India can be supplied. Tenders worth up to ₹200 crore are open to domestic bidders only.[7] On 23 October 2026 India's trade-remedies authority also recommended that the duty on Chinese seamless pipe continue for five more years, at a fixed 268 US dollars a tonne, after finding that imports kept rising despite the earlier duty.[21]

The proof that this position is real, not just claimed, is mixed. Return on capital employed reached 20 percent in FY2023 and 23 percent in FY2024, which shows what the position earns when prices are good.[22] In FY2026, the pipes segment alone earned ₹526 crore on about ₹2,449 crore of its own net capital, a return of 21.5 percent before interest and tax.[3] Against that, the whole-company figure was 13.2 percent in the company's own ratio table, because the ₹3,848 crore of investments earns less than the pipes do.[1] The moat protects volumes and the right to bid. It does not protect the price.

MSL is a price-taker. When Chinese pipe was sold in India at low prices, MSL chose to give up some orders instead of cutting prices by ₹3,000 to ₹4,000 a tonne, and management said it could have filled its mills at that price.[13] The domestic industry as a group ran below 50 percent of capacity throughout the period examined by the trade authority, and MSL itself ran at 70 to 75 percent of its active capacity.[21][16] A market leader that is choosing margin over volume is protected, not dominant.

What helps the business right now

Three outside facts point the same way. The Indian crude basket averaged about $120 a barrel in October 2026, a near 15-year high.[35] Press reports say ONGC plans about ₹1 lakh crore for deepwater exploration and 87 wells through FY2031.[36] And the order book has recovered to ₹1,709 crore from ₹1,149 crore in July 2025, an increase of 49 percent, with oil-sector orders now 42 percent of the book against 25 percent a year earlier.[7][9]

These are good signs for the next two to four quarters, not proof of a new cycle. The company's own market commentary says tender issuance has slowed from earlier years and that spending must still improve in a growing economy.[7] High oil prices usually lead to more drilling with a delay of several quarters, so the first pipe orders from that decision may still be ahead.

What hurts the business right now

Chinese imports remain the main threat. The trade authority found that imports rose in absolute terms and against local production despite the duty, and that Chinese exporters have spare capacity larger than India's total demand.[21] The duty is only extended to 27 January 2027 while the final order is awaited.[7] The recommendation leaves out casing and tubing for oil wells. The domestic producers themselves asked for that, because government-company purchases of those goods are already shielded by the domestic-steel rule.[21]

Supply and trade shocks are also real. First-quarter production fell because gas supply to the Telangana plant was disrupted in April by the war, and sales volumes fell to 96,000 tonnes against a normal 105,000 to 110,000.[7][16] Management says US customers absorb the tariff on exports, but that depends on drilling in North America staying strong.[16]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management speaks through one voice. The deputy general manager for investor relations runs every earnings call, and the company does not publish product-wise margins.[15] What he promises is usually modest and short, and the record on short promises is decent. In May 2025 he said dispatches had beaten growth guidance of 7 to 8 percent, and seamless tonnes sold rose 11 percent to 442,000.[12][8] In July 2025 he warned that the September quarter would be weak on margins, and operating profit per tonne fell to ₹10,315 as warned.[13][10]

The record on longer promises is weaker. The Telangana finishing line was due to start by January 2026, then by the end of FY2026, and in August 2026 management said it hoped to give a specific update on the next call.[13][14][16] The ₹852 crore spending plan has seen about ₹170 crore of capital expenditure in FY2026 and ₹23 crore in FY2025.[2][7] Management said it was “not pushing this aggressively because the market was not so buoyant”, which is a fair reason, but it also means plan figures cannot be trusted as dates.

The most important gap is the cash. In January 2026 management said it was conserving cash for an acquisition at a price it is comfortable with, adding that it would “buy only distressed assets”, and that 'the interest of the equity shareholder does not necessarily have to align with the interest of the management'.[15] In August 2026 it said there was 'no update' on cash use.[16] The company also held no earnings call after the Q4 FY2026 results, and when a shareholder asked why, the reply was “I’m not sure how that is relevant to the financial performance or the growth prospects of the company”.[16]

We added the segment results of steel pipes (₹526.01 crore), power (₹57.85 crore) and rig (minus ₹9.13 crore) to get ₹574.73 crore. We divided that by the net capital of those three segments, which is their assets of ₹3,809.56 crore less their liabilities of ₹449.39 crore, or ₹3,360.17 crore.

The inputs

Segment results, assets and liabilities are in the FY2026 standalone segment note; the standalone figures are used because the segment note is only given there.[3] The treasury segment had assets of ₹3,904.59 crore and liabilities of ₹406.88 crore and is left out.

On the pipes segment alone the figure is 21.5 percent (₹526.01 crore over ₹2,449.07 crore). In FY2025 the pipes segment result was ₹743.87 crore on ₹2,794 crore, which was 26.6 percent, so the range over two years is about 21 to 27 percent for pipes and about 14 to 17 percent for the whole group.

5. What could happen next

A stock price is a bet on the future, so the first step is to ask what the bet is. A reverse DCF starts from today's price and solves for the yearly growth in cash flow that would make the price fair, using a 12 percent discount rate and a 20 times multiple at the end of ten years. If the answer is a high number, the market expects a lot. If the answer is negative, the market expects the business to shrink.

The session ledger could not compute this for MSL because the Yahoo fundamentals feed returned an error, so the working that follows is our own. We took the market value of ₹7,476 crore and removed net cash of ₹3,838 crore to get an enterprise value of ₹3,638 crore.[7][37] The cash flow base is FY2026 free cash flow of ₹763 crore, which is cash flow from operations of ₹932.80 crore less capital expenditure of ₹169.67 crore.[2] The answer is that the price assumes free cash flow will fall about 13 percent a year for ten years.

That number is flattering because FY2026 cash included about ₹384 crore released from stock and bills, so we also ran stricter cases. Without that release the base falls to ₹379 crore and the implied growth is minus 3.1 percent a year. Using market value against trailing profit of ₹737 crore gives minus 2.4 percent.[2][22] All three cases say the market expects no growth at all, which is cheap for a company with a ₹1,709 crore order book, and it is the main support for the turnaround archetype.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is weaker than the rest. This chapter names each one, says what evidence would show it is wrong, and says when that evidence will arrive. It ends with the strongest case against buying the stock, written as an opponent would write it.

The two estimates are the earnings forecast and the ₹600 target. The earnings forecast depends most on profit per tonne of seamless pipe and on the return from the investment pool. The target depends on those two plus the value outside owners can place on the cash.

The cash may never reach you

Our base target values the ₹3,848 crore of investments at 90 paise on the rupee. That rests on trust that the cash stays in the listed company, is invested with care and is eventually shared. Management has said it is conserving cash for an acquisition at a price it is comfortable with, and that it would buy only distressed assets, and that shareholders' interests need not match management's.[15] The three-way split filing does not say which company gets the investments, and the same plan was approved, withdrawn and approved again within five months.[17][16]

The investments are also not all safe. Of ₹3,848 crore, ₹3,462 crore is in mutual funds, including equity, hybrid, bond, gold and silver funds, and the company will not split them further.[7][15] Net gains on investments were ₹305 crore in FY2026 against ₹84 crore in FY2025, and the company reported a return of over 24 percent for nine months to December 2025.[2][15] A sharp fall in markets would cut other income and the carrying value of the pool together. We would know more when the Q2 results (due by 14 November 2026) show other income, and when the court papers for the scheme show where the investments sit.

Profit per tonne may fall back

The earnings forecast needs seamless profit per tonne near ₹14,000. The record is uneven: ₹25,931 in FY2024, ₹16,288 in FY2025 and ₹12,591 in FY2026, with quarters as low as ₹10,315.[8][10][7] The ₹15,660 of Q1 FY2027 is helped by a mix of oil-sector and export orders that management calls higher-margin, and 63 to 64 percent of the order book is of that type and ships in the second and third quarters.[16] After those orders are shipped, the profit per tonne depends on new orders that have not yet been won.

Evidence of error would be a Q2 or Q3 FY2027 per-tonne figure under ₹12,000, or an order book that falls back below ₹1,500 crore. Q1 is already booked at ₹15,660, so only the remaining 319,000 tonnes are exposed. Each ₹1,000 a tonne on those tonnes is about ₹1.8 of earnings per share, so a fall to ₹10,000 from ₹14,000 would take FY2027E EPS from ₹53 to about ₹46, a drop of about 13 percent. A full-year case, as in FY2028E, would cost about ₹2.4 per ₹1,000.

The duty and the oil cycle

The trade authority recommended five more years of duty on 23 October 2026, but the Finance Ministry must still notify it, and the existing duty runs out on 27 January 2027.[21] The form would change from a minimum import price, which management says became less effective after 2021, to a fixed duty of 268 US dollars a tonne.[13][21] The recommendation also leaves out casing and tubing for oil wells at the producers' own request, so the duty protects line pipe, boiler pipe and general engineering grades but not that product.[21] A lapse is the bear case in Chapter 5.

On the demand side, high crude prices usually lead to more drilling only after a delay, and tender slowdowns lasted through FY2026.[1] The notification or a lapse will be known by 27 January 2027, and the next two quarters of tender news will show whether spending follows the oil price.

The red-team bear case

The strongest honest case against buying is that the stock is cheap for reasons that will not go away. Sales have fallen three years in a row, return on capital is below 15 percent, and 41 percent of pre-tax profit comes from an investment pool that management will not describe. The controlling family holds 70.30 percent, management says shareholders' interests do not have to match its own, and a scheme to split the company was approved, withdrawn and approved again without a word on the cash.[2][22][15][17]

A buyer at ₹557.90 pays 1.09 times book value for a business whose reported profit includes market gains, and the Q1 FY2027 profit of ₹271 crore was helped by ₹175 crore of other income. ICRA has again placed the AA+ rating on watch over the split, and a split into three listed companies could leave each one trading at a discount to the sum of its parts.[7][18] A simple stress shows the thin margin of safety: cut the earnings multiple from 10.5 to 8.5 times and change nothing else, and the four-method value falls to about ₹543, below today's price. On that view the right price is closer to book value of ₹512, or below it.

Portfolio heat is the share of the whole portfolio you would lose if every open stop were hit. A basis point is one hundredth of one percent, so a 50 basis point risk budget means the planned loss at the stop is 0.50 percent of the portfolio. TIER_3 is the middle-low conviction band, with positions of 2 to 3.5 percent, and the tier cap is the top of that band. The appreciated weight ceiling of 22 percent means a winner may grow through price gains to that share of the portfolio before it must be trimmed. The 21-day exponential average and 50-day simple average are two moving lines that rise under a healthy uptrend, and we trail the stop beneath them as the price climbs. The stop rule in the table is written in general terms for stocks in a Stage 2 uptrend, using the 20-day low and the 50-day average. MSL is a Stage 1 base, so our stop follows the base rule instead: the 63-day base low of ₹524.50 minus one daily swing of ₹25.68, which is ₹498.82.
