1. What the company does

Action Construction Equipment Ltd (ACE) was founded in 1995 by Vijay Agarwal and runs from Palwal in Haryana[13][2]. It builds machines that lift and move heavy things. Its best-known product is the pick-and-carry crane: a small mobile crane that can lift a load, drive with it, and set it down again. These cranes work on building sites, in factories, in warehouses and on rail and metro projects. ACE also makes tower cranes, crawler cranes, forklifts, backhoe loaders (the digger-and-loader machines seen on road works), road rollers, tractors and harvesters[2]. The company says it is the world's largest maker of pick-and-carry cranes, with over 63 percent of the mobile crane market and about 60 percent of the tower crane market in India[2][20]. The credit rating agency ICRA, which has no stake in the claim, puts the share at 60 to 65 percent[13].

Who pays ACE, and why? Roughly half of its crane sales go to rental firms that lease cranes out by the day or month, and the rest go to contractors and to factories and logistics companies that use cranes in their own yards[8]. Management says 85 to 90 percent of these machines are bought with loans from banks and non-bank lenders[8]. That matters because it ties ACE's sales to how easily its customers can borrow. By end-user, the company puts the split at about 45 percent manufacturing and logistics, 35 percent infrastructure, 13 percent real estate and 7 percent agriculture[2]. ACE sells through more than 125 locations in India and exports to over 37 countries, mainly in the Middle East, Africa, Asia and Latin America[2][20].

How does it make money? It sells machines at a price above what the steel, engines, tyres and hydraulic parts cost to build, and the gap is its operating profit. The gross margin is the share of sales left after the direct cost of materials. The operating margin goes further and takes off wages, freight and other running costs, so it is the share of sales left from the whole business of making and selling. For ACE it was 15.4 percent in FY26 (₹504 crore on ₹3,280 crore of sales)[1]. Steel is the biggest input, so when steel prices jump the margin comes under pressure until ACE can raise its own prices. In 2026 that is exactly what is happening: steel is up by nearly 20 percent and ACE raised list prices three times, by about 10 percent in all[4][5]. Prices of the new machines range from ₹15 lakh to ₹45 lakh for a pick-and-carry crane. Heavy truck and crawler cranes cost ₹80 lakh to ₹3 crore[10]. (A lakh is one hundred thousand rupees and a crore is ten million.)

Sales follow the seasons. Management says 40 to 45 percent of a year's revenue arrives in the first half, from April to September, and 55 to 60 percent in the second half, after the monsoon and into the festival season[4]. So the June and September quarters are the lean ones. This is why ACE's first-half numbers are a poor guide to a full year, and why the results of the March quarter are usually the largest of the year[1].

What changed in 2025 and 2026

In January 2025 India moved to a new emission rule for construction machines, called CEV Stage V. The rule made engines cleaner and machines dearer. Buyers bought old-model machines early, in the months before the rule took effect, and then held back. ACE sold 13,360 cranes, construction and handling machines in FY25 and 10,853 in FY26, a fall of 18.8 percent[29]. The industry fell about 2 percent over the same year, from 140,191 to 136,995 units, so ACE's drop was sharper than the market's, although the two counts cover slightly different machines[14]. Price and mix made up most of the gap: revenue in the cranes segment fell only 4.7 percent[14].

A second shock arrived in March 2026, when the war in West Asia pushed up oil, steel and freight. ACE had planned to start a new tower-crane plant in April and put the decision off to September 2026[4]. Demand held up through May and June, then slowed with the rains, which management says happens every year[3]. The June 2026 quarter showed the strongest growth in the series: sales of ₹786 crore, up 20.6 percent on the year, after a 7 percent rise in the March quarter[1]. Volumes in the cranes, construction and handling segment rose 17.25 percent[3]. ACE sold 2,740 machines against 2,337 a year before[2].

Why the headline margin overstates the business

ACE's slide deck shows an EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) margin of 20.53 percent for the June 2026 quarter, up 428 basis points from the March quarter[2]. That number includes other income, which is mostly investment gains, so it reads higher than the operating margin. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent. Take other income out and the same quarter shows a margin of 15.0 percent (₹118 crore on ₹786 crore), which is 170 basis points lower than the 16.7 percent of the March quarter[1]. The 428-point jump came from other income swinging from a loss of ₹6 crore in March (a mark-to-market loss on the investment portfolio) to a gain of ₹55 crore in June[1][8].

This matters for how we judge the quarter. Other income was 35 percent of the June quarter's pre-tax profit of ₹158 crore, against 20 percent for FY26 as a whole[1]. Operating profit rose 27 percent on the year (₹118 crore against ₹93 crore), which is good, but investment gains lifted the profit line further[1]. We use the operating margin without other income as the test of the business, and we treat investment gains as a bonus that can reverse.

The cash pile and the joint venture

ACE holds ₹1,283 crore of investments, plus about ₹77 crore of cash and bank balances[1][2]. That is 11 percent of the ₹12,038 crore market value[35]. Management says it wants to put this money to work through acquisitions, and says it has identified some targets[6]. In the meantime the idle cash drags down the return on capital, which fell from 42.9 percent in FY24 to 32.7 percent in FY26[2].

On 30 April 2026 the board approved moving the heavy crane business (truck, crawler and rough terrain cranes) into a 50:50 joint venture with KATO Works of Japan, called ACE KATO Private Limited[21]. The venture was announced publicly on 2 July 2026, with a planned investment of about ₹200 crore in an 11-acre plant in Haryana[40][28]. That business had revenue of ₹85.77 crore in FY25, or 2.58 percent of company sales[21]. KATO puts in ₹100 crore of cash. ACE puts in the business and its machine designs, valued at ₹100 crore[6]. Management expects meaningful revenue from the venture only from FY28 and a royalty of 3 percent on KATO-designed export models[3][6]. The transfer is a related-party deal, and the company says it was done at arm's length[21].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Pick-and-carry crane is a small mobile crane that can drive around a site carrying its load. India, Australia and Italy use many of them. Chinese makers build truck-mounted cranes instead[8].
  • Hydra crane is the older-technology pick-and-carry crane. The newer type is called a new-generation (NG) crane. Hydras were 60 to 65 percent of the market in FY25, and management expects a 60:40 split in FY27 and 50:50 later[4].
  • Anti-dumping duty is an extra import tax meant to stop foreign sellers pricing below cost. ACE asked for it on Chinese truck and crawler cranes[24].
  • Slump sale is the sale of a whole business for one lump sum, not asset by asset. ACE is using it to move the heavy crane business into the joint venture[21].
  • Mark-to-market means valuing an investment at today's market price. A fall in the market shows up as a loss even if nothing was sold[8].
  • Rough terrain forklift is a forklift built to move cargo over unpaved ground. The Indian Army ordered 1,121 from ACE[22]. Chapter 2 asks whether ACE's position in all these machines is a lasting advantage.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A company is strong when rivals cannot easily take its customers. ACE has three advantages, and the evidence for each is below. The first is share. ACE says it holds over 63 percent of mobile cranes and about 60 percent of tower cranes, and ICRA puts its share at 60 to 65 percent in its segment. That scale gives it an edge in parts, in service and in dealer reach that smaller makers cannot match[13][2]. The second is product fit. The pick-and-carry crane is a local favourite, and the Chinese makers that sell heavy cranes in India do not build it[8]. The third is the network: more than 125 locations and tie-ups with lenders, so a buyer can get a machine, a loan and a service visit from one brand[2][8].

Does the share translate into pricing power? The best test is what happened in 2026. ACE raised list prices three times: 1 to 1.5 percent in January, 3 to 4 percent in March and 5 to 6 percent in June, close to 10 percent in all[4]. Over the same quarter its machine volumes rose 17.25 percent[3]. Management admits that getting customers to accept the rise is slow and that it cannot always recover the full cost, but volume growth through a 10 percent price rise is the clearest evidence of a franchise we have seen in the filings[4]. One quarter is not a trend, though. The year-earlier quarter was weak, and demand was strong until May and June before the rains[3].

The weak spots are just as clear. Heavy cranes are the one area where ACE is a minor player: the business moving into the joint venture is only 2.58 percent of sales[21]. In the investigation of Chinese crane imports, the Indian industry's market share in the product group under review fell from an index of 100 to 17 while imports from China rose from 163 units to 938 units over the same years[24]. The trade authority recommended duties of 25 to 52 percent in September 2025, but the Finance Ministry did not issue the notification, and management now says it has no leads[8][6]. ICRA also notes that ACE's margins in backhoe loaders and tractors trail those of the market leaders because its products sell at a discount[13]. In agriculture the margin is about 1 percent[7].

Where the competitive edge is real

The first piece of evidence is the credit rating. ICRA rates ACE [ICRA]AA with a stable outlook, and the short-term rating is A1+. It cites a well-established position in cranes and forklifts and a strong financial profile[13]. The second piece is how customers behave. Because 85 to 90 percent of buyers use loans, lenders must be comfortable with the machine's resale value and the dealer's service. That is a quiet moat that favours the market leader[8]. The third is the Hydra and new-generation split. In May 2025 ACE held more than 75 percent of the Hydra segment and 51 percent of the new-generation segment, and management says it has been the leader in new generation for two years[12][6]. That shows ACE can win when buyers move to the newer product, which is what the emission rule forces.

For you as an owner, the point is that this moat protects the volume and the price on mid-size cranes. It does not protect against a fall in construction activity, and it does not protect the company from a fall in how much customers can borrow. The next sections test whether the profit and the cash follow the franchise.

Where it is thin: heavy cranes, forklifts, farm equipment

ACE is in the top three in forklifts, with Godrej and KION, but it is not the leader[13]. In backhoe loaders its share was about 2.5 percent in November 2025, and management hopes to raise it to 5 or 6 percent and later to double digits[11]. The company sold 168 backhoe loaders in the September 2025 quarter[11], and says it now averages 150 to 160 units a quarter[4]. Backhoes are the largest construction machine in India, so there is a big prize, but ACE is a small player against JCB in that market[13].

The joint venture is an honest attempt to fix the heavy crane gap. KATO brings designs and an export network. ACE brings plants and dealers. But the plan is slow: the venture starts with ACE's current Indian models, plans upgraded models from the March 2027 quarter, and expects its first dedicated KATO-designed machines about a year after that[6]. In the meantime ACE has capacity for about 400 truck and crawler cranes and sells 50 to 60 a year[10]. We therefore give the venture no value in our central case before FY28.

Governance and accounting checks

ACE is run by its promoter family. Vijay Agarwal, the founder, is chairman and managing director, and three other members of the promoter group, Mona Agarwal, Sorab Agarwal and Surbhi Garg, are whole-time directors. The promoter group owns 65.42 percent of the shares[17][25][13]. None of the promoter holding is pledged as security for loans[25][39]. Pledging is the practice of borrowing against shares, and it is a warning sign when it rises. The four promoter-group directors were paid ₹11.08 crore in FY26 (₹5.67 crore, ₹3.06 crore, ₹1.31 crore and ₹1.04 crore), which is 2.7 percent of the year's profit of ₹415 crore[17][1]. That is a reasonable level, and their pay rose only 1 to 1.4 percent on the year.

The auditors named one key audit matter, revenue recognition on the sale of products, the standard risk for a manufacturer, and they did not qualify their opinion[19]. Tax and other claims against the company not booked as debts were ₹107.09 crore at March 2026 (down from ₹156.87 crore a year earlier), plus ₹15.83 crore of other claims[16]. That is about 6 percent of net worth, and a Central Excise dispute of ₹70.28 crore was settled during the year[16]. The one accounting point worth knowing is that the company borrows and repays through the year even though year-end debt is only ₹8 crore. In FY26 it drew ₹691.7 crore of short-term loans and repaid ₹706.4 crore[15][1]. ICRA says undrawn limits were ₹156 crore against ₹230 crore of drawing power in November 2025, so about ₹74 crore was in use at that date[13]. The business is not debt-free through the year, but the amounts are small against ₹417 crore of operating cash flow.

The business also runs a treasury

ACE bought ₹831 crore of investments and sold ₹508 crore in FY26[15]. Other income was ₹77 crore, ₹100 crore and ₹110 crore in FY24 to FY26[1]. In FY26 interest income of ₹55 crore and fair-value gains of ₹44 crore made up ₹99 crore of the ₹110 crore[15]. Cash flow from operations is the cash a business collects from selling its goods, less what it pays to run. It does not include this income, because the company shows it in the investing section of the cash flow statement[15]. That is a point in favour of the 1.0 cash-to-profit ratio we report later: the cash side excludes treasury gains while the profit side includes them, so the true operating cash conversion is better than 1.0.

For an owner, the treasury has two effects. It cushions the balance sheet, which is a comfort when sales fall. It also dilutes the return on capital and adds noise to profit, as the ₹6 crore loss in the March quarter and the ₹55 crore gain in the June quarter show[1][8]. Management has said that it does not want the return on capital to drop below 30 to 33 percent and that it wants to use the cash for capacity and acquisitions[9]. We will check in Chapter 2B whether the money is being put to work as promised.

Layer 2 asks which operating measures run this particular business, and whether each is healthy. Our scoring rules ask for three to five measures, each scored PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). A company qualifies at 3.0 of 5.0 or more with no more than one FAIL[36]. A FAIL means the measure has clearly broken. A WATCH means the number is acceptable but moving the wrong way, or not yet proven.

We picked five measures for a crane and machine maker. Market share and pricing power show whether the franchise lets ACE raise prices without losing buyers. Defence, exports and capacity are the three stated sources of growth beyond the home crane market. Return on capital and capital allocation show whether the large cash pile is being put to work or is idle. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after paying for plant and equipment.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Guidance is a company's own forecast for the period ahead, and it is only useful if the people giving it have a record. We went back to the calls of May 2025, November 2025 and February 2026, wrote down what management promised, and checked it against the filings. The record is mixed in a specific way: management delivers what it controls, and over-promises what depends on the market or the government.

On the items it controls, ACE did what it said. It kept margins stable through a down year. Operating margin went from 15.2 to 15.4 percent. It reached negative working capital at year-end, as promised in February. It signed the KATO joint venture on the timetable it gave. It started delivering the defence forklifts in September 2026[1][10][7][21][23].

On growth it missed, and the misses came in the same direction every time. In May 2025 management targeted 14 to 15 percent sales growth for FY26. By November it had cut that to 'flattish to single digit', and by February to 'flat'. The result was a fall of 1.4 percent[12][11][10][1]. The ₹4,400 crore revenue goal first set for FY26 moved to FY27, then became a range of ₹4,000 to ₹4,400 crore in November 2025, and in July 2026 management declined to give any FY27 number until the end of September[12][11][4]. Capital spending was ₹93 crore against a plan of ₹300 to ₹350 crore[15][12]. We read this as optimism about demand, not as dishonesty, because every cut was announced on the next call.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter lays out five years of results in plain terms and then tests them against our Layer 1 rules. Layer 1 compares two things for every company. The first is the level: how good the numbers are today. The second is the trajectory: how fast they are getting better or worse. A business can have good levels and a bad trajectory, and the reverse, and the verdict depends on both.

Three words come up often. EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares, so it is the profit that belongs to one share. ROCE (return on capital employed) is profit before interest and tax divided by the money invested in the business, so it shows how hard each rupee works. ACE reports in crore rupees, and all amounts are consolidated unless we say standalone.

The headline is a good business that stalled for a year. Sales grew by a third a year from FY22 to FY24, then by 14 percent in FY25, and fell 1.4 percent in FY26[1]. Profit held up because the margin kept rising. The June 2026 quarter shows a restart, with sales up 20.6 percent. Whether that restart lasts depends on prices, cost inflation and the monsoon, which is why we look at the trend as well as the level.

This chapter asks what today's price already assumes, how analysts are changing their numbers, where we differ, and what the stock could be worth in twelve months in three cases. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate rests on a few assumptions, and the weakest one decides how much to trust it. For each estimate in the previous chapters we name the weakest assumption, say what evidence would show it is wrong, and say when we will know. We then give the strongest honest case against our own verdict, and name the single claim on which the whole call hinges.

Earnings estimate: the 15 percent margin

The weakest assumption in our earnings estimate is that the operating margin without other income stays near 15 percent. The evidence against it is already visible: gross margin fell about 140 basis points on the year in the June quarter, steel is up nearly 20 percent, and management puts total cost inflation at 11 to 12 percent against a price rise of about 10 percent[4][5]. Price rises take one to three months to reach the invoice, while supplier increases have already flowed in, so the full squeeze lands in the September quarter[5]. Management's stated aim is only to recover costs and it says it is not planning any overall margin gain[5].

What would show we are wrong is a September-quarter operating margin below 14.0 percent, or a gross margin that falls further. The September results are due by 14 November 2026. As of 8 September no date had been set, and the legal deadline is 45 days after quarter-end[27]. A results-data site still showed no announced date when we fetched it on 26 October[44]. The December quarter, reported in February 2027, is the confirmation.

Value: the 27.5 times multiple

The weakest assumption in our value is that the market keeps paying 27.5 times earnings. Our own history shows the multiple fell from a median of 52.9 times in the June 2024 quarter to 24.6 times in the March 2026 quarter as growth stalled[37]. The market is also in a downtrend, which tends to compress multiples on small and mid-sized stocks. If the multiple falls to 24 times, the central value falls to ₹1,016, which is only 1.6 percent above the close[38].

The evidence will be the price itself and the market's trend over the next quarter. We cannot know it in advance, and that is why we size the position at 3.5 percent of net worth at most and keep a stop.

Probabilities: that the recovery lasts into the second half

The weakest assumption behind our probabilities is that the June quarter's growth carries into the second half. Management warns that the effect of a weak monsoon shows in the December quarter, and that demand can move 5 to 10 percent either way[6]. Buyers depend on loans for 85 to 90 percent of purchases, so a rise in borrowing costs or a tightening by lenders would hit volumes directly[8]. A second concern is the base: June 2026 sales grew 21 percent from a quarter that had fallen 11 percent, so part of the growth is a low base and part is a price rise, and we do not know the split[1][3].

We will know more when the September results show volumes, since management expects the second quarter to look like the first, and when the December quarter results arrive in February 2027[4].

The red-team case against our verdict

The strongest honest case against buying ACE is this. The stock is not cheap, at 27.6 times trailing profit and 30.4 times without its investments, for a company whose profit grew 3 percent over the last twelve months[1][35]. Its sales were lower in FY26 than in FY25, and the 21 percent growth in the June quarter was against a low base and helped by price rises. A third of that quarter's pre-tax profit came from investment gains, after a loss on the same portfolio the quarter before[1]. Suppliers fund the business through 151 days of payables, up from 124 in two years, and customers fund their purchases with loans that become harder to get in a market stress. The VIX is up 66 percent in a month[1][8][35]. Management missed its FY26 growth guidance after cutting it twice, the new plant is delayed, and the joint venture moves about ₹86 crore of annual sales out of ACE while adding revenue only from FY28[21][3]. If that is right, the multiple falls toward 21 times on earnings that stay flat near ₹31 a share, and the stock goes to about ₹650.

Our answer is partial. The business has earned a 32.7 percent return on capital with almost no debt, it has pricing power that showed up in a quarter of rising volumes, and its balance sheet carries investments worth 11 percent of its market value[2][3][35]. None of that makes the price attractive today, and we have not said it does. It makes the stock a reasonable candidate to own after a price fall, which is what the order plan is for. The stop at ₹926.66 limits the loss to 5.9 percent from ₹985.00 if the bear case begins without a gap.

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule is the technical routing rule in our scoring rules: a business that passes the red lines and the Layer 2 test but sits in a Stage 3 pullback above a rising 200-day average is routed to a limit order and not rejected[36]. The ratio behind the decision is 2.25 to 1 at the ₹1,000.10 close and 3.09 to 1 at ₹985.00, against a floor of 2.0 for a core compounder[38][36]. The business qualifies. The momentum gate does not.

Why not buy at the market? There are four reasons. The stock fell 5.06 percent on 23 October on 1.57 times normal volume, with an RSI of 24.77, and nothing yet shows that selling is over[35]. The ₹991.20 level that our price snapshot lists as the nearest support is a one-day-old low, not a tested floor, and on 26 October the price traded as low as ₹979.20 before recovering[35][34].
