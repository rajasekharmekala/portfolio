Terms used in this dossier

CMP stands for current market price, the last traded price in the session. Here it is Rs 1,201.10, the close on 12 October 2026. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the operating profit a company earns for each rupee of capital it uses, before tax and after removing the cash it holds. A ROCE of 32 percent means the business earns about 32 paise a year on each rupee of capital, which is a high figure for a manufacturer. ROE (return on equity) is the same idea measured against shareholders' money alone. D/E (debt-to-equity) divides borrowed money by the owners' capital; a figure near zero means the company is almost debt free.

CFO (cash from operations) is the cash a business actually collects from running its activities. PAT (profit after tax) is the accounting profit after tax. Comparing the two tells you whether reported profit turns into cash. A ratio of 1.0 or above means the cash matches or beats the profit. OPM (operating profit margin) is operating profit divided by sales, before interest, depreciation and other income. EPS (earnings per share) is profit after tax divided by the number of shares, and P/E (price-to-earnings) is the price divided by EPS, which is the number of years of current earnings an investor pays for. A forward EPS is an estimate for a future year, usually supplied by analysts.

SMA (simple moving average) is the average closing price over a set number of sessions. The 20-, 50- and 200-session averages describe the short, medium and long trend. ATR (average true range) measures the typical daily price range over 14 sessions; it is the stock's own unit of movement, so a move of 5 ATR is five typical days' worth of travel. RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 gauge of recent momentum; readings above 70 are often called overbought, and this report does not use RSI as a gate. Stage 2 is the uptrend phase in Stan Weinstein's framework: the price sits above a rising long-term average after a base. Swing low is the lowest recent price point that buyers defended. Stage 4 is the downtrend phase, when the price has broken below a falling long-term average. A stop loss is the price at which a position is sold to cap the loss.

Relative strength (RS) compares the stock's return with a market index over a period; a positive excess return means the stock is outperforming. Base pivot is the price level from which a consolidation (base) is measured; the distance from it in ATR units shows how far the price has travelled since the base formed. Asymmetry ratio is the expected reward divided by the risk: the distance from the current price to the target over the distance from the current price to the stop. Anchored target means a target produced by a documented method, here forward EPS times a three-year median P/E. Consensus target is an average of broker price targets, which is useful only when several brokers are behind it.

FAIL_ASYMMETRY is the gate status recorded when the reward-to-risk floor is not met by an anchored target. REJECTED is the action verdict for a candidate that fails a Layer 1 hard gate; it means no allocation and no further scoring. WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT is the posture for a name that passes Layer 1 but is too far above its support to buy at the current price. NO_MOMENTUM is the momentum gate result when a stock passes neither Test A nor Test B; under the rules, that blocks allocation regardless of business quality. Pledge is shares a promoter has offered as security for a loan; a pledge of zero is the cleanest reading. FII and DII are foreign and domestic institutional investors.

How the verdict was reached

The verdict follows a fixed order, and the order itself is part of the discipline. A candidate must pass Layer 1 in full before any other scoring is done. Layer 1 has three parts: a financial floor, a technical floor and an asymmetry floor. The financial floor asks whether the company earns a high return on its capital, carries little debt and turns its profits into cash. The technical floor asks whether the price is in a healthy uptrend and not in a breakdown. The asymmetry floor asks whether a documented target, measured against a documented stop, offers enough reward for the risk. If any part fails, the candidate is REJECTED and Layers 2 and 3 are not evaluated.

The financial floor uses three figures from the consolidated Screener.in statements: ROCE of at least 15 percent (with more than 18 percent preferred for the Core bucket), debt-to-equity below 0.70, and three-year cumulative CFO divided by cumulative PAT at or above 0.70. We used fiscal years 2024 to 2026, the three most recent audited years. The technical floor uses the ledger: a Stage 2 uptrend for the Core bucket, with no Stage 4 breakdown and no death cross on heavy volume. The asymmetry floor uses the ledger's swing low and ATR, the stop rule in the scoring file, and a target from an external source.

The bucket matters because the floors differ. The Core Alpha bucket asks for a 3.0 ratio and a stop below the lower of the swing low and the 50- or 200-session average, minus one ATR. The Tactical Momentum bucket asks for a 3.5 ratio and a tighter stop below the lower of the swing low and the 20- or 50-session average, minus half an ATR. The business's returns look like a Core name, so we applied the Core rule as the primary test. We also ran the Tactical test in full, so the verdict does not depend on the bucket choice. Both fail.

The momentum gate runs separately, in code, from the frozen price snapshot. It has two routes. Test A asks whether the stock is already in a Stage 2 uptrend, rising, close to its support. Test B asks whether a stock is entering an uptrend from a base at its pivot, with volume expanding. A candidate must pass one of the two. Failing both means no allocation, and the gate's verdict is recorded even when the business is excellent. That rule is deliberate: a fine company bought at the wrong time is still a poor position.

Where a figure could not be verified, we say so rather than fill the gap. Some items came back as not found after at least three distinct searches, and we list them in the run notes. The consensus figures were checked against more than one page, and where they disagree we report both. Nothing in this dossier is a forecast we made ourselves; the forward earnings figure, the target and the multiple all come from named sources, and the arithmetic that combines them is shown in full in the Layer 1 section.

What the company does

Action Construction Equipment Ltd, listed under the ticker ACE on the NSE and BSE, makes lifting and earth-moving machines. The business is best understood as a set of product lines that share a factory base, a dealer network and a service organisation. The largest line is cranes: pick-and-carry cranes, which are wheeled cranes that carry their load on the chassis and are common on Indian construction sites; truck-mounted cranes; crawler cranes; rough-terrain cranes; and mobile tower cranes. The company's own profile, reproduced on the Screener page, describes it as the world's largest pick-and-carry crane maker with a claimed 63 percent share of the mobile-crane segment in India and a claimed 60 percent share of tower cranes domestically.[1] Those shares are company-level claims that we could not check against an independent market study, so we treat them as indicative.

The second group is material handling and construction equipment. It includes forklift trucks, telehandlers, aerial work platforms and scissor lifts; backhoe loaders, motor graders and vibratory rollers; and piling rigs and concrete placing booms. The third is agricultural equipment, including tractors, harvesters and combines. The aggregator profile describes the business as two segments: cranes, material handling and construction equipment on one side, and agricultural equipment on the other.[2] In the June 2026 quarter the core segment brought in Rs 738.37 crore of revenue, up 22 percent, on a 17.25 percent rise in volumes, while agricultural equipment brought in Rs 42.67 crore.[3] The agricultural line is shrinking as a share of the whole, which means the investment case rests on cranes and construction equipment.

Three newer strands are worth tracking. Defence is the first. Management said defence contributed about 5 percent of first-quarter revenue and targets 5 to 6 percent for the full year, with a large order execution beginning in August 2026.[3] A report on the call puts the defence order book at Rs 570 crore.[4] The second is exports, which were about 3 percent of the June quarter and were described as subdued because of shipping problems and delayed Middle East orders.[3] These strands matter because they diversify the business away from a single domestic construction cycle.

The company also has a pricing story. Management said it had raised prices three times in calendar 2026, for a cumulative increase of about 10 percent.[3] That is the single most important operating fact of the year, because it tells you the company is passing cost inflation to customers rather than absorbing it. The cost of that approach is that volume depends on customers accepting the higher prices.

Customer concentration is the question the file cannot answer. We searched for a top-customer or dealer-concentration disclosure in the company's own filings and in secondary research and found none readable. The annual report PDF from the National Stock Exchange timed out on two attempts. The dossier therefore does not state how much of revenue rests on the largest customers, and it treats that gap as a risk to monitor rather than a fact to assume away.

Why the business is strong or weak

The clearest strength is the return on capital. ROCE was 42 percent in the year to March 2024, 40 percent in the year to March 2025 and 32 percent in the year to March 2026.[1] A ROCE in the thirties is well above the 15 percent floor and above the 18 percent level the Core bucket prefers. The fall from 42 to 32 is worth noticing, because it means the capital base has grown faster than operating profit. The company's balance sheet explains part of that growth. Investments rose from Rs 594 crore in March 2024 to Rs 918 crore in March 2025 and Rs 1,283 crore in March 2026.[1] That is surplus cash placed in treasury assets, which earns a return but dilutes the ratio. A reader who wants the operating return should look at the operating business, and the operating business is still earning a high return.

The second strength is the balance sheet. Borrowings were Rs 8 crore against equity capital of Rs 24 crore and reserves of Rs 1,987 crore at March 2026.[1] On those figures debt-to-equity is about 0.004, close to zero, against a gate of 0.70. Little debt means the company does not need to use profit to pay interest, and it can keep investing or return cash without financial stress. The third strength is cash generation. Over FY2024 to FY2026 the company produced Rs 1,262 crore of operating cash against Rs 1,152 crore of profit, a ratio of 1.10, and free cash flow reached Rs 330 crore in FY2026.[1] When cash runs ahead of profit, reported earnings are less likely to be an accounting artefact. Working-capital days also look sound: debtor days were 32 and the cash conversion cycle was negative 20 days in FY2026, which means suppliers are funding part of the working capital.[1]

The weakness is growth. Sales fell from Rs 3,327 crore in FY2025 to Rs 3,280 crore in FY2026, a decline of about 1.4 percent. Profit after tax edged up from Rs 409 crore to Rs 415 crore, about 1.5 percent.[1] An independent aggregator reports the same fall, showing revenue of Rs 32.80 billion against Rs 33.27 billion, and earnings up 1.43 percent.[2] Sales dipped 1.4 percent in FY2026 after rising 14 percent in FY2025, and profit was flat, which in a high-return business raises a question about demand rather than quality. The first quarter of FY2027 was strong, with consolidated sales of about Rs 785 to 786 crore and profit of Rs 119 crore, up about 22 percent year on year, but one quarter does not reverse a single year's dip.[1][5] Management declined to give a full-year revenue number until September-end, and the tracker records the FY27 growth target as maintained but not yet quantified.[6][7]

The second weakness is margin pressure. On the call, management said gross margin fell by almost 140 basis points year on year, driven by input costs[8], including higher costs for tyres, belts, rubber, plastics and freight.[6] The operating margin for the consolidated business held at 15 percent in the June quarter and the year to March 2026, and management's stated aim is to keep operating EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) above 15 percent. The reason the margin held is price increases, not cheaper inputs. That is a fragile defence, because price increases can cut volumes if customers push back, and the company itself said a further price move of about 2 percent may be needed if commodity volatility continues.[3]

The third weakness is that the market already prices in a good deal of the strength. The stock trades at 32.8 times trailing earnings and about 29 times the forward multiple quoted by one aggregator.[1][2] Against a book value of Rs 169 per share, the price is about 7.1 times book, which the company's returns can justify but which leaves little room for a disappointment. The fourth is the customer-concentration gap described above, which we could not close. Taken together, the business is strong on quality and returns, but its growth has stalled, its margins depend on price, and its valuation already assumes that the stall ends. That combination is the reason a high-quality company can still fail the asymmetry test.

The price tells you where the market has placed the stock relative to its own history, and in this case the answer is clear: the stock is in a strong uptrend that has run a long way from its base. Relative strength tells you whether the stock is leading or lagging the market. What follows for a reader is simple. The price is strong and the trend is intact, but the stock has already moved a long way from the level at which a disciplined buyer would have entered. A buyer entering now would be buying at a level that gives a poor reward against a sensible stop. The stock might still rise, and a reader who believes the company will compound for years may be right. The discipline here is about the entry price, and the price is the part of the case that is not yet favourable.

The number

The anchored target for the asymmetry test is Rs 1,475 per share: forward EPS of Rs 40.30 multiplied by a three-year median P/E of 36.6 times. Measured against the Core stop of Rs 927.31, the reward-to-risk ratio is 1.00.[21][14]

How it was calculated

The median is the middle value of 51.8, 36.6 and 21.5 times, the fiscal-year-end P/E figures for FY2024 to FY2026, which is 36.6. Multiplying 40.30 by 36.6 gives 1,474.98, rounded to 1,475. The ratio divides the reward of Rs 273.88 (1,474.98 less the price of 1,201.10) by the risk of Rs 273.79 (1,201.10 less the stop of 927.31).

The inputs

The EPS comes from one analyst estimate for the year to March 2027. The multiple comes from the year-end ratios on a financial-data page. The Core stop uses the 200-session average of Rs 970.09 and the 14-session ATR of Rs 42.78, and the price is the 12 October 2026 close of Rs 1,201.10.[21][14]

The momentum gate reaches the same answer by a different route. Test A requires the stock to be in a rising Stage 2 uptrend and within 1.2 ATR of its support. The stock passes the structure test, with the close above the 50-session average and the 50-session average above the 200-session average. It passes the rising 200-session average test, and it passes the relative-strength test. It fails the proximity test, because the stock is 5.69 ATR from its base, almost five times the maximum. Test B requires the stock to be at its base pivot with volume expanding. It passes the 50-session holding test, the relative-strength rising test and the volume dry-up test. It fails the pivot test at 5.69 ATR against a maximum of 1.0, and it fails volume expansion at 0.65 against a minimum of 1.0. Both tests fail, so the result is NO_MOMENTUM.

The verdict is therefore REJECTED. Layer 1 fails on the asymmetry floor, and under the rules a Layer 1 failure ends the review. The momentum result independently blocks any allocation, so the verdict would not change even if the asymmetry gate were argued differently. The company is not rejected for being weak; it is rejected because a buyer at today's price would be paid too little for the risk, and the momentum gate records that the stock is extended.

Layer 2 scorecard

Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five operating measures that drive the particular business, each graded pass, watch or fail, with a minimum of three passes to qualify. It is designed to sit on top of Layer 1 and only be read once the hard gates are passed. Because ACE fails Layer 1, Layer 2 is not evaluated. The rule is written into the Layer 1 disposition: a candidate that is rejected at Layer 1 is not scored at Layer 2 or Layer 3. We state that plainly rather than leaving the section blank, because the reader may reasonably expect a scorecard and should know why it is absent.

It is still useful to say what the scorecard would measure for a capital-goods business of this kind, so that a reader can see what would have to be true for the name to qualify later. The natural measures are fleet or capacity utilisation, which asks whether the plants are running near their capacity; order-book cover, which asks how many months of work is already signed and paid for; and working-capital days, which asks how long the company waits to be paid. The defence order book reported at Rs 570 crore is one input to order cover, and the reported debtor days of 32 would pass a sensible working-capital threshold.[4][1] These are context, not scores. We have no verified total order-book figure and no capacity-utilisation disclosure, so the order-cover and utilisation measures could not be graded even if Layer 2 applied.

The company itself has reasons to be watched on each measure. Management said it is building a new defence facility and expects growth in backhoe, road-machinery, forklift and tower-crane numbers this year.[6] If those volumes come through, the capacity-utilisation measure would become the key test. If price rises reduce volume, the utilisation measure would show it first. Those are the points a future review would check.

What follows for the current decision is that Layer 2 cannot rescue the verdict. A strong scorecard would not change a Layer 1 failure, and the rules are written that way on purpose. The scorecard matters later, if the price and target arithmetic changes and the name is reconsidered. At that point the working-capital and utilisation data would need to be refreshed from the company's September-quarter filing, and the order-cover figure would need to be sourced in full.

For a reader who wants the operating picture now, the business is in good shape on the measures that are available. Cash conversion is strong, debtor days are low, the operating margin is steady, and the defence and export lines are growing from a small base. The unanswered question is whether the core crane volume can keep growing at the pace management implies after three price rises. That question is the one a Layer 2 review would be designed to answer, and it is the one the next results date will start to answer. In practice a reader can test the scorecard's logic now. If the September-quarter filing shows capacity utilisation rising while debtor days stay near 32, the business would be passing the measures that matter most for a capital-goods maker. If utilisation falls while price increases continue, the same evidence would point the other way. Both outcomes are plausible, and neither can be scored until that filing is read.

Layer 3 entry posture

Layer 3 is the timing gate. It asks how far the price sits from the nearest verified support, measured in ATR units, and maps that distance onto a ladder of bands: full size when the price is at support, standard size when it is near, a limit order at reduced size in the next band, and no buy when the price is extended. For ACE the posture is not evaluated, for the same reason as Layer 2: the stock fails Layer 1. The section therefore records the rule and gives the reader the distance so that the posture can be understood when it is needed.

The distance is the important number. The price sits 5.69 ATR above the base low that the momentum gate uses, and the ladder's most generous band ends at 1.2 ATR. On the ladder, a price beyond that limit falls in the no-buy band, which means the rule is to wait for a pullback, a retest or a new base rather than chase the move. That is the same practical outcome as a posture of WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, which is the label the rules give to an extended name that passes Layer 1. We do not assign that label here, because it is not a Layer 3 score for a name that failed Layer 1. The arithmetic is shown only so that the reader sees where the price stands relative to the bands.

A simple way to think about the ladder is distance from the floor. Imagine a tile sitting on a rising slope. If the tile is right at the top of a step, the step is a good place to put down a foot; if the tile has slid a long way beyond the last step, you wait for it to come back. ACE's tile is well beyond the last step. It is not in danger of breaking, because the trend is intact, but it has not paid the buyer a fair price for the wait. The ladder rewards patience at a price the stop can protect.

The ladder also tells you what a better entry would look like. A Core stop at Rs 927.31 and a target of Rs 1,475 would give a ratio of 3.0 at a price near Rs 1,064, which is about 11 percent below the close. A Tactical stop at Rs 1,064.71 and the same target would give 3.5 at a price near Rs 1,155. Those levels are arithmetic, not forecasts, and they hold the stop and the target fixed at today's values. The stops move with the price structure, so the real levels would need to be recomputed at the time. We state them here because they show the reader the scale of the adjustment needed.

The result for the decision is that no buy is recommended today. The posture the ladder would assign at this price is no-buy, and the verdict is REJECTED for the reasons in the Layer 1 section. A reader who wants the company in the portfolio should wait for the rules to be met at a different price, and the next section explains what that would require. A reader who holds no position has nothing to act on; a reader who already holds the stock should treat this dossier as an assessment of the new-entry case only, not as a sell instruction.

ELECON's ROCE is about two-thirds of ACE's, and ESCORTS and BEML are well below both. On price, the picture is more mixed. ACE's trailing P/E of 32.8 is above ESCORTS's 21.0 and slightly above ELECON's 30.3. BEML's 91.1 reflects weak recent profit rather than a rich multiple, since its ROCE is 7.66 percent. The useful comparison for ACE is with ELECON, whose returns are lower and whose multiple is a little lower. ACE's higher return explains part of its higher multiple; the rest is the market's expectation for its growth, which the flat FY2026 figures have not yet confirmed. A market that pays more for a stock with flat sales is betting on a return to growth.

The peers also show why the sector has no simple benchmark. Escorts and BEML carry different product mixes and cycles, so their ROCE and P/E say less about ACE than ELECON does. For that reason we rely on the company's own three-year median P/E of 36.6 times for the asymmetry test, rather than on a peer average. A peer average would mix businesses with very different returns and would produce a target that is not specific to ACE.

What follows is that ACE's quality is not in doubt against its peers, and its price is not out of line with its own history. The issue is narrower: at this price, the reward for buying now is small compared with the risk to a sensible stop. The peer table supports the view that the company is a good business, and it does not support a view that the stock is cheap. A reader should hold both points at once, because they are the reason the verdict is a rejection on entry price and not a judgement on the company. The peer set is also small and is not a sector index. A proper benchmark would cover the full list of crane and material-handling makers, their order books and their multiples over several years, and the file does not hold that. The three peers were chosen because their pages carry the same measures in the same layout. That makes the comparison honest about what it measures, namely returns and multiples, and silent about what it cannot measure, such as order books and export mix.

Fatal flaws and the red-team case

A fatal flaw is a fact that would, on its own, stop an investment from working. The red-team exercise asks what a sceptical investor would say about the case, and it tries to make that case as strong as the evidence allows. The bear case against ACE is not that the company is poor. It is that the company has stopped growing, its margins depend on price, its valuation already assumes growth, and its entry point is poor. Each is supported by the figures above.

The first flaw is the growth pause. Sales dipped in FY2026 and profit barely rose. A company with a 32 percent return on capital should be compounding; instead it stalled, and the first quarter of FY2027 is one result, not a trend. If the strong June quarter was partly a catch-up from weaker months, the bear case gets stronger, because the price increases are doing the work that volume should do. Management has refused to commit to a full-year number until September, which is a reasonable choice in an uncertain year, but it leaves the growth question open.

The second flaw is the cost squeeze. Gross margin fell by about 140 basis points, the company is passing commodity costs through three price rises, and management expects further inflation of 11 to 12 percent. If the volume response to price is stronger than management expects, operating profit could fall even with the same sales. The company's own commentary treats the 15 percent margin as an aim to be defended, not a level that is secure. A margin that depends on price is the kind of margin that a downturn in demand exposes quickly.

The third flaw is valuation dependency. The stock trades at 32.8 times trailing earnings, and the single analyst estimate the asymmetry test relies on implies a rise in earnings in the year to March 2027. The bear case is that the market already expects the stalled growth to resume, so any disappointment would produce a larger fall than the earnings change alone. The median P/E is a useful anchor, but it is an anchor built on past multiples that may not repeat if the growth story changes.

The fourth flaw is the gap in the evidence. We could not find a customer-concentration figure, a verified order-book total, or an independent market-share study. The company's market-share claims are its own. A reader who wants to judge the moat in full would need those figures, and their absence means the moat is asserted more than it is shown. The fifth is ownership. Foreign institutions have reduced their holdings from 9.58 to 7.82 percent in a quarter, which is a small but real signal that a large investor is taking profit.

The bear case also has a counterweight, and a fair red-team exercise has to state it. The balance sheet is almost free of debt, cash generation is strong, the franchise earns very high returns, and the price has not yet broken its trend. The stock is not in danger of a structural collapse on these figures. The bear case is about price, not about survival. Its strongest form is this: the company may be excellent, the stock may rise, and a buyer at today's price would still be paid too little for the risk. That is the argument the rules are designed to make, and the figures support it.

What would change the verdict

Price trigger for Core Alpha: a close near Rs 1,064 with the stop at Rs 927.31 and the target at Rs 1,475, or a documented target of Rs 2,022.5 or more reached by the three-year median P/E method.

Price trigger for Tactical Momentum: a close near Rs 1,155 with the stop at Rs 1,064.71 and the same target, with the momentum gate passing.

A verdict is only useful if the reader knows what would reverse it. For ACE the verdict is a price and target question, so the triggers are numbers. The first trigger is a higher anchored target. On the Core stop at Rs 927.31, the target needs to reach Rs 2,022.5 to clear 3.0. With the three-year median P/E of 36.6 times, that would need forward EPS of about Rs 55.3, against the Rs 40.30 estimate for the year to March 2027. That is a large revision, and nothing in the published estimates supports it. A documented broker target near Rs 2,000 would not be enough on its own, because the anchor must be the method the rules specify, not a broker's chosen multiple.

The second trigger is a lower price with the same stop and target. Holding the Core stop at Rs 927.31 and the target at Rs 1,475, the ratio reaches 3.0 at a price of about Rs 1,064. Holding the Tactical stop at Rs 1,064.71 and the same target, the ratio reaches 3.5 at about Rs 1,155. Either level would make the asymmetry gate pass on the arithmetic. The momentum gate would still need to pass, and the stock would need to be back near its base pivot with volume expanding. A pullback to those levels would reopen the question, but only if the trend held. A close below the 200-session average would change the technical posture and would end the case for entry.

The third trigger is a change in the earnings path. If the September-quarter results and the full-year guidance show revenue growth that restores the earlier pace, the forward EPS estimates would rise and the anchored target would follow. A sustained rise in margins above 15 percent without further price increases would also count, because it would show that the business is earning its margin rather than buying it. Either would change the anchor, and the review would be repeated on the new numbers.

The fourth trigger is a change in the momentum picture. The gate requires either a stock close to support or a stock breaking out of a base with volume. If the stock consolidates for several weeks, forms a new base and then expands volume on a breakout above its pivot, Test B could pass. That would be a different kind of entry, with a fresh stop, and the arithmetic would be done again from the new base.

The last results board meeting was held on 20 July 2026.[29] The same timing appeared last year, when the September-quarter results were approved on 6 November 2025.[30] The reader should watch three dates. The first is the next results release, which the aggregator estimates for 3 November 2026 and which the listing rules require within 45 days of the quarter end, by 14 November 2026.[2][31] The second is management's full-year guidance, which the company said would come after September-end. The third is any update to the analyst estimates. Each could move one of the inputs, and each would be a reason to repeat the review. Until one of them changes the arithmetic, the verdict stands.
