Start here

This report is a research dossier for a beginner. It explains one listed company, Voltamp Transformers, and asks one practical question: should a Tactical momentum portfolio hold capital in this stock today? It is not a recommendation to buy or sell. It is also not a full valuation of the business, and it does not predict the share price. It shows what the numbers say, which rules were applied, and where the evidence is weak. Read the decision box first, then the Layer 1 gate table, then the sections that explain each gate.

The report uses a framework with layers. Layer 1 is a set of pass-or-fail gates on quality, cash, price trend and expected return. A company that fails Layer 1 is not scored further. That is why Layers 2 and 3 are not scored here. Each gate has a floor, which is the minimum that passes, and a company value, which is what the sources show.

Return on capital employed, or ROCE, asks how much operating profit a business earns on the money tied up in it. Think of it as the profit a shopkeeper makes for every rupee locked into shelves and stock. A floor of 15% is the minimum used here.

Cash from operations divided by profit after tax, or CFO/PAT, asks whether reported profit arrives as cash. It is like checking whether a salary is actually paid into the bank, not just promised on paper. A ratio of 0.70 or more is the floor used here.

Debt-to-equity compares borrowed money with the owners' net worth. It is like comparing a household's loan balance with the value of the house it owns. A ratio below 0.70 passes.

Stage 1 to Stage 4 describe where a share sits in its price cycle. Stage 1 is a quiet base, Stage 2 is a markup with buyers in control, Stage 3 is a topping or pullback, and Stage 4 is a breakdown. It is like the life of a house sale: listing, bidding war, slowing offers, and then a price fall.

A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, such as 20, 50 or 200. It is like a rolling average of your last few class test marks, smoothing out daily noise. A price below its 50-day average suggests recent buyers are losing money.

The relative strength index, or RSI, runs from 0 to 100 and measures the balance of recent gains and losses. It is like a thermometer for buying pressure. A reading near 45 is neutral, not extreme.

The average true range, or ATR, measures a typical daily price swing over 14 days. It is like the usual gap between a day's high and low temperature. Stops and tests are set in ATR units so that they scale with how much the stock normally moves.

Test A and Test B are the momentum gate's two entrance exams. Test A asks whether the trend is intact. Test B asks whether the timing is right for a buy, such as a breakout on heavy volume. Both must pass for the stock to be allocated.

Asymmetry compares the possible gain with the possible loss. It is like a bet that pays 3.5 units for every 1 unit risked. The floor is 3.5 for the Tactical bucket and 3.0 for the Core bucket.

A stop-loss is a price level at which you exit to limit the loss, like a fire exit set before you enter a building. The Tactical stop here is set below the swing low and below the moving averages, minus half an ATR.

An anchored target is a price goal built from a measurable base, such as forward earnings per share multiplied by a typical price-to-earnings multiple. It is not a guess. An unanchored analyst target does not count toward the gate.

What the company does

Voltamp Transformers makes the transformers that step electricity voltage up or down between the grid, substations and factories. In plain words, it builds the large metal units that sit in substations and industrial plants. Its main product is oil-filled power and distribution transformers up to 120 MVA. The company also makes dry-type transformers up to 10 MVA, compact substations up to 2.5 MVA, and 33 kV class and 12 kV ring main units. [1]

Products made up 96% of FY26 business, according to the segment note that Screener prints. The company sold 16,514 MVA in FY26, up from 15,460 MVA in FY25. [1] Transformers are sold by capacity, so MVA is the closest thing to a unit count for this business. A rise in MVA shows more equipment shipped, even if prices move.

The forward business sits in the order book. At the start of FY27 the company held a backlog of ₹1,200 crore, equal to 10,270 MVA. Since April 2026 it has won fresh orders of ₹1,142 crore for 7,775 MVA. Together these give revenue visibility of about ₹2,342 crore, or 18,045 MVA. [10] That visibility equals about 1.09 times FY26 sales of ₹2,154 crore. [1] It is a useful buffer, but orders still have to be built, shipped and paid for.

Capacity is the second story. Commercial production started on 7 October 2026 at a new Unit-III power transformer plant near Jarod, Vadodara. [1] The new Extra High Voltage factory is built, but its start was deferred by about two months because imported equipment deliveries were late. [12] A separate ₹90 crore project, funded from internal accruals, is planned to add 2,300 MVA a year of dry-type capacity near Vadodara. [12] [14]

The company reports on a standalone basis. Screener's consolidated page for Voltamp carries the label "Standalone data" next to its operating figures and shows no separate consolidated tables. [16] The business is therefore best read as one manufacturing company. The customer split was not verified from a source read in this run, so no percentage is given here.

The weaknesses are in cash conversion, margins and execution. A reader should keep both in view. A company can be a good business and still be a poor buy at a given price, and the momentum gate is designed to test the price, not only the business.

A simple way to read the balance is to ask what would have to be true for the stock to work. The business needs to keep its returns above the floor and keep borrowing near zero. It then needs to turn its growing order book into cash at a better pace than it did in FY26. The first two conditions are already met. The third is not met yet. Debt-to-equity compares borrowed money with the owners' net worth. A ratio near zero means the company is funded almost entirely by its own shareholders. Asymmetry and the anchored target

Asymmetry compares the upside to the target with the downside to the stop. The formula is the base target minus the close, divided by the close minus the stop. The Tactical floor is 3.5. The target must be anchored: forward earnings per share multiplied by a three-year median P/E, or a technical measured move. An unverifiable target fails. Here the anchored base uses FY27 consensus earnings of 331.45 [5] and the three-year median P/E of 28.77. [3] That gives a base target of ₹9,535.82. The stop under the Tactical rule is ₹10,005.15, which leaves ₹378.85 of risk per share below the close.

The arithmetic gives a base asymmetry of -2.24, because the target sits 8.17% below the close. The bull target, built on FY28 consensus earnings of 397.90 with the same median multiple, is ₹11,447.58. That gives an asymmetry of 2.81, still below the 3.5 floor. To pass, the base target would need to reach about ₹11,710, which would require a P/E of about 35.3 on FY27 earnings, well above the company's own three-year median. The gate therefore fails. The target is anchored, so the failure is not a data gap. It is a result.

Layers 2 and 3: not scored

Layers 2 and 3 are the parts of the framework that score the company's operating scorecard and its entry posture. Layer 2 is a set of key performance indicators that measures how well the business is run, such as margins, growth, capital use and cash quality. Layer 3 sets the entry posture, meaning the timing and sizing of a position once the company and the price are approved. These layers only apply to a company that passes Layer 1. The reason is simple. A company that fails the first filter should not be given a quality score that could be read as an endorsement, and an entry posture would imply a buy that the rules forbid.

Voltamp fails Layer 1 on three gates, so both layers are skipped. No Layer 2 score and no Layer 3 posture is given in this dossier. This is not a judgement that the operating scorecard is poor. Some Layer 2 measures would look reasonable: the ROCE is strong, the debt is almost nil, and the order book is large. Other measures would look weaker, including the cash conversion, the margin trend and the execution delay. Scoring the company on those measures now would invent a number the framework has not earned, so none is shown. Had Layer 1 passed, the scorecard would have been the next step, and the entry posture would have depended on a confirmed breakout on heavy volume. Neither step applies today. The reader should treat the absence of Layer 2 and Layer 3 as the framework working as designed.

Fatal flaws: the red-team case

A red-team case is the strongest honest argument against the company, written as if the reader is trying to find the flaws. It does not repeat the gate failures in a different form. It looks for risks that a reader could miss if they only saw the headline numbers. The points below are the ones that would change an investor's mind most. Each has evidence and a consequence, and the first three would stop a buy even if the gates were passed.

Each flaw in the list is written to be tested. Where a flaw has a number, the number comes from a source in the research record, and the citation sits beside it. Where the evidence is thin, the text says so rather than filling the gap. The flaws run from the most serious for the investment case to the least. A reader who stops after the first two still has the core picture, which is that the business is sound but the cash and the price are not yet supportive. Each flaw is also tied to a consequence for the decision, so the reader can see why a flaw matters and not just that it exists. The list is not exhaustive. It covers the risks the sources made visible, and it leaves out speculation about events that no source reports.

Flaw one: the business is not turning profit into cash

The most serious flaw is that reported profit has not been arriving as cash. Profit after tax was ₹305.38 crore in FY26, yet cash from operations was only ₹139.71 crore. That is a ratio of 0.46, the lowest of the four years shown. Over three years the ratio is 0.61. [2] In a manufacturing company, a gap like this usually means money is tied up in receivables, stock or work in progress. The company has just finished a new plant and is planning another, and both consume cash before they earn it. The consequence is that the profit figures overstate the cash available to shareholders. A reader who values the stock on earnings alone would be paying for cash that has not yet appeared, and the framework's cash gate exists to catch that exact gap. The same logic applies to any year in which profit is booked on sales that are not yet collected.

Flaw two: the price already pays a premium to its own history

The second flaw is valuation against the company's own past. The stock trades at 33.0 times current earnings. [1] Its three-year median year-end P/E is 28.77, and its fiscal year-end values ranged from 21.95 to 32.39 across FY24 to FY26. [3] The current multiple is therefore above the middle of its recent range. If earnings growth slows, the multiple has less support from growth and more room to fall back toward its median. Consensus expects FY27 earnings to rise about 9.8%, which is a fair pace but not a surge. [5] The consequence is that a small disappointment in earnings could push the multiple down, and the share price would fall faster than earnings did. A multiple that is already above its median leaves little room for error in the next two results.

Flaw three: margins are falling while sales grow

The third flaw is margin compression. Sales grew about 11% in FY26, yet profit fell about 6%. The operating margin dropped to 16% from 19% in FY25. [1] The June 2026 quarter shows the same pattern, with the EBITDA margin at 14.77% against 17.15% a year earlier, as input costs rose. [12] Growth that comes with thinner margins is worth less per rupee of sales, and the same rupee of sales now carries less profit than it did a year ago. The consequence is that the company must keep winning volume just to hold profit steady, and any slip in pricing or input costs would hit earnings directly. The sector growth story does not remove this problem. A rising tide lifts the peers too, and the company still has to keep its own margins.

Flaw four: execution risk on the new plants

The fourth flaw is that the growth plan depends on factories that are not yet running at full speed. The new EHV factory is built, but its start slipped about two months because imported equipment was delayed. [12] A second source reports the same delay. [24] The dry-type plant is still at the approval and construction stage, and the approval date that the company expected, September 2026, has passed without a confirmation read in this run. The consequence is that the order book of ₹2,342 crore may take longer to convert into shipments and cash than the market expects. Delays also raise the cost of capital tied up in unfinished factories, because the money is spent before the plant earns anything. The order book is a promise until the plant ships the equipment.

Flaw five: the stock is in a pullback with no volume behind it

The fifth flaw is technical. The stock is a Stage 3 pullback below its rising 50-day average, and the volume multiple is only 0.7 in the ledger. Quiet volume during a pullback can mean the sellers have run out, or it can mean the buyers have stepped back. The ledger alone cannot tell which. The consequence is that the stock could drift lower toward its swing low at ₹10,161.00 and then toward the stop at ₹10,005.15 before a clear base forms. A position entered now would sit inside that zone of uncertainty, which is exactly what the Tactical rule is designed to avoid. Waiting for a clean base, even at a higher entry price, keeps the stop distance and the risk within the framework's limits.

Glossary

Market capitalisation is the total value of a company's shares at the current price. A figure of ₹10,451 crore means the shares together are worth that much. Price-to-earnings, or P/E, is the share price divided by earnings per share. It tells you how many rupees you pay for each rupee of annual earnings. Earnings per share is net profit divided by the number of shares. Consensus is the average of analysts' published forecasts, and S&P Global collects the one used here. Return on equity, or ROE, is profit divided by shareholders' net worth. Operating profit margin, or OPM, is operating profit as a share of sales. EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, and the EBITDA margin is that figure as a share of sales. MVA stands for mega volt-ampere, the unit of transformer capacity. An EHV factory makes Extra High Voltage equipment, usually 220 kV and above. CARE AA is a long-term credit rating meaning very high safety for debt, and Stable means the outlook is unchanged. A promoter pledge is shares owned by the founders that are offered as security for a loan. Asymmetry is the ratio of expected upside to the risk taken, measured against the stop. An anchored target is one built from earnings and a multiple, not from opinion. The momentum gate is the code's test of trend and timing, with Test A for trend and Test B for timing. NO_MOMENTUM is its verdict when both tests fail. Layer 1 is the first set of pass-or-fail gates. A REJECTED verdict means the stock is not allocated.

Method and limits

The method has three steps. First, the ledger and the momentum gate file were read for the 13 October 2026 close, and every technical number was taken from the ledger without change. Second, the Layer 1 gates were researched from primary and secondary sources, with each value tied to a quoted passage and an as-of date. Third, the arithmetic for the stop, the targets and the asymmetry was done from those values using the framework's formulas. The research record and the cache were checked to confirm that each quoted passage appears in the fetched page text. The research record holds 25 distinct source pages. Company disclosures are represented by the company's investor desk and by the exchange announcements printed on Screener.

The sources used, with their as-of dates, are these. Screener's standalone company page gives ROCE, borrowings, reserves, the shareholding table and the exchange announcements, as of 31 March 2026 for the annual figures and 13 October 2026 for the market data. [1] EtMoney gives the annual cash flow and profit series, as of 31 March 2026, and the 3:31 pm price. [2] The Q1 FY27 results come from investywise, Univest, Power Peak Digest, Angel One, Sahi and ScanX, as of 30 June 2026 and 31 July 2026. [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] The consensus figures come from StockAnalysis and Trendlyne, as of 13 October 2026. [5] [7] The shareholding pledge comes from Trendlyne, as of 30 June 2026. [8] The peer data comes from Screener's pages for Transformers and Rectifiers, GE Vernova T&D India and Hitachi Energy India, all as of 13 October 2026. [17] [18] [19] The sector context comes from the Ministry of Power reply of 30 July 2026, reproduced by a trade site, as of 30 June and 30 July 2026. [20]

The limits are these. The company's own audited PDFs could not be read as text, so the audited figures are taken from aggregators that repeat them. Screener was rate-limited for part of the run, so its figures were read after a cooling period and are shown as printed. The CARE rating PDF and the earnings call transcript were not readable. Two data sources are AI-assisted summaries and are used only where a second source confirms the point. Every date in this dossier is the as-of date of the figure, not the date of this report, and none of them has been changed.
