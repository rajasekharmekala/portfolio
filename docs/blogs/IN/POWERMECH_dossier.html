1. What the company does

Power Mech Projects Limited was incorporated in 1999 and is based in Hyderabad. Its founder, Sajja Kishore Babu, is chairman and managing director[14]. The company builds and maintains power plants. It erects boilers, turbines and generators and the plant around them, pours the civil foundations, and then runs the plant for the owner under long maintenance contracts. It has also moved into railway depots, water pipelines, roads, and coal mining as a contractor for state-owned mines[14][4]. Management says it has erected more than 80 gigawatts of capacity and handles the upkeep of more than 42 gigawatts of plant, which are the company's own figures[5].

Who pays, and for what, is easiest to see in the order book, which is the signed work still to be done. At 30 June 2026 the headline total was ₹55,398 crore, but ₹39,330 crore of that is two coal mine contracts that run for 25 to 26 years[4][5]. The part that turns into revenue over the next two to three years is the order book without the mines, at ₹16,229 crore[3]. Its split is civil works ₹8,480 crore, plant maintenance ₹3,322 crore, industrial engineering and construction ₹2,375 crore and ₹1,891 crore (two lines in the filing), and the rest is mining[4]. Customers named on the August 2026 call were BHEL with about ₹5,300 crore of orders, Adani with about ₹2,400 crore across 14 projects, and Vedanta with about ₹1,551 crore across six projects[3]. BHEL is a state-owned equipment maker. Adani and Vedanta are private groups.

Revenue in the June 2026 quarter was ₹1,624 crore, and it came from five places. Civil work gave ₹796 crore, up 28 percent on a year earlier. Plant maintenance gave ₹431 crore, up 8 percent. Erection and commissioning gave ₹217 crore, down 13 percent, because an accident at a Vedanta plant stopped work for three to four months. Mining gave ₹84 crore, up from a small base — a flat consolidation floor —, and industrial EPC gave ₹96 crore[3]. Power plants were 54 percent of revenue and other sectors 46 percent. Ninety-six percent of revenue was earned in India[3].

The company earns its money by charging for work done, and most of each rupee of revenue goes straight back out. In the year to March 2026 the cost of executing contracts was ₹3,612 crore on ₹6,062 crore of revenue, about 60 percent[4]. Management says plant maintenance earns about 18 percent at the operating level, the core construction business earns 10 to 11 percent, and the mining contracts are meant to earn 15 to 16 percent at one mine and about 25 percent at the second once they reach full output[3][5]. That mix is why management wants a bigger share of maintenance and mining, and it is also why the quarter-to-quarter margin matters so much to the stock.

Payment terms explain much of the balance sheet. BHEL pays within 30 days of a certified bill. Adani and other private customers pay 70 percent when the bill is submitted and the rest before month end. The usual 5 percent retention is replaced by a bank guarantee, so the company receives the full running bill[3]. The price of that arrangement is large guarantee lines: bank guarantees issued for the parent alone were ₹2,807.58 crore at March 2026, up from ₹1,661.46 crore a year earlier[13]. A guarantee is a bank's promise to pay the customer if the contractor fails, so it is a contingent claim and not a debt, but it uses up credit lines.

Who owns and runs the company

The promoter group, led by the founder's family, held 58.42 percent of the shares at September 2026, and none of it was pledged as security for loans[17]. The stake was 64.17 percent in March 2023 and 60.38 percent in March 2024[1]. The company also raised ₹350 crore in a placement to institutions, whose use of proceeds a rating agency monitored in 2024[22]. The filings we read do not split the fall between that placement and other causes, and they do not explain the drop from 60.38 to 58.29 percent between March and June 2024, so we give no reason. In December 2024 the family moved shares among its own members and reported no encumbrance on the 58.28 percent total[21].

Rohit Sajja is an executive director who leads overseas business and strategy, and Nani Aravind is the chief financial officer[4]. The auditor is Brahmayya and Co, whose report on the March 2026 results carried an unmodified opinion[13][16]. The managing director and a second director have personally guaranteed the company's bank loans[13]. That is common in family-run contractors and means the lenders look to the family as well as the company.

The two mines and the guarantee the company gave in October 2026

A mine developer and operator contract, called MDO here, pays the company a fixed fee per tonne to open a mine and dig coal for the mine owner. The first contract, for the KBP mine of Central Coalfields, is worth ₹9,294 crore over 25 years at about 16 percent operating margin at full output. The second, for the Tasra mine of Steel Authority of India, is worth ₹30,300 crore over 26 years at about 25 percent[5]. A coal washery, the plant that cleans the coal, is due to be commissioned in December 2026 and the higher revenue comes after it[5]. On 15 October 2026 the company issued a ₹469 crore corporate guarantee for the financing of the subsidiary that holds the Tasra mine[1]. The CARE rating agency calls the parent's duty to fund these projects an added credit risk[14].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business rests on scale, a long record, and a lender view that has improved. The company has been in business for 27 years and built the boiler, turbine and generator packages of the first two ultra mega power projects, Mundra and Sasan, and 19 other supercritical plants, according to CARE[14]. CARE reaffirmed its A+ stable rating in August 2026 and raised the bank limits it rates from ₹2,600 crore to ₹2,900 crore; in September 2023 the rating had been one notch lower, at A with a positive outlook[14][15]. Debt is modest: total debt was 1.59 times operating profit before interest and depreciation, interest cover was 6.09 times, and CARE expects debt to stay under 2 times[14].

The case against it rests on thin construction margins, heavy working capital and new, unproven mining projects. CARE says the business is working-capital intensive: current assets stood at 268 days of sales in the year to March 2026, collection took 165 days, and the cause was advances paid to subcontractors and for resettlement at the mines, plus unbilled work waiting for milestones[14]. It also says execution risk is significant for the mining, road and solar projects the company now builds as a developer, because the company is new to coal mining and must fund part of each project[14].

A third weakness is that the headline order book flatters. Excluding the two mine contracts, the order book fell from ₹17,327 crore in December 2025 to ₹15,899 crore in March 2026 and the company changed its description of the visibility from 3-plus years to 2-plus years. It stood at ₹16,229 crore in June 2026 and ₹17,317 crore in the September 2026 presentation[7][6][3][5]. Management's own estimate of its share of the Indian power maintenance market moved from about 20 percent in the May 2026 deck to about 16 percent in the August 2026 deck, and the filings do not explain the change[6][4].

Evidence for the moat: maintenance scale and an execution record

A moat is something that protects profit from competitors, as a moat protects a castle. For this company the moat is a large, trained workforce and a record of delivering big plants on time. About 50,000 people work for the group, including about 4,000 engineers and supervisors, and 80 to 90 percent of the construction equipment is owned and not hired[3]. Maintenance contracts renew, which gives repeat revenue: the company says it keeps its bid success rate in power at 60 to 65 percent[3]. The honest limit is that large rivals such as L&T and BHEL also build plants, and the filings do not give the company's share of any market except its own maintenance estimate. The moat is therefore real but is shown mostly by its ratings and order wins, not by pricing power.

Cost pass-through: what the margin shows

Pass-through means the company charges its customers for changes in the cost of steel, gas and diesel. Most contracts carry price-variation clauses, but management says they sometimes pay out a quarter later, and some costs are never fully recovered[3]. The evidence is mixed. The operating margin on the company's own definition fell from 13.98 percent in the June 2025 quarter to 11.17 percent in March 2026 and 10.78 percent in June 2026[4]. Management names three causes: costlier materials after the Middle East conflict, a royalty-sharing order that cut the margin on the river-dredging royalty-checking contract (KRBM) from about 14 percent to about 10 percent, and the heavy stripping of waste rock as the KBP mine opened new coal seams[3]. Some of this should fade as mine output rises, but the first cause depends on commodity prices that the company does not control.

The weak spots: working capital, guarantees and a changing business mix

The business is changing from a pure contractor into a contractor that also owns mining and road projects. That is where the weak spots sit. The parent has given guarantees for its subsidiaries of ₹128.90 crore for Tasra and ₹79.99 crore for the Deoghar ring road at March 2026, and a new ₹469 crore guarantee in October 2026[13][1]. CARE expects term debt to rise in the year to March 2027 and names GCA days above 250, which are the days of sales tied up in current assets, on a sustained basis as a trigger for a downgrade[14]. On the plant side, management says erection capacity is close to its limit at 14 to 15 large boilers at once, so that business can grow only 5 to 7 percent a year[3].

Finally, the stock has had no help from owners. The promoter stake fell by about 5.6 percentage points over three years and the number of shareholders fell by 12.5 percent in the latest quarter, from 79,426 to 69,526[1][28]. We do not draw a conclusion from the share-count change alone. Chapter 2B turns this into five scored measures.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 asks why the estimates might be wrong. Each estimate has a weakest assumption, and the red-team case that follows is the strongest honest argument against our central case. We list the evidence that would show each problem and the date by which it will be known.

Weakest assumption in the EPS estimate: that the margin slide stops in the second quarter

Our central case needs the operating margin to stop falling after 10.3 percent in Q1. Three of the causes are not in the company's control. Material and diesel costs followed the Middle East conflict, and price-variation clauses pay a quarter later[3]. The river-dredging royalty-checking contract (KRBM) lost about four points of margin because the state now keeps half of the penalty money[3]. The KBP mine's stripping cost depends on where the coal seams are. If these persist, FY27 EPS is nearer ₹105 than ₹119. With FY28 unchanged at ₹146 the 12-month EPS is about ₹126, and at 20.1 times the value is about ₹2,530; a value near ₹1,900 would also need the multiple to fall to about 15 times (₹126 × 15.1). The company's own FY27 plan needs the margin to reach about 13 percent in the second half; we assume 11 to 11.5 percent. The 13 percent figure is our arithmetic: ₹912 crore at 12.5 percent of ₹7,300 crore, less ₹176 crore earned in Q1, over the remaining ₹5,676 crore of revenue.

Weakest assumption in the cash view: that customer advances do not reverse

FY26 cash from operations of ₹387 crore leaned on ₹284 crore of extra customer advances. Advances are repaid as work is done, so if new orders slow, cash falls even when profit holds. Read with the 268 days of current assets, the red-team case is that cash conversion goes back to the FY25 level of near zero[13][14]. On that path the three-year ratio would fall from 0.59 toward 0.28 and rating agency triggers would be tested. We do not forecast this. The Chapter 3 stress is a historical what-if on the FY24 to FY26 figures, not a forecast of the future rolling ratio and not a claim about solvency. The ratio of 0.59 passes the 0.25 line but misses the 0.70 standard, and CARE's current-asset days are above its 250 trigger and have risen for three years. The half-year cash flow, the advances and the current-asset days are therefore re-checked as separate conditions in Trigger 1. The evidence that would show the problem is a negative half-year cash flow in the Q2 results, a fall in advances received, or gross current-asset days above 250 in the FY27 annual report. Management says it is working on collections, milestone certification and mobilisation advances[3], so the intention is the opposite of our red-team case. The cost to the estimate is a lower free-cash reading and possibly a higher borrowing bill, which would pull EPS below our central ₹119.

Weakest assumption in the target: that the multiple recovers toward 20 times

The central value assumes the shares re-rate from 17.2 to 20.1 times earnings. The market is in a downtrend and the benchmark had its worst month since March in September[23]. A de-rating to 14.5 times is inside our poor case. The three-year median of 26.8 times was set while earnings were growing 25 percent a year, and growth is now slower. If the median is really 22 times, the central value falls 18 percent. The evidence that would show this assumption wrong is the price itself: a close under ₹2,068.65, a Stage 4 reading, or a Q2 print under the margin line. Peers trade between 17 and 35 times earnings on Screener's page, so a 20-times multiple is inside the range, but Power Mech has historically earned a premium only when its profit growth was above 20 percent a year[27]. The cost of being wrong is large because the target is the product of two estimates, earnings and a multiple, and both can miss together in a falling market.

Red-team case against owning the stock

The strongest bear case has four parts. The margin keeps falling, because maintenance and mining were only about 32 percent of June-quarter revenue (₹515 crore of ₹1,624 crore) and construction is competitive. Working capital stays above 250 days, which CARE lists as a downgrade trigger[14]. The company funds more mines, a road project and a ₹469 crore guarantee for a subsidiary while term debt rises[1][14]. And the stock, already down 29 percent, enters Stage 4 in early November. Against that, the debt is moderate, the rating is A+, no promoter shares are pledged, and the order book is 2.5 times annual revenue. The bear case is therefore about cash timing and price trend, not about solvency.
