Terms used in this report

This chapter explains each technical and financial word once, with the plain meaning first. Later chapters assume the reader has read it here.

ROCE stands for return on capital employed. It is the profit a company earns before tax and interest, divided by the money tied up in the business. A figure above 15 percent means the business can grow without constant new money.

Debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' equity. A value near zero means little borrowed money. The framework sets a limit of 0.70: the ratio must stay below it.

Cash conversion here means three-year operating cash flow divided by three-year profit after tax. It shows whether reported profit arrives as cash. The framework floor is 0.70. A ratio below that means profit is running ahead of cash, which is a warning sign in a contractor that funds large balances of receivables and inventory.

Stage 2 is the markup phase of the four-phase cycle. The price is above a rising 50-day average and a rising 200-day average. Stage 3 is the topping phase, where the price has lost its upward pull but has not yet broken the long-term trend. Stage 4 is the markdown phase.

50-day and 200-day averages are the average closing prices over about two months and about a year. A rising line means buyers have had control over that period.

ATR stands for average true range, the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions. The framework measures distances from support in units of ATR, so that volatile and calm stocks are measured on the same scale.

Momentum gate is a set of code-run tests. Test A asks whether the stock is in a healthy uptrend with relative strength. Test B asks whether it is entering an uptrend from a consolidation. Failing both means no allocation, whatever the quality of the business.

Asymmetry is the planned gain to the target divided by the planned loss to the stop. The Tactical floor is 3.5 and the Core floor is 3.0.

Forward EPS is the analyst-consensus earnings per share for the next financial year. Three-year median P/E is the middle value of the P/E ratio at each of the last three year-ends.

RSI stands for relative strength index. It is a 14-day speed reading from 0 to 100. Readings above 70 mean the stock has run hot, and readings below 30 mean it has been sold hard.

EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is an operating profit measure that sets aside financing and accounting charges. Its margin is EBITDA divided by sales.

Operating margin is operating profit divided by sales, shown as a percentage. It shows how much of each rupee of sales is left after the cost of running the business.

Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus the money spent on equipment and other long-term assets. It is the cash left over after the business has kept up and grown its assets.

Working capital is the money tied up in receivables and inventory, net of what the company owes its suppliers. Rising working capital means cash is taking longer to come back from customers.

Price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by the profit per share over the last year. A P/E of 20 means investors pay twenty rupees for each rupee of annual profit.

Base is a sideways range that a stock builds after a fall, usually lasting several weeks. The low point of the range is the base low. A pivot is the price level just above the top of the base, where a move out of the range would begin.

Breakout means a close above the pivot on heavy volume, which signals that the base has resolved upward. A retest is a return to the broken level that holds, which the framework accepts as an alternative entry.

Guidance is what management says it expects to achieve in the coming year. It is a company statement, not an audited figure, so the record treats it as a claim to be tested.

Measured move is an estimate of the next target, equal to the height of a base added to the breakout level. The framework accepts it as an alternative anchor to forward earnings.

Consensus estimate is the average of analyst forecasts for a future year, collected by data providers. It is useful, but the company does not verify it.

What the company does and how it earns

The company is an engineering and construction contractor. Its core work is erection, testing and commissioning of boilers, turbines and generators, plus balance-of-plant work, civil works and operation and maintenance of power plants [1] [2]. Its work is tied to the building of new power plants and to the running of existing ones. The business also has a mining arm, with contracts counted separately in the order book as MDO projects [4].

In the year to March 2026 the company reported consolidated sales of ₹6,062 crore [1]. Almost all of this was earned at home. Outside India contributed ₹2,524 million, or about 4 percent of the total of ₹60,616 million [5]. The company's registered office is in Hyderabad [6].

How it earns: a contractor is paid as work is certified against a fixed contract price. Margins are thin: the consolidated operating margin in the year to March 2026 was 11 percent [1].

Why this matters to a reader: a contractor with a large order book has years of visible revenue, but the revenue arrives with thin margins and heavy working capital. A large share of profit can sit in unpaid receivables and in inventory. That is why the cash test in the framework matters more here than it would for a company that sells a product for cash.

The framework and the verdict rule

Every candidate passes through three layers in order. Layer 1 holds the hard gates: financial health, technical posture, the momentum gate and the asymmetry floor. If any Layer 1 gate fails, the verdict is REJECTED, the gate status is written as FAIL with the reason, and Layers 2 and 3 are not evaluated.

Layer 2 scores three to five company-specific operating measures as pass, watch or fail. Layer 3 maps the distance from the nearest support, in ATR, onto a single entry ladder. It decides whether the entry is immediate, on a dip, or should wait. Layers 2 and 3 only run if Layer 1 passes.

The momentum gate result comes from code, not from judgement. The verdict for this run therefore rests on three things that can be checked: the ledger close and averages, the momentum gate output, and the cached research record for the financial figures. No composite score is used.

Asymmetry and target anchoring

Asymmetry asks how much the stock could gain to its target compared with how much it could lose to its stop. The framework computes it as the target minus the close, divided by the close minus the stop. The target must be anchored to a forward EPS times a three-year median P/E, or to a measured move. An arbitrary target is not accepted.

The stop comes from the Tactical rule: the lowest of the 20-day swing low and the 20-day and 50-day averages, minus half an ATR. The 20-day swing low of ₹2,278.80 is the lowest of the three levels, which sit at ₹2,278.80, ₹2,413.25 and ₹2,456.30. The stop is therefore ₹2,278.80 minus ₹39.52, which is ₹2,239.29 (ledger). The risk per share is therefore ₹203.71.

The forward EPS is ₹141.4 for FY2027, an analyst-consensus estimate from MarketScreener [9]. The historical P/E at each year-end was 30.9 times in FY2024, 26.3 times in FY2025 and 15.1 times in FY2026 [9]. The middle of those three is 26.3 times. The anchored target is therefore ₹141.4 times 26.3, which is ₹3,718.82, or about ₹3,719. The upside to that target is ₹1,275.82, and the asymmetry is 6.26. That passes the Tactical floor of 3.5.

Fatal-flaw red team

The red-team step asks what would make this verdict wrong, and what would make a buy a mistake even if the gates passed. Three flaws stand out.

Flaw one: the business is good, the trend is not. A reader might say the order book of ₹55,398 crore makes the stock a safe buy on a pullback. The framework does not allow that. A pullback to support is only a buy once a base forms and the momentum gate passes. The stock is below a falling 50-day average, and under the rule any entry must wait for a base to form and a breakout to be confirmed (ledger).

Flaw two: profit is not cash. The company reported profit after tax of ₹1,008 crore over FY2024 to FY2026 [1]. Operating cash flow over the same period was ₹593 crore [1] [3]. Working-capital days are high, and debtor days were 93 at March 2026 [1]. If the receivables are slow to collect, the reported profit could be overstated for a period. The cash gate exists for this reason.

Flaw three: a single-window valuation. The asymmetry passes only on the three-year median P/E and on forward EPS. On FY2026 earnings the anchored asymmetry is 2.87, which fails the floor [9]. On a five-year window it fails outright. A stock that only passes its valuation test on one window is not a clean buy, even before its technical test is considered.

The customer concentration figure was looked for and not found. Three searches returned no client split, and the company's annual report page does not show one in the cached text [21]. The red team therefore cannot test how much of the order book sits with a few public-sector or private-sector clients. A future run should check the annual report notes directly.
