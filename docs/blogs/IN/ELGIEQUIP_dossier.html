Terms used in this report

This chapter explains each technical and financial word once, with the plain meaning first. Later chapters assume the reader has read it here, so the terms are used the same way throughout.

ROCE stands for return on capital employed. It is the profit a company earns before tax and interest, divided by the money it has tied up in the business, so a 22 percent figure means each rupee of capital earns about 22 paise a year before tax. We use it as the first financial test, and the framework asks for at least 15 percent.

EPS stands for earnings per share, which is the company's profit after tax divided by the number of shares. A forward EPS is an estimate of that figure for a future year, usually taken from the average of analysts' forecasts, which analysts call the consensus estimate, meaning the middle view of the professional forecasts.

RSI stands for relative strength index. It is a 14-day speed reading from 0 to 100 that rises when recent gains outweigh recent losses. Readings above 70 mean the stock has run hot, and readings below 30 mean it has been sold hard.

ATR stands for average true range, the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions. We use it as a ruler: a stop or a support distance is counted in units of ATR, so a volatile stock and a calm stock are measured on the same scale.

A 50-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 50 sessions. It is a trend line: when the price sits above it and the line itself is rising, buyers are in control. A 200-day moving average works the same way over about ten months and marks the longer trend.

Stage 2 is the rising phase in the four-stage cycle, when the price is above a rising long-term average and the trend is intact. Stage 4 is the falling phase, when the price has broken down below a falling average. Stage 3, the phase used for this stock, is the topping or pullback stage that sits between them, where the uptrend is losing strength.

A base is a sideways pause after a run-up, where the price trades in a narrow range and volume dries up. The pivot is the price level just above that range, and a breakout is a close above the pivot on heavy volume, which is the signal that a new advance may start.

A stop loss is a price level at which a position is sold to cap the loss. Here it is set below the nearest structural support, plus a buffer of one ATR. The asymmetry ratio is the distance from today's price to the target, divided by the distance from today's price to the stop. It is the framework's measure of reward-to-risk, and it sets a floor for each strategy.

Debt-to-equity is borrowed money divided by shareholders' funds. A figure of 0.24 means the company owes 24 paise of debt for each rupee of owners' capital. An operating margin is the profit from running the business before interest and tax, shown as a share of sales. The price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E, is the share price divided by earnings per share, so it tells us how many years of current profit the price represents.

EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is operating profit before the accounting charges for wear on plant and for borrowing, so it is a rough measure of cash earnings from the core business. A measured move is a price target found by adding the height of a chart pattern to the level where it breaks out, which gives a chart-based goal rather than a valuation-based one.

Cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates from its trading, before money spent on new plants. We compare it with reported profit over three years, because profit can be inflated by accounting while cash is harder to fake. Guidance is the outlook management gives for the coming quarter or year, and capital expenditure, or capex, is money spent on plant and equipment that will earn returns over many years.

What the company does and how it earns

Elgi makes air compressors, which are machines that pressurise air for factory tools, production lines and processes. Its largest business is that equipment, and it also sells the spare parts, service and maintenance that keep those machines running. The company says it is the sixth largest compressor maker in the world and the second largest in India [1]. It reports that it sells in more than 120 countries and manufactures in three locations [24].

The equipment business is the larger of two segments. In fiscal 2026, which ended in March 2026, air compressors brought in ₹36,134 million and automotive equipment brought in ₹3,386 million, against a reported total revenue of ₹39,507 million [8]. The two segments add to ₹39,520 million, which is ₹13 million more than the total. We have not found an explanation for that gap in the sources, so the segment figures are shown as published. The split matters because the quality of the revenue matters: the company says aftermarket work, which means parts and service on the installed machines, can carry margins about six times higher than selling a new machine [22].

The product line that matters most for the growth story is the AB series, an oil-free screw compressor that the company says delivers efficiency, reliability and air quality benefits [16]. Its North American channel runs through independent distributors. One of them, Pattons, says it is an authorised ELGi distributor for the Southeast United States [26], and its installer listing names Charlotte, North Carolina as the headquarters of that business [25].

The latest reported quarter is June 2026. Consolidated revenue was ₹10,622 million, which is ₹1,062 crore, up 22.56 percent on a year earlier, and net profit was ₹1,033 million, up 20.68 percent [17]. Management said total revenue grew 23 percent, of which 7 points came from exchange rates and 16 points were organic, meaning from volume and price rather than currency [19]. Growth was reported in India (+28 percent), North America (+37 percent), Europe (+21 percent) and Australia (+17 percent) [19]. These are the latest reported figures. They are older than our 60-day freshness rule, so the run notes at the end list them as an exception.

What this means for an owner: the business is growing at a healthy pace and the quarter was strong on the top line. The question for the trend test is not whether sales are rising, but whether the share price has priced that in. The rest of this report answers that question with the price data.

Ownership is concentrated but shifting. The promoters held 31.19 percent of the shares at June 2026, unchanged in every quarter shown since September 2023 [1]. Foreign institutions held 21.90 percent at June 2026, down from 29.54 percent in March 2024, while domestic institutions rose to 10.12 percent [1]. The shareholding data shows that shift but not its cause, so we do not read it as a signal for the share price. No pledge figure was found in our sources; the pledge record is listed as not found in the run notes.

The company is also investing. Management guided to capital expenditure of about ₹200 crore in FY27, mainly for moving production to a new plant [21]. Over four to five years it has described a move from the city campus to a larger facility costing ₹500 to ₹600 crore [22]. Both plans come from calls held in February and May 2026, so they are older than our freshness rule (see the run notes).

Management's five-year plan, presented in February 2026, targets revenue of USD 750 million by FY31, an EBITDA margin of 18 percent and a return on capital employed of 35 percent [24]. Those are company targets, not forecasts, and they show the bar the management has set for itself. That bar is well above the framework floor of 15 percent for return on capital.

How the framework works for this company

The framework has three layers that run in order. Layer 1 holds the hard floors: a company must pass them before it is scored any further. Layer 2 scores three to five operating measures for the business, and Layer 3 decides the timing of an entry relative to a support level. A failure at Layer 1 ends the sequence for this run, so Layers 2 and 3 are not evaluated.

Layer 1 has three parts. The financial health floor asks for a return on capital of at least 15 percent, a debt-to-equity below 0.70, and three-year cash from operations at least 0.70 times profit. The technical posture floor asks for a stock in Stage 2 or a Stage 1 breakout for a Core Alpha name, and no Stage 4 breakdown. The asymmetry floor asks that the reward-to-risk ratio reach the strategy minimum, which is 3.0 for Core Alpha and 3.5 for Tactical Momentum. The target must be anchored to forward earnings times a three-year median P/E, or to a measured move on the chart, and an unverified target fails.

Before any of that, the momentum gate runs in code. A candidate needs to pass Test A, which is an established uptrend, or Test B, which is a stock entering an uptrend from a base. A name that fails both has no allocation however good the business is. That rule is the reason this review ends at the technical stage.

Asymmetry and target anchoring

Asymmetry compares what a trade could make with what it could lose. The framework requires that the target be anchored to a number we can check: forward earnings multiplied by a three-year median P/E, or a measured move on the chart. An arbitrary target is not allowed, and a target that cannot be verified fails the gate.

The forward EPS used is ₹15.68 for fiscal 2027. It is the analyst consensus shown by StockAnalysis, from five analysts polled by S&P Global [10]. The same page shows ₹13.96 for the prior year [10]. The trailing EPS over the last twelve months is ₹14.18 [6]. The P/E history, taken at each March year-end, was 61.01 for fiscal 2024, 43.49 for fiscal 2025 and 34.28 for fiscal 2026 [7]. The median of those three is 43.49, which is the multiple we apply.

Fatal-flaw red team

The red team asks what would make the case for this stock wrong, even when the figures look good. We test three flaws: margin pressure from costs and tariffs, a slowing overseas growth engine, and an earnings forecast that is too high. Each one is tied to a figure in the record.

Flaw one: margin pressure from costs and tariffs. In the June quarter, management said exceptional reorganisation costs are being incurred in Australia, Europe and the United States, and are expected to continue through the year [19]. On the Q4 FY26 call, the effect of tariff changes was described as marginal. The call summary also gives a range of 10 to 50 percent, but it does not say what that range measures, so we do not rely on it [18]. If margins fall while sales grow, the forward EPS would fall too, and the target falls with it. The test is the next quarter's operating margin against the 15 percent level in fiscal 2026 [1].

Flaw two: a slowing overseas engine. In February, management said US growth of 10 to 11 percent was plateauing under the current strategy [22]. Foreign investors have also reduced their stake, from 29.54 percent in March 2024 to 21.90 percent in June 2026 [1]. The business still grew strongly in June. Our reading, which is not a sourced fact, is that the market may already be pricing a slower overseas phase, which would keep the multiple from returning toward its three-year median.

Flaw three: the forecast is too high. The consensus expects EPS to rise from ₹13.96 in fiscal 2026 to ₹15.68 in fiscal 2027 [10]. If that does not happen, the target falls. Flat EPS at the trailing ₹14.18 would give a target near ₹616.70 on the same multiple, which is a reward of about ₹29 against a risk of ₹59, a ratio of about 0.5. The ratio already fails at the consensus figure, so this stress test does not change the verdict.

The three flaws are not why this stock was rejected. That rests on the failed trend test and the 1.60 ratio. They are the risks a future bullish case would have to clear. Each should be checked against the next quarter's results before any allocation is considered.
