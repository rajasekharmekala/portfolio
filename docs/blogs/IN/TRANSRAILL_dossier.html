1. What the company does

Transrail Lighting Limited is a contractor that builds power lines. The group's first foundation job for a 132 kV line dates to 1984, the company was incorporated in 2008, and it took over the transmission business of Gammon India in October 2016.[2][12] It listed on the NSE and BSE on 27 December 2024, at an issue price of ₹432 inside a ₹410 to ₹432 price band.[12][15] The business is called engineering, procurement and construction (EPC): the company designs a project, buys or makes the materials, builds it, and hands over a working line for a fixed contract price. For a transmission line that means designing the steel towers, making them, erecting them, stringing the aluminium conductors (the wires that carry the current) between them, and testing the finished line.[2]

Several measures recur in this report, so here are plain definitions up front. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is operating profit before financing and accounting charges. EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit earned on the money the business uses. The operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' equity, and net debt is borrowings minus cash. Working capital is the cash tied up in unbilled work, customer bills and stock, minus supplier credit. Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying plant. Guidance is the forecast that management gives for its own results.

Several price and trade terms also recur. The RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast a price has moved, where below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought. The ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in rupees. Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend. A base is a sideways stretch where a price rests, and the pivot is the top of that base. The entry zone is the price range where we would be willing to buy. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, so 3 to 1 means ₹3 of possible gain for each ₹1 risked.

The customers are power utilities and developers. Repeat clients include Power Grid Corporation of India, which CRISIL rates AAA, Adani Energy Solutions and several state transmission utilities.[12] Outside India, the work is funded largely by multilateral lenders such as the World Bank and the African Development Bank.[2][12] Customer concentration is high. Two government customers, including a foreign government, supplied ₹3,239.30 crore of the standalone revenue from operations of ₹6,778.47 crore in FY26, which is 47.8%. A year earlier the same two supplied ₹2,180.79 crore, or 41.8%.[5]

The company makes money in two ways. First, it earns the margin between the fixed contract price and what it pays for steel, zinc, aluminium, labour and sub-contractors. In FY26 it spent ₹3,210.20 crore on materials and ₹1,607.05 crore on sub-contracting, up from ₹901.70 crore the year before, against revenue from operations of ₹6,779.95 crore.[5] Second, it earns a manufacturing margin because it makes its own towers, conductors and poles, which CRISIL credits for supporting profit margins.[12] Management says that 30% to 35% of its contracts carry a price-variation clause, which passes some raw-material price rises to the customer. The other 65% to 70% do not, so the company carries that price risk itself.[7]

A typical contract runs 24 to 30 months, so cash arrives slowly. Customers hold back retention money until a line has passed its guarantee period, and some billing follows project milestones.[7][12] CRISIL puts receivables at about 270 days of revenue.[12] The company funds the gap with bank loans, letter-of-credit discounting and supplier credit, which is why chapter 3 spends so much time on cash. Plant capacity has been doubled to feed the order book. Tower capacity stood at 184,400 tonnes a year on 30 September 2026, up from 84,000 tonnes before the expansion, with 196,000 targeted. Conductor capacity is 40,800 km a year, up from 24,000 km, with 49,500 km targeted by December 2026.[20][17][2]

Why the business is hard to read quarter by quarter

An EPC company recognises revenue as work is completed, not when it signs a contract. A quarter with many projects finishing and few starting looks weak even when nothing has gone wrong. Management made this point on the May 2026 call: about 20 projects finished by December 2025, and the new ones were still in design and approvals, so the March quarter fell.[7] For that reason this report judges the company on annual numbers and on the order book, and treats any single quarter as a clue rather than a verdict. The next chapter asks whether the position behind those numbers is strong.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Unexecuted order book: signed contracts whose work is not yet done or billed. Divide it by yearly sales to see how many years of work are on hand.
  • L1: Transrail has submitted the lowest bid and expects the award but does not yet have a signed contract.
  • Retention money: a slice of each bill that the customer holds back until the line has passed its guarantee period.
  • Contract assets: work done but not yet billed, plus retention money. They sit on the balance sheet as money owed to the company.
  • Letter-of-credit acceptance: a bank promises to pay a supplier on a later date. It works like a loan, and CRISIL counts it as debt.[12]
  • Price-variation clause: a contract term that lets the contractor pass raw-material price changes to the customer.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Transrail is a top-tier Indian transmission contractor, but it is not a business with a wide moat. Management says it is among the top three or four EPC players in the field and that its edge is its pre-qualifications, its own tower and conductor plants, and its work for utilities.[7] The evidence supports part of that claim. The Deoli tower plant has an 'A grade' from Power Grid, and the company completed seven 765 kV projects in India in FY26.[2][7] CRISIL calls its market position established and its operating efficiency strong, and in March 2026 it kept the AA-/Stable rating, the long-term bank-loan rating, on Transrail.[12] India Ratings raised its own rating from IND A+ to IND AA-/Stable in August 2026.[13][2]

The same CRISIL note names the weaknesses plainly: working-capital-intensive operations, intense competition because entry barriers are low, and exposure to project delays, cost overruns and litigation.[12] On the May 2026 call an analyst pointed out that road builders have started bidding for transmission jobs. Management answered that rivals lack its own conductors and pre-qualifications, and that it will not chase growth at poor margins.[7] That is a reasonable answer, but it is a promise about future behaviour, and the only check is the margin, which we track in chapter 2B.

The profit margin is where Transrail looks best against peers. On Screener's consistent operating-margin measure, Transrail earned 12% over the last twelve months, against 7% at KEC International, 8% at Kalpataru Projects and 10% at Skipper.[1][23][24][25] KEC's own quarterly EBITDA margin fell to 5.8% in Q1 FY27 from 7%, and a broker cited the West Asia crisis, higher execution costs and supply-chain disruption.[32] So the whole sector is under cost pressure, and Transrail's margin is holding up better. It is still falling: the company's own EBITDA margin was 11.9% in FY26 and 11.7% in Q1 FY27, and management guides about 11% for FY27.[2][7]

Demand is not the problem. On 30 September 2026 the Union Cabinet approved Phase III of the Green Energy Corridor, with an outlay of more than ₹1.86 lakh crore to carry up to 135 GW of renewable power by FY2032-33.[31] Greenfield projects in it will be awarded by competitive bidding, but they are state-utility projects. State utilities are only 3% to 4% of Transrail's revenue, and management said in May that it had not yet bid for such jobs.[31][7] So the pipeline is large, but Transrail's access to the newest part of it is limited for now.

Risk from abroad and from clients is real. In FY26 the international share of revenue was 50.5%, down from 57.9% in FY25.[5] The Bangladesh river-crossing project was about 35% of the order book in fiscal 2024 and is now almost finished: ₹300 crore is left from a ₹4,500 crore contract.[12][8] African contracts are about 20% of the order book, and Bangladesh about 3% to 4%.[7] Africa is mostly funded by multilateral lenders, which lowers payment risk but not execution risk.[12]

Governance and capital-allocation flags

Between March and October 2026 the company disclosed several items that a careful owner should weigh. The Income Tax Department searched Transrail's offices, plants and the homes of some directors, key managers and an executive from 24 to 28 March 2026.[22][21][5] The shares fell more than 7% on the first day, to an intraday low of ₹478.[29] The company says no assets were attached and no amount was surrendered. On 22 July 2026 it received a notice to file a block return covering April 2019 to March 2026.[4] The statutory auditors added an emphasis-of-matter paragraph to both the standalone and consolidated FY26 reports, saying the effect, if any, is not yet ascertainable.[5]

Capital allocation is also moving. The company bought Gactel Turnkey Projects for ₹10 crore from its parent, Ajanma Holdings, which is a related-party deal.[4] On the August call the chief financial officer said a ₹80 crore loan to a related party had been outstanding for about three years, that ₹30 crore had been repaid, and that the rest would be repaid by 30 September 2026.[8] We found no filing yet confirming that repayment. The company has also advanced ₹19 crore to Gammon Engineers and Contractors for a proposed acquisition, against ₹16 crore a year earlier.[5]

On 28 July 2026 the board approved raising up to ₹600 crore by a qualified institutions placement, and shareholders approved it on 10 September, with 96.61% of votes polled in favour.[4][30][34] At today's price that is about 1.4 crore new shares, which is 10.4% of the 13.43 crore shares in issue.[35][5] The chief financial officer described the purpose as long-term working capital, which sits oddly beside the company's claim of strong cash flow.[8] On 19 October 2026 the board also agreed to pay ₹20 crore for 49% of a 990 MW solar developer with an indicative project cost of about ₹3,900 crore, which moves the company from building power assets toward owning one.[19] None of this is wrong in itself. Together, it means the reader must trust management with more capital, more related-party dealings and a wider business scope while a tax matter is open.

What this means for an owner

The business earns well and its lenders are comfortable. The weak points are thin pricing power against rivals, slow cash collection, client concentration, and a run of governance items that need to close. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management walk-the-talk means comparing what the company said it would do with what it did. Transrail has a good record on its yearly financial promises. In May 2025 the chief executive guided FY26 revenue growth of 23% to 25%, later ranged as 22% to 25%, then raised it from 25% to 27% after the half-year, and delivered 30%.[9][10][7] He also pointed to EBITDA margin guidance of about 12% given in the IPO document, and the company delivered 11.9%.[9][7] Working-capital days fell from 91 to 81 and net debt fell from ₹502 crore to ₹267 crore in FY26.[2][7]

The misses are in order intake and in timing. In November 2025 management guided ₹9,000 to ₹10,000 crore of FY26 orders, and the year closed at ₹8,520 crore. The chief executive admitted that some bids slipped into the next quarter.[11][7] A conductor plant expected in Q1 FY27 slipped to the second or third quarter, which an analyst raised on the August call and the chief executive called 'a month or two'.[8] For FY27 the company has promised 20% revenue growth, a margin of about 11%, ₹10,000 crore or more of orders, working-capital days of 81 or less, and net debt back near 0.33 times EBITDA by March 2027.[7][8] The first quarter delivered 5% growth and a 0.67 times net-debt ratio, so these promises are open rather than kept.[2]

Five labels recur below, so here is what each means and what you should do under it. REJECTED means do not buy and do not add; the name comes back only on the triggers listed here. PASSED_CONDITIONAL means the name is allowed back onto the candidate list with conditions attached. WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT is the order status that goes with it: place no market order, and buy only through a limit order, which is an order that fills only at or below the price you set, at a stated support price or lower. A conviction tier is a ladder that sets how much of the portfolio's risk a position may take. TIER_0 means none, and the third tier, TIER_3, is a small starter size. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so a risk budget of 50 basis points means the portfolio may lose 0.50% of its value if the stop is hit. Portfolio heat is the total risk the portfolio is running at one time, and the risk budget divided by the stop distance sets the position size.
