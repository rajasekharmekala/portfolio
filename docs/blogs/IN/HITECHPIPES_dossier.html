Hi-Tech Pipes makes steel tubes and pipes for buildings, solar farms and water lines, and it can now make 1.05 million tonnes a year[12]. We reject it because three hard rules fail: the stock is in a Stage 4 downtrend, promoters have pledged 14.70% of their shares (2.54% in March 2025), and three years of operating cash flow were minus ₹22 crore against ₹193 crore of profit[1][9][25][37]. 1. What the company does

A steel pipe is a hollow steel tube, and most of the tubes in a building frame, a solar farm or a water line start as a flat roll of steel. Hi-Tech Pipes feeds that roll through a mill that bends it into a round or square shape and welds the seam with electric current. The result is called an ERW pipe, which stands for electric resistance welded. Square and rectangular versions are called hollow sections, and they are the beams and columns of steel-framed sheds, malls, airports and metro stations. The company also galvanises tubes, which means it coats them in zinc so they do not rust, and it rolls and sells cold rolled steel strips and coated sheets[12][13]. It runs eight plants in Uttar Pradesh, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra and Jammu and Kashmir, with 10,50,000 tonnes (1.05 million tonnes) a year of installed capacity, and it sells through more than 550 dealers and distributors in over 20 states[12].

The company makes money by converting steel into tubes, and that is a thin business. It buys hot rolled steel coil, which is the raw material and the biggest cost, and it sells the tube at a price that moves with the steel price. What it keeps is called EBITDA, which stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. In the June 2026 quarter that was ₹3,162 for every tonne sold, or about 4% of the normal selling price of ₹75,000 a tonne that management quoted on its call[12][21]. A move of ₹500 a tonne in cost, on the 6.6 lakh tonnes we expect for FY27, is ₹33 crore, which is more than a quarter of the profit before tax we estimate for that year. This is why the report spends so much time on margin per tonne and on cash.

A second source of revenue is trading, and it matters for reading the headline growth. The company also buys finished steel and resells it, and its own management calls the margin on this 'very nominal'[20]. Purchases of stock in trade, the accounting line for these goods, were ₹841 crore in FY26 against ₹241 crore in FY25, which is 20% of FY26 revenue. In the June 2026 quarter they were ₹280 crore, or 20% of revenue again[2][11]. Revenue grew 79% in that quarter, but tonnes sold grew 26%[12]. The gap between the two is mostly steel price and trading, not more tubes.

Volume is the cleaner measure, and it has grown steadily. The company sold 2.77 lakh tonnes in FY22 and 5.32 lakh in FY26, and a lakh is one hundred thousand[3]. It then sold 1,56,136 tonnes in the June 2026 quarter and 1,65,016 tonnes in the September 2026 quarter, a record, which makes 3,21,152 tonnes for the half, up 29% on a year earlier[12][15][24]. Three new plants started in the March 2026 quarter: Sanand Unit II Phase 2 in Gujarat (1 lakh tonnes), a greenfield plant at Kathua (80,000 tonnes) and Sikandrabad Unit III in Uttar Pradesh (1.2 lakh tonnes)[14][19]. Management now plans another million tonnes by FY29 for about ₹650 crore, including a direct forming technology line and an API pipe line at Sanand and a coated-tube plant at Hindupur in Andhra Pradesh[14][22].

The company is owned and run by the Bansal family, who held 43.43% of the shares at 30 September 2026. CRISIL, a rating agency, rates its bank debt A+ with a stable outlook, and the bank limits it rated rose to ₹565 crore from ₹510 crore in October 2026[25][29][30]. The group also has five wholly owned subsidiaries, and the results notes describe one operating segment, steel pipes and cold rolled products[11]. Reading the growth honestly

Three kinds of growth are mixed in the headline revenue number. The first is more tonnes from new plants, which is real and which management has delivered: volume rose 26% in the June 2026 quarter and 32% in the September 2026 quarter[12][15]. The second is a higher steel price, which raises revenue per tonne without raising profit. The third is trading, which adds revenue at a near-zero margin. A fair reading of FY26 is that revenue rose 37% to ₹4,200 crore while tonnes rose 9.7% and net profit rose 4.4% to ₹76 crore[2][3]. The new plants only began to sell in the March 2026 quarter, so their full effect is still ahead. That is the bull argument. The bear argument is in the next chapter: the plants arrive with interest and depreciation bills that are already running ahead of profit.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ERW pipe: a pipe made by rolling flat steel into a tube and welding the seam with electric current; ERW stands for electric resistance welded.
  • Hollow section: a square or rectangular steel tube used as a beam or column in buildings and structures.
  • Galvanised and pre-galvanised: coated with zinc to stop rust; pre-galvanised steel is coated before it is made into a tube.
  • Hot rolled coil (HRC): the flat steel roll that is the main raw material for a tube mill.
  • Value added products (VAP): coated, galvanised and special-grade tubes that sell at a higher margin than plain black pipe.
  • DFT and API pipe: direct forming technology, a higher-grade tube-making route, and pipe built to American Petroleum Institute oil and gas standards, both being added at Sanand.
  • Stock in trade: finished steel bought and resold without further processing.
  • Lakh and crore: a lakh is 100,000 and a crore is 10 million; ₹100 crore is ₹1,000 million.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Some things about this business are strong. It is large for its trade: capacity of 10.5 lakh tonnes at eight plants in five states means a tube can be made close to where it is used, and the company says the Sanand, Sikandrabad and Kathua plants cut logistics cost and delivery time[12][14]. It sells through 550 dealers and has launched a national television campaign, and CRISIL gives its bank debt an A+ rating with a stable outlook, which is a sign that lenders trust the group[12][17][29]. Debt is modest: borrowings are ₹242 crore against equity of ₹1,333 crore, so debt-to-equity, which compares what a company owes with what its owners put in, is 0.18[2][4]. Demand is also real, because management reports that new infrastructure projects that were on hold are now starting[21][22].

The weakness is the thin margin, and the evidence is in the filings. EBITDA per tonne was ₹3,634 in FY22 and ₹3,260 in FY26, so it has not improved in four years[3]. The peer APL Apollo Tubes reported more than ₹5,500 a tonne in the June 2026 quarter[36]. Management said in August 2025 that ₹4,000 a tonne was 'not a very big deal', and it has said so again in 2026, but the company has not reached it[16][21]. Net profit per tonne fell from ₹1,687 to ₹1,283 between the June 2025 and June 2026 quarters (our division of the reported profit by tonnes sold)[11][12]. A moat is the ability to earn more than rivals for years, and the numbers do not show one yet.

Return on capital employed, called ROCE, is the clearest test. It divides operating profit by the money tied up in the business, so it says what each rupee of capital earns. The company's own ratio was 16.3% in FY22 and 11.1% in FY26, and ours, which also counts lease debt, is 9.8%[3][4]. The three peers we checked earn 15.4% to 31.8%[33][34][35]. A company that earns 9.8% while borrowing at rates that show up as ₹47.6 crore of finance cost on about ₹230 crore of average reported debt is not yet earning its keep[2][27]. That finance cost is 21% of average reported borrowings, which suggests that some credit sits inside trade payables, a point we return to in Chapter 6 (our inference, not a company statement).

The competitive pressure comes from three places. The first is steel itself: hot rolled coil prices fell about ₹2,500 a tonne in the December 2025 quarter, and the government answered with a 12% safeguard duty on 30 December 2025[18]. The second is energy: management says gas prices more than doubled year on year in the June 2026 quarter, which squeezed margin[21]. The third is competition from tube makers that use cheaper secondary steel; management put the price gap between primary and secondary steel at ₹5,000 to ₹6,000 a tonne in November 2025[17]. Because the company cannot control any of these, it can only win by volume, mix and cost.

Cycles and seasons

This is a cyclical business with a seasonal pattern. The monsoon holds back construction in the July to September quarter, and management had said in August it expected second quarter volume to stay close to the first, although the quarter came in 6% higher. The second half is usually stronger[15][21]. Steel prices move in cycles too. When prices fall, a dealer waits and a tube maker holds stock bought at higher prices, which hurts profit. When prices rise, the opposite happens. The December 2025 quarter showed the first effect: tonnes sold rose 10% but profit after tax fell 9% to ₹17 crore[18]. A buyer should expect lumpy quarters, and should judge the company over a year, not a quarter.

What this means for an owner

For an owner, the business is a real, growing, low-margin converter with decent lenders and a clean balance sheet by one measure. It is not yet a business that earns a high return, and its cash flow does not yet back its reported profit. Cash is where the story turns. Cash flow from operations is the cash a business generates from its trading, before it spends on plant. The group generated minus ₹95 crore in FY24, ₹70 crore in FY25 and ₹3 crore in FY26[1][9]. Over the same three years, it reported profit of ₹193 crore. Free cash flow, or FCF, is cash flow from operations less spending on plant, and it was minus ₹167 crore in FY26[27]. In FY26 receivables rose ₹146 crore and inventories rose ₹181 crore, while payables rose ₹118 crore, so those three lines absorbed ₹209 crore, and after other items the group lost a net ₹151 crore of cash to working capital in one year[1]. Note also that this company's cash flow statement books interest of ₹47.6 crore under financing, so operating cash flow is shown before interest, which flatters it further[1].

Debt is not the problem. Borrowings were ₹242 crore at 31 March 2026 against equity of ₹1,333 crore, and the company's own measure of debt to EBITDA was 1.39 times[2][3]. Claims that are not on the balance sheet are small: contingent liabilities were only ₹3.5 crore of bank guarantees, and commitments for plant still to be paid were ₹21.5 crore[7]. The warning sign is different: interest cover, which is operating profit divided by interest, fell to 2.7 times in the June 2026 quarter from 4.6 times a year earlier, because finance cost doubled with the new plants[11]. Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 compares the company with fixed rules on financial strength, trend and entry price. A static level is where a number stands today. A trajectory is the direction it is moving. Four hard red lines apply to Indian companies: debt-to-equity of 1.20 or more, or interest cover below 2; a promoter pledge of 20% or more of promoter shares, or a rising pledge into a falling stock; three-year cash flow from operations divided by profit below 0.25, or below 0.40 without turnaround evidence; and a Stage 4 breakdown. Stage 4 is the phase where the price falls below a falling 50-day average that is itself below a falling 200-day average, and where the recovery test, called Test C, fails[38]. Two measures of entry price also appear. Asymmetry is the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss, and it is called reward-to-risk when it is measured from the entry price to the stop. The third archetype, a tactical breakout, needs the price to rise above its recent highs on strong volume, together with rising earnings. Hi-Tech Pipes passes the debt rules and fails the other three.

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on the future, and a reverse DCF works backwards from the price to the bet. DCF stands for discounted cash flow. It asks: if I want a 12% yearly return and I expect the company to be worth 20 times its profit in ten years, how fast must profit grow for today's price to be fair? The ledger solves this for Hi-Tech Pipes at +4.62% a year for ten years. We reproduced it and got 4.62%[37].

That growth rate is very low. The company grew profit 26.3% a year over three years and the Street expects FY28 profit per share to rise about 30%[3][32]. On this test the stock is cheap: the price already assumes growth far below what the company has done. The result rests on net profit, because free cash flow was negative (minus ₹167 crore in FY26, and expected minus ₹137 crore and minus ₹144 crore for FY27 and FY28)[27][32]. If cash flow, not profit, is the measure, no growth rate can justify the price, because there is no positive cash to grow. That gap between profit and cash is the central point of this chapter.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a weakest assumption, and a good report names it. This chapter takes each estimate in turn, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when we will know. It then gives the red-team case against our own verdict. The red-team case matters here because our verdict is a rejection, and the strongest argument against a rejection is that the stock is cheap, volumes are growing and the lenders are comfortable.

Weakest assumption 1: margin per tonne rises to about ₹3,300

Our FY28 profit per share of ₹5.71 needs EBITDA of ₹3,300 a tonne on 8.0 lakh tonnes. The record argues against it: ₹3,634 in FY22, ₹2,915 in FY23, ₹2,936 in FY24, ₹3,297 in FY25, ₹3,260 in FY26 and ₹3,162 in the June 2026 quarter[3][12]. Management says new direct forming technology lines earn ₹4,500 to ₹5,000 a tonne and API pipes ₹6,000 or more, and that extra dealer rebates will end once the three new plants reach critical mass[21]. Those are the right levers, but none is running yet. The evidence that would prove us wrong on the downside is a second quarter margin below ₹3,000 a tonne. We will know when the September 2026 quarter results are published, probably in the second week of November, as last year's call was on 13 November[40][17].

Weakest assumption 2: working capital will stop absorbing cash

Our model does not assume a cash shortfall, but the Street expects free cash flow of minus ₹137 crore in FY27 and minus ₹144 crore in FY28[32]. Cash and bank balances were ₹87 crore at March 2026, capital spending is planned at ₹200 crore in FY27, and promoters have paid only ₹22.5 crore of the ₹90 crore of warrant money[2][21][31]. Finance cost of ₹47.6 crore in FY26 is 21% of average reported borrowings of about ₹230 crore, which suggests that some of the credit sits inside trade payables, such as vendor financing and letters of credit. CRISIL's rated limits of ₹565 crore include cash credit, letters of credit, vendor bill discounting and vendor financing[2][30]. That is our inference, not a company statement. Position size is nil, and the reason lies in how the scoring file sizes positions. It gives every idea a tier, which is a size class: TIER_1 is the highest conviction and may reach 7% to 10% of net worth, TIER_3 allows 2% to 3.5%, and TIER_0 means no position at all. A rejected stock always gets TIER_0. Each tier also has a risk budget measured in basis points, and one basis point is one hundredth of a percent. A TIER_3 budget of 50 basis points means losing no more than 0.5% of net worth if the stop is hit. Had the stock qualified as TIER_3, 50 basis points over a 3.6% stop would give 13.7% of net worth, capped at the 3.5% tier ceiling. That is a ceiling, not a plan. The strategy bucket, TACTICAL_MOMENTUM, is the book for shorter trades of about three weeks to six months in stocks that are already rising. The let-winners-run rule lets a winning position in a Stage 2 uptrend grow up to 22% of the portfolio before any trim, and never trims it just because RSI is above 70. It has no use here because there is no position and the stock is in Stage 4. Nobody should average down into a Stage 4 stock with a rising pledge.
