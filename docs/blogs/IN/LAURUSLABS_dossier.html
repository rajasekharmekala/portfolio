1. What the company does

Laurus Labs is a drug-ingredient and drug-manufacturing company headquartered in Hyderabad, with its large plants around Visakhapatnam on India's east coast. It was founded in 2005 and today runs 15 manufacturing sites, about 8,300 kilolitres of reaction vessels for making ingredients, and tablet capacity of 12 billion units a year.[1][6] Its revenue in the year to March 2026 was ₹6,813 crore, and in the June 2026 quarter it was ₹2,026 crore.[12][4] It employs more than 8,000 people, of whom over 3,000 are scientists and quality staff.[4]

The company sells to three kinds of customer, and each pays for something different. Global funding agencies and generic drug makers buy anti-retroviral (ARV) ingredients and tablets for HIV treatment, a business where price is set by tenders and volume is large. Pharmacy chains and distributors in the United States, Europe and emerging markets buy finished generic tablets. Large drug companies and biotech firms pay for custom development and manufacturing of ingredients for their own patented drugs, which the industry calls contract development and manufacturing, or CDMO.[12][5] Direct exports are roughly 55% to 60% of sales, according to the finance chief.[5]

The three businesses earn money in different ways. In ARV and other generics, Laurus makes an ingredient in huge volume at a thin profit per kilogram and a steady schedule. In CDMO, one customer asks for one molecule, Laurus develops the manufacturing process, and the customer then buys the ingredient for years if the drug is approved; management calls this business lumpy but predictable.[5] In the June 2026 quarter, about 55% of small-molecule CDMO revenue came from commercial supply, meaning drugs already on sale or about to launch, and the rest was mostly for late-stage trials.[5] Management said gross margin, the share of sales left after raw material costs, ranks in the order CDMO highest, then formulations, then ingredients.[9]

The mix is shifting. CDMO was 13% of sales six years ago and is now about 43%: ₹870 crore of the ₹2,026 crore in the June 2026 quarter.[8][4] ARV revenue was ₹2,800 crore in the year to March 2026 and management expects it to stay near that level while its share falls, because the CDMO share is targeted at 50% of sales by the year to March 2030.[8][5] That target is a promise, not a result, and Chapter 2B tracks it.

Laurus also holds smaller bets that earn little today. Laurus Bio, a fermentation business, had ₹35 crore of sales in the June 2026 quarter, and its large new plant is expected to open by the end of 2026.[4][6] The company in-licensed two cancer antibody drug conjugates in India, owns a stake in a cell-therapy firm, and holds 49% of a tablet joint venture with Krka.[4][1] Management said these new areas need four to five years before they matter, so for an investor they are a cost now and an option later.[5]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A competitive position is the set of things that make customers keep buying and make rivals struggle to copy. For Laurus, the evidence points to four: scale in HIV medicines, manufacturing depth in hard chemistry, a clean inspection record, and customers who cannot easily move once a drug is approved. We weigh each below, and we also name where the position is thin.

Where the business is strong

Scale in HIV medicines is real and measurable. Management says Laurus supplies about one-third of the world's people on HIV treatment, and its ARV revenue reached ₹2,800 crore in the year to March 2026, above the ₹2,400 to ₹2,600 crore range it had indicated a year earlier.[8][10] A buyer of ARV ingredients cares about reliable supply and price, and a plant running at full use with 92 filed drug master files is hard to displace quickly.[4] The cost of that strength is a low profit per sale, so ARV is the base of the company, not the engine of its growth.

Manufacturing depth is the second strength. Laurus has built large vessels for flow chemistry, hydrogenation, enzyme-based steps and high-potency compounds, and its plan for the years to March 2022 through 2027 totals more than ₹4,700 crore of plant spending, over 85% of it for growth.[6] Management argues that big drug companies now want validation batches made at commercial scale, so a supplier that has already built the vessel wins the programme.[8] The evidence for that argument is the revenue: small-molecule CDMO sales rose from ₹493 crore to ₹835 crore in one year, and the quarterly rate has moved from about ₹220–250 crore to about ₹800 crore in two years, according to a broker's summary of the call.[4][15]

The third strength is the inspection record. The company reports 54 successful inspections by major regulators since inception and 24 audits in the June 2026 quarter, one by a regulator, without critical findings.[6] The rating agency CARE counts 132 audits without critical observations and seven plants approved by the US drug regulator.[14] That matters because a patented drug's maker will not change supplier lightly once a plant is named in its approval, which gives CDMO revenue some stickiness.

Where the business is weak

The first weakness is lumpiness, and the numbers show it. Net profit fell from ₹793 crore in the year to March 2023 to ₹162 crore in March 2024, after one very large purchase order that boosted the earlier year did not repeat.[1][5] The finance chief called that period seven quarters of poor results.[5] CARE also lists lumpy CDMO revenue and debt-funded capacity spending as constraints on the rating, which still stands at CARE AA with a stable outlook.[14] A business whose profit can fall by four-fifths in a year does not deserve the same multiple as a steady compounder.

The second weakness is the amount of cash the business ties up. Inventory days were 317 and debtor days 115 in the year to March 2026, a cash cycle of 263 days on the aggregator's basis.[1] CARE names an operating cycle beyond 200 days as a negative rating trigger, though its own method may differ from the aggregator's.[14] The third is spending: capex guidance for the year to March 2027 went from over ₹1,000 crore in January 2026 to ₹1,500 crore in April and ₹2,000 crore in July, and management said the two-year total of ₹3,000 crore may be exceeded.[9][8][5]

A fourth point is not a weakness but a limit on what we know. Management gives no profit figures by segment and no customer or molecule names, so we cannot test how concentrated the CDMO revenue is beyond the statement that no product or customer contributes significantly.[5] We treat that statement as a company claim, not as evidence.

How we weigh it

On balance the position is strong in the parts that earn the profit today and thin in the parts that must keep earning it. The ARV base is durable but slow, and the CDMO growth is real but rests on a few commercial programmes at an early stage in their sales life; management said three ingredients reached commercial supply in the last 18 months.[8] Layer 2 in Chapter 2B turns this into five scored measures, and Chapter 3 shows what the profit and cash numbers look like once the cyclical swings are laid side by side.

What this means for an owner: the strengths justify owning the shares, and the weaknesses justify caring about the price paid. The two are linked through the multiple. A steady compounder can earn a high multiple because its profit is predictable, whereas a business whose profit has swung by a factor of five in three years earns a lower one unless the swings have clearly ended. The company's own record, with seven poor quarters followed by five strong ones, is evidence that the swings are narrowing but not that they are over. Chapter 2B therefore scores the measures that would reveal a relapse early, and Chapter 7 sets the exit price in terms of those measures.[5]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 stands on one assumption that is weaker than the rest, and this chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong and says when we will know. We also set out the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, which the process calls the red-team case, and pick the one claim on which everything hinges. The reasoning is that an estimate with a named failure test is more useful than a confident one without.

Profit per share estimate: the contract-sales run-rate

The weakest assumption behind the ₹29.68 estimate is that small-molecule CDMO sales stay near ₹835 crore a quarter. Management called a plus-or-minus ₹100 crore band around that run-rate a fair statement for the next two quarters, but about 45% of those sales were not commercial, and most of that share was Phase III supply that may or may not convert into commercial orders.[5] If a Phase III programme is paused or a customer finishes stocking, the quarterly figure can step down quickly. The first test is the September 2026 quarter result, scheduled for 29 October 2026 (date per Screener and an alert service quoting the 13 October board notice; the exchange filing was not fetched), and the test after that is the December 2026 quarter (expected in late January 2027, not confirmed; the December 2025 quarter was reported on 23 January 2026).[1][9]

Central value: a trailing multiple applied to forward profit

The weakest assumption behind ₹2,101.07 is that the market will pay 70.8 times profit per share two years out, a number taken from the stock's own trailing history. A trailing median measured on earlier, smaller profits may overstate what buyers will pay once growth slows. If the multiple falls to 60, the central value is ₹1,780.56, below today's price. The evidence that would show this wrong is a fall in the price while estimates rise, which would mean the multiple is compressing; the test is observable at every result and does not wait for a date.[18]

A related risk is that estimates and the multiple move together. When analysts raise their numbers, buyers often pay a higher multiple on the new numbers, and when estimates fall the multiple often falls too. That double effect is what the bad case captures: profit per share of ₹20.54 at 50 times gives ₹1,027.25, a fall of 48.7%. We do not know which way the next revision will go, and the page we used shows a rise of about 26% in 90 days, which means the market has already been paying for good news. What would change our mind is a result that lifts estimates without lifting the price, because that would show buyers are not paying up and the central value is conservative.

Reverse DCF: the starting profit and the capex bill

The ₹1,093 crore starting profit includes the June 2026 quarter, the strongest on record, and the model ignores that spending of ₹2,000 crore in the year to March 2027 will be funded partly by debt, which management expects to rise slightly while net debt to EBITDA stays near the current level.[5][8] If part of the Q1 profit is a one-quarter benefit, the true starting profit is lower and the implied growth higher than 28.1%.

The test of this assumption is simple. If quarterly profit stays near the June 2026 level of about ₹368 crore, the starting profit is representative and implied growth is close to 28%. If quarterly profit falls toward the FY26 average of about ₹222 crore, the starting profit was inflated and the implied growth needed is higher, which makes today's price harder to justify. The September 2026 result on 29 October 2026 is the first reading, and the December 2026 result, expected in late January 2027 and not confirmed, is the second. We also note that the model treats plant spending as the cost of growth already reflected in profit, which is generous when spending is running at nearly double last year's level.

The strongest case against holding is that the market is paying 98.8 times last year's profit for a company whose earnings fell 80% three years ago, whose capex plan has doubled in six months, and whose Q1 margin of 31.8% is the highest in the series we have, so the easiest direction for margin is down.
