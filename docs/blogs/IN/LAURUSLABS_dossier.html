1. What the company does

Laurus Labs Limited (NSE: LAURUSLABS, BSE: 540222) is a Hyderabad drug maker with plants in Vizag, Bangalore and Hyderabad and, through an associate, in Mumbai.[15] It makes the active ingredient of a medicine, called the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API), and it presses that ingredient into finished tablets, called finished dosage forms (FDF). It also makes ingredients to order for drug inventors, a service called contract development and manufacturing, or CDMO. In the year to March 2026 it sold ₹6,813 crore of goods and services, 23 percent more than a year earlier.[7] It runs 15 manufacturing sites and employs 1,570 research scientists.[15][4]

The company has two divisions. Affordable Medicines, formerly called Generics, was 57 percent of June-quarter revenue.[4] Its largest line is antiretroviral (ARV) medicine for HIV, sold to global funding agencies and generic makers, and ARVs were about one third of total revenue.[5] CARE says Laurus supplies ingredients or tablets for about one third of the world's HIV patients.[15] The other division, CDMO, was 43 percent of revenue. Here a drug inventor pays Laurus to develop, and later to make, the ingredient of a patented medicine.[5] Laurus has served over 275 clients, seven of them big drug companies, and no single customer is above 10 percent of revenue.[25][1]

Laurus earns its money by selling goods that it makes in plants with large fixed costs. Gross margin, which is sales less the cost of materials as a share of sales, was 62.7 percent in the June quarter. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit) was ₹644 crore, a 31.8 percent margin.[3] Exports were ₹4,569 crore, or 67 percent of FY26 revenue.[1] Because plants cost the same whether they are full or half full, extra sales lift the margin. Screener's operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, rose from 15 percent in FY24 to 32 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[32] Customers also pay part of the price early: advances from customers rose from ₹395 crore to ₹645 crore during FY26.[1]

The CDMO division is both the prize and the risk. Its core small-molecule sales rose from ₹917 crore in FY22 to ₹2,167 crore in FY23, fell to ₹922 crore in FY24, reached ₹1,896 crore in FY26 and were ₹835 crore in the June 2026 quarter alone.[14][4] The FY23 peak came from one large purchase order, as the chief financial officer said on the July 2026 call, and he added that the company then had seven weak quarters.[5] The chief executive says the CDMO business is lumpy but predictable.[5] The word to remember is lumpy.

Around the core, Laurus is spending on new areas. Laurus Bio makes products by fermentation, sold ₹35 crore in the June quarter, and expects a plant of more than 400 kilolitres to be commissioned by the third quarter of FY27.[3][4] A joint venture with Krka of Slovenia, in which Laurus holds 49 percent, is building a tablet plant in Hyderabad; ₹400 crore has been invested and ₹400 crore more is needed.[21][5] Laurus has opened gene therapy and antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) development labs in Hyderabad and has a manufacturing facility under construction there.[9] It has been allotted 531.77 acres in Anakapalli for a pharma zone that management put at over ₹5,000 crore of investment.[19][20][12] These bets earn little now, and management says the advanced biologics spending will not give returns for some years.[5]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • CDMO: contract development and manufacturing organisation; a company paid by drug inventors to develop and make their medicines.
  • API and FDF: the active ingredient of a medicine, and the finished tablet or capsule made from it.
  • ARV: antiretroviral medicine used to treat HIV.
  • Commercial supplies: ingredient sold for a medicine already approved for sale, as opposed to clinical supplies for trials.
  • DMF: drug master file, a dossier on an ingredient that lets a buyer rely on it in its own regulatory filing.
  • Form 483 and EIR: the US FDA's list of observations after a plant inspection, and the final inspection report that closes it.
  • Cash conversion: operating cash flow divided by profit; above 1 means profit arrived as cash.
  • Reverse DCF: a calculation that starts from today's price and solves for the yearly profit growth that price needs.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Laurus is a strong business for a reason that is easy to state. It spent about ₹4,400 crore on plants between FY22 and FY26 (the company's own deck says ₹4,300 crore; its yearly figures add to ₹4,369 crore), and customers are now filling them.[14] Reactor volume for APIs and intermediates is 8,300 kilolitres, tablet capacity is 12 billion units a year, and 92 drug master files have been filed.[4][3] Management explains the logic. Drug inventors want to see capacity before they hand over a product, and once a product is approved they do not want to move it to another line or site.[5] The chief executive also says that adding a second supplier takes three to four years.[13] That delay, not a patent, is the moat. No source publishes Laurus's market share, so we make no claim about it.

CARE Ratings rates the company CARE AA with a stable outlook. It cites 15 plants, 132 audits in FY26 with no critical finding, and a margin on profit before interest, lease rentals, depreciation and tax that rose from 19.04 percent to 26.53 percent.[15] CARE had already revised the outlook on the rating a year earlier, in July 2025, citing better FY25 performance.[16] It also names the two weak points: lumpy CDMO revenue, and capital spending that is partly funded by debt.[15]

Where the edge is real: scale, quality and switching cost

Quality is the first test for a supplier of medicine ingredients. Laurus reports 54 successful inspections by the major regulators since inception and 24 audits in the June quarter, with no critical finding.[4] The US FDA inspected Unit 4 in January 2025 and left one observation on a Form 483, and the company received the closing inspection report (EIR) in April 2025.[22][23] CARE found no active USFDA warning letters or import alerts at company-owned plants in June 2026.[15]

One older item deserves a mention. A secondary website reports an FDA letter dated 16 May 2024 about the Laurus Synthesis site, after a December 2023 inspection, and describes it as an untitled letter.[24] We have not confirmed it against FDA's own database. CARE's June 2026 statement says nothing is open, so we treat it as closed, but it is a reminder that one bad inspection can delay approvals.[15]

The industry tailwind and how much of it is proven

Two outside forces are said to favour Indian CDMOs. The first is the BIOSECURE Act, signed on 18 December 2025 as Section 851 of the 2026 defence law. It limits US federal contracts with “biotechnology companies of concern”, and the list of such companies is due by December 2026.[26] The second is the wish of drug inventors to hold a second source outside China. India Ratings says supply-chain rebalancing has helped CDMOs as a group.[31] Laurus's own deck sizes the global small-molecule CDMO market at about $85 billion growing above 7 percent a year, and the Indian market at about $2 billion growing above 14 percent a year.[14]

These are real forces, but Laurus's own numbers do not yet show them. Management said it cannot link requests for proposals to world events, and its growth came from late-stage and commercial supplies of programmes won earlier.[11][5] In the last 18 months it delivered three APIs for commercial use, and it says their patents run for several years.[6] That is evidence of a pipeline converting, not of a policy windfall.

Where the business is weak: lumpy sales, the HIV third and tariffs

The first weakness is lumpiness. Core CDMO sales fell 57 percent in a single year, from ₹2,167 crore in FY23 to ₹922 crore in FY24.[14] CARE says each CDMO programme follows the customer's development timeline, so a delayed approval or a rescheduled batch campaign can move revenue, and it expects the variability to last until commercial programmes are the majority of CDMO sales.[15] In the June quarter only 55 percent of small-molecule CDMO revenue was commercial, and most of the rest was Phase III supply, which stops or becomes commercial depending on regulators' decisions.[5]

The second weakness is the HIV third. The Clinton Health Access Initiative reports that US government funding for HIV is set to fall by 42 to 97 percent by 2030 across 11 country agreements, and that Global Fund HIV allocations are down 18 percent on average.[29] The Global Fund says ARVs are almost a quarter of its pooled procurement spend.[30] Laurus told investors in July 2025 that it had seen significant price drops in ARVs but that extra volume would offset them.[12] It did: ARV sales were about ₹2,800 crore in FY26 against a guide of ₹2,500 crore give or take ₹200 crore.[6] The risk is that a donor-funded market can shrink faster than a plant can be redeployed.

The third weakness is US trade policy. A US customs notice lists a 100 percent extra duty on patented pharmaceutical articles and a zero duty on generic ones.[27] A law firm summary says generics and their ingredients are explicitly excluded for now, and that the Commerce Department must report within a year on whether to tax them.[28] Because most CDMO ingredients go into patented drugs, the effect on Laurus depends on how its customers import their finished products. We found no statement from management on this, so the exposure is not measured here.

The fourth weakness is the size of the spending. Capital expenditure, meaning spending on plants and equipment, was ₹394 crore in the June quarter, or 19 percent of sales.[4] Management now talks of ₹2,000 crore for FY27, and more than ₹3,000 crore across FY27 and FY28.[5][14] CARE lists the plan, ₹3,000 to 3,500 crore for FY27 to FY29, as a key monitorable.[15]

What this means for an owner: you own a well-run, well-inspected manufacturer whose profit now depends on a few large programmes that are still young. The next chapter picks the numbers that show whether those programmes keep converting.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions that could be wrong. This chapter takes each estimate in turn, names its weakest assumption, says what evidence would show the error and when we will know. It then sets out the strongest case against our own conclusion and ends with the single claim on which everything hangs.

Weakest assumption behind the base-case profit

The base case assumes small-molecule CDMO sales stay near ₹835 crore a quarter. Management said a band of ₹100 crore either way was a fair statement, but 45 percent of the June quarter's small-molecule CDMO revenue was not commercial and was mostly Phase III supply.[5] Phase III supply stops or turns commercial depending on regulators' decisions, and nothing in the filings fixes the timing. Evidence that we are wrong would be September-quarter CDMO sales below ₹735 crore, which is the bottom of the band. We will see it in the Q2 FY27 results on 29 October 2026.[18]

Weakest assumption behind the 68-times multiple

The base case lets the multiple ease from 73 to 68 times. That is a guess about market mood. The multiple has been between 59.5 and 101.8 times in the last year, and it rose from 79.1 to 101.8 in the last twelve weeks with no new profit.[33] If the market decides Laurus deserves a Divi's-style multiple of 81.5 times, the same profit gives about ₹2,590. If it decides the multiple should be nearer Neuland's and Syngene's 52 times, the value is about ₹1,650.[36][37][38]

The sharpest test uses our own peer average. The 68 times was drawn from today's 73.1 times and the three-peer average of 62.2 times. If the market simply pays the peer average of 62.2 times on the unchanged ₹31.80 of profit per share, the value is ₹31.80 × 62.2 = ₹1,977.96, which is 4.0 percent below the close. A purchase at ₹1,950 would then have a payoff of (₹1,977.96 − ₹1,950) ÷ (₹1,950 − ₹1,879.70) = 0.40 to 1, and a 3-to-1 payoff would need a purchase near ₹1,904.27, which is below the 50-day average and below our own sell trigger. Evidence that we are wrong about the multiple would be estimates continuing to rise 5 percent a quarter while the price holds above ₹2,086. We will know after the October results and the January 2027 results.

Weakest assumption behind the cash and funding view

The cash KPI is on WATCH because FY26's operating cash flow was helped by ₹249 crore of advances and because capex is set to rise to ₹2,000 crore.[1][5] Our model has free cash flow near zero in FY27. The weak spot is the working capital: inventory days rose to 317 while receivable days eased to 115 from 132.[32] Evidence that we are wrong would be a half-year cash flow statement, filed with the September results, that shows operating cash flow below ₹700 crore for the six months. Our model's FY27 operating cash flow is about ₹1,830 crore, or about ₹915 crore for a half, so ₹700 crore would be a clear miss and would put the cash KPI under pressure.

Weakest assumption behind the probability weights

The 25, 50 and 25 percent weights are judgement, not measurement. The weakest is the 25 percent on the bear case, which may be too low for a division that has fallen by more than half before, or too high if the three new commercial APIs behave as management says. Moving ten points of weight between bear and bull moves the weighted value by about ₹203 either way. A September quarter with CDMO above ₹870 crore would lift the bull weight; one below ₹735 crore would lift the bear weight.

The case against our conclusion: the red-team view

The sceptic's case is that Laurus is priced as if the CDMO boom were permanent, and the company's own history says it is not. Core CDMO sales were ₹2,167 crore in FY23, then ₹922 crore in FY24.[14] The June quarter was the best ever, but it was a quarter in which no customer advances came in, net debt rose ₹371 crore, and operating cash flow was not disclosed.[5] The capex guide has doubled from ₹1,000 crore to ₹2,000 crore in six months for a business whose return on capital was 6 percent in FY24 and 10 percent in FY25.[5][14]

On this view the price should fall back to the range implied by peers: 52 times for Neuland and Syngene on ₹31.80 gives about ₹1,650, a fall of 20 percent. The cash flow statement due with the September results would show whether ₹249 crore of advances has reversed. Trade policy adds a tail: patented-drug ingredients face a 100 percent US duty by default, and Laurus has not said how much of its CDMO output is exposed.[27]

Our reply is that the quality of the business is not the issue. The FY26 figures are audited, the balance sheet is solid, and the audit record is clean. The issue is the price. 7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is KEEP_COMPOUNDING as a labelled exception: hold the 31 shares you own and do not add at the market. The portfolio list has no plain “hold” label, so KEEP_COMPOUNDING is the nearest, and its own condition (all gates passed) is not met, because the reward-to-risk gate fails at 0.57 against the 3.0 core floor. Two rules decide the verdict. The first is the support ladder, which sorts a stock by how far its price sits above the nearest floor, counted in average true ranges: up to 0.6 allows a full-size purchase, 0.6 to 1.2 is the STANDARD band and allows a normal-size purchase, 1.2 to 1.6 allows only a small starter order, and anything further is the NO_BUY band, which means do not chase the price. The close is 0.84 average true ranges above support, so it is in the STANDARD band, and RSI is 60.45, so a purchase would normally be allowed. The second rule is the payoff test, and it blocks the purchase today. The verdict changes to ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP only if, after the 29 October results, the close is at or below ₹1,950 and a refreshed valuation gives a payoff of at least 3 to 1 from the actual buying price. The entry zone, which is the band of prices where buying would be considered, is ₹1,930 to ₹1,950, and we would buy with a limit order, an order that fills only at your price or better and does not chase the price.

No trim or replace rule fires. The stock is not in the NO_BUY band, RSI is below 75, the bull target of ₹2,965.50 is far away, and the 1.40 percent weight is far below the 15 percent hard cap.[41][42] The structure is intact, the stop has not closed, and only one KPI is on WATCH, so the REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER rule does not apply either. The let-winners-run rule applies in the opposite direction from a trim: a Stage 2 holding is not sold because RSI passes 70.

Two readings of the stop are possible, and both lead to the same conclusion. The rule says to place the stop below the lower of the swing low and the 50-day or 200-day average, minus one ATR. We use the 50-day reading, which gives ₹1,879.70, 8.8 percent below the close and 3.6 percent below the ₹1,950 top of the entry zone. A literal reading with the 200-day average gives ₹1,353.59, 34.3 percent below the close, which would cut the risk-unit size to 1.46 percent, almost exactly the 1.40 percent held, and the reward-to-risk to 0.14. We prefer ₹1,879.70 because a stop 34 percent away on a stock 47 percent above its 200-day average is a disaster line and not a position-management level.

The sizing rule is risk-unit sizing. The risk budget is the share of net worth you accept losing if the stop is hit, quoted in basis points, and 50 basis points is 0.5 percent. A tier is how much confidence a case earns, and each tier has a cap on the share of net worth: TIER_3 allows 2 to 3.5 percent with a 50 basis-point budget. The weight is the lower of the cap and the budget divided by the stop distance. We assign TIER_3 because Layer 1 shows a failed gate and CDMO sales are lumpy. At a ₹1,950 entry the stop is 3.6 percent away, so the budget would allow 13.9 percent, far above the cap, and the 3.5 percent cap binds. That is about ₹1,60,000, or 82 shares at ₹1,940, against 31 held. The headroom of about 51 shares is a ceiling and not a plan, and it is used only if the add trigger below passes, including its payoff test.

The add is not justified by earnings alone. At the base profit of ₹31.80 per share, the 68-times multiple gives ₹2,162.40. At the three-peer average of 62.2 times, from which that multiple was drawn, the same profit gives ₹31.80 × 62.2 = ₹1,977.96, which is 4.0 percent below the close. At a ₹1,950 purchase the payoff would then be (₹1,977.96 − ₹1,950) ÷ (₹1,950 − ₹1,879.70) = ₹27.96 ÷ ₹70.30 = 0.40 to 1, and 3 to 1 would need a purchase near ₹1,904.27, below the 50-day average and below our own sell trigger. At 52 times, the Neuland and Syngene level, the value is ₹1,653.60, which is 19.7 percent below the close. Holding is defensible on these numbers, because the position is only 1.4 percent of net worth and the stop is cheap. Adding is defensible only if a valuation refreshed after the results passes the 3-to-1 test. Without that evidence, hold and do not add, even at ₹1,950.
