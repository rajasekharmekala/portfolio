1. What the company does

BEL was set up in 1954 under the Ministry of Defence and is a Navratna defence public-sector company, a status the government gives to its larger state-owned firms.[2] Its main customer is the Government of India. In FY26 its own (standalone) sales were ₹26,680 crore; ₹23,513 crore, or 88 percent, came from defence orders, ₹1,654 crore from non-defence government work, ₹181 crore from other domestic buyers and ₹1,331 crore (5 percent) from exports.[2] The state owns 51.14 percent of the shares, and none of that holding is pledged.[2][19]

The company makes electronic systems that soldiers, sailors and pilots use: radars and fire-control systems, weapon systems, communication and network-centred systems, electronic warfare and avionics, anti-submarine warfare, electro-optics, tank electronics and strategic components.[2] It works through 30 strategic business units and nine manufacturing units.[1][2] Outside defence it sells to civil aviation, rail and metro, space, cyber security and medical electronics, and it built the electronic voting machines used in Indian elections.[2]

The money flows in a fixed order. The Ministry of Defence signs a contract, which BEL calls an order. The customer then pays an advance and later instalments as work is done. At 31 March 2026 those customer advances stood at ₹12,571 crore, which is customer money that BEL holds until it delivers.[2] BEL buys parts, with imported semiconductors at about 17 to 19 percent of its material cost, builds and tests the systems, and books revenue when it delivers.[4] Two numbers show how much cash this cycle ties up: customers owed BEL ₹12,876 crore at 31 March 2026, and the working capital (cash tied up in stock and customer bills, less what BEL owes suppliers) was 135 days of sales on Screener's measure, up from 18 days two years earlier.[2][1]

What BEL earns depends on how much it has already been promised. Its order book was ₹73,882 crore on 1 April 2026. That is 2.7 times its FY26 sales of ₹27,610 crore on the consolidated basis. It was ₹72,258 crore on 1 July 2026.[2][1][3] The CMD says the book splits about evenly among Army, Navy and Air Force, and the seven biggest items add up to about ₹20,000 crore.[3] For FY27 management gives guidance, which is its own forecast for the year. It expects sales growth above 15 percent and an EBITDA margin above 28 percent. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a measure of operating profit before those charges. It expects order inflow above ₹55,000 crore, including about ₹30,000 crore from one missile programme. It plans more than ₹1,200 crore for plant and equipment and more than ₹2,200 crore for research.[4][3]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Order book: the value of signed contracts that BEL has not yet delivered; ₹73,882 crore at 1 April 2026.
  • Customer advance: money the customer pays before delivery; it sits on BEL's balance sheet as a liability and was ₹12,571 crore at 31 March 2026.
  • CCS: the Cabinet Committee on Security, which must clear the biggest defence purchases before a contract is signed.
  • QRSAM: the quick-reaction surface-to-air missile system, a roughly ₹30,000 crore Army and Air Force programme that BEL leads and is still waiting for CCS clearance.
  • DcPP: development-cum-production partner, the role BEL plays for DRDO (the government's defence research body), building systems that DRDO designs.
  • LRU: line-replaceable unit, an electronic module that can be swapped in the field; BEL sells these to HAL for the Tejas fighter.
  • Crore and lakh: Indian units; one crore is ten million and one lakh is one hundred thousand, so ₹100 lakh equals ₹1 crore.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

BEL's strength is that it is the default builder of defence electronics for its own government. It is an owner-run supplier (the state holds 51.14 percent) with a seat inside every big programme: DRDO designs, and BEL calls itself DRDO's largest development-cum-production partner on the Project Kusha air-defence system.[2][3] A January 2025 brokerage note estimated its share of the specialised defence-electronics market at nearly 60 percent and its share of all defence spending at 12.8 percent in FY24, but BEL itself does not publish a share figure, so we treat that number as an outside estimate.[24]

The evidence of depth is in the numbers. BEL spent ₹2,048 crore on research in FY26, which is 7 to 8 percent of revenue, and says 78 to 80 percent of its turnover now comes from products and technology it owns.[2][3] Its ROCE (return on capital employed, the operating profit a company earns for each rupee of capital in the business) was 36.4 percent, and it has no meaningful debt: borrowings were ₹65 crore against equity of ₹24,007 crore.[1][2] ICRA's current ratings are [ICRA]AAA (Stable) and [ICRA]A1+, and Screener's ratings list shows the last update on 20 February.[20][1]

The business is thinner in four places. First, one customer buys 88 percent of its output, so a delayed cabinet decision stops the inflow, as the long wait for the QRSAM order shows.[2][15] Second, big contracts have competition: in the Netra 2 programme BEL bid, was not the lowest bidder, and lost the lead role to Adani Defence, although the CMD expects to supply radar, electronic-warfare and data-link modules to the winner.[3] Third, imported semiconductors are 17 to 19 percent of material cost.[4] Fourth, a government wage settlement is due from 1 January 2027, after the last one in January 2017.[3]

Pricing power is real but uneven. The company's operating margin (operating profit as a share of sales) rose from 22 percent in FY22 to 29 percent in FY25 and FY26, which management ties to richer product mix and to making more parts in India.[1][3] The same management says quarterly margins range from 25 to 31 percent depending on which products ship, and the June 2026 quarter was at the low end: 25 percent on Screener's measure against 28 percent a year earlier.[3][1] That swing is a mix effect, not input-cost pressure, in the CFO's words, but it is also a reminder that BEL's margin is not set by BEL alone.[3]

What is hard to copy

A private firm cannot easily match BEL's place in the supply chain of the armed forces. The company says the major electronics inside most Indian missile programmes come from BEL, and for the P-75I submarine programme it expects more than half of the electronics content.[3][4] Its order book is also built on long supply contracts: electronic fuzes alone have about ₹4,300 crore still to be delivered over roughly seven years.[4] Those years of visible work give BEL time to plan plants and hire engineers that a small rival would struggle to match.

What a rival could still take

The risk is the new kind of programme that rewards software and system integration over legacy parts. Private firms such as Data Patterns and Astra Microwave sell similar electronics at far smaller scale, and L&T is BEL's partner, not rival, on the AMCA fighter bid, where the request-for-proposal response was due on 27 August 2026.[27][28][3] BEL says it is also working on high-power laser and microwave counter-drone weapons and expects a good share of the large integrated systems.[3] The honest summary is a strong, defended position in the existing book and an open contest for the next wave.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record on numbers is good. In May 2025 it guided FY26 sales growth of about 15 percent, an EBITDA margin of about 27 percent and order inflow above ₹27,000 crore; the year ended with growth of 16 percent, 30 percent and ₹30,045 crore.[8][4] Research spending beat its ₹1,600 crore target by 28 percent. Capex of ₹892 crore fell short of the ₹1,000 crore promise, and the defence share of sales was 88 percent against a 90 percent plan, which is a minor shortfall that comes from non-defence growth rather than weaker defence work.[2][1]

Its record on timing is poor. On 31 October 2025 the CMD was "fully confident" that the QRSAM order would arrive before March 2026; on 28 January 2026 he was "more than 90%" confident it would come in the fourth quarter; on 20 May 2026 he said before the end of June with only a 5 to 10 percent chance of slipping to July; on 27 July 2026 he said by September at the latest.[6][5][4][3] The Defence Acquisition Council cleared the programme on 3 July 2025.[7] As of 11 and 22 October 2026 the order was still waiting for Cabinet Committee on Security approval.[15][17] The delay is outside BEL's control, but three missed dates after high stated confidence tell us to treat management's order timing as a hope, not a plan.

On cash, management has twice told investors there was no problem with customers paying. In May 2025 the CFO said receivables were stable, the 2025 fall in operating cash flow came from lower advances, and there was "no problem on the receivables side".[8] Receivable days then rose from 140 to 170 in FY26 on Screener's measure, and the CFO himself quoted 176 days at 31 March 2026 and 140 at 30 June 2026.[1][3] The improvement to 140 days is good news but unaudited, since BEL files no quarterly balance sheet, and the half-year cash-flow statement due on 30 October 2026 is the first hard test.[3][18]

3. The numbers, in plain words

BEL's five-year record is one of fast, steady growth. Consolidated sales rose from ₹15,368 crore in FY22 to ₹27,610 crore in FY26, about 15.8 percent a year, and profit after tax rose from ₹2,400 crore to ₹6,062 crore, about 26 percent a year.[1] The operating margin climbed from 22 to 29 percent, so profit grew faster than sales. EPS (earnings per share, profit divided by the number of shares) went from ₹3.28 to ₹8.29, and the trailing twelve months to June 2026 show sales of ₹28,717 crore and profit of ₹6,148 crore, up only about 1 percent from FY26.[1]

Debt is not a concern. Screener shows borrowings of ₹65 crore. The annual report shows no bank borrowings, and that amount equals the lease liabilities (₹6,243 lakh non-current plus ₹303 lakh current). Equity capital and reserves are ₹23,988 crore, and finance costs were ₹248 lakh, or ₹2.5 crore, in FY26.[1][2] Reserves grew from ₹12,042 crore to ₹23,257 crore in four years. Profit growth is slowing, though: the June 2026 quarter showed sales up 25 percent but profit up only about 9 percent (8.9 percent on these rows), since operating profit rose 12 percent while the margin fell 3 points.[1][3]

Cash is the weak line. Cash flow from operations (cash actually collected by the core business after tax) was ₹4,207 crore in FY22, ₹1,199 crore in FY23, ₹4,659 crore in FY24, ₹564 crore in FY25 and ₹1,541 crore in FY26.[1] Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after paying for plant and equipment, and it was ₹4,015 crore in FY24 but -₹444 crore in FY25.[1] In the last two years BEL earned ₹11,385 crore of profit and collected only ₹2,105 crore of cash from operations.
