1. What the company does

In plain words: Mahindra Holidays sells holiday memberships and runs the resorts its members use. This chapter explains how the money works and where the cash goes.

Mahindra Holidays & Resorts India Limited sells holiday memberships under the Club Mahindra brand and runs the resorts its members use. It trades on the NSE as MHRIL and on the BSE as 533088; its filings are listed on the company's investor page[10][19][30]. It is a Mahindra group company: the promoters hold 66.73 percent[17][19]. The company began in 1996 and says it has about 3,03,000 members, 106 resorts (80 in India, 26 abroad) and 5,762 room keys[11][12][13]. A key is one lettable room. A crore is ten million rupees and a lakh is one hundred thousand.

This model is called vacation ownership: a member pays an upfront fee and then a yearly subscription for the right to stay a set number of weeks. The upfront fee is not booked as income at once. It is recognised over the life of the membership, so the unearned part sits on the balance sheet as a contract liability called deferred revenue. Deferred revenue means membership money collected but not yet booked as income; it is a promise to deliver stays, not a loan. It was ₹6,017.47 crore at 30 September 2026 on the consolidated accounts, and ₹5,925.41 crore at 31 March 2026[1][10]. The company's own figure for the Indian business alone is ₹5,828 crore[11].

The business has two segments. MHRIL is the Indian business, with membership income, subscription fees and resort income. HCRO is Holiday Club Resorts Oy in Finland, a timeshare and spa-hotel group that the company owns through Luxembourg and Mauritius holding companies[7][10]. HCRO had €36.5 million of revenue in the September 2026 quarter and a profit after tax of minus €0.3 million[13]. The table splits the half-year to 30 September 2026 by segment. Where the cash goes

The India business spends on two things at once: new and refurbished resorts, and rent on leased resorts. Capital spending was ₹420.62 crore in FY26 and ₹298.35 crore in the first half of FY27, and the company says it will spend about as much again in the next six months[2][10][14]. Leases are the other call on cash. A lease liability is the rent the company has promised to pay for leased resorts, which Ind AS 116, the Indian lease standard, shows as a financial liability. Lease payments (principal plus interest) were ₹351.69 crore in FY26 and ₹182.00 crore in the first half of FY27[2][10].

Resorts earn from rooms, food, spa and experiences, and behave like a hotel. The company does not publish an average room rate. It started reporting total revenue per available room night (TRevPAR) this year: ₹8,777 in the quarter, up 6 percent[13][14].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

In plain words: the resorts are busy and the group is well rated, but profit is thin and the Finnish unit loses money. This chapter gives the evidence on both sides.

The case for the business is a long-lived membership base, high occupancy and growing resort income. Resort revenue rose from ₹342 crore in FY23 to ₹443 crore in FY26, and occupancy was 84.4, 84.6, 84.0 and 80.7 percent over those four years[7]. The company's bank-loan rating from India Ratings is IND AA- with a stable outlook[8]. The Indian business has no borrowings of its own, which the company's own ratio table says directly: its standalone debt-to-equity and interest-cover ratios are 'Not applicable'[7]. The case against it is the consolidated balance sheet and the chart. Chapter 2B scores the operating evidence and Chapter 3 tests the accounts.

Where the strength is

Standalone MHRIL earned EBITDA of ₹593 crore in FY26, up 21 percent, and profit after tax before one-offs of ₹241 crore, up 22 percent[7]. EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. The standalone figure is the company's, not an audited line, so treat it as management's framing. Occupancy was 75.6 percent in the September 2026 quarter, up 220 basis points from 73.4 percent a year before, and resort revenue grew 15 percent[13]. Membership upgrades, where existing members move to a higher plan, grew 26 percent to ₹83 crore[11].

The balance sheet also holds real financial assets. Cash and liquid investments were ₹872.37 crore at 30 September 2026 on our count (cash, other bank balances and investments), and the company's own March 2026 table, which also deducts 'other deposits', shows net cash of ₹356.78 crore[4][10]. Part of those deposits are inter-corporate deposits placed with Mahindra & Mahindra Financial Services, a group lender: ₹150 crore was invested in FY26[9]. That is a related-party exposure.

Where the weakness is

Consolidated profit is thin and falling. Profit after tax attributable to owners was ₹127.59 crore in FY25, ₹69.51 crore in FY26 and ₹11.29 crore in the first half of FY27 against ₹25.73 crore a year before[1][10]. Interest and depreciation are the reason: finance costs were ₹181.05 crore and depreciation ₹409.29 crore in FY26 against EBITDA of ₹740.91 crore[1]. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is 8.12 percent on Screener and has been under 15 percent for each of the last five years[19]. The Finnish unit lost ₹70.92 crore before tax in the half-year and its assets are growing[10].

Receivables are the second weak joint. Net trade receivables were ₹1,387.74 crore at 31 March 2026, which is 169 days of revenue, and 45.9 percent of them were more than a year past due, against 38.0 percent a year earlier[1][6]. They are instalments on memberships, and the company defers income at the start so the credit risk is smaller than in an ordinary sale. Members lose access until overdue amounts are paid[6].

Governance and ownership

Promoters held 66.73 percent at 30 September 2026, unchanged for the quarter. Foreign investors fell from 4.01 to 3.41 percent in the quarter and from 5.30 percent at March, and public holders rose to 18.93 percent[17][19]. The company-hosted June 2026 shareholding pattern answers 'No' to whether any promoter shares are pledged[16]. The September 2026 primary filing was not readable here, so for that quarter we say only that no pledge was observed in the readable evidence: two aggregators show 0.0 percent[17][18]. Promoter holding has moved by less than a tenth of a point in three years, so the controlling group is not selling. The selling has come from foreign funds, not from the controlling group.
