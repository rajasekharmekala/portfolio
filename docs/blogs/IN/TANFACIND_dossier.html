1. What the company does

Tanfac turns a mineral into chemicals. It imports fluorspar, a rock rich in fluorine, mostly from South Africa, Thailand, Vietnam and Kenya, and reacts it with sulphuric acid to make hydrofluoric acid, called HF. Almost every fluorine product starts from HF, much as most plastics start from a few oil-based building blocks.[7] Everything happens at one 60-acre complex in Cuddalore, about 200 kilometres south of Chennai, where the company began commercial production in March 1985.[7][15] About 700 people work there, and installed capacity is roughly 1,35,000 tonnes a year across all product lines.[1][7]

The plant makes four product families. Sulphuric acid, with capacity of 1,00,000 tonnes a year, is made first and used inside the plant to make HF, with the surplus sold. Anhydrous hydrofluoric acid (AHF), the pure form, has capacity of 30,000 tonnes. Diluted HF (DHF) is the same acid mixed with water, and a purer grade of it, called solar-grade DHF, is used to clean silicon wafers during solar-cell manufacture; Tanfac has 20,000 tonnes of that capacity and says it is the only Indian producer.[1][7] Specialty fluorides, a set of salts used in aluminium smelting, pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals, have 5,000 tonnes of capacity. In FY26 the acid plants ran close to full, at 101 percent and 95 percent, while the specialty fluorides plant ran at 41 percent.[7]

Who pays for all this? About 75 percent of revenue comes from HF-based products. Management splits that as roughly 35 percent from solar-grade DHF, 40 percent from AHF and commercial-grade DHF, and the rest from other value-added products.[14] Named customers include Jindal, Piramal, Tata, Adani and Premier Energies.[14] One-third of the HF goes to solar customers, one-third to pharmaceutical, agrochemical and refrigerant customers, and one-third to steel, glass and the company's own fluoride salts.[14] In FY26 about 95 percent of sales were in India and 5 percent were exports.[5][7] The company does not publish what share of revenue its biggest single customer provides, so concentration is something we cannot measure; we record that as not published.[8]

The money-making rule is simple: sell the acid for more than the raw materials cost. Materials were ₹453.47 crore of the ₹711.07 crore of FY26 revenue, about 64 percent, and the share left after direct costs, the gross margin, was 37.46 percent against 45.20 percent a year earlier.[2][5] The cheap and expensive ends of the range are far apart. Commercial-grade AHF sells monthly or quarterly with no long contract, at ₹275 to ₹280 a kilogram in the June 2026 quarter, while solar-grade DHF earns ₹15 to ₹20 a kilogram more for about ₹3 to ₹5 of extra processing cost.[14][15] That is why management keeps moving volume from commercial grades into solar grade, and why the plan below adds a refrigerant-gas plant on top of the same HF.

The growth plan has three legs. First, the solar-grade DHF plant, finished in two phases in June and October 2025 for about ₹47 crore, has orders of ₹1,068 crore to be executed over about 3.5 years, covering roughly 85 percent of its capacity.[13][1] Second, a 20,000-tonne plant for HFC-32, also called R-32, a refrigerant gas for room air conditioners, is being built for about ₹390 crore. The annual report targets commissioning by the end of the third quarter of FY27, which is 31 December 2026. On the July 2026 call management was more specific: commissioning at the end of November 2026, with ramp-up in December and January.[13][12][14][1] Third, a pipeline of electronic-grade acid, more HF and fluoropolymers is being studied, with about ₹300 crore of near-term spending and ₹1,500 to ₹1,700 crore over four years mentioned on the July 2026 call.[14] Each leg moves the company from selling a commodity toward selling a specified product under contract, and each needs cash, which explains the share sales described next.

Who owns the company, and why that matters

Tanfac is a joint-sector company, which means a state agency and a private company share it. TIDCO, the Tamil Nadu Industrial Development Corporation, holds 23.99 percent and Anupam Rasayan India Limited holds 24.99 percent. Together the two promoters held 48.98 percent at 30 September 2026, and none of those shares is pledged to lenders.[17] Anupam Rasayan bought its stake from the Aditya Birla Group in March 2022, and ICRA, the credit rating agency, says Anupam has management control despite holding under 25 percent, because of a government guideline for joint-sector firms.[12] Anupam has also promised not to sell its holding, through a non-disposal undertaking, as of 30 September 2026.[12]

The link has costs and benefits. The benefit is technical help, customer access and a parent that can support Tanfac if needed, which ICRA counts in its rating.[12] The cost is a related-party relationship: sales to related parties were 1.88 percent of FY26 sales, of which about ₹9 crore, or 1.2 percent of revenue, was to Anupam at what the company says is arm's length, meaning the price an outsider would pay.[8][15] Anupam also subscribed to the September 2026 share issue at ₹2,341 a share, paying ₹60.88 crore for 2,60,065 shares, which means the parent added to its stake at a price 34 percent below today's ₹3,544.30.[20][31]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A business like this is strong when four things hold together. It makes its key input itself. It sells at prices that cover that input. Its customers sign up in advance. And it funds its growth without strain. Tanfac has the first and fourth today. It has the third for two of its three growth lines. It has only a partial claim to the second. The evidence follows, and each claim carries its source.

The strongest point is integration. Tanfac makes its own sulphuric acid and HF. A captive power plant recovers waste heat to cover a large part of the site's energy. So the company does not buy its two main intermediates from rivals.[7] That matters most for refrigerant gas, where HF is the largest input, because a producer that already owns HF avoids a margin that merchant buyers pay.[7] The 2024 doubling of HF capacity from 14,850 to 29,700 tonnes cost about ₹100 crore. That is roughly ₹6.7 lakh a tonne, found by dividing ₹100 crore by the 14,850 added tonnes. The company calls this the lowest in its industry. We can confirm the arithmetic but not the comparison.[11][9] Handling HF is also a barrier in itself: ICRA notes the product is hazardous, transport needs a permit from the explosives regulator PESO, and Tanfac holds one.[12]

Returns are good for a business of this type. Return on capital employed (ROCE), meaning profit before interest and tax divided by the money invested in the business, was 20.19 percent in FY26, and return on equity was 20.48 percent.[1] Both are lower than the 33.67 and 32.50 percent of FY25, which the board itself calls an exceptional year when a temporary shortage lifted prices.[1][6] The balance sheet is sound: borrowings were ₹92.81 crore against equity of ₹373.17 crore at March 2026, a ratio of 0.25 (the debt-to-equity ratio), and the interest bill was covered 20.8 times.[3][12] ICRA rates Tanfac [ICRA]A+ with a stable outlook and says its financial profile is healthy.[12]

The weaknesses are the ones typical of an acid maker. HF and sulphuric acid are commodities, and ICRA says competition from domestic makers and from Chinese imports limits pricing power.[12] The FY26 gross-margin drop from 45.20 to 37.46 percent shows the cost. Sulphur averaged about ₹38 to ₹40 a kilogram against ₹30 a year earlier. By July 2026 management said it had risen to about ₹105. Selling prices catch up after a lag of 30 to 45 days.[5][14][15] Management also described a 'temporary demand-supply gap' in the third quarter of FY25 that gave a profit of about ₹30 crore in that single quarter, which the finance chief called unusual; that quarter, not FY26, is the outlier.[15] Output risk is real as well: Tanfac lost about ₹70 crore of HF production in FY26 to an unplanned stoppage of its old HF plant.[15]

Where Tanfac stands against its listed peers

Tanfac is the smallest of the four listed fluorine names compared here, and it sits upstream of the others. Navin Fluorine earned an operating margin of 34 percent over the last twelve months, on a return on capital of 21.0 percent. SRF earned 22 percent and 14.6 percent. Gujarat Fluorochemicals earned 26 percent and 9.64 percent. Tanfac's operating margin was 15.3 percent in the June 2026 quarter, on a return on capital of 20.19 percent.[24][25][26][13][1] A lower margin than downstream peers is what an acid maker should expect. The refrigerant plant is designed to close part of that gap by moving up one step in the chain. Tanfac is the most expensive of the four on earnings. Navin trades at 52.7 times earnings against Tanfac's 104.6 times, so the market is paying more for Tanfac's future than for Navin's present.[24][31]

The government rule that decides the refrigerant business

India has agreed with other countries to cut the production of HFC refrigerants. An Environment Ministry memorandum of 1 April 2026 sets the schedule. Production and use freeze from 1 January 2028. The freeze level is the average of 2024, 2025 and 2026 plus a share of an older refrigerant baseline. Output then has to fall 10 percent by 2032, 20 percent by 2037, 30 percent by 2042 and 85 percent by 2047. No environmental clearance is granted for new HFC capacity after 31 December 2027, and capacity already cleared must start production by that date.[27][28] Tanfac holds its clearance, and the plant is on course to start before the deadline.

The open question is quota, the share of the freeze limit each producer may make. Management says quota will be set in proportion to the capacity of 'operational units as on 1 January 2028'. It says 2027 is a 'free year' for production. It puts India's total quota at 115 to 125 thousand tonnes a year, against about 50,000 tonnes of existing capacity.[15] The baseline averages 2024 to 2026, years in which Tanfac made no refrigerant. A literal reading would give it little or none. Management reads the text differently, and the government has not yet published the rules.[27][15] We treat this as the single largest unresolved fact about the company and come back to it in chapters 6 and 7.

Competition inside HF itself

Peers are also adding HF capacity, and the chief executive acknowledged the point on the July 2026 call. His answer was that one-third of Tanfac's HF goes into solar-grade DHF 'where we don't have any competition'. About 4,000 tonnes are used inside the plant, and more will be used once refrigerant gas starts.[14] He also argued that Chinese AHF is not a threat because it is a controlled, dual-use chemical that needs export licences, and its tank logistics are poor.[14] Our reading is that the moat is strongest in solar-grade DHF, where approval cycles are slow and the customer specifies the product. It is weakest in commodity AHF, which is sold month by month at market prices. The company's cited study has India demand rising from 110 to 175 thousand tonnes between FY26 and FY31, a 9.7 percent yearly rate. The call and the annual report quote different starting figures for domestic demand, and the report does not reconcile them.[5][14]

5. What could happen next

A stock price is a bet on future profit, and a reverse DCF works backwards from the price to the profit growth the bet needs. DCF means discounted cash flow, the standard method of valuing a business by the present value of what it will earn. In reverse, we fix the price, discount at 12 percent a year (our Indian rate), assume the business sells for 20 times its profit in year ten, and solve for the yearly growth that makes today's value fit. The session ledger does this on trailing net profit of ₹74.74 crore, itself derived from market value and the trailing price-to-earnings ratio, and gets 28.85 percent a year for ten years.[31][33]

That figure is a useful alarm, but it has limits. It uses profit and not cash, so it ignores that Tanfac spent more on plants than it earned in operating cash in FY25 and FY26. It uses trailing profit, which excludes the refrigerant plant that most of the price is paying for. And its market value of ₹7,818 crore counts shares differently from the company's own filings, which give ₹7,668 crore. The rows below add four more readings built from audited numbers.

Consensus means the average forecast of analysts. Here it is one analyst, so the word overstates it. That analyst expects FY27 earnings per share of ₹46.3 and FY28 of ₹106.1, which is 129 percent growth, and sales of ₹925.5 crore in FY27 and ₹1,602 crore in FY28; operating profit of ₹166.4 crore and ₹367.2 crore; and a price target of ₹3,750.[22][21][31] No history of revisions is published, so we cannot say whether estimates are rising or falling, and the ledger labels the trend NO_CONSENSUS.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 stands on a weakest assumption. This chapter names it, says what evidence would prove it wrong, and gives the date by which we will know. For the base price of ₹4,190.95 the weakest assumption is that the refrigerant plant earns what management says it will. For the bull case it is the 25 percent margin. For the bear case it is that a miss would be large enough to reset the multiple.

We then do something less comfortable. We write the strongest honest case for selling the whole holding, as a reviewer who owns none of it would argue it. The case uses only facts in this report. We do not claim it is correct, and we answer it at the end.

Flaw 1: the new plant may be shut out by the quota rules

This is the largest flaw. The plant makes the refrigerant gas HFC-32. India's phase-down freezes HFC production from 1 January 2028 at a baseline equal to the average of 2024, 2025 and 2026 output, with a further cut of 10 percent by 2032. Tanfac made none of this gas in those years. The Environment Ministry memorandum bars new clearances after 31 December 2027. Management says the quota will be set in proportion to the capacity of units operating on 1 January 2028, with 2027 a free year.[27][28][15] The government has not published that quota method, and a different method could give a new entrant little.

Here is why it matters in rupees. The contracted volumes are worth about ₹649 crore a year. At the 30 percent margin management cites for the project, that is about ₹195 crore of operating profit. It is more than half of the ₹367 crore the analyst expects for FY28. If the plant were blocked from 2028, FY28 earnings per share would fall by roughly half. [13][14][21]Two pieces of evidence would settle it. One is a published quota rule or allocation, which management expects during 2027. The other is first output at the plant, which the third-quarter results due in February 2027 should show.

Flaw 2: margins may stay near 15 percent

Margin has been 15 to 16.5 percent for five straight quarters. Management's own range for the existing business is 15 to 18 percent. The promised lift to 25 percent in FY28 depends on the new plant, so the old business may stay stuck. Sulphur, the key input, rose from about ₹30 to about ₹105 according to the July call. Selling prices catch up only after 30 to 45 days. Commercial HF has no long contract. The old HF plant ran at 73 percent in the June quarter.[14][18] The chief executive also accepted that peers are adding HF capacity. In a commodity market that is a reason to expect price pressure.

The evidence to watch is the September 2026 quarter, which must be reported by 14 November 2026 under the 45-day rule. A margin of 16 percent or more, with HF use back above 85 percent, supports the base case. A margin under 15 percent with HF use under 70 percent would turn two of our five KPIs to FAIL and trigger the replace rule.[33]

Flaw 3: a few customers and fixed prices

Both growth lines depend on very few buyers. The ₹1,068 crore of solar-grade orders is held by two customers, Kredence Electronics Materials (₹732 crore) and Krishna PV Resources (₹336 crore).[16] The Japanese customer alone is ₹337.5 crore of the ₹649 crore a year of refrigerant contracts, or 52 percent.[13] The February 2026 presentation described that Japanese deal as a letter of intent. Management said on the July call that it is a firm contract. A 5,000-tonne agreement with a large multinational was still a memorandum of understanding that month.[16][14]

The prices are also fixed in a way that limits the upside. About 65 percent of refrigerant volume is sold at around $5.5 a kilogram, on a formula that passes through raw-material cost. The chief executive says this is deliberate, to win customers fast. An analyst put spot at $9 to $10.[14] The solar-cell industry plans to grow from 35 to 210 gigawatts, and the chief executive said growth has been slower than hoped. If factories expand slowly, solar-grade capacity stays full on existing orders and growth slows to the pace of those orders.[14]

Flaw 4: the cash raised may not be enough

The company raised about ₹349 crore of equity. The refrigerant plant costs about ₹390 crore. Another ₹300 crore of near-term plants is planned. Management mentioned ₹1,500 to ₹1,700 crore over four years.[19][12][14] Free cash flow was negative in both FY25 and FY26. ICRA expects the balance to come from internal cash and term debt. It lists total debt above 1.5 times operating profit as a trigger for a downgrade.[4][12] That means more borrowing, or a third share issue, within two years. The share count has already grown 8.4 percent.

Flaw 5: thin trading and holders sitting on large gains

The ledger shows 17,432 shares traded on 16 October, about ₹6.2 crore, so a large seller can move the price. Two companies hold 14 percent of the shares outside the promoters: Kamaljyot Investments at 9.45 percent and Anshul Specialty Molecules at 4.62 percent.[31][17] The QIP buyers paid ₹1,985.83 and the promoter's issue priced at ₹2,341. Many holders therefore sit on gains of 50 to 78 percent after four months, which gives them a reason to sell on bad news.[19][20] Your position is small enough that this does not limit your ability to sell. It does affect how fast the price can fall.

Flaw 6: one site and a hazardous product

All output comes from a single complex in Cuddalore, and the main product, HF, is hazardous. ICRA calls accident risk low in frequency and high in impact, and notes that the company has run safely for many years.[12] The cost of an outage has already shown up. About ₹70 crore of HF production was lost in FY26 to an unplanned stoppage of the old HF plant. The sulphuric acid plant was also shut for maintenance in the June 2026 quarter, which meant buying grid power.[15][14]

The base case needs two things at once: the analyst's FY28 EPS of ₹106.1, and a multiple of 39.5 times. We tested the multiple alone, holding earnings exactly at ₹106.1. At 39.5 times, the price is ₹4,190.95, a gain of 18.2 percent. At 33.62 times, the FY25 year-end ratio, it is ₹3,567.08, a gain of 0.6 percent. At the ten-year average of 21.09 times, it is ₹2,237.65, a loss of 36.9 percent.[22][23] The last figure is below our rule stop of ₹2,325.10 and below the ₹2,951.00 exit we use for what is left. So even full delivery of the forecast gives almost nothing at the FY25 multiple, and a return to the long-run average would cost more than the published bear case of ₹2,497.00 without any earnings miss.

We do not assign a probability to the ten-year-average case, because the company is changing shape and the old average is not necessarily fair value. The point is the size of the dependence: the same earnings give a gain of 18.2 percent, 0.6 percent or a loss of 36.9 percent depending only on the multiple. The price itself is the evidence on whether the market keeps paying more than 33 times forward earnings, so the protective exits matter more than the forecast.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict for the 55 shares you hold is KEEP_COMPOUNDING, and we use it as a labelled exception. The strict conditions of that label are not met. It needs every gate passed and at least 4 of 5 operating checks passing (3 of 4 where four checks are used), and Tanfac has two tests below their floors and 2 of 5 checks passing. Our label list has no value for hold-but-do-not-add, and no other label fits. Trim needs a stock that is extended, above RSI 75, at its bull target or over the 15 percent cap. Replace needs a closed stop or two failed checks. None of those holds. So we use the nearest label and give the operating instruction in plain words: hold the 55 shares, do not add, and trim 17 to 27 shares on two closes below ₹3,306.33. Do not read the label as maintain a full position.

Why not buy more? The stock fails the reward-to-risk floor, at 0.53 to 1 against 3.0 for the core bucket and 3.5 for tactical. Only 2 of 5 operating checks pass. Cash conversion is 0.52 against 0.70. The price sits in the standard band of our support ladder, 1.19 ATR above the 20-day average. That band permits a standard-size purchase only for a name that has passed the quality gates, and this one has not. RSI is 63.88, so the rule against chasing above 70 is not what blocks us. The fundamentals gates are.[31][33]

Why not sell? No sell rule has triggered. The stock closed at ₹3,544.30, far above the structural stop of ₹2,325.10. The trend stage is 2 and not 4. No operating check has failed. RSI of 63.88 is under the 75 level at which a trim for being extended would apply. The bull target of ₹4,746.05 has not been reached. Your weight of 4.26 percent is far under the 15 percent hard cap and the 22 percent runaway-winner ceiling.

The let-winners-run rule is not satisfied here, and we do not claim it. That rule protects a Stage 2 winner whose estimates are steady or rising, and with one analyst and no revision history we cannot show that.
