1. What the company does

Tanfac makes fluorine chemicals. The most important is anhydrous hydrofluoric acid, usually called AHF, which is the basic ingredient for a long list of other fluorine products. Think of it as a raw material supplier further up the chain: the company itself turns part of its AHF into aluminium fluoride and solar-grade dilute acid. Its own list of applications covers chemicals, pharmaceuticals, refrigerants, metallurgy and fertilisers. [3] The company's own website describes it as among the largest producers of AHF and aluminium fluoride in India. [3] The product list on the aggregator profile includes sulphuric acid, potassium fluoride and boron trifluoride complexes, which broadens the range beyond AHF. [2]

How the three layers work

Every candidate passes three layers in order. Layer 1 is a set of hard gates, each a pass or fail question with no room for judgement. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five operating numbers chosen for this business, each marked PASS, WATCH or FAIL. Layer 3 is timing: how far the price sits from a verified support level, measured in average true range (ATR). ATR is the typical daily price swing over the last 14 sessions. Dividing a distance by ATR tells you how many normal days of movement separate the price from its support.

For this holding, Layer 2 is not used to qualify the stock, because Layer 1 has failed. The operating tests in chapter 3 are run only to test the replacement rule for a holding, which is a separate question.

The verdict words

For a holding, the verdict is one of four words. KEEP_COMPOUNDING means the position stays at full size. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP means add on a pullback to support. TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING means sell 30 to 50 percent to lock in gains because the stock is extended. REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER means the thesis or the trend has failed and the capital should move to a stronger candidate. Each word has a rule that triggers it, and this dossier names the rule for the decision it reaches.

Three rules that shape the numbers

The first rule is the stop. For a Core name, the stop sits below the lowest of the 20-day swing low, the 50-day average and the 200-day average, minus one ATR. The second rule is the asymmetry ratio. It compares the distance from today's price to a target with the distance from today's price to the stop. A Core name needs at least 3.0 to one. The third rule is the target itself. It must be anchored, meaning built from forward earnings multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, or from a measured move on a documented base. A brokerage target that is not built this way does not count.

A price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share. A ratio of 20 means investors pay 20 rupees for each rupee of yearly profit. A measured move adds the height of a base to the level at which the price breaks out of it. Every number in this dossier should also be no older than 60 days. Annual and quarterly figures often are older than that, so each one is dated and flagged in the run notes rather than hidden.

What the stage words mean

A stock in Stage 2 is in a sustained uptrend: its price sits above rising 50-day and 200-day averages. Stage 3 is a topping phase, where the uptrend has stalled and the price drifts around its averages. Stage 4 is a breakdown, usually below a falling long-term average. A breakout is the move up and out of a sideways base, which often marks the start of a new uptrend. The run's price ledger assigns this stock to Stage 3, and the ledger is the authority for the stage gate.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when it earns high returns on the money invested in it, turns those earnings into cash, and does not need heavy borrowing to grow. It is weak when one of those three is missing. Tanfac passes the first and third tests comfortably. It fails the second, because its reported profit has not been turning into cash, and its profit has fallen while its sales rose. The rest of this chapter explains each point.

The strengths: returns and a light balance sheet

Return on capital employed, or ROCE, measures how much operating profit a company earns for each rupee tied up in the business. Tanfac's ROCE was 23.9 percent in the year to March 2026, and its return on equity was 20.3 percent. [1] Both clear the 15 percent floor in the gate table. The figure fell from 42 percent in the year to March 2025, so the return is still high but has fallen sharply. [1]

Leverage is low. Borrowings were ₹93 crore at March 2026, against equity of about ₹373 crore made up of ₹10 crore of share capital and ₹363 crore of reserves. That gives a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.25, well below the 0.70 limit. [1] A second provider reports total debt of ₹92.8 crore and equity of ₹373.2 crore at March 2026, which agrees. [31] The company says the equity raises made this year have left it net debt-free. Net debt means borrowings minus cash, so a net debt-free company holds more cash than it owes. [8][9] Working capital is the money tied up in stock and debtors, net of what the company owes its suppliers. It has also improved. The working-capital cycle fell from 51.6 days to 34.2 days, which means the company is holding less cash in stock and debtors for each rupee of sales. [1]

What this means for an owner: the company does not need much money to grow and is not carrying a heavy debt load. Those are real strengths. They do not, on their own, answer the question of whether profit is reliable, which is the subject of the next heading.

The weaknesses: profit is falling and is not turning into cash

The profit trend is the main concern. Sales rose from ₹557 crore in the year to March 2025 to ₹711 crore in the year to March 2026. Net profit fell from ₹88 crore to ₹70 crore over the same period. [1] More sales with less profit means each rupee of sales is earning less. The operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, fell from 24 percent to 16 percent. [1] A second provider defines operating income differently and reports 13.9 percent for the year to March 2026, so the two measures should not be mixed. [30] The company blames higher sulphur, fuel and power costs, and the cost of shipping through the West Asia route. [6][9]

Cash conversion asks whether reported profit is turning into real cash. Over the three years to March 2026, operating cash flow totalled ₹110 crore against net profit of ₹210 crore. That is a ratio of 0.52. The floor in the gate table is 0.70. [1] A second data provider, StockAnalysis, uses S&P Global figures and reports the same operating cash flows: ₹43.4 crore, ₹32.8 crore and ₹34.2 crore for FY2026, FY2025 and FY2024. The ratio therefore does not rest on one source. [29] A ratio below one means the company has booked more profit than it collected in cash. A ratio this low is a warning that the profit may include amounts not yet paid, or that the company is spending heavily on working capital. The reported profit may still be correct, but the cash has not followed.

Margin fell in FY2026, from FY2025's 24 percent to 16 percent, and the June quarter slipped a further point or so against the same quarter last year. FY2025 was the weakest year for cash conversion, although its margin was the high point. Revenue rose 6.3 percent to ₹187.2 crore, but net profit fell 13.4 percent to ₹16.8 crore, from ₹19.4 crore a year earlier. [9] EBITDA, or earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, fell 1.4 percent to ₹28.6 crore, and its margin was 15.3 percent. [6]

What this means for an owner: the business is earning a good return on capital, but the profit it reports is not yet matched by cash, and the operating margin has fallen by a third in a single year. That is why the strengths in the previous heading do not settle the question. The next chapter tests the numbers against the rules.

How it was calculated

The formula is (target minus close) divided by (close minus stop). The close is ₹3,197.70 and the formula stop is ₹2,315.07, so the risk is ₹882.63. The target is the missing piece. The range

Without a verified target, the range cannot be drawn. The only reference points are the prices needed to reach each ratio. A ratio of 1.0 needs a target of about ₹4,080, and a ratio of 3.0 needs ₹5,846. Neither is anchored to a published earnings figure, so neither is used as a target.

If the number is off

If a verified anchor appeared, the ratio would replace N/A and the gate would be run again. The stop also matters. If the stop were set at the prior-run level of ₹2,920, the risk would shrink to ₹277.70 and the ratio would rise for the same target.
