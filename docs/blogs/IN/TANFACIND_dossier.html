1. A Fluorine Platform Through Several Operating Eras

TANFAC’s history is best read as a series of operating transitions within one chemistry family, rather than as a sequence of unrelated diversifications. The company reports commercial production beginning in March 1985 at its Cuddalore complex, initially with hydrofluoric acid (HF), aluminium fluoride and sulphuric acid. The subsequent record includes HF liquefaction and specialty fluoride production in the late 1980s, a sulphuric-acid expansion and captive power in 2002, and further inorganic-fluoride and specialty-chemistry work through 2004–20. The FY26 annual report gives an incorporation year of 1972, while ICRA’s company history gives 1974; the sources therefore do not agree on the incorporation date, but both place the manufacturing platform’s start in the 1980s. The post-2022 sequence is concrete. The expanded HF plant was commissioned in October 2024, increasing reported capacity from about 14,850 to 30,000 tonnes per year. Two 10,000-tonne-per-year solar-grade diluted HF (DHF) phases followed in June and October 2025. FY26 was a record year for revenue and production, but the company also described FY25’s unusually strong realizations as a temporary demand–supply gap; higher sulphur costs and depreciation on new assets then coincided with lower FY26 operating margins. This is a business-cycle lesson: capacity additions can lift output and sales while input spreads and depreciation pull earnings in the opposite direction. [1][2][3]

You should keep the longer-cycle evidence in proportion. The CEO Magazine profile says that global turmoil in 2007 and strong Chinese competition left TANFAC with minimal operations; it offers historical context but does not provide audited annual figures. The current public record is richer for FY23–FY27 than for the earlier period. It supports a narrative of past disruption, a later ownership and operating reset, and a new build-out, but not a quantified decade-long cycle comparison. [9][1][2][6]

The distinction to preserve

A commissioned plant, an announced project, a customer order and a profitable ramp are four different milestones. The HF and solar-grade DHF plants have moved into operation; the refrigerant-gas facility was still progressing toward commissioning in the latest supplied transcript. Treat these as separate evidence states. [1][6]

A more precise chronology helps separate what the company has actually built from what it now intends to build. The audited FY26 report places the early platform at Cuddalore: commercial production began in March 1985, with hydrofluoric acid, aluminium fluoride and sulphuric acid. The investor presentation adds HF liquefaction in 1987, specialty fluoride production in 1989, sulphuric-acid expansion and a captive power plant in 2002, and diversification into inorganic fluorides and specialty organic chemicals across 2004–20. The annual report states incorporation in 1972, while ICRA’s profile says 1974; that discrepancy should remain visible rather than be silently resolved. Both accounts place the operating base in the same Cuddalore industrial complex. [1][2][7]

The ownership transition is operationally relevant but should be parsed carefully. TANFAC remains a joint-sector company with TIDCO and Anupam Rasayan as co-promoters; ICRA says Anupam has management control and is strategically linked through fluorination. Management credits the relationship with technical expertise, specialty-chemistry capability and access to global customers. The development path since 2022 has remained within fluorine chemistry: expand HF, add a high-purity grade, and prepare for a refrigerant-gas step. This is a coherent adjacency strategy. It is not yet proof that all future applications will share the same unit economics, customer qualification duration or working-capital profile. [1][3][7]

Counterfactual test: what if the cycle turns before the new platform matures?

If commodity HF demand or customer activity weakens, existing high core utilization may fall before newer downstream products reach repeatable scale. If raw-material costs rise faster than contractual pass-through, revenue can grow while percentage margins compress; management describes a lag, and ICRA flags spread volatility. If competition adds capacity, first-mover status alone may not defend commodity economics. A sound history therefore gives weight to both the completed HF / solar-grade projects and the older evidence of cyclicality. [1][3][6][7][9]

3. From Feedstock to Customer: Integration, Logistics and Cash

The physical chain begins with fluorspar, which the company says it imports from multiple geographies including South Africa, Thailand, Vietnam and Kenya, and sulphur, sourced domestically. Sulphuric acid is made at the site and used in HF production; HF then moves into AHF/DHF grades and downstream derivatives. The company also reports captive power supported by waste-heat recovery, dedicated acid tanks, an HF cylinder-filling station and effluent-treatment infrastructure at its 60-acre Cuddalore complex. [1][2]

The plant’s position near Chennai Port supports inbound raw-material logistics and export shipments. TANFAC reports more than 105 clients across more than ten countries, with FY26 sales about 95% domestic and 5% export. The supplied public materials identify customer types and some long-term supply arrangements but do not describe distributor margins, route-to-market economics, customer concentration by product or named confidential buyers. Management says raw-material cost pass-through in contracts can lag input changes by roughly 30–45 days; ICRA separately flags spread, working-capital and hazardous-HF transport risks. [1][2][3][6][7]

One practical, illustrative chain is a solar-grade DHF shipment to a qualified photovoltaic-wafer producer: TANFAC converts captive HF into the high-purity grade, the customer qualifies samples, and contracted deliveries are scheduled against the disclosed order book. Another illustrative chain is HFC-32 supply to an HVAC buyer after commissioning and qualification. These describe plausible end-to-end archetypes from disclosed product uses and project information; they are not named customer cases or claims about individual shipments. [1][2][6]

4. Where the Moat Holds—and Where Competition Bites

TANFAC’s strongest advantage is operating know-how. It has made HF for decades. It also makes sulphuric acid and HF at one site, has captive power, and uses equipment built for hazardous materials. These assets can support steady quality and reliable supply. But they do not give TANFAC control of all fluorochemicals. ICRA says local rivals and imports, especially from China, limit pricing power in HF and sulphuric acid. [1][2][7][8]

Solar-grade DHF is more specialized. TANFAC says it was first to make this grade in India. It says a local wafer maker tested and approved its product. Purity and repeat supply can make a customer slow to switch. This is a lead, not a permanent monopoly. Other firms can invest and qualify over time. [1][2][6]

ICRA lists several risks: cycles, supply shocks, raw-material spreads, currency moves, customs rules, environmental policy and HF transport. Capex may also strain cash and debt cover. A CEO Magazine profile says TANFAC was badly hit by the 2007 crisis and Chinese competition. That is a retrospective, not an audited data series. It still points to a real risk: low-cost imports can meet a demand slump. [7][9]

Refrigerants face both demand and policy. India ratified the Kigali Amendment and must reduce HFC use on its national schedule. TANFAC calls HFC-32 a lower-GWP option. Rules, customer approval and other suppliers still shape sales. On the FY26 call, management said the government would set future quotas. [2][3][10][11]

9. Both Sides of the Aisle, Likely Outcomes & No-FOMO Action Plan

Core Investor Question: What must be true for TANFAC’s growth plan to compound—and what do you do with new cash and the current holding?

The strongest bull case is operationally specific. TANFAC has captive sulphuric acid, in-house HF, high FY26 utilization in its established core and a qualified solar-grade DHF product whose capacity has been debottlenecked to 25,000 TPA. The HFC-32 project was about 90% complete by the November 3 call, part of the planned output was under long-term supply arrangements, and management expects 95% utilization in FY28. If commissioning is on time, buyers qualify supply, 55–60% FY28 revenue growth arrives and the 24–26% EBITDA-margin guide is approached, revenue mix and operating leverage could improve materially. [1][2][4][6]

The steelman bear case is not that fluorine chemistry lacks demand. It is that TANFAC’s economics still depend on volatile HF and sulphur spreads, imported fluorspar, a single Cuddalore site, working capital and timely utilization of new assets. FY25/FY26 free cash flow was negative after investment; FY26 CFO ₹43.4 crore was below PAT ₹70.1 crore; H1 FY27 margins and PAT softened even as sales grew. The new plant and ₹250-crore future AHF/solar capex consume capital before their full cash returns are visible. ICRA cites Chinese/import competition, end-market cyclicality, policy/transport risks and execution as rating monitorables. [1][4][6][7]
