1. What the company does

Tanfac turns two cheap raw materials, sulphur and fluorspar, into a chain of fluorine chemicals. Sulphur becomes sulphuric acid in the company's own plant. The sulphuric acid then reacts with fluorspar to make anhydrous hydrofluoric acid, called AHF, which is the starting point for almost everything else it sells.[13][6] Everything sits on one 60-acre site leased from SIPCOT, the Tamil Nadu industrial park authority, with about 135,000 tonnes a year of installed capacity: 100,000 of sulphuric acid, about 30,000 of hydrofluoric acid and 5,000 of specialty fluorides.[6][2] The ownership is unusual. Tanfac is a joint-sector company, which means a state agency (Tamil Nadu Industrial Development Corporation, TIDCO, 23.99%) and a private chemical group (Anupam Rasayan, 24.99%) share the promoter stake, and Anupam holds management control.[14][7]

Who pays, and for what? In the first quarter of FY27 about 75% of sales came from hydrofluoric-acid products: roughly 35% of sales from solar-grade dilute hydrofluoric acid (DHF), about 40% from plain AHF and commercial grades, and the rest from sulphuric acid, oleum and specialty fluorides.[3] Solar-grade DHF is a high-purity grade, with metal impurities below 10 parts per billion, that cell makers use to etch and clean silicon wafers. Tanfac says it is the only Indian supplier of that grade.[3][4] The other buyers are steel and glass processors, and pharmaceutical and crop-chemical makers. Management named Jindal, Piramal, Cohizon, Tata, Adani and Premier Energies among its large customers.[3] In FY26 the ten largest customers bought 70.67% of revenue, up from 59.32% in FY25 and 57.10% in FY24, so a loss of one or two accounts would be felt immediately.[13]

How does the company earn its money? It earns the gap between what it charges and what sulphur, fluorspar, power and labour cost. AHF and commercial DHF are sold month by month or quarter by quarter, so their prices move with the market. Solar-grade DHF is sold under contracts of about three and a half years, with fixed volumes and prices that follow raw-material cost.[3] Management says higher input costs reach customers after a lag of 30 to 45 days.[3] That lag is the reason margins fall when sulphur jumps and recover later. Sulphur was around ₹30 a kilogram in FY25, averaged ₹38 to ₹40 in FY26 and, by the July 2026 call, was quoted at ₹105.[4][3] Revenue in FY26 was ₹711.1 crore, of which about 95% was domestic.[10]

What is changing is the product mix. The company is building a 20,000-tonne-a-year plant at the same site to make HFC-32, also called R-32, a refrigerant gas used in room air conditioners. Management targets commissioning at the end of November 2026, with sales from January 2027.[2][3] About 65% of the plant's capacity is covered by supply agreements or understandings, including a firm seven-year contract with a Japanese buyer for 7,500 tonnes a year worth about ₹337.5 crore a year.[2] This plant matters more than anything else in this report. It would roughly double the company's revenue within two years if it runs as planned, and it depends on a government permit system that is not yet written, which chapters 5 and 6 examine in detail.

Why one site and one acid explain most of the risk and most of the edge

Because everything starts from hydrofluoric acid, Tanfac behaves like a single-chain business, not a conglomerate. When the acid sells well, nearly every line benefits. When the acid price or the cost of sulphur moves against it, the whole company feels it at once. The fluorine chain is also hazardous. Anhydrous hydrofluoric acid is a controlled chemical that needs dedicated tanks and permits, and the rating agency ICRA lists transport rules and plant safety among the main credit risks, while noting that the company holds the required permit and has reported no safety lapse in recent years.[7]

The edge is cost and integration. Management says it doubled acid capacity from about 15,000 to about 30,000 tonnes in October 2024 for around ₹100 crore, which it calls the lowest cost per tonne in the industry.[4] Owning the acid plant means the new refrigerant plant buys its main input at cost, not from a rival, which is the core of the company's case for entering that market.[4] Fluorspar is bought from South Africa, Vietnam and Thailand under annual contracts, and management says supply is not a constraint while price and freight are.[3][4] The next chapter tests whether this edge is real, and chapter 2B tests whether management has done what it said it would do.

  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is profit from running the business before financing and accounting charges.
  • ROCE means return on capital employed. It is the yearly profit earned on each rupee the business uses.

  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money. Net debt is borrowings minus cash and liquid investments.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates. Free cash flow is that cash less spending on plant. FCF stands for free cash flow.

  • A pivot is the top of a base. A breakout is a move above it, and a retest is a pullback that tests it. A measured move is the height of a base projected above it as a target.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The honest summary is that Tanfac is a good small chemical company with a promising but unproven expansion, and its price already assumes the expansion works. The label 'partial fit' in the key figures means the company passes the compounder test only through the 0.50 manufacturing relief on cash conversion, and with one pass out of five operating measures. The strengths are real and documented. The weaknesses are mostly about what has not happened yet. This chapter lays out each side with its evidence, then compares Tanfac with larger fluorine peers.

Where the business is strong

The first strength is the balance sheet. At 31 March 2026 the company's debt-to-equity ratio was 0.25 and its debt service coverage ratio was 31.17.[10] ICRA reaffirmed an A+ (Stable) long-term rating on 16 October 2026, rated interest cover at 20.8 times for FY26 and called liquidity strong.[7] After a ₹250 crore equity placement in June 2026 the company described itself as net debt-free, and ICRA repeats that status.[2][7] A balance sheet this clean is why the hard debt and interest tests in chapter 3 are passed by a wide margin.

The second strength is contract cover. Two solar-grade DHF contracts worth ₹732 crore and ₹336 crore run to FY29, which is about ₹305 crore of sales a year against FY26 revenue of ₹711 crore.[6][2] The refrigerant plant has contracts or understandings worth about ₹649 crore a year, of which only the Japanese contract for about ₹337.5 crore a year is described by management as firm.[2][3] Contract cover of this kind lowers the chance that a new plant sits idle, though it does not prove the plant will be allowed to run, a point chapter 6 returns to.

The third strength is the backing of Anupam Rasayan, which has management control and, according to ICRA, has given a non-disposal undertaking over its entire 24.99% holding.[7] The promoters hold 48.98% of the shares and none are pledged.[14] The fourth is execution so far: the acid expansion and both solar-grade phases were finished on the dates management gave, as the walk-the-talk table in chapter 2B shows.[4][11] Chapter 2B gives the full record, including the misses.

Where the business is weak, and why that matters

The first weakness is that the margin has fallen for a year. The operating margin was 23.1% in FY25, 15.8% in FY26 and 15.3% in the June 2026 quarter, and gross margin fell from 45.2% to 37.5% over FY25 to FY26.[2][10] Management blames higher sulphur, fuel and power costs, a one-off rupee loss, and about ₹70 crore of lost acid output from an unplanned distillation-column problem in FY26.[4] Whatever the cause, every quarter since June 2025 has printed an operating margin between 14.9% and 16.5%.[2][6]

The second weakness is that the next growth engine is outside the company's experience. Tanfac has never made a refrigerant gas. The placement document that raised ₹250 crore says plainly that the project may not reach its goals, may run over time or cost, and needs production quotas and permissions that are limited and subject to change.[13] The third is concentration: ten customers account for 70.67% of revenue.[13] The fourth is cash. Spending on plant and equipment was ₹82.5 crore in FY26 against operating cash flow of ₹43.4 crore, so free cash flow was negative ₹39.1 crore.[10] The company has funded that gap with borrowing and, now, new shares.

Share count is a fifth point. The company had 19,950,000 shares at 31 March 2026 and 21,633,565 after the placement and a preferential issue in October, an increase of 8.4% that spreads each year's profit over more shares.[10][9] The new shares were sold at ₹1,985.83 (the institutional placement) and ₹2,341 (the preferential issue), both well below the current price, so the people who funded the expansion are sitting on large gains while the stock sits near the top of its range.[13][8]

How Tanfac compares with the larger fluorine producers

Tanfac's FY26 revenue of ₹711 crore is small next to Navin Fluorine (₹3,314 crore), Gujarat Fluorochemicals (₹4,996 crore) and SRF (₹15,787 crore).[1][15][16][17] Those companies already sell refrigerant gases and hold the production history that sets India's national baseline, which is the average of 2024, 2025 and 2026 output. Tanfac has no HFC-32 output in that window, which makes it the newest entrant in a crowded race.[20][22] An industry commentary from September 2026 lists SRF, Gujarat Fluorochemicals, Navin and a private entrant, Stallion, all expanding HFC-32 capacity before the freeze, and it describes the way the national limit will be divided among companies as the single largest unresolved question.[22] That commentary is secondary evidence and is labelled as such.

Against that, Tanfac has two things the larger peers lack in India: it is the only domestic maker of solar-grade DHF, and its acid is made in-house at a low capital cost. The strength of that position depends on how fast competitors qualify the same grade, which management says takes four to five months of learning and a ₹50 to ₹70 crore plant.[4][3] Anyone able to spend that money could try. The edge is therefore a head start, and a head start is worth less than a licence or a patent. This is why the report scores the business as sound but not as a wide-moat compounder, and it sets up the peer table in chapter 3.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Over five years Tanfac has grown sales faster than it has grown profit, and it has grown both faster than it has grown cash. Revenue rose from ₹320 crore in FY22 to ₹711.1 crore in FY26, a rise of 2.2 times, and the three-year compound growth rate from FY23 to FY26 is 23.8%.[1][2] Profit after tax moved from ₹53 crore to ₹70.1 crore over the same span, with a peak of ₹88.1 crore in FY25 in the middle, so the three-year profit growth rate is only 7.7%.[1][2] The gap between those two growth rates is the operating margin, which fell from 23.1% to 15.8%.

The balance sheet changed in the other direction. Borrowings were zero until FY24, reached ₹41.4 crore in FY25 and ₹92.8 crore in FY26 to part-fund new plants, and then the June 2026 placement and the October 2026 preferential issue brought in about ₹349 crore of equity.[2][13][8] Return on capital employed, which the company reports at 20.2% for FY26, has halved from 40% in FY23 because the capital base grew faster than profit.[10][6]

Cash is the weakest part. Operating cash flow has stayed between ₹32.8 crore and ₹43.4 crore for four years while profit and sales rose, because customers and inventory absorbed cash as the business grew. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in chapter 5 rests on an assumption, and some assumptions are weaker than others. This chapter takes each estimate, names the weakest input behind it, says what evidence would show the input wrong, and gives the date by which we would probably know. Dates that come from a company calendar are labelled expected, not confirmed, because the company had not announced them by 23 October 2026.[24] The chapter then states the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, which is the red-team test the portfolio method requires, and ends with the single claim on which the whole conclusion turns.

Estimate one: FY28 earnings per share of ₹77.3 assumes the plant may run at 65% from January 2028

The weakest input is the production quota. India has agreed to freeze its production and use of the hydrofluorocarbon gases from 1 January 2028 at a baseline equal to the average of 2024, 2025 and 2026 production plus 65% of an older baseline for a different gas, HCFC-22, and then to cut it in steps to 2047.[20] A government office memorandum of 1 April 2026 tells the environment-clearance authorities not to approve new capacity beyond 31 December 2027 and says plants that already hold clearance must be producing by then.[20][21] Tanfac's plant has its clearance and is due to start in late 2026, so it should meet the start-by date. What nobody has published is how the national limit will be divided among companies.[22]

Management reads the memorandum to mean that quotas will follow the production capacity of operating units on 1 January 2028, and says 2027 is a free year for production.[4] The primary text of the memorandum could not be fetched for this report, so we rely on management's quotation of its clauses and on secondary summaries, and we label both.[4][20] A different reading is possible: the baseline is built from production in 2024 to 2026, a window in which Tanfac made none. The Ozone Cell already issues production quotas for HCFC-22 each calendar year by office order, so a company-by-company system is a familiar tool for the ministry.[25] Management also says its contracts carry no penalty if the quota is not granted, which protects the company but also means buyers have not committed to take gas it cannot make.[4]

What would show the assumption wrong is a published rule or order that gives Tanfac a quota below about 13,000 tonnes a year, or none. The company's own placement document lists the quota and the approval from the external-affairs ministry as risks that could materially harm the business.[13] We would know when the amendment to the Ozone Depleting Substances Rules is published, which the secondary sources say is to come; no date has been announced, so the timing is unknown. The plant's start, by contrast, should be visible by about December 2026 or January 2027.[3]

Estimate two: a 25% margin on refrigerant output assumes prices and costs hold

The weakest input is the selling price. Management has contracted about 65% of output at about $5.5 a kilogram, which an investor on the July call put at roughly half the spot price of $9 to $10, a figure management did not contest.[3] Management replied that it chose speed to market and expects prices to soften to $6 to $7 as new capacity arrives, so it is content with a formula contract that passes cost through.[3] If spot prices stay above the contract price, the remaining 35% sold on the spot market would earn more than we assume. If new competitor capacity pushes spot below the contract price, buyers would have an incentive to keep to the contract, but the quota question above would then decide who may supply.

Cost is the second input. Management puts the cost of making the gas at ₹240 to ₹280 a kilogram against a contract price of about ₹450, a gap of ₹170 to ₹210 before overheads.[4][2] Our 25% margin leaves room for that gap to narrow. Sulphur is the main risk to cost, because it rose from about ₹30 a kilogram to ₹105 between FY25 and July 2026, and pass-through takes 30 to 45 days.[4][3] Management says anti-dumping duties apply to Chinese R-32, and a trade-remedies notice of April 2025 refers to a definitive duty imposed in 2021; we did not verify whether that duty has been extended since, and a change in it would alter local prices.[3][23] The evidence that would show our margin wrong is the operating margin in the first full quarter of sales. The company's results for the March 2027 quarter would show it; the date is not announced and, if the company follows its usual practice, would fall in the spring of 2027 (expected, not confirmed).[24]

Estimate three: the 44.4 times multiple assumes the market keeps paying the three-year median

The weakest input is the multiple itself. Our weekly series puts the stock at 34.5 times earnings in October 2023 and 106.3 times in October 2026, a threefold re-rating in three years.[19] The median of 44.4 times describes a period when the multiple was rising, not a stable level. A data site's average of year-end ratios from March 2022 to March 2026 is 27.1 times.[26] If the market came to treat Tanfac as a commodity acid maker with one good new line, a multiple in the high twenties or low thirties would be normal, and the central value would be ₹2,095 to ₹2,574, well under the price. The ₹2,574 figure is central earnings at the lowest weekly multiple of 33.3 times, an assumption, not a forecast.

The peers give no help in either direction. Navin Fluorine trades at 49.5 times and earns a 33% operating margin, Gujarat Fluorochemicals at 75.3 times with a 24% margin and falling profit, and SRF at 29.0 times.[15][16][17] The 44.4 times median is a trailing multiple applied to forward-year earnings, so we watch the band on trailing earnings. What would show the multiple wrong is a sustained move of the stock's own trailing ratio outside a band of roughly 33 to 56 times. We would see that at every quarterly result, so the evidence arrives continuously, not at a single date. The portfolio method asks for the three-year median and we have supplied it, but we flag that this is the input in which we have the least confidence.
