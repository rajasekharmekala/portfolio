1. What the company does

Fiem Industries makes the lights on a motorcycle or scooter: the headlamp, the tail lamp, the turn indicators and the number-plate lamp. It also makes rear-view mirrors and moulded plastic parts. The company was incorporated in 1989 as Rahul Auto Private Limited and sells almost nothing outside the automotive trade[1]. Automotive products were 99.87% of sales in the June 2026 quarter, and LED lamps alone were 48.23% of that quarter's sales, with older-style lighting at another 28.79%[18]. In the year to March 2026, lighting brought in ₹2,095 crore of the company's ₹2,791 crore of standalone sales, which is about 75%[12].

The people who pay Fiem are vehicle makers, called OEMs (original equipment manufacturers), not riders. An OEM pays for each lamp set that arrives at its assembly line. Fiem lists TVS, Honda, Yamaha, Suzuki, Royal Enfield and Hero Motocorp as two-wheeler customers, and Tata Motors, Mahindra and Hyundai in four-wheelers[18]. The annual report says three customers each exceed 10% of revenue, at 32.46%, 22.71% and 14.01%, which together make 69.2% of sales[7]. It does not name them. Analysts on the calls put Honda Motorcycle & Scooter India (HMSI) at about a quarter of revenue and HMSI plus TVS at almost 65%, and neither figure was challenged, but neither is a company disclosure[5][4]. Direct exports are only 2.1% of revenue, because Honda and Yamaha ship the finished vehicles themselves[7][4]. A smaller aftermarket business supplies spares for the life of each model, which management says can run 15 years or more[2].

The money is made in programmes. An OEM asks several suppliers to quote for a new model. The winner designs the lamp, builds the moulds and tools, and wins approval. Revenue starts only when the model goes into production, and then repeats for the model's life. A new four-wheeler programme takes about three years from award to supply, according to management[2]. Prices are agreed with the customer. The operating margin is the share of sales left after operating costs. Plastic resin, LED chips and electronics prices move, and management says it passes them on with a lag of a couple of quarters[2]. That lag explains the Q1 FY27 margin. The company-reported EBITDA margin was 13.52% (EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a measure of operating profit before capital charges), down from 14.67% in the March quarter. Wage and material costs rose first and price relief followed later[2][3].

The unit economics turn on LED. Management says an LED lamp set is worth three to four times a conventional halogen set[3]. It also says the margin at product level is about the same, so more LED means more rupees of profit on each vehicle but not a higher margin percentage[3]. LED was 63% of automotive lighting by value in FY26, up from 59% the year before, and it stayed at 63% in the June 2026 quarter[12][2]. Management says lighting content is the reason electric vehicles matter: LED is mandatory on them. For the Honda Activa electric, management said Fiem has a 71% share of the lighting business, and put the lighting content at around ₹4,000 per vehicle[6].

Sales follow two-wheeler production, which makes the business cyclical. Revenue fell 11.5% in FY21, from ₹1,379 crore to ₹1,221 crore, in the pandemic year, then grew 28.7% in FY22 as the industry recovered[1]. Production for the industry reached a record 26.7 million units in FY26, up from 23.9 million in FY25[9]. Fiem has no long-dated order book, because customers schedule production month by month, so a downturn in two-wheeler output reaches its sales within a quarter[2]. That is why the quarterly numbers and the customers' monthly dispatch data matter more for this company than for a company that reports an order book.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • OEM — original equipment manufacturer: the vehicle maker (Honda, TVS and others) that buys Fiem's lamps for new vehicles.
  • RFQ — request for quotation: an OEM's invitation to suppliers to bid for a part on a new model.
  • LED versus halogen — LED lamps use light-emitting diodes and cost three to four times as much as the older halogen bulb lamps, according to management.
  • Content per vehicle — the rupee value of a supplier's parts on one vehicle; higher content means more revenue from the same vehicle count.
  • Wallet share — the share of a customer's spending on a part type that goes to this supplier.
  • Capacity utilisation — how much of a plant's output capability is in use; Fiem's was about 75% at the end of FY26.
  • Stages and Test C — Stage 2 is a rising uptrend and Stage 4 is a falling downtrend; Test C is the house test for a stock that has stopped falling and may be turning up.
  • Guidance — guidance is management's own forecast of revenue, margin or spending; it is a forecast and not a promise. The next chapter asks how strong the business is behind these terms.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Fiem's advantage is real but narrow. Lighting is hard to switch once a model is in production, because the lamp is designed into the vehicle's front or rear styling and approved by the customer. Fiem keeps its plants near its customers' plants, runs design centres in India, Italy and Japan, and spent ₹52.58 crore on research and development in FY26, about 1.9% of standalone sales[18][14]. It built an in-house electromagnetic testing laboratory so lamp electronics can be validated without outside labs[14]. Customers have rewarded this: Honda named it best supplier on cost competitiveness for 2024-25, Suzuki gave it a best new-model part award for 2025, and Royal Enfield recognised its co-creation work[12].

The evidence of shares at the customers is mixed. On the positive side, Fiem had a 71% share of the Honda Activa electric's lighting at its launch (as stated in June 2025), more than 90% on the Royal Enfield Classic, and sole supply of Ather's new model[6][5][2]. Management says more than 100 models with the top four customers are in development[2]. On the other side, in the December 2025 quarter Honda's production grew 18% while Fiem's own growth with HMSI was 14%, and an analyst put Fiem's full-year FY26 revenue growth with HMSI at 2% to 5%[4][3]. Yamaha was flat year on year in the June 2026 quarter while Yamaha India's volumes were much higher, which management attributed to model mix[2]. A competitor, Lumax Industries, reported HMSI revenue growth above 50% over nine months, according to an analyst[4].

Pricing power is limited. Management says raw-material moves are passed on both ways, but with a delay, and that the margin on LED and non-LED lamps is similar[3][6]. In June 2025, management said margins were not expected to be materially higher over the next couple of years[6]. With three customers at 69% of revenue, the buyers set terms. The annual report adds that customers above 10% of receivables account for 78% of the amount owed, up from 65%[7]. A listed peer describes itself as the market leader of automobile lighting. Uno Minda makes lights among more than 25 component types, and Varroc, which sold its global lighting business, still wins four-wheeler lighting orders. A customer can always ask a rival to quote[26][27][36][37]. An analyst on the Q1 FY27 call (13 August 2026) said the competition has had a very high win rate with Fiem's top customers[2].

Customer concentration and the Honda question

The annual report shows the size of the concentration: three customers at 32.46%, 22.71% and 14.01% of revenue[7]. If the largest is TVS and the second is HMSI, the analyst figures on the calls (HMSI about 25%, HMSI plus TVS almost 65%) fit that pattern, but the company has not said so[5][4]. The risk is not that a customer defaults. The receivables note says there has been no default by these customers in the past[7]. The risk is that a customer moves a model to another supplier, or that Fiem fails to win the next model, and one lost model at a 25% customer would show up fast in sales.

On Honda specifically, management has said at least four times since June 2025 that its share has not changed[6][4][5][2]. The numbers do not contradict that outright, but they do not confirm it. In the December 2025 quarter, Honda's production rose 18% and Fiem's HMSI growth was 14%; management said it was model mix and that Fiem does not supply some models, such as the H'ness[4]. In the June 2026 quarter, management said sales grew in line with customers' volumes, and declined to publish wallet share by customer because of customer confidentiality[2]. Until it does, the honest reading is that Honda share is stable on management's word and slightly weaker on the figures an outsider can see.

The two-wheeler cycle, the GST cut and the electric shift

The demand backdrop is strong now. India's GST 2.0 reform cut tax on petrol two-wheelers up to 350cc from 28% to 18% from 22 September 2025, left electric two-wheelers at 5%, and raised bikes above 350cc to 40%[33]. The annual report says the recovery accelerated in the second half of FY26 after the GST cuts[9]. Industry production in the June 2026 quarter was 7.25 million units, a record first quarter and 22.8% higher than a year earlier[2]. September 2026 dispatches were up 24% at Honda, 23% at TVS, 12% at Hero and 8% at Suzuki and Royal Enfield[25].

Two things follow for Fiem. First, the GST cut helps its volumes now but the benefit turns into a harder comparison: from late September 2026 each month is measured against a month that already had lower prices. Management still guides to 15% to 20% revenue growth for FY27, so it expects the strength to hold[2]. Second, the cut made entry-level halogen bikes cheaper. An analyst on the Q1 FY27 call suggested that entry-level halogen demand is one reason LED share has stuck in a 61% to 64% band for seven quarters, and management agreed that the share had stagnated for two years because some models kept doing well[2]. So the GST cut lifts Fiem's sales but can slow its content growth.

Electric vehicles are the other side of the same trade. Electric two-wheelers were more than 9% of industry volume in the June 2026 quarter, up from about 6% a year earlier, and LED lighting is standard on them[2]. Fiem supplies TVS iQube (which crossed one million cumulative sales), Honda Activa electric, Royal Enfield Flying Flea, the new Ather model and Hero Vida[2][6]. Hero plans to triple Vida's capacity from 15,000 to 45,000 units a month and TVS to raise two-wheeler capacity from 6.8 to 8.3 million units[2]. So the electric shift favours Fiem's content per vehicle, and the GST cut favours the old petrol models: both raise volumes, but they pull LED share in opposite directions.

Capacity, working capital and the fire claims

Capacity is not a constraint today. Utilisation was about 75% at the end of FY26, 77% to 78% in the December quarter and close to 80% in the September 2025 quarter[3][4][5]. Capital expenditure — cash spent on plant and equipment — was ₹108.31 crore in FY26. It is guided at about ₹100 crore for FY27 and ₹200 crore over two years, mostly for the Hosur plants that serve TVS and electric programmes[3][2]. That is about 3.9% of sales, funded from cash and operating profit. Working capital — cash tied up in receivables and inventory, net of supplier credit — is the pressure point. Trade receivables rose 60% in FY26 to ₹388 crore against 16% sales growth[10]. Management says this is mainly because it stopped discounting bills from its two largest customers, one of them TVS, now that it has surplus cash, so payment terms did not change[3].

Two plant fires add a smaller, one-off item. A fire at the Tapukara unit on 23 August 2025 led to an insurance claim of ₹82.30 crore, filed on 30 January 2026, and insurance claim receivables on the balance sheet rose from ₹5.10 crore to ₹53.13 crore in FY26[14][13]. An earlier fire at the Rai plant in June 2023 produced ₹50 crore of interim insurance payments in FY25[14]. Collecting the Tapukara claim would be a cash inflow and could be a one-off profit item, but I did not find how it is recognised, so I have not put it into any estimate in Chapter 5.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has been consistent on the headline guide and less successful on the new businesses. For two years it has said revenue would grow 15% to 20% with an EBITDA margin of 13% to 14% or more, and the results have met that: FY25 net sales grew 19.39%, FY26 grew 16.04% and the FY26 margin was a record 14.09%[6][3]. Its capex plan was also met: about ₹100 crore was guided in November 2025 and ₹108.31 crore was spent[5][3].

The misses are in four-wheelers and LED mix. On 1 June 2026, management guided to ₹100 crore to ₹150 crore of four-wheeler revenue in FY27 and ₹200 crore to ₹250 crore in FY28[3]. 3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter reads five years of accounts and asks two separate questions. The first is about static levels: is the business good today? The second is about trajectory: is it getting better or worse? A business can pass the first test and fail the second. The price usually reacts to the second. All figures are consolidated, in ₹ crore, from Screener's tables, unless a line says they come from the company's own filings[1].

Three measures carry most of the weight. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is the profit a company earns on the money invested in it. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by the owners' funds, so a figure of 0.05 means five paise of debt for each rupee of equity. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by EPS; it says how many years of current profit the market is paying for.

Revenue rose from ₹1,572 crore in FY22 to ₹2,816 crore in FY26. That is a gain of 79%. It is a 15% compound annual rate over three years and 18% over five[1]. Profit rose faster, from ₹94 crore to ₹256 crore. The operating margin moved from 12% to 14%. Interest cost fell from ₹9 crore to ₹3 crore. The tax rate stayed near 26%[1]. ROCE climbed from 21% to 29% over the same years[1]. The twelve months to June 2026 show revenue of ₹2,932 crore (+17%) and profit of ₹263 crore (+25%)[1].

Cash is the weaker line. Cash from operations was ₹752 crore over FY22 to FY26 against ₹861 crore of profit, a ratio of 0.87[1]. Over FY24 to FY26 it was ₹529 crore against ₹627 crore, a ratio of 0.84[1]. FY26 alone was 0.63, with ₹161 crore of operating cash on ₹256 crore of profit[1]. The company's own cash flow statement shows why. Trade receivables absorbed ₹149.53 crore in FY26, against ₹44.27 crore in FY25[10]. Free cash flow fell from ₹132 crore to ₹33 crore[1]. The balance sheet is not the problem. Borrowings are ₹66 crore against reserves of ₹1,189 crore, and cash is about ₹276 crore[1][10].

Five-year balance sheet

My view differs in three places. First, I use 15% revenue growth a year and a 13.8% to 14.0% margin, which gives FY27E EPS of ₹107.82 and FY28E EPS of ₹125.50, 5.9% below consensus for FY28. Where could I be too optimistic? If employee costs stay about 35% higher in Haryana and material cost is passed on late, a 13% margin gives FY28E EPS of ₹115, about 8% below my base figure[2].
