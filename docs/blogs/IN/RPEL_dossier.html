Chapter 1

Brothers Rajesh Kabra and Sanjay Kabra founded Raghav Ramming Mass Private Limited on December 16, 2009, in Jaipur, Rajasthan [1][2]. Their idea targeted a glaring operational flaw in India's secondary steelmaking sector. Thousands of coreless induction furnaces in Mandi Gobindgarh, Raipur, Jalna, and Durgapur melted scrap and sponge iron [9][11]. Yet these mills lined their furnaces with crushed quartz mixed by hand on the shop floor by unorganized local grinders [9][11]. Any shift in silica grain size, moisture, or iron-oxide contamination caused early lining erosion [2][12]. That erosion forced unplanned furnace shutdowns and risked molten-metal runouts through the water-cooled copper coils [2][12].

The company built its first plant in the Kaladera industrial area near Jaipur, right inside Rajasthan's high-purity crystalline quartz belt [2][19]. Instead of using basic hammer mills, the promoters invested in automated crushing, vertical shaft impactor (VSI) shaping, and high-intensity magnetic separation to strip out metallic impurities [2]. In December 2015, the company converted into a public limited entity and debuted on the BSE SME platform in April 2016 [4][5][7]. In September 2017, it became Raghav Productivity Enhancers Limited and later migrated to the BSE (Scrip Code 539837) and NSE (Symbol: RPEL) mainboards [4][5][7]. The new name marked a clear strategic shift. RPEL stopped positioning itself as a mineral crusher selling rock by weight, choosing instead to sell furnace uptime, lower power use per melt, and more heats per lining [2][21].

RPEL supplies specialized refractory consumables and engineered mineral products [2][3][25]. Its core product is silica ramming mass for coreless induction furnaces, alongside high-purity quartz powder, tundish boards and continuous-casting accessories, and custom foundry mixes [2][3][25]. Industry benchmarks show secondary steel producers use roughly 30 kg of ramming mass per tonne of steel produced [9]. RPEL's product portfolio is engineered around circular material efficiency—from premium boron-oxide premixes down to turning silica dust fines into tundish boards.

A quick melt-shop example shows why mills switch from unorganized local vendors to RPEL. In a 15-tonne coreless induction furnace, workers pour dry silica ramming mass between a sacrificial steel former and the coil insulation [12]. They then vibrate the dry mass to reach maximum bulk density [12]. As the first charge heats past 500°C–700°C, the minor boron additive (0.8%–2.0%) reacts with silica grains to form a viscous borosilicate glass phase [12]. This reaction sinters the inner face into a dense ceramic crucible, while the outer layer stays loose to absorb thermal expansion [12].

Unorganized grinders often supply uneven grain sizes or let iron impurities slip past weak magnets. Those impurities lower the lining's melting point and cause early spalling after 12–15 heats. RPEL solves this with RM 9901 Premix Boron, an in-house blend built with boron oxide sourced from global suppliers such as Rio Tinto Borax [12][26]. The premix removes manual boric-acid dosing errors on the factory floor [12][26]. Even 3 to 5 extra heats per lining save a mill far more in power, labor, and uptime than RPEL's price premium per tonne [21].

Commercially, RPEL has shifted steadily toward higher-value exports and engineered grades [21][22]. In FY25, exports reached 30% of total sales volume at 76,645 MT, up 25% YoY from 61,480 MT [21]. By FY26, consolidated revenue hit ₹257 Cr across 39–40 destination countries in the Middle East, Africa, South Asia, and Europe [22]. Export realizations jumped sharply as standalone export turnover reached ₹55.54 Cr, or ~48.59% of standalone sales [22]. Over this arc, RPEL's EBITDA per metric tonne more than doubled from roughly ₹1,245/MT in FY17 to ~₹2,650/MT by Q1 FY27, proving real mix-led pricing power [2][23].

Beyond furnace linings, RPEL's move into Tundish Boards and continuous-casting consumables (garpack, garseal, and nozzle-filling compounds) creates a second revenue stream with zero raw-material friction [25]. Crushing quartz into tight ramming-mass grades always produces ultra-fine silica dust [25]. RPEL feeds those plant fines straight into its Newai tundish board line, turning a disposal cost into specialty margins [25].

Circular Economics: Waste-to-Tundish Boards

Crushing quartz into tight ramming-mass grades always creates ultra-fine silica dust [25]. Instead of dumping those fines at a loss, RPEL built a dedicated Newai plant to turn plant waste into Tundish Boards, garpack, garseal, and nozzle-filling compounds [25]. This circular loop cuts unit waste while lifting consolidated gross margins [25].

Chapter 3

Geography and process engineering anchor RPEL's supply chain. Its main plants in Kaladera (Jaipur) and Newai (Tonk, Rajasthan) sit right beside India's purest crystalline quartz deposits (97.5%–99.5%+ SiO2) [2][19]. Inside the automated Newai lines—including wholly owned subsidiary Raghav Productivity Solutions Private Limited (RPSPL)—raw quartz lumps go through multi-stage crushing, vertical shaft impactor (VSI) shaping, and high-intensity magnetic separation [2][24]. Automated optical sorting and computerized silo blending then lock in batch consistency [2][24]. Technical advisor Jan W. Kjellberg (JWK AB Sweden, former president of Svenska Silica) helped RPEL's engineers refine granulometry curves and foundry protocols [24].

Because silica ramming mass is dense and bulky, freight cost per tonne limits a single plant's economic radius. Shipping bagged ramming mass from Rajasthan to steel belts in Odisha, Jharkhand, and West Bengal added heavy road freight [8][9]. That cost left Eastern India open to regional rivals [8][9]. RPEL's September 2026 joint venture with TRL Krosaki Refractories Ltd builds a 350,000 MTPA plant in Odisha with captive access to TRL Krosaki's quartzite mines [6][8]. That plant removes the freight penalty and opens a coastal export route through eastern ports [6][8].

Chapter 4

India's secondary steel sector differs sharply from global peers. Roughly 38% to 43% of India's crude steel output comes from induction furnaces rather than integrated blast furnaces [9][11]. Industry estimates value the Indian ramming mass market at roughly $235 million [9]. That market is growing at an estimated 8.4% CAGR toward $382 million as steel capacity expands and mills shift from local stone grinders to pre-mixed refractory linings [9]. At ~30 kg of ramming mass per tonne of induction-furnace steel, India's domestic demand exceeds 1.5–1.8 million metric tonnes each year [9][10].

For years, silica ramming mass was a fragmented business run by regional crushers near quartz belts in Rajasthan, Jharkhand, and Odisha [9]. Today, higher power tariffs, tighter environmental rules, and larger induction furnaces (moving from 5–10 tonnes to 20–40 tonnes per heat) have raised the cost of a lining failure [2][11]. Organized players are steadily taking share from unorganized vendors [8][13][14][15]. Key peers include RPEL, East India-focused Monolithisch India, and diversified refractory majors such as TRL Krosaki Refractories, RHI Magnesita, IFGL Refractories, and Vesuvius [8][13][14][15].
