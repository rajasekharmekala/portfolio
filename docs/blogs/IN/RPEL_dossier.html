1. What the company does

Raghav Productivity Enhancers (RPEL) makes silica ramming mass. Silica ramming mass is a dry powder of crushed quartz mixed with binder chemicals. Steel plants and foundries pack it inside an induction furnace, an electric furnace that melts scrap metal, to form a wall between the glowing metal and the steel shell. The wall wears away with every melt, so the powder is a consumable that must be bought again and again.[7][3]

Quality is judged by the number of melts, called heats, that a lining survives before it must be rebuilt. A lining that lasts longer saves furnace downtime and electricity, and those savings are worth more to a steel plant than the powder costs. This is why RPEL sells on productivity rather than on price per tonne, and why its own presentations stress heats and customised formulations for each customer's plant.[8][7]

RPEL earns money by selling this powder by the tonne. In FY26 (the year to 31 March 2026) it sold 332 thousand tonnes (KMT) for consolidated revenue of ₹257.07 crore, which is about ₹7,740 a tonne.[19][1] In the June 2026 quarter it sold 97,000 tonnes for ₹86.91 crore, or about ₹8,960 a tonne, and the company said it passed higher ocean freight on to export customers in full.[15][16] That freight pass-through lifts revenue per tonne without adding profit, so revenue per tonne is not a clean pricing signal.

The plants are at Newai in Tonk district, Rajasthan, close to quartz deposits. They are run by two companies: RPEL itself (144,000 metric tonnes a year, MTPA, of capacity) and its wholly owned subsidiary Raghav Productivity Solutions Pvt Ltd (270,000 MTPA). The board approved a 120,000 MTPA brownfield expansion (36,000 at RPEL and 84,000 at the subsidiary) on 24 April 2026, to take group capacity from 414,000 to 534,000 MTPA, with full capacity due from 1 October 2026.[3][2]

The Kabra family owns 62.89 percent of the shares and none of them are pledged. Sales reach 28 Indian states and 39 or more countries, and the company says exports are about 24 percent of volume and its domestic market share is 14 percent, up from 3.5 percent in 2016.[26][24][4] A new 350,000 MTPA joint venture plant in Odisha, announced on 8 September 2026, is meant to cut freight to eastern steel clusters and to start the company's push toward one million tonnes of capacity.[22][23]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

RPEL's strength is a mix of process, scale and proof. It holds an Indian patent for its manufacturing process, runs a fully automated plant that it designed itself, and has a government-recognised research laboratory at the plant. The company says this lets it make a more consistent powder than the small manual plants that make most of the product in India.[7][3] These are company claims, but several outcomes support them: the plant added in October 2023 reached more than 85 percent utilisation by the June 2024 quarter, and the company says its last expansion was filled in under two years.[8][6]

Customer data points the same way. In the half year to September 2025 the company served 248 customers, the ten largest made 26 percent of sales (down from 33 percent a year earlier), and it onboarded 25 new customers. The FY26 annual report puts the top ten at under 30 percent.[21][6] For a business that sells a consumable to cyclical steel plants, a customer list that is spreading out is a real strength. One furnace closure then matters less.

Profit per tonne is the best test of pricing power. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is operating profit before those charges. EBITDA per tonne rose from about ₹1,245 in FY17 to about ₹2,650 by the June 2026 quarter. The company says per-tonne profit has improved for four quarters in a row.[17][15] The operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, has stayed between 29 and 30 percent for five quarters while revenue rose by half. The company is keeping its pricing as volume grows.[9][12] That is the evidence for a moat in a product that looks like a commodity.

The weak points are just as clear. First, almost every market-size and share figure comes from the company, not from an outside study, and the claim to be the world's largest cannot be checked against a rival's filing. Second, ramming mass is carried over long distances, with freight reaching 50 to 150 percent of product cost in the company's own words, so local makers near each steel cluster remain a threat until the Odisha plant runs.[7][23] Third, demand depends on induction-furnace steel output (38 percent of Indian crude steel in FY25 by the company's data), which swings with scrap prices and steel margins.[7]

Evidence that the advantage is real, and where it is thin

The three best pieces of evidence are fast utilisation after each expansion, a falling share of sales to the top ten customers, and steady margins while the mix moves toward value-added grades such as foundry products and Tundish FerroShield, a newer product the company says is spreading across its existing customers.[10][13] The three thinnest points are export growth (flat in FY26 against domestic growth of 57 percent), cash conversion (weaker in the latest half year, see chapter 3), and the lack of any outside coverage: no brokerage publishes estimates, so nobody outside the company tests its numbers.[5][11][32]

What this means for an owner: the business earns its high margin through a real process edge, and the edge is being converted into volume. The risk is not the franchise but the price paid for it, which chapters 4 and 5 test. The next chapter turns the edge into five measurable indicators.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has a good record on the things it controls and a weaker record on forecasts. Capacity and utilisation promises were met. The August 2024 deck said the new plant had reached 85 percent or more utilisation in the first quarter of FY25, and the August 2025 deck promised that new capacity would fill in under two years; the FY26 annual report says the previous expansion did just that.[8][7][6]

The forecast that stands out is revenue. A summary of the October 2025 results presentation reports a target of ₹500 to ₹550 crore of revenue for FY27, utilisation above 90 percent, and ROCE (return on capital employed, the profit earned on each rupee of capital used) of 20 percent. An independent results note repeats the revenue target and says the June 2026 quarter annualises to about ₹348 crore.[21][16] Our own FY27 estimate is ₹385.8 crore (chapter 3). Even if all 534,000 tonnes were sold in a full year at our assumed price of ₹9,450 a tonne, revenue would be about ₹505 crore, and the new capacity only arrives in October. We could not retrieve the company's own presentation, so the target is third-party reported; if it is accurate, it will be missed by roughly a quarter or more.

Capital allocation has been prudent so far. The expansion costs about ₹20 crore and is paid from internal cash, the dividend is ₹1.00 a share (about 8 percent of profit), borrowings are ₹5.41 crore against ₹60.8 crore of cash, deposits and fund investments, and capital commitments at 31 March 2026 were nil.[20][1][5] The new ₹100 crore joint venture will test that record, because RPEL's 80 percent share is large against its cash, and the plan for funding it has not been disclosed.[22][11]

Reporting quality needs care. The annual report's discussion section contains arithmetic that cannot be right, for example current assets said to have "surged" from ₹169.43 crore to ₹135.69 crore, and a line saying export volumes rose 26 percent in FY26 while export sales in rupees fell 2.8 percent.[2][5] The audited statements are internally consistent and we rely on them, not on the prose. The auditor firm is also changing: the September 2026 review was signed by Ravi Sharma & Co. and earlier periods by another firm.[11][1]

3. The numbers, in plain words

The financial record is the best part of this story. Sales grew from ₹137 crore in FY23 to ₹257 crore in FY26 and ₹315 crore over the twelve months to September 2026. Net profit grew from ₹25 crore to ₹55 crore and then to ₹70 crore. EPS (earnings per share) is net profit divided by the number of shares: ₹5.48 in FY23, ₹11.94 in FY26. The net margin is net profit as a share of sales, and it was 21.3 percent in FY26. Borrowings fell to ₹5 crore, which is 0.02 of equity.[9][1]

Two features make the profit look better than it feels. First, other income is small (₹2.14 crore of FY26's ₹69.63 crore pre-tax profit), so earnings are mostly operating. Second, the effective tax rate is about 21 percent, which is lower than the 25 percent rate a few years ago, so some of the profit growth came from a lower tax bill.[1][9] Neither changes the picture, but both are worth knowing.

The balance sheet is clean but has a drag. Cash, bank balances and fund investments were about ₹60.8 crore against ₹5.41 crore of borrowings at 31 March 2026. Against that, inventories grew 65.9 percent to ₹57.45 crore, receivables grew 13.8 percent to ₹58.22 crore, and the group spent ₹22.8 crore of cash on stock in FY26 alone. The company says the build was a precaution against Middle East shipping disruption.[1][2]

Working capital at RPEL equals about 129 days of sales, and inventory days (272) are the highest in five years. A business that ships a powder over long distances and waits for customers to pay will always carry a lot of it. The trend still matters: inventory days were 149 in FY22. The H1 FY27 cash flow of ₹18.36 crore against profit of ₹41.21 crore says the squeeze has not eased.[9][11]

The Core Alpha reward-to-risk floor is 2.0. At the ₹1,632.20 close the ratio is 1.36, so there is no purchase today. It reaches 2.09 at ₹1,585.00.[38]

A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and it does not chase the market. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned. Ours runs from ₹1,535.50 to ₹1,585.00. No order may rest in that zone until three conditions are met together: the momentum gate stops returning NO_MOMENTUM, the cash and inventory evidence described later has arrived, and a refreshed weighted value clears our return hurdle at the actual entry price. The gate condition is harder than it sounds, and the next paragraph says why.[38]

On the frozen data, Test A needs a close above the 50-day average of ₹1,697.64, and Test C needs a close at or above the 20-day average of ₹1,759.30. Both levels are above the zone top. Test B needs the close within 1.5 average true ranges of the 63-day base low, which is about ₹1,359 and below our stop. So the gate cannot pass inside the zone on today's averages. There are two routes. In the first, the price stays near the zone for several weeks and the 20-day average slides down toward it. Test C then passes only if the close is at or above that lower average and either the 28-day relative strength has turned positive or the two-week change is a gain. In the second, the stock bounces above the 20-day average first. Then the gate passes, but the price is far above the zone and the ratio at that price is below 2.0 (0.44 at ₹1,759.30). That second route is a re-underwrite trigger, not a buy trigger. We would redo the support, stop and target on the new bar. We would buy only if the ratio is 2.0 or better and the three conditions in the paragraph on what would qualify it later are also met: operating cash of at least 0.70 of profit on the second-half or full-year FY27 cash flow statement, inventory days falling, and a probability-weighted value at least 12 percent a year above the entry price. Why we do not reject: every hard red line passes by a wide margin. Layer 2 scores 3.5 of 5.0 with one fail, and one fail does not trigger rejection. What would qualify it later. We state a hurdle as our own choice: the probability-weighted value must be at least 12 percent a year above the entry price, which is about 17.4 percent over the 17-month horizon. Today it is 5.9 percent a year at the zone top. Three pieces of evidence would have to move it.
