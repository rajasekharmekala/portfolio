Before you read

Nestlé India makes MAGGI, KITKAT, NESCAFÉ and MILKMAID, and earned ₹3,545 crore on ₹23,072 crore of sales in FY26.[7][3] The business is excellent: it earns about 85 paise a year on each rupee of capital and carries almost no debt.[2][7] Our verdict is REJECTED for now, because the ledger shows Stage 4 — a long downtrend — with the recovery test failing at ₹1,168.00, and the price assumes 21.6% a year of cash-profit growth for ten years.[1] We would reopen the idea if the recovery test passes while the ₹1,147.40 floor holds.[1] We are wrong if double-digit volume growth lasts through the second half, because profit would then at least match the 10.8% growth the street expects for FY28[1][12].

1. What the company does

Think of Nestlé India as a company that sells ordinary kitchen habits in a packet. A family buys MAGGI because it is a two-minute meal, NESCAFÉ because coffee is part of the morning, KITKAT because a break needs a treat, and MILKMAID or CERELAC because a child needs milk or cereal. The company was founded in India more than 110 years ago, makes most of its products in the country, and says close to 96% of its ingredients are sourced from India.[5] The Swiss parent, Nestlé S.A., owns 62.76% of the shares and none of that stake is pledged against loans.[32] The parent also supplies brands, recipes and research, and the Indian company pays a licence fee for them: ₹1,024.5 crore in FY26, which is about 4.4% of sales.[14][7]

Who pays, and how does the money arrive? The company sells almost everything to distributors. In FY26, 94.6% of sales went to 2,358 dealers and distributors, and the ten largest took 19.3% of that, so no single buyer can hurt the company by walking away.[7] The distributors reach about 5.3 million retail outlets, and a Nestlé product reaches two out of three Indian households.[7] The company says it now has around six million touch points where a shopper can buy its brands, and its count of distribution points has risen about four times since 2021 as it added rural reach.[5] Online grocery and quick-commerce apps are a fast-growing extra channel, and management says they now play a pivotal role in its cooking and nutrition lines.[5]

How does the company make money? It buys milk, coffee beans, cocoa, wheat, sugar, edible oil and packaging, turns them into branded foods in nine factories (a tenth in Odisha has been announced)[8], and sells them at prices that leave about 55% to 57% as gross profit. In the September 2026 quarter the cost of raw materials was 43.1% of sales, down from 45.5% a year earlier, and gross margin was 56.9%. Gross margin is what is left of each rupee of sales after paying for ingredients and packaging.[3][12] Out of that gross profit it pays for people, factories, freight, advertising and the licence fee. What is left before interest, tax and depreciation was 23.8% of sales in the quarter.[3] Because shops and distributors pay quickly while the company pays its own suppliers more slowly, the business runs on negative working capital of about 24 days of sales. Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid bills, and a negative figure means suppliers help fund the company.[2]

Two features make this a different animal from most listed companies. First, it spends heavily on brands: advertising and sales promotion was ₹1,224 crore in FY26 against ₹966 crore a year earlier, and advertising spend rose 46% in the September 2026 quarter.[7][3] Second, it pays out most of what it earns. Dividends per share were ₹12.00 for FY26, and a special dividend of ₹2.00 was declared at the July 2026 annual meeting, so shareholders get cash instead of watching it pile up inside the company.[7][10] That payout is one reason the book value of equity is small (₹30 a share) and the return on equity looks so large.[2] Chapter 2 explains why the brands are strong, and Chapter 3 shows what that strength looks like in the accounts.

How the business behaves across the year

A packaged-food company is not cyclical the way a car maker is, but it has its own swings. Demand is steady because people eat every day, yet profit moves with input costs and with how much the company spends on advertising. In FY26 standalone sales grew 14.9% (the news source quotes 14.6% on the consolidated basis) but profit grew only 6.9%, because coffee, cocoa and milk cost more, the company spent more on advertising, and depreciation rose almost 30% as new factories started to run.[18] In the first half of FY27 the picture reversed: standalone profit was ₹2,002 crore against ₹1,412 crore a year earlier, and EBITDA margin was about 24.0% of sales. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, and it is a measure of operating profit.[3] The lesson is that the margin, not the sales line, is where the risk lies, and the next sections test that with numbers.

One more timing point matters for the price. India cut the goods and services tax on most packaged foods to 5% from 22 September 2025, which lowered shelf prices and lifted demand.[21] Goldman Sachs argues that Nestlé was among the beneficiaries and that the boost fades from the third quarter of FY27, when each quarter is compared with a stronger year-earlier quarter.[12] That is a reason why even a very good business can see its share price fall: the market is paying today for growth it fears cannot repeat.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Stage 4 (Weinstein stage): the stage of a long downtrend, when a share trades below its falling 50-day average and below the 200-day average.
  • Return on capital employed (ROCE): yearly operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business; 85% means each rupee of capital earns about 85 paise.
  • Free cash flow (FCF): cash from operations minus spending on plants and equipment; it is what is left for owners.
  • Earnings per share (EPS): profit divided by the number of shares; the figure the market multiplies by a price-to-earnings ratio, which is the price paid for each rupee of earnings.
  • Reverse DCF: a calculation that starts from today’s price and asks what yearly growth in cash profit it assumes for the next ten years.
  • Volume-led growth: growth that comes from selling more units, not from raising prices; it is the stronger kind because it is harder to reverse.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of this business sits in three places, and each can be checked in the filings. The first is brand habit. MAGGI is eaten by about half of Indian households in a year, and the penetration figures for MAGGI, NESCAFÉ and KITKAT are still in the mid-50s, which management reads as room to grow.[5] A brand that a family buys without thinking can lift its price a little, or hold it, when costs rise. The second is distribution. The company has built direct reach into villages, and 5.3 million outlets stock its products.[7] A rival that wants to match this must build trucks, depots and retailer relationships, which takes years and a great deal of money. The third is research and technology from the parent, which is part of why CRISIL rates the company AAA with a stable outlook.[11]

The evidence that the brands have pricing and share power is in the volumes. Over five years the company grew volumes about 4.2% a year, but in the last several quarters volume growth has been in double digits, helped by the GST cut, more rural reach and premium products.[5] The September 2026 quarter showed double-digit volume growth in every product group, with sales up 20.5% and domestic sales up 20.4%.[3] Standalone profit rose 36.3% to ₹1,026.8 crore from ₹753.2 crore.[4] Elara Securities estimates the quarter’s volume growth at about 12%.[13] The premium portfolio rose from 11% to 14% of sales and grows about 500 basis points faster than the whole company, which matters because premium products usually carry better margins.[5]

The weaknesses are real, and they explain why we do not treat quality as a reason to buy at any price. Input costs are outside the company’s control. Management’s own commodity outlook says coffee and wheat should stay range-bound, sugar and edible oils have settled at high levels, cocoa is volatile and milk prices rose seasonally.[3] Food inflation in India reached 7.74% in September 2026, up from 5.97% in August, and general inflation was 5.54%.[20] Management says that in a price-sensitive country it will protect price points rather than raise prices quickly, because keeping packs affordable is what drives penetration.[5] The result is that gross margin must be defended with efficiency savings and mix, not with price rises, and that is a harder job when food inflation is accelerating. Britannia’s management made the same point about its own costs, saying that industrial fuel inflation was very steep after LPG and PNG prices spiked in April and May.[25]

A second weakness is dependence on one parent. The licence fee to Nestlé S.A. was ₹1,024.5 crore in FY26, and the company proposed raising it by 0.15 percentage points a year to 5.25% of net sales, but shareholders voted the proposal down in 2025.[14][15] That vote is evidence of good governance, because the minority shareholders were able to stop a related-party payment. Related parties also made up 5.4% of sales and 10.8% of purchases in FY26, which is modest but not zero.[7] The third weakness is the share price itself. Elara notes that Nestlé now trades at 1.55 times Hindustan Unilever’s multiple, against a five-year and ten-year range of 1.2 to 1.3 times.[13] A great moat does not protect a buyer from paying too much, and Chapter 5 puts a number on that.

Competitive position against named rivals

The closest listed packaged-food rivals are Britannia, Tata Consumer Products and Hindustan Unilever, a wider consumer-goods group, with CCL Products as a coffee supplier and the seed peer. Britannia reported Q1 FY27 revenue up 8.2%, Tata Consumer reported Q2 FY27 revenue up 14%, and Nestlé reported 20.3% growth on a consolidated basis, so Nestlé is growing faster than both.[26][27][30] Its return on capital is also higher: 85.3% against 56.0% for Britannia, 28.4% for Hindustan Unilever, 15.8% for CCL and 9.2% for Tata Consumer.[2][34][36][37][35] The parent’s own group grew 3.7% organically over nine months of 2026, with India among its double-digit markets, which shows India is the parent’s strongest region.[23]

What this means for an owner: the franchise is strong enough to defend its position through a cost shock, and it has been taking share. It does not mean the share price is safe. When a stock trades at 56.9 times earnings, the position is judged on how fast earnings grow from here, and the best growth quarters in nine years are already in the numbers. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management at Nestlé India does not publish numeric targets for sales or margins, so we audited what it did say: stated plans and capital-allocation choices from the FY25 annual report and the 2026 investor meeting, compared with what the FY26 annual report and the latest results show. The record is good on growth, margins and reach, partial on capital spending, and unconfirmed on one item: the tenth factory in Odisha. One further item, the licence-fee proposal, was rejected by shareholders, so we do not count it as a promise kept.

The strongest examples are capital spending and the licence fee. The FY25 report said capital spending had risen to 10.0% of sales, and it described the Odisha factory as the next step.[8] In FY26 spending fell to 3.6% of sales, or ₹826 crore against ₹2,004 crore, while the company reported starting a new MAGGI line of about ₹170 crore and a new MUNCH line of about ₹225 crore, and free cash flow rose to about ₹4,221 crore.[7][6] Management also told analysts after the fourth quarter that advertising spend had risen 40% to 50% over the last three quarters.[19] On the licence fee, management proposed raising the rate to 5.25% of net sales, shareholders rejected it, and the FY26 fee stayed at about 4.4% of sales.[15][7] Management accepted the vote. That shows the related-party rule working, but it is a governance point and not a delivered promise.

The Odisha factory is the item we cannot confirm. The FY26 annual report shows only ₹409.3 million (about ₹41 crore) of capital work in progress for Odisha, placed in the bucket for projects older than three years, and we found no filing that reports the plant as commissioned.[7] That does not mean the plan has failed. It does mean the company has built nine factories in a row of busy years and still has capacity decisions ahead, and a mid-2027 capacity update would settle the question.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Nestlé India reports two sets of accounts: standalone, which covers the company itself, and consolidated, which adds a small investee. They are close. FY26 consolidated sales were ₹23,155 crore and standalone sales ₹23,072 crore, and the company says it had no subsidiary on 31 March 2026.[2][7] The audited FY26 statements were filed with the exchanges on 21 April 2026.[9] The audited standalone figures in the annual report are our main source, and we use the consolidated Screener page for the balance sheet and the headline ratios.[2] The seed figures on the watchlist, a return on capital of 85.3% and a return on equity of 74.2%, are confirmed on the consolidated page, and the annual report shows 75.2% return on average equity on the standalone basis.[2][7] One accounting wrinkle matters for reading the tables: in 2023 the company changed its year-end from December to March, so the period labelled FY24 below covers 15 months.[7]

Share counts also changed. A 1:1 bonus issue with a record date of 8 August 2025 doubled the number of shares, so every per-share figure in the tables is restated and older price charts will look different.[28] That is why the stock trades near ₹1,168 today, against more than ₹2,000 before the bonus, judging by the year-end market values in the annual report.[7] A bonus does not change what an owner owns, but it does mean earlier EPS figures printed in old reports are twice the figures shown here.

Four market terms are used later in this chapter. A base is a sideways price range that a stock builds before a move. A breakout is a move above the top of that range on rising volume. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend phase that follows a breakout. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the possible gain divided by the possible loss from the entry price. They matter here because the archetype test in Layer 1 asks for a minimum asymmetry.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks two questions. The first is static: does the company clear the floors for return, debt, cash conversion and ownership? The second is about the rate of change: is each measure getting better or worse? Then it asks whether the share fits one of three archetypes, which are labelled boxes in our rules: COMPOUNDER (high quality at a fair price), TURNAROUND_VALUE_INFLECTION (a lapsed business that is cheap and turning up) and TACTICAL_BREAKOUT (a breakout from a base, with accelerating earnings). The hard red lines for Indian companies are debt-to-equity of 1.20 or more or interest cover below 2, a promoter pledge of 25% or more, a three-year cash-to-profit ratio below 0.40 for a mature company, and a literal Stage 4 breakdown with the recovery test failing.
