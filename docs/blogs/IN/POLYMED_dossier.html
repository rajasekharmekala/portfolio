1. What the company does

Poly Medicure makes single-use medical devices. The range includes the plastic tubes, needles and catheters that deliver drips and draw blood, the filters and machines that run kidney dialysis, and, more recently, heart stents and bone-fixing implants. A hospital uses most of these items once and throws them away, so demand repeats for as long as patients keep arriving. By mid-2026 the group ran 15 plants in 5 countries, held 399 patents, and sold more than 225 devices in over 125 countries[1].

The company earns money in a simple way. Hospitals and distributors place orders, and the company ships boxes of devices at a set price per piece. It makes most of what it sells, so a large share of each sale is left after the cost of materials: 73.4 percent in the quarter to June 2026[1]. More than 1,000 distributors carry the products worldwide, and the company has about 590 direct sales staff in India, the United States, Italy and Mexico[1].

Most of the sales are made outside India. In the year to March 2026 India brought in ₹582 crore, Europe ₹597 crore and the rest of the world ₹696 crore, out of ₹1,875 crore in total[1]. (A crore is ten million rupees.) The European share rose after two purchases in 2025. In the June 2026 quarter, international sales were 72 percent of the total[1].

The company describes its history in three eras. The first, to FY20, was a quiet climb funded by its own profits, from a tiny start to ₹687 crore of yearly sales. The second, FY20 to FY26, began with a first outside equity raise and brought ₹1,400 crore of new capital in total, a doubling of plant capacity, and a move into heart care, intensive care and bone implants[1]. Sales rose 18 percent a year over those six years. The third era, which management calls Ascent, began in FY27 with a stated goal of doubling sales by FY30[1][3].

Two purchases in 2025 changed the shape of the group. PendraCare, a Dutch maker of heart catheters, was bought for an enterprise value of €18.8 million, and the company holds 90 percent. Citieffe, an Italian maker of bone-fixing implants, was bought outright for €31.0 million[1]. The two entered the accounts from 23 September 2025 and 7 November 2025[8]. In the June 2026 quarter they added ₹72.3 crore of sales, which is why reported growth of 30.3 percent shrinks to 12.4 percent once they are removed[3]. A third and tiny deal, a Brazilian importer called Medyneo, cost about ₹34 lakh and was bought mainly for its import licences[2][4].

The customers are hospitals, large and small, and the dialysis and cardiology lines sell to doctors who specify the product by brand. Management says its devices are in almost every corporate hospital chain in India[3]. In the United States the company is small: it expects exports there of only $3.5 to 4 million this year[3].

How the business turns a device into cash

A box of disposable devices is made, sterilised and shipped in weeks, but it is paid for much later. Management says the cycle from buying materials to collecting cash runs about 140 days, and that it was the same in March and in August 2026[3]. That long gap is the main reason the company keeps spending more cash than it brings in, even though it is very profitable on paper. Chapter 3 shows the effect in five years of cash figures.

Raw materials are mostly plastics and petroleum-based packaging, so their price follows crude oil. Management said that the aggregate hit to input costs in spring 2026 was about 20 percent, and that it had two to three months of stock on hand, which delayed the pain[4]. The company raised prices by about 4 to 5 percent from April[1].

Who owns the shares

The founding Baid family and related entities hold 62.42 percent, a figure that has been flat at about 62.4 percent since December 2024, and none of those shares is pledged as security for loans[25]. Mutual funds hold 9.77 percent, of which one small-cap fund run by Quant holds 4.56 percent[25]. Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Relative strength index (RSI) - written RSI in this report, short for relative strength index - is a meter from 0 to 100 that shows how fast a price has moved lately. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR stands for average true range. It is the typical size of a stock's daily price swing in rupees, so a price two ATR from a level is about two ordinary days of movement away.
  • EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is the profit from running the business before financing costs, tax and the accounting write-down of machines. The company calls its version operating EBITDA.
  • ROCE stands for return on capital employed. It is operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business, so it shows how well each rupee of capital works.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share. It is the company's profit divided by its number of shares.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow. It is the cash left after the business pays for its own running costs and for new plants and machines.
  • Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by the profit per share. A figure of 40 means buyers pay ₹40 for each ₹1 of yearly profit.
  • Gross margin is what is left of each sale after the cost of materials, shown as a share of sales.
  • Operating margin is profit from running the business, before financing and tax, as a share of sales. Net margin is the final profit after everything, as a share of sales.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowed money divided by shareholders' money. A figure of 0.1 means the company owes ten paise for every rupee the owners have put in.
  • Net debt is borrowing minus cash. A negative figure means the company holds more cash than it owes.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the business produces from its day-to-day work, after tax and before spending on plants or buying companies.
  • Capital expenditure is money spent on plants, machines and other long-lived assets.
  • Working capital is the money tied up in day-to-day items, mainly stock in warehouses and bills customers have not yet paid.
  • Guidance is what management says it expects for the coming period. We check later whether it came true.
  • Consensus estimate is the average of the forecasts made by the analysts who follow the stock.
  • Dilution is the fall in each owner's slice of the company when it issues new shares. Buyback is the opposite: the company buys its own shares and the count falls.
  • Base is a sideways stretch where a price rests before its next move. It is the platform a rise starts from.
  • Pivot is the top of a base, the level a price has to clear for a rise to count as real. A breakout is a move above that level on strong trading volume, and a retest is the pullback that checks the level from above.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days. The 50-day and 200-day versions work the same way over longer spans, and prices tend to bounce off these slow lines.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. Stage 1 is a flat floor after a fall, and Stage 3 is a topping or pullback phase.
  • Stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, so it never chases.
  • Entry zone is the band of prices where we would be willing to buy.
  • Asymmetry is how much we can gain compared with how much we can lose. Reward-to-risk is the same comparison as a ratio, for example 2.5 to 1 means ₹2.50 of possible gain for each ₹1 of possible loss.
  • Measured move is the height of a base projected upward from its top as a price target.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Poly Medicure describes itself as the second-largest medical devices company in India, the largest exporter of medical consumables, and the third-largest maker of IV cannulas in the world[1]. Its position rests on three things that rivals cannot copy quickly: 399 patents, product registrations in more than 100 countries, and long relationships with hospitals[1][3]. The best single number for that position is the gross margin. Consolidated gross margin was 73.4 percent in the June 2026 quarter, up 495 basis points from 68.4 percent a year earlier (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point)[1]. The standalone figure of 71.5 percent is the cleaner one, and management says it expects 67 to 69 percent once one-time inventory gains fade[1][3].

A strong brand shows up as the power to raise prices when costs rise. The company raised average prices by about 4 to 5 percent in the June 2026 quarter, and standalone revenue still grew 12.3 percent[1]. That is evidence that customers pay for performance and not only for price. It is not proof, because the same quarter also had a price-led slowdown in renal care, where the company chose to lift prices and lose some volume rather than match Chinese importers[3].

The costs that threaten the margin come from outside the company. Plastics and packaging follow crude oil, and management put the aggregate input-cost hit in spring 2026 at about 20 percent[4]. Minimum wages in Haryana, where the main plants sit, rose 35 percent from 1 April 2026, which pushed standalone employee cost up about 29 percent[3]. Freight costs have gone up two to three times in six months, according to the managing director, and shipping to the Middle East has been disrupted[3]. The company kept its operating margin inside the 23 to 25 percent range it guided, helped by the price rises.

The industry itself is a tailwind. A report by the Boston Consulting Group and the Confederation of Indian Industry, quoted by the company, puts India's medical technology market at about $16 billion, with local makers supplying about 45 percent of demand and the market growing about 13 percent a year toward $30 to 32 billion by 2030[13]. The India Brand Equity Foundation puts the import share of devices higher, at 70 to 80 percent, which suggests the room for local substitution is large, even though the two sources define the market differently[16]. Poly Medicure says most of the new products it is developing replace imports, which is the segment that hospitals buying under insurance schemes are likely to favour on price[3].

Evidence that the position is strong

Europe is the clearest recent proof. Sales there rose 43.8 percent in the June 2026 quarter, and 17.6 percent without the acquisitions, after a weak FY26 when European customers were working off excess stock[1][4]. Management says it added new customers and that the pipeline is strong[4]. In India, infusion sales grew more than 20 percent in the quarter on higher-value products and price rises[3].

The credit rating agency CRISIL, which looks at repayment risk and not at the share price, raised the outlook on the company's AA- rating to Positive on 25 June 2026 and lifted the bank facilities it rates from ₹400 crore to ₹500 crore[12]. Lenders see a company that can pay its debts easily. The company also now has about 1,100 dialysis machines installed, which sets up repeat sales of the consumables that run on them[1][13].

Evidence that the position is thin

Growth was weak in FY26 once the purchases are removed. Consolidated revenue rose 12.3 percent to ₹1,875.3 crore, but ₹115.3 crore of that came from the acquired companies, so organic revenue was ₹1,759.5 crore, an increase of only 5.4 percent[8]. The standalone company grew about 4 percent, helped by a domestic rise of about 20 percent and held back by weak exports[4].

Renal care is under attack from cheap imports. Chinese makers ship dialyzers into India with zero duty under a trade pact with the ASEAN group, according to the managing director, and the company's renal sales fell 3.8 percent in the June 2026 quarter[1][3]. In response, Poly Medicure filed a complaint, and India's trade remedies authority began an anti-dumping investigation into dialyzers from China and Malaysia on 19 June 2026[14][15]. The company has claimed an injury margin of 20 percent[3]. A duty would help, but investigations of this kind take many months, and the company itself says only that it hopes for a decision by the end of the year[3].

The Middle East is the other weak spot. Sales there fell 32 percent in the June 2026 quarter because ships were not calling at the ports, although management says orders are intact and piled up at the docks[3]. The region is 6 to 8 percent of revenue[4]. PendraCare, which had about 20 percent of its own business there, is not growing[3]. Until shipping resumes, this is demand the company cannot invoice, and it is a reason to be careful about the year's growth target.

Interest cost also rose, to ₹18.3 crore, as borrowings doubled[9].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions. This chapter finds the weakest assumption behind each estimate, says what would show it wrong, and says when we would find out. It then gives the strongest honest bear case, in the voice of someone who thinks we are too generous.

Weak point one: the second-half step-up in sales and margin

Our FY27 forecasts need about ₹592 crore of revenue a quarter for the rest of the year to reach the guided floor of ₹2,300 crore, against ₹525.4 crore in the June quarter and ₹534 crore in March[1][3][4]. Our own base of ₹2,290 crore needs a similar pace, and it rests on our own assumptions of a September quarter near ₹565 crore and a second-half step-up of about 10 percent, which is above the 7.5 to 9.1 percent of FY24 and FY25[17]. It also needs the consolidated margin to stay near 24 percent while wages, freight and crude-linked materials push the other way[3][4].

The evidence that this is wrong would be a September quarter with revenue under ₹540 crore, or a margin under 22.5 percent. Revenue between ₹540 and ₹565 crore would leave us behind our base without breaking it. The company held its September 2025 quarter call on 10 November 2025, so the next result is likely in the first half of November 2026[6]. That is the nearest dated test, about two weeks away.

Weak point two: the multiple holds near 40 times

Our target pays 40.5 times next year's earnings. The stock has traded between 35.4 and 107.5 times trailing earnings in three years, and is now at 46.9, about 16 percent of the way up that range[18]. The market has taken the multiple down from a peak of 107.5 times on 1 November 2024 as growth slowed and foreign investors cut their stake from 14.1 percent to 5.4 percent[18][17]. There is no law that says the fall stops at 47.

Evidence that this is wrong would be a close below ₹1,347 or a September quarter in which profit falls again. The multiple is not tested on a date. It is tested every day the share trades, and it will show up first as another leg down in the price.

Weak point three: the return on capital recovers

The turnaround label needs the return on capital to climb back toward 15 percent in FY28. Two new plants add depreciation before they add sales: the Palwal plant in March 2027 and the Noida plant in the first quarter of FY28[3][13]. Goodwill rose from ₹28.6 crore to ₹273.8 crore and acquired intangibles from ₹15.2 crore to ₹157.1 crore, so the acquisitions added about ₹387 crore and purchased assets now stand at about ₹431 crore of capital that earns little so far[9]. Receivable days of 103 and negative free cash flow add to the drag[17].

Evidence that this is wrong would be a return near 12 percent in the FY27 annual report, due in May 2027, or a half-year cash flow statement, which the rules require twice a year, that shows another year of cash conversion under 0.7[9][32]. If that happens, we would drop the turnaround label and mark the stock REJECTED.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule behind it is the momentum gate. Test C passes two of four conditions, and the ledger states that a stock in this state is not to be bought at market and needs limit-order patience at support[31]. The support distance is 1.26 average true ranges. That falls in the house ladder's limit-order-and-reduced-size band (1.2 to 1.6), not the full-size band (under 0.6)[31][32]. We therefore place no order today. Price alone does not change the decision. It changes when two things are true together: the price is inside ₹1,402 to ₹1,434, and the September 2026 results and a fresh rerun of our checks confirm the case, as set out in the first trigger below. Until then a dip into the zone is not a buy. A close below ₹1,347 cancels everything.

The entry zone is ₹1,401.50 to ₹1,434.00. The bottom is the 63-day low. The top is 0.6 average true ranges above that low, the edge of the house ladder's full-size band[31][32]. At ₹1,434 the reward-to-risk ratio to our ₹1,780 base target is 3.98 to 1, against a floor of 2.5 for a turnaround. At today's ₹1,470 it is 2.52 to 1, which passes only by a hair. Both ratios assume the market keeps paying 40.5 times FY28 earnings. At 36 times they fall to 0.93 to 1 and 1.72 to 1, so the zone is a better price, not a safe one. We also do not like buying at the edge of a floor before the chart has confirmed anything.

The stop is ₹1,347.00. It is the 63-day low of ₹1,401.50 minus one average true range of ₹54.37, which is the house rule for a Stage 1 or turnaround base[31][32]. That is 6.1 percent below the top of the entry zone. A close below it would show the floor failed, and we would sell on that session's close, not on an intraday dip. A stop is a plan, not a guaranteed price. A gap from ₹1,434 to the 52-week low of ₹1,182 would be a 17.6 percent loss, nearly three times the planned distance. If the close is below the stop and the 50-day average has also fallen under the 200-day average, the chart is a Stage 4 breakdown and the verdict becomes REJECTED.

Before the table, four terms need plain words. Portfolio heat is the share of net worth we would lose if every open position hit its stop together, and the house caps it at 12 percent[32]. A basis point is one hundredth of 1 percent, so a risk budget of 50 basis points means we accept losing 0.5 percent of net worth if the stop is hit. The tier cap is the largest position the conviction grade allows, which is 3.5 percent for TIER_3. The 21-day average is a 21-day exponential average, a trend line that gives more weight to recent days than the 50-day average does. The stop row in the table quotes the house rule in its configuration wording. For a base like this one, read it as the 63-day low minus one average true range.

Sizing uses the house risk-unit rule. The tier allows a 2.0 to 3.5 percent position and a risk budget of 50 basis points[32]. At a 6.1 percent stop distance the raw weight would be 8.2 percent, but the tier cap of 3.5 percent binds. The table, and the Risk-Unit Size of 3.5 percent in the key-figures strip at the top of this report, show that 3.5 percent as the full target weight. We would not buy all of it at once. The ledger's regime is a downtrend with a capitulation-bounce watch, and the house rule for that regime limits a starter position to 50 percent of the tier weight[31][32]. That rule is written for names that pass Test A, B or C, and this one passes none. We still use it as a ceiling, so the first tranche is 1.75 percent of net worth, and it is placed only after the confirmation in the first trigger below. The regime rule's own tighter stop of 0.5 average true ranges below the floor, about ₹1,374, is not used, because it sits too close to the floor to survive normal noise. If the stop is hit, the first tranche loses about 11 basis points. The second tranche of 1.75 percent waits for more: a fresh rerun of the gate that passes all four Test C conditions, or September results that meet our base case (revenue of at least ₹565 crore with a margin of at least 24 percent), on top of the confirmation needed for the first tranche.

We chose TIER_3 because Layer 1 is only conditional, Layer 2 scores the minimum, and the chart is unconfirmed. At the full 3.5 percent weight, a gap to ₹1,182 would cost about 62 basis points, above the 50 basis point budget. That is a second reason to start with half. Targets are ₹1,780 base, ₹2,420 bull and ₹1,200 bear. If the stock rises toward the base target with the fundamentals intact, the let-winners-run rule applies. Do not trim just because the relative strength index passes 70 or the position grows past 15 percent of net worth. Trail the stop up to the rising 21-day or 50-day average until the trend or the earnings break[32]. We would still reduce if the price reaches the bull target and the forecast has not risen.
