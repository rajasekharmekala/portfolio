How to read the method

How to read it. If you have ten minutes, read the lede, the key figures box and the verdict case note in the chapter called Verdict and the rule behind it. If you have an hour, read the chapters in order. The layers are a sequence: a company that fails an early layer is not judged on the later ones, so the order of the chapters is the order of the reasoning. Every table has a reading guide above it and a takeaway below it. Every number that comes from the outside world has a link to its source, and every price and technical figure is taken from the session ledger closed on 13 October 2026.

The three layers. Layer 1 is a set of hard floors that must each pass. Return on capital must be at least 15 percent, borrowing must be small against shareholders' equity, and the company must turn at least 70 percent of its three-year profit into operating cash. The technical floor asks whether the share price is in a basing or rising phase. The asymmetry floor asks whether the possible reward is at least three times the possible loss, measured against a target that can be traced to a number. If any Layer 1 floor fails, the candidate is rejected and the later layers are not run. Layer 2 scores three to five operating measures as PASS, WATCH or FAIL, and it needs most of them to pass. Layer 3 asks how far the price sits above a verified support level, measured in units of the stock's normal daily move, and turns that distance into an entry posture.

Terms you will meet. A moving average is the average closing price over a fixed number of sessions, which works like a trend line drawn through the price. SMA20, SMA50 and SMA200 are the 20-, 50- and 200-session averages. Weinstein's stages describe four seasons of a stock, and Stage 2 is the rising season this method wants to buy into, while Stage 4 is the decline that the Core rule avoids. A base is a period of sideways trading in which a stock builds its next move, and a pivot is the price level just above that base at which the stock is expected to move up. A breakout is a close above the pivot, ideally on heavy volume. RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer running from 0 to 100. A reading above 70 is called extended, which means the stock has risen fast, and a reading below 30 is called oversold. ATR, the average true range over 14 sessions, is the normal daily wobble of the stock, and we measure every distance in ATR units so that a quiet company and a volatile company can be compared on the same scale. Volume is the number of shares traded, and a breakout on heavy volume carries more conviction than one on thin trade. Relative strength compares the stock's return with the benchmark index over a set window, here the Nifty 50.

Stop-loss, risk and reward. A stop-loss is a price fixed before you buy at which you sell, so that the most you can lose is decided in advance rather than in the heat of a fall. For a Core holding the rule is to place the stop below the lower of the 20-session swing low and the 50- and 200-session averages, and then subtract one ATR. Risk per share is the price minus the stop. Reward is the target minus the price. The asymmetry ratio divides reward by risk. Position size is the last line of defence: no single name should be large enough that a stop-out would hurt the whole portfolio. The worked example in the asymmetry chapter shows these numbers with a 90-share holding.

The labels. CORE_ALPHA is the bucket for long-compounding companies with returns on capital above 18 percent that could be held for one to three years or more. The action verdicts are IMMEDIATE_BUY (at verified support with high asymmetry), ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP (near support), WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT (extended, so do not chase) and REJECTED (a hurdle failed). A gate status of FAIL followed by a reason names the single rule that failed, and where two rules fail both are named. The momentum gate is computed in code from the frozen price snapshot. Test A asks whether the stock is already in an uptrend, and Test B asks whether it is entering one. A candidate that fails both tests receives no allocation, however good the business. The thresholds behind the momentum gate are marked as needing calibration in the scoring configuration, and that caveat is carried into the run notes.

Financial terms. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is the operating profit before financing and accounting charges. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the operating return on all the capital the business uses. EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares. A gross margin is the share of sales left after the direct cost of making the product. An operating margin is the share of sales left after all running costs. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share, which shows how many years of profit the market is paying for. A debt-to-equity ratio compares borrowings with shareholders' equity, so a low figure means the company depends little on lenders. Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and receivables, net of payables, and capital expenditure is money spent on plants and equipment. Guidance is management's own forecast for the coming period. A measured move is a price objective set by adding the height of a base to its breakout point. A limit order is an instruction to buy only at a stated price or better.

What the company does

Acutaas makes chemicals that other companies turn into medicines, electronics and batteries. Its main business is advanced pharmaceutical intermediates, which are custom-made chemical building blocks sold to drug makers. The company describes a portfolio of more than 610 products [Screener]. A simple comparison helps. A medicine is a finished meal, and an intermediate is one of the prepared ingredients the chef buys before the meal can be cooked. Customers buy the ingredient from a supplier whose process has been checked by regulators, and switching suppliers means the process has to be checked again, which takes time and money. That is why contract work of this kind tends to repeat from year to year, and it is the reason the business earns the high returns shown in the Layer 1 chapter.

The company is changing shape. Pharma intermediates made up 87 percent of revenue, and management expects that share to fall to about 80 percent by FY2029 as battery chemicals and semiconductor materials grow [Cofacto]. The newer businesses carry different margins. The segment margin of pharma intermediates is about 36 percent and that of specialty chemicals about 24 percent, so a shift in mix toward specialty chemicals can move the blended margin [Cofacto]. So the mix shift matters for the margin story as much as for the growth story, and the margin question returns in the red-team chapter.

For a beginner, the most useful numbers are the scale of the business and the quality of its cash. Layer 1: the hard gates

They are binary by design. A company can be excellent in four respects and still fail one check, and when it does the method stops, because a great business at a poor price is still a poor purchase. The first three checks are about the company, and the last two are about the price, so the chapter below takes them in that order.

The debt test asks whether the company depends on lenders. The cash test asks whether the reported profit arrives as cash. Profit is an accounting figure that includes sales not yet paid for, so a company with large profit and little cash can be less sound than its earnings suggest.

Asymmetry is the ratio of what you could win to what you could lose. The method computes it as the target minus the price, divided by the price minus the stop. The Core floor is 3.0, which means a reward of at least three times the risk. A ratio of 3.0 is demanding, and it is meant to be. A stock that only offers a modest gain for a deep stop will eat the holder's capital through a run of ordinary losses, even when the business is good. The stop comes from the Core rule, which is set under the lower of the recent swing low and the two moving averages, minus one ATR. The target has to be anchored to something a reader can check, either forward earnings multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, or a measured move from a base. An arbitrary target is not accepted.

Glossary

This glossary repeats the terms used in the dossier so that a reader can return to a definition without rereading the primer. Each entry is written for a reader who has not used the method before, and each term is used in the same sense throughout the chapters.

ATR (average true range)
The average daily price movement over the last 14 sessions, including gaps. It is the stock's normal wobble, and it is the unit in which all distances are measured.
Asymmetry ratio
The possible reward divided by the possible loss. The Core floor is 3.0, meaning the reward must be at least three times the risk.
Base pivot
The price level at which a stock that has been moving sideways is expected to break out upward. The distance from the pivot is measured in ATR units.
Cash conversion (CFO/PAT)
Operating cash flow divided by profit after tax over the same period. A ratio of 0.70 or more means profit is arriving as cash.
CDMO
Contract development and manufacturing. A supplier runs a customer's chemical or drug process from development to commercial batches under contract.
Debt to equity
Borrowings divided by shareholders' equity. A ratio of 0.70 or less means the company does not depend heavily on lenders.
Intermediate
A chemical building block made on the way to a finished medicine or product. It is the ingredient the next manufacturer buys.
Momentum gate
A code-computed test with two parts. Test A asks whether a stock is already in an uptrend, and Test B asks whether it is entering one from a base. Failing both means no allocation.
NO_MOMENTUM
The result of the momentum gate when both Test A and Test B fail.
RSI (relative strength index)
A speed gauge from 0 to 100 for recent price changes. Above 70 is called extended and below 30 is called oversold.
ROCE (return on capital employed)
Operating profit before financing costs divided by the capital the business uses. It shows how well the company earns on the money invested in it.
ROE (return on equity)
Profit after tax divided by shareholders' equity. It shows how well the company earns on the owners' money alone.
SMA (simple moving average)
The average closing price over a fixed number of sessions. SMA20, SMA50 and SMA200 are the 20-, 50- and 200-session averages.
Stage 1 to Stage 4
Weinstein's four stages of a stock's cycle: a base, a rising phase, a top or pullback, and a decline. The Core bucket accepts Stage 1 or Stage 2 only.
Stop-loss
A price fixed in advance at which a holder sells to limit a loss. The Core rule sets it below the lowest of the swing low and the 50- and 200-session averages, minus one ATR.
Swing low
The lowest price over a recent window, used as a floor for stops and support.
Verdict labels
IMMEDIATE_BUY (at verified support), ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP (near support), WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT (extended, do not chase), and REJECTED (a hurdle failed).
