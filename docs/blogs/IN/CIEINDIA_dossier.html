1. What the company does

CIE Automotive India is a Pune-based maker of metal components. Its customers are vehicle makers, not drivers. A car, a motorbike, a tractor or a truck is built from thousands of parts, and CIE makes some of the heavy ones: forged crankshafts that turn engine power into motion, steering and driveline parts, iron and aluminium castings for engine and gearbox housings, large stamped body panels, gears, plastic-composite parts and small magnets. It runs plants in India, Spain, Lithuania, Italy and Mexico. About 65.7% of the company is owned by the Spanish parent CIE Automotive S.A., and the parent has named the Indian business a strategic growth driver for its global business.[34][33][21][32]

Who pays the bills? Mostly big carmakers and the large suppliers that sit between them and the factory. India produced about 66% of the group's nine-month revenue to September 2026 and Europe about 34%.[6] Inside India, light vehicles (cars, utility vehicles and small vans) took 53% of sales in CY25, two- and three-wheelers 23%, tractors 13% and heavy trucks 11%.[10] The three largest Indian customers named by one broker are Mahindra & Mahindra, Bajaj Auto and Maruti Suzuki.[23] Europe is different: about 80% of European sales are forgings and 20% are gears, and more than half go to light vehicles.[10] A year in this report means a calendar year, because the company closes its books on 31 December.

How does it make money? Vehicle makers award a part to a supplier for the life of a vehicle programme, usually several years. The supplier invests in tools and machines, then ships parts every week at an agreed price. The company says it wins roughly Rs 800 crore to Rs 1,000 crore of new annual business in India each year, and it won about Rs 970 crore in India and about Rs 300 crore in Europe in the first nine months of CY26.[10][7] Steel and aluminium costs are passed on to customers by price formulas. Energy, gas and consumables costs are not, and the company is negotiating those increases customer by customer.[8][22]

What does the owner end up with? In the twelve months to September 2026 the group sold Rs 10,259 crore of goods and kept Rs 933 crore as profit after tax.[2] It spends heavily on machines, yet in CY25 it still produced Rs 885 crore of cash after that spending, and it ended the year with Rs 1,881 crore more cash than bank debt.[3][12] The group's profile is that of a steady, cash-generating factory business that has not grown much in five years. The rest of this report asks whether that is a cheap bargain or a fair price for a slow business with a shrinking European half.

Seven technologies and why the mix matters

The company lists seven ways of shaping metal and plastic: forging (hammering hot steel into shape), iron casting and aluminium casting (pouring molten metal into moulds), stamping (pressing sheet steel into panels), gears, composites and magnets.[34] One broker note calls it a rare supplier that houses seven technologies under one roof and notes that it is the largest supplier of passenger-vehicle crankshafts in India and the second largest in Europe.[23] A carmaker that wants one supplier for several parts can therefore deal with one company. The same breadth also means that management attention and spending are spread across many plants, and that is part of why group growth has lagged the market.

For you as an owner, the mix means two things. First, a slump in one end market, such as tractors, is cushioned by the others. Second, the company cannot be valued like a single-product champion: it is a collection of mid-sized plants, each judged by how full it is and what it earns on the money invested. The next chapter looks at whether that collection has a lasting edge.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit after tax divided by its number of shares, so it shows profit for one share.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is the profit from running the plants before finance costs, tax and the yearly write-down of machines.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is the operating profit as a percentage of all the money tied up in the business, from shareholders and lenders.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before spending on machines or paying lenders and owners.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is cash flow from operations minus spending on plants and machines, which is the cash that could be paid out or saved.
  • Capital expenditure is money spent on new plants, presses and tools; the market shortens it to capex.
  • Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company owes its own suppliers.
  • Net debt is bank borrowings minus cash; a negative number means the company holds more cash than debt.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money; below 1 means shareholders fund more of the business than lenders do.
  • Operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales, so it shows how many paise of every rupee of sales remain after the plants' running costs.
  • Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by the profit per share, which shows how many years of current profit the price pays for.
  • Consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow the stock.
  • Guidance is the company's own forecast or promise about its coming results.
  • Buyback is when a company buys its own shares from the market with spare cash.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend on the Weinstein scale, which sorts every stock into four phases of the price cycle.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing price over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days; they are slow lines that a price tends to bounce off or fall below.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 meter of how fast the price has moved; under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the average daily price swing in rupees, used here to measure distance in units of normal movement.
  • Base is a sideways stretch of trading where a falling price stops and builds a floor; the pivot is the top of that floor, and a breakout is a close above the pivot on rising volume, followed often by a retest, which is a pullback that tests the old level from above.
  • Entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned, and a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better and does not chase the market.
  • Stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut.
  • Asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the potential gain from the entry price to the target divided by the potential loss from the entry price to the stop.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position is solid in India, thinner in Europe, and best described as an operating edge rather than a monopoly. Four pieces of evidence support the edge. First, the credit rating agency ICRA rates the company [ICRA]AA with a stable outlook. It cites the company's scale, its mix of products, customers and countries, and its mid-teens margins over five years. It also cites the Spanish parent, which supplies technology and long-standing links with global carmakers.[13][14][16] Second, the company keeps winning work: about Rs 970 crore of new annual business in India and about Rs 300 crore in Europe in nine months.[7] Third, the iron foundry is close to full and is already booked for next year. Customers are asking for more than the plant can make.[7] Fourth, it holds a leading share in specific parts, for example the largest share of passenger-vehicle crankshafts in India.[23]

The edge has limits, and the numbers show them. Group sales grew only 2% a year over the last three years, and ICRA lists as a watch-point that the company's growth has run below the pace of the markets it serves.[1][14] In the June 2026 quarter India grew about 13% while the weighted Indian market grew about 16.7%. Management named the cause itself. It dropped some loss-making aluminium products, exports did not grow, and a few customers were weak.[9] A supplier with a real price advantage would not need to renegotiate costs customer by customer. In the September 2026 quarter India's margin fell to 16.3% from 17.3% a year earlier. Energy and consumable costs rose after the conflict in West Asia. The company is still negotiating how much it can pass on.[4][8]

Europe is where the position is weakest. Euro sales fell about 6% in CY25 and about 3% in the first half of CY26, and the chief executive put the September 2026 quarter at about minus 10% in euros, partly because a Mexican customer, GKN, now in-sources forging work after being bought by American Axle.[15][8][7] ICRA says 10-15% of the group's business depends on parts for petrol and diesel engines, and the annual report says plainly that the company has a large dependency on forged crankshafts that an electric car does not need.[15][19] The offset is profit quality: after last year's restructuring, Europe's margin before interest and depreciation rose to 16.3% in the September 2026 quarter from 14.1% a year earlier, and the Italian gear business is back to margins near 20%.[4][9]

Put together, the moat is moderate. The balance sheet is strong, the customer base is broad and the India order flow is healthy, but the pricing power is unproven and half the group faces a structural decline in its main product. We therefore treat the business as a good operator at a low price rather than a compounding machine, which is why the report classifies it as a value-inflection case in Chapter 3 instead of a quality compounder.

What a rival would have to do to copy it

A new rival would need years to win nominated programmes, qualification approvals and plant capacity. Heavy forging presses and foundry lines cost real money and take months to install: the company is ordering two new presses for its Chakan plant and moving one press from Mexico to its Hosur plant, due in the first quarter of 2027.[7] That is a barrier, but not a high one, because large listed peers such as Bharat Forge and Sansera Engineering already compete in the same field in India.[27][28]

The practical meaning for an investor is that the edge protects the existing business from being taken away quickly, but it does not by itself create fast growth. Growth has to come from new orders, which the next chapter measures against what management said it would deliver.
