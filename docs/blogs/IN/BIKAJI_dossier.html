Bikaji Foods makes bhujia, namkeen, sweets, papad and western snacks, and earned Rs 254 crore of profit in FY26 with almost no net debt.[2] The verdict is REJECTED because of price and trend, not quality: the stock closed at Rs 463.85, under a falling 50-day and a falling 200-day average, and the reward-to-risk is only 1.52 to 1 against a 2.0 floor.[34][35] The business itself is sound, with returns near 20%, no pledged shares and cash from operations at 1.04 times profit over three years.[2][19] The view is wrong if the 5 November results show a smaller margin squeeze than warned, or if the chart builds a floor and turns up.[22][15] Status: PUBLISHED_WITH_GATE_EXCEPTIONS, explained in the run notes before the sources.

1. What the company does

Bikaji Foods International sells salty snacks and sweets that Indian households already know. Its first product is Bikaneri bhujia, a thin gram-flour noodle snack from Bikaner in Rajasthan, and the family still sits at the top of the company. The business grew from a local sweet and snack shop into a listed maker with ten manufacturing facilities and an installed capacity of 3,25,320 tonnes a year.[2] In the year to March 2026 the consolidated company booked Rs 2,993.86 crore of revenue and Rs 254.41 crore of profit after tax.[2] A customer pays at a shop counter or an online checkout, a distributor or wholesaler buys from the company in bulk, and the company keeps the gap between selling price and the cost of oil, pulses, flour, packaging and labour.

Where the money comes from matters. Ethnic snacks (bhujia and namkeen) gave Rs 1,924.6 crore, packaged sweets Rs 333.9 crore, western snacks such as chips and puffs Rs 229.7 crore and papad Rs 167.2 crore in FY26.[2] The company puts ethnic snacks at about 69% of its own revenue base; measured against total consolidated revenue of Rs 2,993.86 crore, the Rs 1,924.6 crore is 64.3%, and the table below shows shares of the four named categories only.[33][2] Management says it is the third largest ethnic snacks company in India, and its own annual report puts its share at 7.6% of traditional snacks and 4.1% of salty snacks.[8][2] That share figure is the whole story in one number: the brand is strong in its home states and small in the country.

Geography shows the same thing. Core states (Rajasthan, Bihar, Assam and a few others) gave 69.4% of FY26 revenue and 72.4% in the June 2026 quarter; the focus states where the company wants to grow gave 14.3%; exports gave 5.3% for the year.[33][5] The rating agency ICRA notes that the top three states supply around 70% of sales and calls the dependence on Rajasthan a credit challenge.[14] Reach is the lever management pulls: direct coverage grew from 3,11,331 outlets in March 2025 to 3,70,893 in June 2026, out of 14.46 lakh outlets served in total.[5] Management wants 5 lakh direct outlets within three years.[9]

The company is also building new legs. It consolidates The Hazelnut Factory, a premium sweets and gifting retail chain, inside a retail business whose revenue grew 71.8% to Rs 36.4 crore in Q1 FY27 across 28 stores, a frozen-food arm, a US subsidiary and, since 27 August 2026, a bakery subsidiary in which it kept 70% after selling 30% to a bakery expert.[5][24] A Nepal plant with a local partner is planned, with about Rs 15 crore from each side.[6] These bets are small today. The next chapters ask whether the core snack engine is strong enough to carry them, and what the price already assumes.

How a rupee of sales is made and kept

Gross margin is revenue minus the cost of the raw materials and packing that go into the product, shown as a share of revenue. For FY26 it was 35.1%, up 290 basis points (hundredths of a percentage point) on the year before, and 35.7% in the June 2026 quarter.[2][5] The company then pays staff, power, freight, advertising and trade schemes. What is left is EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), which management reported as Rs 410.59 crore or 13.7% of revenue in FY26, including a production-linked incentive from the government of about Rs 50 crore a year.[2][6] Management said that incentive adds about 150 basis points to the margin and that the incentive ends after FY27, so the underlying margin is nearer 12%.[6][15]

Input costs decide the margin. Palm oil and edible oil, moth and chana dal, gram flour, potatoes and laminate packaging make up the bill, and management reported oil up 12% to 14% and packaging up 25% to 30% after the Middle East war began in February to March 2026.[9] The company answered with a price rise of about 3% in April and a second rise within the quarter, and HDFC Securities quotes a weighted input inflation of about 7% against a 5% price rise.[9][6][15] Packs are priced at fixed points (Rs 5, Rs 10 and larger family packs), so the company often cuts the grams in a pack instead of the price.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Ethnic snacks: traditional Indian salty snacks such as bhujia and namkeen, as opposed to western snacks like potato chips.
  • Family pack and impulse pack: family packs are the larger packs bought for the home (61.4% of FY26 sales); impulse packs are the small Rs 5 and Rs 10 packs bought on the go.[33]
  • Direct reach: shops the company's own salesforce serves, compared with shops reached through wholesalers.
  • Core and focus states: core states are the home markets where Bikaji already leads; focus states are the larger markets where it is small and wants to grow faster.
  • Production-linked incentive (PLI): a government payment tied to output; here about Rs 50 crore a year, booked in revenue.[6]
  • Underlying volume growth: growth in tonnes sold, which strips out price increases (9.5% in FY26 and 7.7% in Q1 FY27).[8][5]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A snack brand is strong when shoppers ask for it by name, and when rivals cannot buy that habit away with a price cut. Bikaji has a real version of this in its home markets. Bikaneri bhujia carries a geographical indication tag, the company is the largest maker of the product, and family packs, which are 61.4% of sales, earn better margins than small packs, as management has said.[2][1] The company says it holds 7.6% of traditional snacks, and it has a wider direct reach every quarter: 3.71 lakh outlets in June 2026, up 13.6% on the 3.26 lakh a year before.[2][5] ICRA rates the bank lines AA minus with a stable outlook and cites the brand, the distribution network and low debt as strengths.[14]

The competitive position

The weakness is scale against rivals. ICRA names Haldiram, PepsiCo India, ITC, Balaji Wafers and Prataap Snacks, and adds that Bikaji faces local makers who sell loose, cheaper product.[14] Euromonitor, quoted by the Economic Times, puts the two Haldiram companies at about 13% of India's $6.2 billion savoury snacks market.[26] Temasek, Alpha Wave and IHC bought roughly 15% of Haldiram's together, and L Catterton became a fourth outside investor in December 2025, so the largest rival is now well funded.[27] Management calls quick commerce a threat only in private labels, which it thinks will not work because the gap between a brand and a store label is small; its own quick-commerce sales more than doubled in Q1 FY27.[6]

Evidence on the moat, and where it is thin

A moat is a lasting advantage that lets a company keep its margins. Two facts support one here: gross margin has held at about 35% for five quarters even as oil and packaging rose, and the company has passed through price rises without losing volume.[5][6] Two facts cut the other way. First, EBITDA margin has swung between 7.8% and 14.9% over eight quarters, which a strong moat would smooth.[5] Second, ordinary growth in Q1 FY27 came with a margin that fell 130 basis points year on year to 13.5%, because advertising and fuel costs rose.[5][6] Management also admitted that bhujia is made in one place only, Bikaner, and that a four-day plant stoppage in April 2026 (a chairman's funeral plus a loss of migrant labour during the Bengal elections) cut sales for about 45 days.[6][9]

Management, ownership and governance

The founder and chairman, Shiv Ratan Agarwal, died on 23 April 2026, and his son Deepak Agarwal became chairman and managing director on 21 May 2026.[21][2] Promoters hold 73.88% of the shares, and the BSE filing for September 2026 states that no promoter shares are pledged. The same filing shows that 8,44,93,200 shares (33.70%) still stand in the late founder's name, which suggests that the transfer of those shares to heirs is still pending.[19] In September 2025 the company disclosed that the Enforcement Directorate had summoned its managing director over a sponsorship of the Rajasthan Premier League; it said its role was limited to sponsorship in the ordinary course.[20] The auditors' report on the FY26 accounts carries a reservation: they could not confirm that books kept in electronic form were backed up in India for January to March 2026, because a service-organisation report was missing for some applications.[2] None of these items is a loss today. Together they say that governance has more open threads than the clean balance sheet suggests.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means comparing what management promised a year or two ago with what happened. The record here is mixed. On the things management controls directly (gross margin, distribution, a new retail chain) it delivered or beat its promises. On the things that depend on input prices, festive timing and the plan for FY27, it has lowered its margin guidance twice since January.

The margin guidance is the clearest case. In January 2026 the CFO said EBITDA margin should rise at least 50 basis points in FY27.[10] In August 2026 he said the full year would be 13% to 13.5%, which is below the 13.7% earned in FY26.[6][8] By the October meeting that HDFC reports, management guided to 100 to 150 basis points of compression in FY27, with 200 to 250 basis points in Q2 alone.[15] A guide that moves from up 50 to down 125 basis points in nine months is a broken promise, even if the causes (a war, oil, packaging) were outside the company's control.

Capital allocation is the second half of the audit. In FY26 the group generated Rs 304.10 crore of operating cash, spent Rs 178.42 crore on plant and equipment, put a net Rs 116.29 crore into term deposits, lent Rs 21.36 crore and paid Rs 25.06 crore of dividends, about 10% of profit.[2] It also raised a net Rs 75.12 crore of short-term borrowings in the same year, so the company borrowed at the working-capital line while building a deposit pile (Rs 276.63 crore of bank balances at March 2026).[2] The filing does not say why, and cash and investments of Rs 340.31 crore still exceed borrowings of Rs 209.76 crore, so this is a question, not a charge.[2][35]

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter lays out five years of the company's accounts and then runs Layer 1 of the underwriting. Layer 1 asks two questions. The first is static: do the levels (return on capital, debt, cash conversion) meet the standard for a compounder? The second is about direction: are those levels improving or sliding? A company can pass the first and fail the second, and the reverse.

The accounts come from the audited consolidated statements in the annual reports for FY23, FY25 and FY26, with Screener.in used for the operating-profit line and as a cross-check.[4][3][2][1] One caution on comparability: the FY26 report restated FY25 to include a group merger effective 1 April 2024, so FY25 figures below are the restated ones.[2] Revenue grew from Rs 1,611 crore in FY22 to Rs 2,994 crore in FY26, a rise of 86% in four years, while profit after tax rose from Rs 76 crore to Rs 254 crore.[4][2]

Over FY24 to FY26 the group turned profit into cash at 1.04 times: Rs 741.54 crore of cash from operations against Rs 712.11 crore of profit after tax.

How it was calculated

Add the three years of audited consolidated cash from operations (Rs 244.68 crore, Rs 192.76 crore, Rs 304.10 crore) and divide by the three years of profit after tax (Rs 263.46 crore, Rs 194.24 crore, Rs 254.41 crore).[35]

FY24 cash from operations Rs 24,468.26 lakh and profit Rs 26,346.26 lakh from the FY25 report; FY25 restated Rs 19,275.94 lakh and Rs 19,424.12 lakh and FY26 Rs 30,409.78 lakh and Rs 25,440.98 lakh from the FY26 report.[3][2]

Using the unrestated FY25 figures (cash Rs 193.46 crore, profit Rs 194.34 crore) the ratio is still 1.04. For FY22 to FY24 it is 1.03. A strict reading that treated tax paid as outside operations would lower it, but the filing places tax paid inside the operating line, so we use it as filed.[3][4]

If the number is off

If the true ratio were 0.70 instead of 1.04 the name would sit at the compounder floor rather than well above it, but the red line (0.25) would still pass. The number is not what drives the verdict.

The number

Return on capital employed is about 19.7% for FY26 on our arithmetic, against 21.62% as reported by the company.

How it was calculated

Profit before exceptional items Rs 34,911.33 lakh, finance costs Rs 1,783.59 lakh, total equity Rs 1,70,740.83 lakh and Rs 1,48,049.46 lakh, borrowings Rs 20,975.59 lakh (non-current Rs 619.98 lakh plus current Rs 20,355.61 lakh) and lease liabilities Rs 8,957.79 lakh.[2]

If the number is off

4. What the price is doing

The stock closed at Rs 463.85 on 27 October 2026, down 0.13% on the day, 19.36% over a month and 39.64% below its 52-week high of Rs 768.50.[34] The price sits below its 20-day average (Rs 482.68), its 50-day average (Rs 545.21) and its 200-day average (Rs 624.79).
