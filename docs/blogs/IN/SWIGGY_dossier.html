Swiggy (NSE: SWIGGY) runs a food delivery app, the Instamart grocery service and Dineout table booking, and it lost ₹922 crore in the quarter to September 2026.[1][2] Our verdict is REJECTED because the shares are in a confirmed downtrend and the company fails the interest-cover red line by the rule (and we judge cash conversion a fail as well), even though it holds ₹13,662 crore of cash and no bank debt.[2][26][27] Food delivery earns ₹341 crore a quarter, but Instamart loses ₹895 crore and needs about 2.3 times its size to break even.[2] At ₹185.25 our central value is ₹201, so the gain-to-loss ratio is 0.97 to 1 against a floor of 2.5 to 1.[26][27] We would be wrong if Instamart starts to earn money on each order in the December 2026 quarter while the price holds above ₹174.20.

1. What the company does

Swiggy is an app that brings three everyday needs together: a hot meal from a restaurant, groceries and household items from a nearby warehouse, and a table booking at a restaurant. Founded in 2014, it earned ₹23,053 crore of revenue in the year to March 2026 and ₹26,914 crore over the twelve months to September 2026, about 42% more than a year earlier.[1] It reported a loss in every year Screener shows, FY20 to FY26.[1] Revenue has grown faster than costs, but not yet fast enough to close the gap, so the company funds itself from cash raised from investors, including the November 2024 stock market listing and a ₹10,000 crore share sale to institutions in December 2025.[5][16]

Food delivery is the oldest and best-run part. A customer orders from a restaurant, a delivery partner (a gig worker on a motorbike) carries the food, and Swiggy keeps a slice of the money. The slice comes from a commission the restaurant pays, advertising the restaurant buys to appear higher in the app, and fees the customer pays. The company calls that slice the take rate. It reached 26.1% of order value in the September 2026 quarter, the highest it has recorded.[2] Orders are measured as gross order value (GOV): the full value of completed orders before discounts. Food delivery GOV was ₹10,054 crore in the quarter, up 17.7% on a year earlier, and adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, here after rent and without share pay) was ₹341 crore, or 3.4% of GOV.[2]

Instamart is the grocery arm. It runs 1,240 dark stores, which are small warehouses closed to the public and stocked so that an order can be packed and delivered in about ten minutes, across 133 cities.[2] The customer pays little or nothing for delivery, so the business earns mostly from brand advertising, a commission on each item and small fees. In the September 2026 quarter, 14.8 million customers a month placed 130 million orders, with an average order of ₹708. Net order value (NOV), meaning the amount customers actually paid after discounts, was ₹6,594 crore, up 34.8% on a year earlier.[2] The business lost ₹895 crore at the adjusted EBITDA level in that quarter.[2] A rule change matters here: shareholders approved capping foreign ownership at 49.5% so Swiggy can qualify as an Indian-owned-and-controlled company, which would let Instamart hold its own stock instead of passing goods through sellers. Management expects the switch to start in the middle of the December 2026 quarter and to take two to three quarters.[2][7]

Two smaller parts complete the picture. Dineout, called Out-of-Home Consumption in the filings, books tables and sells dining vouchers. It turned a small profit of ₹8 crore in the quarter on GOV of ₹1,508 crore.[2] Platform Innovations is the experimental box. It holds Toing, a separate app for cheap meals, which lost ₹162 crore at the adjusted EBITDA level in the quarter, and an analyst on the call put Toing's annual loss near ₹400 crore, an estimate that is not a company figure.[2][7] Swiggy also agreed in September 2026 to sell Lynks Logistics, its wholesale distribution unit, to Udaan's parent for about ₹500 crore, which management says will improve segment profit by about ₹20 crore a quarter from the December 2026 quarter.[2]

The company has no promoter, meaning no founder or family holds a controlling block. At September 2026, domestic funds held 34.49% of the shares, foreign investors 8.86% and the public 51.67%.[1][18] That matters for two reasons. First, there is no promoter pledge to worry about, so one of our red lines is not applicable. Second, the shareholders are institutions who can sell quickly, which is part of why the shares fell about 15% on the day after the results.[14][16]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • GOV (gross order value) is the full value of completed orders before discounts. It measures how much people buy through the app, not what Swiggy earns.
  • NOV (net order value) is the amount customers actually pay after all discounts; Swiggy now reports it for Instamart because discounts differ so much between grocery and non-grocery items.
  • Adjusted EBITDA is the company's own profit measure: it adds back share pay and subtracts building rent, so it is close to a cash view of the business before interest and tax.
  • Contribution margin is what is left of each order after delivery cost, discounts and other per-order costs, before head-office costs. A positive number means each order pays for itself.
  • Dark store is a small warehouse used only for filling online orders. The more orders it fills per day, the lower the cost of each.
  • MTU (monthly transacting users) is the number of different customers who placed at least one order in a month, averaged over the period.
  • IOCC (Indian-owned-and-controlled company) status is the rule change that lets Instamart own the goods it sells. Shareholders approved a cap on foreign shareholding of 49.5%, on a fully diluted basis, to qualify.[2]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The honest summary of competitive position is: strong in food delivery, sub-scale and under-earning in quick commerce, and outspent by a better-funded rival that is already profitable in both. In food delivery, Swiggy and Eternal's Zomato are the two large platforms. Swiggy has 19.8 million monthly customers, up 15.2% on a year earlier, and about 266,000 restaurants order through it each month.[2] Its margin is improving because advertising income and delivery efficiency rise as orders become denser. Contribution margin reached 7.7% of GOV in the September 2026 quarter.[2] The weakness is relative growth: Swiggy's food GOV grew 17.7% while Zomato's net order value grew 21.5% over the same quarter, and Zomato's growth has accelerated for five quarters in a row.[2][22] Bernstein attributed much of the growth gap to self-cannibalisation from Toing. JM Financial saw zero rationale for Toing as a separate business.[13][14]

Quick commerce is where the competitive gap is widest. Blinkit, Eternal's grocery arm, had net order value of ₹20,940 crore in the September 2026 quarter, up 79.3% year on year, and 2,751 stores after adding 308 in a single quarter. It earned ₹184 crore of adjusted EBITDA, against a ₹156 crore loss a year earlier.[22] Instamart's net order value was ₹6,594 crore, about a third the size, with 1,240 stores and a loss of ₹895 crore.[2] Eternal also told investors it now expects to cross 3,500 stores by March 2027, which would widen the store gap.[22] In its Q2 FY27 letter management said competitive intensity remains high and is not expected to ease soon, with large online retailers also active in the festive quarter. An analyst on the call described a market of seven players.[2][7]

Swiggy's answer is differentiation rather than more stores. Its Switch programme partners with about 400 brands and sells its own brand, Noice, which appears in 1 in 8 baskets and brings about 15 percentage points better retention. Items from Switch were part of more than 20% of orders by September 2026.[2] Orders per store per day rose 9.5% on the quarter to 1,192, the highest of the five quarters reported, so each store is working harder.[2][7] The company also said in its Q1 FY27 letter (July 2026) that its network was only about 40% used, which is why it prefers to fill existing stores before opening new ones, and on the October call it said it starts a new store when an existing store nears 2,000 orders a day.[3][7] These facts show real progress. They do not yet show a moat, because Swiggy's retention gains from private labels have to hold up against a rival that is growing more than twice as fast. Eternal's management said on its latest call that it has looked at a Blinkit private label and believes it does not pass its tests.[22]

Three outside forces shape the economics. First, tax: the 57th GST Council recommended cutting the tax on delivery services sold through e-commerce platforms from 18% to 5%, effective 1 November 2026, which lowers what customers pay and may help order volumes.[23][24][25] Second, labour: delivery partners are gig workers, and in the weeks before its May 2026 letter harvest season and state elections caused a temporary but widespread migration of delivery partners, which lasted about four weeks.[4] Third, seasonality: the June quarter is weak because of the monsoon and annual pay rises, and the October-December quarter has the festival season, so quarter-on-quarter comparisons mislead.[3] A contingent liability also exists: GST authorities issued a show-cause notice for ₹327 crore plus interest on delivery charges for July 2020 to March 2022, which the company disputes and has stayed in the Karnataka High Court.[18]

The balance sheet is the strongest part of the case. Swiggy reports a cash balance of ₹13,662 crore, no bank borrowings, and ₹2,514 crore of lease obligations for dark stores and offices.[2][1] That cash is about 27% of the ₹51,065 crore market value.[1] It buys time to fix Instamart. It does not make the shares cheap if the losses never end. The next chapter tests this with five operating measures, scored one by one.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management guidance is the company's own forecast for the coming period, and Swiggy's is unusually specific, which makes it testable. The chief executive's own Q1 FY27 letter says the company committed a year earlier to break even on Instamart's contribution margin by the June 2026 quarter, and it reported that the target was hit in May 2026, with a full-quarter reading of -0.2%.[3] That is a real delivery on the hardest promise. The same letter set a range of zero to -100 basis points for the next two quarters, and the September reading of -1.0% sits exactly on the edge of that range.[2][3]

The record is less clean elsewhere. In the Q4 FY26 letter, the company said capital spending would come down meaningfully in FY27. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Swiggy's numbers tell a story of fast growth and slowly shrinking losses. Revenue grew from ₹5,705 crore in FY22 to ₹23,053 crore in FY26 and ₹26,914 crore over the latest twelve months.[1] The operating margin is operating profit divided by revenue after all running costs. It has improved from -64% in FY22 to -14% in FY26 and -11% over the latest twelve months.[1] That is a large improvement, but the margin is still negative, and in absolute rupees the operating loss has not moved much: ₹3,650 crore in FY22, ₹3,235 crore in FY26 and ₹2,904 crore over the last twelve months.[1] Growth has bought scale, and scale has bought margin, but not yet profit.

Three features of the accounts need explanation. First, the large borrowings shown for FY22 to FY24 (about ₹16,000 crore) were investor preference shares, not bank loans. They converted into ordinary equity before the listing, which is why borrowings fall to ₹1,702 crore in FY25 and equity turns positive.[1][18] Today the ₹2,514 crore of 'borrowings' on the Screener page is almost entirely lease obligations: the September 2026 balance sheet shows no financial borrowings and ₹2,514 crore of lease liabilities.[1][2] Second, interest income is large. The company earned ₹212 crore of other income in the September quarter, mostly interest on its cash, and without it the loss before tax would have been ₹1,134 crore instead of ₹922 crore.[2] Third, share pay is large: the cost of employee share options was ₹966 crore in FY26, equal to 23% of that year's net loss of ₹4,154 crore.[1][18]

Cash tells the clearest story. Operating cash flow, also called cash flow from operations, is the cash the business itself generates before financing. It was negative in each of the last five years: -₹3,900 crore, -₹4,060 crore, -₹1,313 crore, -₹2,169 crore and -₹2,898 crore for FY22 to FY26.[1] Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying assets. It was -₹3,809 crore in FY26.[1] The first half of FY27 shows improvement: operating cash flow was -₹938 crore, against -₹1,522 crore a year earlier.[2] The company's own cash reconciliation shows a burn of ₹695 crore in the June quarter and ₹708 crore in the September quarter, after adding back ₹131 crore of interest income.[2] The improvement is real. The distance to cash break-even is still large.

The business is concentrated in two ways. By profit, one business (food delivery) earns all of the operating profit, while Instamart absorbs more than two and a half times that profit in losses each quarter.[2] By cycle, the business is seasonal rather than economically cyclical: monsoon and wage rises weigh on June, festivals lift December, and an LPG gas supply disruption raised restaurant-driven order cancellations in the June 2026 quarter.[3] Working capital is the cash tied up in everyday operations, and it is also moving the wrong way: receivable days (the average time customers and partners take to pay) rose from 59 in FY25 to 64 in FY26, and trade receivables were ₹5,124 crore in September 2026 against ₹2,895 crore a year earlier, growing about 77% against revenue growth of 36%.[1][2] That is the opposite of what a marketplace with strong pricing power normally shows.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 compares static levels, where the company stands today, with trajectory, where it is heading. It then checks the hard red lines. The rulebook says almost any solvent, honest business can be owned at the right price if its numbers are turning up. Swiggy is solvent, and its margin is turning up. Even so, the interest-cover red line and the chart rule fail by the rule, and we judge a third line, cash conversion, a fail as well.[27] The static tests use return on capital employed (ROCE), which is the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses, and debt-to-equity, which is total debt divided by shareholders' money. The chart rule uses the Stage 4 label, which is the confirmed downtrend phase of the Weinstein stage scale. The price test uses asymmetry, which is the same as the reward-to-risk ratio: the gain if we are right divided by the loss if we are wrong. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 has a weakest assumption. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong, and when we will know. The red-team bear case is the harshest honest version of why we could be wrong in either direction: wrong to reject, or wrong about the value we gave. The three flaws below are the ones that would break the base case.

Flaw 1: Instamart never gets to break-even

The base case values Instamart at 0.5 times its order value, which is a bet that it can reach break-even. Management says it needs about 2.3 times today's annualised net order value (₹60,000 crore against ₹26,400 crore) and a contribution of ₹30 an order, two-thirds of it from higher revenue per order and one-third from lower costs.[2] That is a large leap. Contribution margin fell 82 basis points in the latest quarter even while orders per store rose, so the economics are sensitive to the discounts used to buy growth. Blinkit, which grows faster and is profitable, added 308 stores in one quarter, and its management says it will keep reinvesting where it sees good returns.[22] If the market keeps competing on price, Instamart could be stuck growing but not earning.

Evidence this risk is overstated: contribution margin above -1.0% and adjusted EBITDA margin improving by about 100 basis points in the December quarter, which management guided. Evidence this risk is real: another quarter of double-digit growth with contribution at or below -1.0%, or higher marketing spend.

Flaw 2: food delivery loses share and the margin stalls

Food delivery is the profit that supports the whole valuation, at 15 times next-year profit in our base case. Its weak point is growth: 17.7% against Zomato's 21.5%, with Bernstein pointing to Toing cannibalising the core.[2][13][22] JM Financial said it saw 'zero rationale' for treating Toing as a separate business.[14] Toing's annualised loss is near ₹400 crore on annualised revenue of about ₹500 crore, per an analyst's estimate on the call.[7] On the October call management said that only Rapido is live to the general public among the new food delivery entrants it is watching.[7] If growth falls toward 15% and margins stall near 3.5%, the food value falls by about a third.

Evidence this risk is overstated: food GOV growth of at least 18% in the December quarter with margin at or above 3.7%. Evidence this risk is real: growth below 17% or a second quarter of rising Platform Innovations loss.

Flaw 3: the cash buys less time than it looks, and owners pay for it through dilution

Cash of ₹13,662 crore looks like safety, but three things reduce it. First, the move to the inventory model means Instamart must hold stock. Management says faster payment cycles will offset it, but trade receivables are already ₹5,124 crore against ₹2,895 crore a year ago.[2] Second, share count rose 14% in FY26, from 2,28,64,80,881 to 2,60,60,54,682 shares, after the listing and the share sale. Another raise would dilute owners again.[18] Third, share pay of ₹966 crore in FY26 is a real cost to owners even though it is not a cash cost.[18] The GST show-cause notice for ₹327 crore plus interest is an extra contingent liability.[18]

Evidence this risk is overstated: operating cash outflow in the second half of FY27 below ₹800 crore and no new share issue. Evidence this risk is real: operating cash outflow above ₹1,000 crore in a half, or a new equity raise announced.
