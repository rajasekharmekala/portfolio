1. What the company does

Esab India Limited makes the rods, wires and machines that join and cut metal, and it sells them across India and to its sister companies abroad. It is based in Chennai and trades on the BSE (code 500133) and the NSE (ESABINDIA).[10][12] It is a subsidiary of ESAB Corporation, a US-listed group, which owns 73.72 percent through ESAB Holdings Limited of the UK (37.31 percent) and Exelvia Group India B.V. of the Netherlands (36.41 percent). Mutual funds hold 12.52 percent, foreign funds 1.52 percent and the public 11.99 percent.[1][12] The company has no subsidiary, joint venture or associate. It reports a single business segment, which it calls Fabrication Technology.[1][4]

Welding is how steel and aluminium parts are fused into bridges, pipelines, railway wagons, car bodies and ships. Two things are needed: a machine that makes the heat, and a consumable that melts into the joint. A consumable is a rod or wire that is used up in every weld, so a fabricator buys it again and again for as long as the factory runs; a machine is bought once and replaced over many years. The company's own filing names the end markets as automotive, general fabrication and construction, pipelines and pipe mills, power generation, process industry, railways, shipbuilding and offshore, and transport and mobile machinery, plus repair and maintenance work for steel, cement, power and mining plants.[1] Because the rods are consumed and the machines are visible, the business grows when more steel is fabricated, which ties it to factory output and building activity.

The company sells mainly to businesses that resell. In FY26, 69 percent of sales went through 393 dealers and distributors, and the ten largest took 26 percent of the dealer total, so no single dealer is critical.[1] No single customer gave more than 10 percent of sales.[1] It runs three plants, at Ambattur in Chennai, Sriperumbudur in Tamil Nadu and Kalmeshwar near Nagpur. It had 1,003 employees at March 2026, up from 933 a year earlier.[1][2] About 17 percent of turnover is exports, and the largest named buyers are group companies such as ESAB Middle East FZE.[1] It serves customers directly only in India, Nepal, Bangladesh, Bhutan and Sri Lanka. The group serves the Middle East, Africa, Australia, South-East Asia, China and North Asia through its own companies.

Take ₹100 of FY26 revenue from operations, which was ₹1,508.15 crore. About ₹58.25 went on materials, meaning the rod and wire inputs plus finished goods bought from group companies. Staff took ₹9.62 and other costs ₹14.24. That left ₹17.89 of operating profit before depreciation.[1] That last figure is close to what the market calls EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), the profit from selling goods before interest, tax and the wear on equipment. By accounting category, FY26 revenue was ₹1,052.70 crore of goods the company makes itself (69.8 percent), ₹321.21 crore of goods bought from others and resold (21.3 percent) and ₹128.09 crore of services (8.5 percent).[1] The annual report also lists two product groups, at 71 percent and 29 percent of turnover, but does not say which is rods and wires and which is machines, and it publishes no consumables-versus-equipment split, so that split is N/A in this report.[1] We would rather show a gap than guess. The answer matters, because consumables repeat and machines do not.

Revenue has three parts that behave differently. India sales were ₹1,249.51 crore in FY26, up 6.4 percent. Revenue from outside India was ₹252.49 crore, up 31.1 percent, and is now 16.8 percent of the total.[1] The third part is work and goods for sister companies. Nearly all service income is billed to the group: ₹124.63 crore from related parties, mostly research and development and shared-service work done in India, against ₹91.27 crore a year earlier, out of ₹128.09 crore of total services. Product sales to group companies rose to ₹209.81 crore from ₹178.59 crore.[1] Against that, the company pays its group a trademark licence fee of ₹51.93 crore, which is 3.4 percent of revenue from operations and up from ₹47.20 crore. It also buys ₹89.36 crore of goods and raw materials from group companies.[1] So a growing share of the headline growth comes from the group, and the table below separates it.

Who owns the company, and why that matters to a minority shareholder

ESAB Corporation owns the brand, for which the Indian company pays a trademark licence fee, and group companies supply part of the product range and buy from Esab India. ESAB Europe GmbH of Switzerland alone supplied ₹54.57 crore of goods and was owed ₹38.56 crore at year end, and the Indian company owed the group ₹49.39 crore in total.[1] The Audit Committee approves these dealings as ordinary-course and at arm's length, which is the standard the law requires, but a minority shareholder cannot see the prices. The group also grants shares of the US parent to Indian staff under its 2022 Omnibus Incentive Plan; the Indian company books a cost of ₹3.29 crore for this, and the Managing Director's share-based expense was ₹2.92 crore.[1]

Rohit Gambhir has been Managing Director since 2013 and was reappointed on 1 November 2023 for five years, to 31 October 2028. B Mohan is Director and Chief Financial Officer. Kevin Johnson stepped down as chairman on 30 April 2026 and Curtis Evan Jewell, nominated by ESAB Holdings Limited, took the chair on 1 May 2026.[1] Independent directors chair the audit, nomination, stakeholder and social-responsibility committees, while the group's nominee chairs the board. Promoters have pledged none of their shares, a point we test in Chapter 3.[12] The practical result is a company run for steady cash, not for growth at any price: it has paid out 69 percent of profit as dividends in each of the last two years.

The industry the company sells into

One research house sizes India's welding consumables market at about US$1,323 million in 2026, growing 5.76 percent a year to US$1,852 million by 2031, a little faster than the 4.89 percent a year of 2020 to 2025.[25][26] It counts about 120 players, with local producers holding about 70 percent, and it names Esab India, Ador Welding, D&H Sécheron, EWAC Alloys and Lincoln Electric as the five major firms. EWAC Alloys is itself a company under common control with Esab India, and the Indian company sold it ₹83.46 crore of goods in FY26, so the competitor list is less independent than it looks.[1] Imported coated electrodes face a duty stack of about 31 percent, which shields domestic makers from the cheapest imports.[25]

Demand follows steel use. The Indian Steel Association projects apparent steel use of 159.8 million tonnes in 2025, 171.6 million in 2026 (up 7.4 percent) and 187.4 million in 2027 (up 9.2 percent), and says private capital spending is recovering in manufacturing, data centres and offices.[27] Welding volumes do not grow one for one with steel, because fabricators also switch to faster wires and machines, but the link is strong enough that a stall in steel use would show up here within a few quarters. The company's own annual report calls the year to March 2026 one of muted industrial growth and weak capital spending, which is why Chapter 2B tests growth against this backdrop.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence of a good business is how little money it needs to earn a lot. Esab India's fixed assets were ₹153.58 crore against FY26 sales of ₹1,508 crore, and capital spending was only ₹29.78 crore, about 2 percent of sales.[1] Return on capital employed, ROCE, was 64.7 percent on the latest reading and 65 percent for FY26, in a five-year path of 44, 71, 77, 70 and 65 percent.[10] Listed industrial peers earn far less: Kennametal India 33.2 percent, Grindwell Norton 21.2 percent, Timken India 18.3 percent and Carborundum Universal 10.5 percent.[19][20][22][21] Two things help explain it, though the filings do not put numbers on them: the company buys finished goods from group companies (₹89.36 crore in FY26) instead of building plants for them, and the group supplies the brand and technology, so the Indian company has not needed large expansions.

Pricing power shows up in the gross margin, the share of each rupee of sales left after materials. It rose from 40.7 percent in FY25 to 41.8 percent in FY26, in a year when management says commodity prices and energy costs surged and the rupee weakened.[2][1] Operating margin held at 17.9 percent against 17.8 percent, which means the company passed most cost increases on to buyers, with a lag. The lag is visible: the March 2026 quarter margin was 15.9 percent, down 202 basis points, before recovering to 18.8 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[25][15] In the June quarter, materials took 57.8 percent of sales against 58.5 percent a year earlier, and other costs fell to 13.7 percent of sales from 14.8 percent, which together explain the two-point margin gain.[4]

Reach is the third source of strength. A network of 393 dealers, over 120 trained sales and service engineers and three plants in the south and centre of the country gives the company coverage that a small rival cannot copy quickly.[1] Fabricators buy a consumable on the strength of repeatable weld quality and a dealer who can deliver tomorrow, so a known brand and a stocked dealer are real barriers, not slogans. Ken Research says entry barriers rise sharply for qualified alloys, automated wires and institutional approvals, and that demand is moving toward traceable, procedure-qualified products and away from undifferentiated rods, which favours a company with a long record and group technology behind it.[25]

What the competitors show

The nearest listed rival, Ador Welding, shows both the opportunity and the gap. Its June 2026 quarter revenue was about ₹309 crore, up 22.9 percent, and it returned to a profit of ₹27.6 crore after a loss a year earlier.[23][18] Its September 2026 quarter revenue was ₹345 crore, up 22.6 percent, but the operating margin was 10.9 percent against 12.4 percent a year before, and net profit rose only 7.7 percent.[24] Esab India's June 2026 quarter margin was 18.8 percent against about 12 percent at Ador in the same quarter, so it earns about seven points more on each rupee of sales than its closest peer, and the market pays about 39.2 times profit for it against 20.2 times for Ador.[15][10][18][23]

A margin gap of that size has two readings. The kind one is that brand, mix and group technology let Esab India charge more per kilogram of weld metal. The harsh one is that Ador is buying growth with lower prices and could push Esab India to follow if demand slows. The filings do not settle which is true, because neither company publishes price or volume by product. What can be said is that Ador's revenue is growing twice as fast as Esab India's did in FY26, which means Esab India's 9.8 percent growth is not simply the market's pace. Where the business is thin

Home growth is modest. India revenue rose only 6.4 percent in FY26, and goods bought from others and resold were flat at ₹321.21 crore, up 0.2 percent, so the growth came from the company's own factories (up 10.7 percent) and from services (up 35.1 percent).[1] The India figure is below the 7.4 percent growth projected for steel use in 2026, and the company itself describes the capital goods cycle as weak and the short-term outlook as challenging.[1][27] Foreign revenue rose 31.1 percent, but much of it is sales and services to group companies such as ESAB Middle East FZE (₹71.03 crore, up 55 percent) and ESAB Arabia (₹27.43 crore), and the parent says its Middle East revenue fell by double digits in the June 2026 quarter.[1][28] Sales and services to group companies rose by ₹64.58 crore in FY26, which is 48 percent of the ₹134.68 crore rise in revenue from operations (our arithmetic), so a pullback by those group customers would hit growth directly.[1]

Disclosure is also thin. We found no earnings-call record for the company, and it does not publish a segment split, order data or numeric guidance, which is management's own forecast of its figures, and no brokerage publishes estimates for it.[14][30][13] Customers owed it ₹254.07 crore at March 2026, and ₹95.66 crore of that, or 37.7 percent, was owed by group companies, of which ₹48.85 crore was owed by EWAC Alloys. That balance equals about 56 percent of a year of billings to EWAC (our arithmetic), against stated credit terms of 0 to 120 days.[1] None of this is a sign of fraud, but it does mean that a portion of profit has not yet become cash, and the market has no analyst to ask about it.

What this means for an owner

Esab India is a high-quality, low-capital business whose reported profit is real and whose margins are the best among its listed peers. It is not a fast grower at home, and a meaningful part of its recent growth depends on a parent that decides prices, products and where to buy. That mix supports a premium price but not an unlimited one: the market already pays 39.2 times profit, which is near its own three-year low, about double Ador's 20.2 times and below Grindwell's 47.0 times. For you as an owner, the practical meaning is a business to respect and a price to watch, not a bargain. Layer 1 is the quality check. It asks two things of every company: is the level of each measure good enough, and is the trend moving the right way? A company can pass today and still be fading, or fail today and be recovering, so we read both. One adjustment matters before the tables. Reported FY26 profit after tax of ₹206.69 crore includes two one-off items that the company shows as exceptional: a gain of ₹30.91 crore on selling land at Khardah in West Bengal, and a charge of ₹13.65 crore for higher gratuity cost under the new Labour Codes. The price-to-earnings multiple, or P/E, is the share price divided by EPS. Debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' equity.

4. What the price is doing

A price chart asks one simple question. Are buyers or sellers in control right now? We sort every chart into four stages. Stage 1 is a base, a flat stretch of trading after a fall. Stage 2 is the rising phase. Stage 3 is a top or a deep pullback. Stage 4 is the falling phase. A pivot is the top edge of a base, and a breakout is a close above it. A reading below 30 is called oversold, and above 70 overbought. ATR (average true range) is the usual daily price swing in rupees. We use it as a ruler for distance. 5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on future growth. A reverse DCF flips the usual question: instead of forecasting growth and working out a price, it takes today's price and solves for the growth rate that would justify it. DCF stands for discounted cash flow, the method of valuing a business by adding its future cash flows after discounting each for the time it takes to arrive. The ledger uses a 12 percent discount rate, a terminal multiple of 20 times profit in year ten, and trailing profit of ₹221.91 crore as the starting point.[31][32] The answer it returns is the compound growth the market is already pricing in.

The second question is whether the people who study the company are raising or cutting their forecasts, because rising estimates tend to pull prices up. Our variant perception

A variant perception is a view that differs from what the price or the consensus already says. Here the consensus is absent, so the price is the only expectation we can read, and it says: about 14.6 percent a year of profit growth for ten years, from a company whose sales grew 9.8 percent in FY26 and whose recurring profit grew 8.9 percent.[31][1] The price is therefore not cheap on growth. A first-time reader should not take the 36.6 times on the ledger as the valuation either, because it rests on profit that includes a one-off gain; on recurring profit the multiple is 39.5.

Our view differs from the market's in three ways. First, we think the headline of 37 percent profit growth in the June 2026 quarter overstates the run-rate: revenue grew 19.6 percent against a soft 6.8 percent a year earlier, so two-year growth is about 13 percent a year, and other costs fell to 13.7 percent of sales from 14.8 percent, a gain that may not repeat.[4][8] We pencil 11 percent revenue growth for the rest of FY27, giving earnings per share of about ₹144 for FY27 and ₹160 for FY28. That is 13.9 percent a year from recurring FY26 earnings of ₹123.4, close to the 14.6 percent the price needs, so the price looks about fair, not wrong.

Second, the tape and the filings disagree. The shares stood at ₹5,879 on the day the June quarter was reported, 11 August, and have since fallen 10.3 percent to ₹5,275.00, while operating profit rose 34 percent year on year.[33][15][31] The 63-day relative strength of +0.12 percent says the fall matched the index, so we read it mostly as the market's weakness, not as news about Esab India. We do not find company-specific bad news in the filings, but we also lack the September quarter, which is due on 4 November 2026.

Third, the shareholders are unusual. With no analyst coverage, the price is set by a handful of holders: two mutual-fund schemes hold 6.13 percent and 5.55 percent, foreign funds hold 1.52 percent, and the typical day sees only a few thousand shares change hands.[12][31] That can create gaps in either direction and gives a patient buyer a chance to buy a good business cheaply from a forced seller. Our variant, then, is narrow: we do not think the price is wrong, we think the margin of safety is thin at ₹5,275.00 and decent near ₹5,100 to ₹5,180, and we think the chart, not the business, is what is stopping us.

The number

The price of ₹5,275.00 asks for profit growth of 14.62 percent a year for ten years on reported profit, or 15.64 percent on recurring profit. If profit grows at the 11 percent it has delivered over three years, the same model gives about ₹4,009 a share on the ledger basis, 24 percent below today's price.[31][10]

How it was calculated

The model adds ten years of profit growing at a chosen rate, each discounted at 12 percent a year, to a terminal value of 20 times year-ten profit, also discounted. We solved it for the growth that returns today's market capitalisation of ₹8,119.82 crore, and then reversed it to price an 11 percent and a 13 percent path. We reproduced the ledger's 14.62 percent exactly before changing the starting profit.

The starting profit of ₹221.91 crore, market capitalisation of ₹8,119.82 crore, 12 percent discount rate and 20 times terminal multiple are the ledger's.[31][32] The recurring profit of about ₹205.5 crore comes from the quarterly filings in Chapter 3.[6][5][4] The delivered growth rates are Screener's three-year compound rates.[10] There are 1.5393 crore shares.

If the model is too harsh, because Esab India keeps a premium multiple for years, the ₹6,270 central value in the next estimate is the better guide. If it is right, the price at 39 times profit is already paying for good news, and the support at ₹5,096.50 is not a bargain level but a fair one. This tension is the reason we scale the bear probability to 25 percent and keep the size small, and it is why a 12-month target built on the market's multiple has to be read with the 11 to 13 percent DCF value in mind.

The number

We estimate earnings per share of about ₹144 for FY27 (year to March 2027) and about ₹160 for FY28, against recurring FY26 earnings of about ₹123.4 and reported ₹134.30.

How it was calculated

For FY27 we took the June 2026 quarter as reported, with sales of ₹421.12 crore and operating profit of ₹79.17 crore, and grew the other three quarters' FY26 sales of ₹1,156.13 crore by 11 percent at an 18.2 percent operating margin, which gives sales of about ₹1,704 crore and operating profit of about ₹313 crore. We added ₹6 crore of other income, subtracted ₹18.5 crore of depreciation and ₹1.2 crore of finance cost, applied 26 percent tax and divided by 1.5393 crore shares. For FY28 we grew sales another 11 percent at the same 18.4 percent margin.

The inputs

Quarterly sales and profit come from the company's results filings, other income, depreciation and finance cost run-rates from the FY26 accounts and the June quarter, and the 11 percent growth from the three-year sales growth of 11 percent.[4][5][1][10] Margins of 18.2 to 18.4 percent match the FY26 level of 17.9 percent and the June 2026 level of 18.8 percent.[1][15]

The range

In the bull case, sales grow 16 and 15 percent and the margin is 19.5 percent, giving FY28 earnings per share of about ₹180. In the bear case, sales grow 6 percent a year and the margin falls to 16 percent, the level near March 2026's 15.9 percent, giving about ₹123. The central case is therefore a 13.9 percent a year rise in recurring earnings over two years; the bull earnings are 12.5 percent above it and the bear earnings 23 percent below it.

If the number is off

Each ₹10 of FY28 earnings per share is worth about ₹392 a share at 39.2 times, or 7.4 percent of today's price. If September's margin prints at 18 percent or better with sales growth of 11 percent or more, FY28 earnings per share would move toward ₹163 and the release level in Chapter 7 would rise. If the margin is under 16.5 percent, the central case is wrong by about ₹17 a share of FY28 earnings, or about ₹660 of value, and we would drop the idea.

The number

Our probability-weighted 12-month value is ₹5,985 a share, 13.5 percent above the ₹5,275.00 close, or about 14.9 percent with the ₹75 a share of dividends declared for FY26.

How it was calculated

We multiplied each scenario's FY28 earnings per share by its multiple and weighted the three values: 20 percent of ₹7,920, 55 percent of ₹6,270 and 25 percent of ₹3,810. That is ₹1,584 plus ₹3,448.50 plus ₹952.50, or ₹5,985. The hurdle is 12 percent, which is ₹5,908 on a ₹5,275.00 starting price.

The inputs

FY28 earnings per share come from the previous estimate. The central multiple of 39.2 is the current price-to-earnings, which our rule uses when it is below the three-year median of 47.5; the bull multiple of 44 is just under that median, and the bear multiple of 31 is below the three-year low of 38.7.[11][32] The dividend is the three declarations of ₹25 a share for FY26.[1]

The range

Values run from ₹3,810 in the bear case (27.8 percent below today) to ₹7,920 in the bull case (50.1 percent above). The result passes the hurdle by only 1.5 percentage points: if the bear probability rises above about 28 percent, taking the weight from the central case, the weighted value falls below ₹5,908 and the hurdle fails. At a 30 percent bear weight the value is ₹5,862, a return of 11.1 percent at the close.

If the number is off

If instead the multiple returns to the 47.5 median, the central case would be worth ₹7,600. The weighted value is a guide, not a promise; because the margin over the hurdle is thin, the pass here supports waiting for the entry zone and does not support buying at the close.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions, and one of them is always weaker than the rest. This chapter names the weakest assumption under each estimate. It says what evidence would prove it wrong and when that evidence will arrive. It then gives the strongest honest bear case against our own view, and picks the one claim on which the whole view hangs.

The dates that matter are few. The September 2026 quarter results and a possible interim dividend are due at a board meeting on 4 November 2026.[10] The December 2026 quarter will probably be reported in early February 2027, but the date is not announced; last year the board met on 10 February.[6] The FY27 full-year results should come in late May 2027, going by the 27 May 2026 meeting that approved FY26, and the annual report in July.[5][1] The next chapters turn those dates into a decision, so each flaw below ends with its date.

Earnings estimates: the weakest assumption is an operating margin near 18.4 percent

Our FY27 and FY28 earnings of ₹144 and ₹160 per share assume the margin stays near 18.4 percent. The five-quarter range is wide: 17, 19, 20, 16 and 19 percent. The March 2026 quarter showed a drop of 2 points in one quarter, when costs rose before prices.[10][25] The June 2026 gain came mainly from other costs falling to 13.7 percent of sales from 14.8 percent. That item can reverse.[4] Staff costs also rose 20.3 percent in FY26 against sales growth of 9.8 percent, partly from a new ₹3.29 crore share-award cost. Separately, the Labour Code charge was booked as a one-off while the rules are not final, so a further charge is possible.[1]

The evidence that would prove us wrong is a September 2026 operating margin below 16.5 percent. Another sign would be staff cost above 10 percent of sales, against 9.6 percent in FY26 and 9.7 percent in June. We will know on 4 November 2026 and again in early February 2027. If the margin is 18 percent or better, the estimate stands and the release level in Chapter 7 can rise.

Valuation: the weakest assumption is that the market keeps paying 39 times profit

The ₹6,270 central value multiplies FY28 earnings by 39.2, the multiple the shares carry today. That multiple has already fallen from 60.9 in November 2023. The three-year low is 38.7, so a further fall is possible if growth stays under 12 percent.[11] The reverse DCF in Chapter 5 shows the other side. At 11 to 13 percent growth and a 20 times exit multiple, the model's value is ₹4,009 to ₹4,666 a share, well below the price. Our central case therefore depends on the market choosing to value this company as a quality compounder. It has made that choice for years, but it need not keep making it.

The evidence that would prove us wrong is a price-to-earnings multiple under 36 on recurring profit with no cut to earnings. That would mean the market is re-rating the whole class of multinational-owned industrial names. Peers trade between 42.4 and 51.5 times, so the group multiple is a good watch point.[19][20][22] This can be seen in any session. The reaction to the 4 November results is the first test.

The group relationship: the weakest assumption is that group buying and fees stay stable

About 48 percent of the FY26 rise in revenue came from sales and services to group companies. Related-party receivables were ₹95.66 crore, and EWAC Alloys owed ₹48.85 crore of that. By our arithmetic that is about 56 percent of a year of billings to that company.[1] The parent says its Middle East revenue fell by double digits in the June 2026 quarter. ESAB Middle East FZE and ESAB Arabia together bought ₹98.46 crore of goods from the Indian company in FY26.[28][1] The trademark licence fee rose 10.0 percent to ₹51.93 crore. It is paid to a group entity, so the rate is a related-party term that minority shareholders cannot see being negotiated.[1]

The evidence that would prove us wrong is a licence fee above 4 percent of revenue, group-owed receivables above 40 percent of the total, or a fall in sales to Middle East group companies. The annual report for FY27, due around July 2027, will give the full related-party schedule. The quarterly filings give some of it sooner.

The red-team bear case: the strongest honest argument against our view

The bear says: you are paying 39 times profit for a company whose India sales grew 6.4 percent. Its margin is at a cyclical high of 18 percent after 13 percent in FY22, and nearly half of its revenue growth came from its own parent.[1][10] On the ledger's own reverse DCF, growth at the level of the past supports ₹4,000 to ₹4,700 a share, which is 11 to 24 percent below the price. The chart is weak, with the 50-day average falling under the 200-day, and the price is only 12 percent above the 52-week low. A handful of funds hold the shares, and no analyst follows them.[31][12]

The bear adds that prices can gap. Just 1,588 shares traded on the NSE on 28 October, about ₹84 lakh, and the 20-day average implied by the ledger is about 2,600 shares, or about ₹1.4 crore a day. At the portfolio's net worth of ₹43.52 lakh, a 3.5 percent position is about ₹1.52 lakh, roughly 29 shares and under 2 percent of a day's volume, so getting in and out is possible. The risk is a thin print that jumps through the stop, so the stop price is not a guaranteed exit.[31] If the margin reverts to 16 percent and growth halves, earnings of about ₹123 at 31 times give ₹3,810. That is a loss of 27.8 percent, with a gap through the stop on the way.

Our answer is partial. The multiple is already near its three-year low, and the business quality is real, with 65 percent ROCE and no debt. A drop to 31 times needs a story, not just time. But the bear's point about thin trading and group dependence is sound. That is why we keep the size at Tier 3, weight the bear case at 25 percent and decline to buy at the close.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, with gate status PASSED_CONDITIONAL. The rule is the technical routing rule in scoring.json. It rejects a stock on the chart only when the close is below a falling 50-day average, that average is below a falling 200-day average, and Test C fails. The 50-day average is falling. But the 200-day average rose about 1.5 percent over 63 sessions, from ₹5,697.82 to ₹5,780.70. So that red line is not met.[31][32] No other red line is met. Layer 2 scores 3.5 of 5.0 with no failure. Reward-to-risk is 3.11 at the close and 4.84 at the zone top, against the Core Alpha floor of 2.0. No momentum test passes, and Test C fails on the short-term turn. So we do not buy at the market. This is a wait on timing, not a rejection. Esab India is not in the portfolio, so it is a pipeline challenger, and the action today is that no order is placed.

The plan is a conditional limit order, and no order stands across the 4 November 2026 Q2 results. After the results we re-run the target, the stop and the momentum gate. We also re-run two tests at the actual fill: reward-to-risk of at least 2.0, and a weighted 12-month return of at least 12 percent. An order goes live in the zone of ₹5,100.00 to ₹5,180.00 only if all of these hold. The September quarter keeps operating margin at 16.5 percent or better, with India growth of 5 percent or better. FY28 earnings per share stay near ₹160 or higher. The 200-day average is not falling. Both tests still pass at the price. The tests are binding. On today's inputs the 2.0 floor allows a fill up to about ₹5,393. The 12 percent hurdle allows about ₹5,344 on a 25 percent bear weight, or about ₹5,234 on a 30 percent weight. So the zone top of ₹5,180 passes both, while the close of ₹5,275 passes only the 25 percent case. The event that changes the plan is a close above ₹5,438.35 with a positive two-week change or positive 28-day relative strength, which passes Test C. Then a dip entry becomes eligible. A fill at ₹5,438.35 would need a central value of about ₹6,406 to keep the 2.0 floor. That is FY28 earnings of about ₹163.4. Eligibility does not mean a buy at that price. After a Test C pass the order still goes live only in the ₹5,100 to ₹5,180 zone, because a fill near ₹5,438 fails the 12 percent hurdle on today's inputs.

Layer 3 asks how far the price is from support, measured in ATR. The nearest support is the ₹5,096.50 swing low, and the close is 1.26 ATR above it.[31] Our ladder gives full size at 0.6 ATR or less, standard size to 1.2, starter or limit orders only to 1.6, and no chase beyond that.[32] At 1.26 the stock sits in the band for limit orders only, at reduced size, so even with a clean chart we would not buy at the market. A limit order is an order to buy only at a price we choose, or lower. The entry zone of ₹5,100.00 to ₹5,180.00 runs from just above the swing low to 0.6 ATR above it. The structural stop is ₹4,954.72, the 63-day base low of ₹5,096.50 minus one ATR of ₹141.78, which is the Core Alpha placement rule for a base. A close below it cancels the order and the idea.

Size comes from risk, not from conviction alone. We assign Tier 3, which risks 50 basis points of portfolio value; a basis point is one hundredth of a percent. From the zone top of ₹5,180 the stop is 4.35 percent away, so risk alone would allow 11.5 percent of the portfolio, and the Tier 3 cap of 3.5 percent binds.[32] Tier 2 would allow 6.5 percent on a 75 basis point budget. Group dependence, no analyst coverage and thin trading argue against that much conviction, so we stay at Tier 3. The planned size if filled is 3.5 percent and the live size today is 0.0 percent. At the portfolio's net worth of ₹43.52 lakh that is about ₹1.52 lakh, or roughly 29 shares at ₹5,180. A loss at the stop would be about 15 basis points of the portfolio, well inside the 50 basis point budget.[31] The size is small against a daily volume of about ₹1.4 crore. The real risk is a gap through the stop on a thin print, not an inability to sell.

Two stresses from the red-team review frame the risk. First, FY28 earnings stay at ₹160 but the multiple falls to 31 times. The value is then ₹4,960, a loss of 6.0 percent from the close and 4.2 percent from ₹5,180. That level lies just above the stop. Second, the bear weight is 30 percent instead of 25. The weighted value is then ₹5,862. That is +11.1 percent at the close, which fails the 12 percent hurdle. It is +13.2 percent at ₹5,180 and +14.9 percent at ₹5,100, which pass. That is why we plan an order only in the zone and never at the close. It is also why a chart cure alone does not release the order. The valuation must be re-checked with the results.

If the idea is ever bought and the stock recovers into Stage 2, the rule is to let the winner run. We would not sell because RSI passes 70 or because the weight passes 15 percent. The position could grow to 22 percent of the portfolio while we trail the stop up behind the rising 21-day exponential average or the 50-day average, and we would sell only when the trend or the business breaks, for example on a close below the trailing stop or an operating margin under 16.5 percent for two quarters.
