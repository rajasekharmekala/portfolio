Before you read

DLF Limited is India's largest listed real estate developer, and it sells luxury apartments mostly in Gurugram and owns offices through DLF Cyber City Developers Limited (DCCDL)[1][2]. Our verdict is ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP: buy only with limit orders between Rs 640 and Rs 655, with a stop at Rs 625.80. At Rs 655 the reward-to-risk ratio is 3.94 to 1, but at today's Rs 671 it is only 2.19 to 1. The balance sheet is safe, with net cash of Rs 15,084 crore and no promoter pledge, but earnings are weak because revenue fell 23% and the Rs 1,301 crore profit includes a one-time Rs 738 crore gain[1][2][3]. The idea fails if the big Gurugram projects are not finished by March 2028, because that is when most booked profit reaches the income statement[1][2].

1. What the company does

DLF began as Delhi Land and Finance, has close to eight decades of track record, and built well-known neighbourhoods of Delhi and Gurugram, including DLF City in Gurugram.[32][9][24] Today it earns money in two ways. First, it builds homes and sells them to buyers, who pay in instalments while the building is under construction. Second, through DCCDL, it owns offices and malls and collects rent from tenants.[1]

The two businesses behave differently. The home-building business is lumpy: DLF launches a project, sells it out within weeks, and then collects cash while building it, a cycle management puts at about four years.[10] Sales booked in a quarter can be very large or very small. In the last four quarters, bookings were Rs 419 crore, Rs 3,967 crore, Rs 657 crore and Rs 3,800 crore, a total of Rs 8,843 crore, against Rs 20,143 crore in the whole of FY26.[12][8][5][1] The rental business is steady: DCCDL has long leases, 96% of the area is let, and its profit grew 17% in the September quarter.[1]

Revenue and profit are not the same as cash for this company. DLF follows the completed contract method, which means it records revenue and profit on a home only when the building receives its occupancy certificate. Management states that about Rs 75,000 crore of launched projects are waiting to be recognised, and it says the revenue turning point falls in FY27 to FY28 as the Arbour project and the Privana projects are completed.[2] Until then, cash flow looks strong and reported revenue looks weak. That gap is the main puzzle in this dossier.

Because DLF owns 66.67% of DCCDL but accounting rules do not let it consolidate DCCDL, its income statement shows only the share of DCCDL profit and the dividend. Management confirmed that DCCDL's revenue of about Rs 2,000 crore a quarter and Atrium Place's Rs 150 to 175 crore a quarter are not added to DLF's reported revenue.[2] A reader who compares DLF with a company that does consolidate its rentals will see a smaller company than the one that actually exists.

Who pays DLF, and why concentration matters

Home buyers pay DLF in instalments linked to construction milestones. The rental arm is paid by corporate tenants. DLF does not publish customer concentration, so that figure is not published.[24] The more useful concentration measure is geography. The rating agency ICRA noted in December 2025 that the National Capital Region (NCR) made up 84% of DLF's sales in the first half of FY26 and that two project launches supplied most of those sales.[19]

Buyers also pay a booking amount of Rs 50 lakh on the Aureva retirement project, which shows the price tier DLF now sells into: about Rs 1,985 crore of potential sales were sold out in one quarter.[2] That price tier explains both the strong margins and the dependence on a few large launches.

The cash rules every buyer must follow (RERA 70% accounts)

Indian law requires developers to place 70% of buyer payments in a separate escrow account that can be used only for that project. DLF held Rs 12,270 crore in these accounts out of its Rs 15,084 crore of net cash on 30 September 2026.[1] So most of the cash cannot be paid out as dividends today. It is released as projects are built and completed, which management says starts in FY27 and FY28.[10]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

DLF's strength is a combination of land, brand and balance sheet. It owns a very large, low-cost land bank in the most expensive part of the Delhi region: management puts the development potential at 188 msf, of which 27 msf is under construction and 25 msf is in the launch pipeline.[1] That land was bought years ago, so each new launch carries a high embedded margin. Management's figures show about Rs 28,700 crore of margin yet to be recognised on homes already sold and another Rs 10,825 crore on launched but unsold inventory.[1]

The brand lets DLF raise prices as it sells. On the Dahlias super-luxury project, management said the price had already risen by almost 10% in the latest quarter, that 274 apartments had been sold, and that average realisation on what it sold in the quarter was much higher than Rs 1,00,000 per square foot.[2] A company with weak pricing power cannot do that. This is the clearest evidence of a competitive advantage in the dossier, and it is supported by management's own words only; no independent price study was found.

The balance sheet is now unusually clean for a developer. Gross debt in the home-building business reached zero in the year to March 2026, which management had stated as a goal and ICRA used to raise its rating to AA+ in December 2025.[13][19] CRISIL also rates DLF AA+ with a stable outlook, and it rates DCCDL AAA.[20][21] Net cash was Rs 15,084 crore on 30 September 2026. DLF paid Rs 1,980 crore of dividends in the quarter, and net cash slipped only slightly from Rs 15,200 crore in June.[1][3][5]

The weaknesses are real. Reported revenue fell 23% to Rs 1,261 crore in the September quarter and fell 25.6% in the first half, because few large projects reached completion.[1][4] Most of the Rs 1,301 crore quarterly profit came from items that are not repeatable: a one-time Rs 738 crore valuation gain, and profit shared from DCCDL and other joint ventures.[1][2] Staff cost and other expenses grew about 8% in FY26 even as management had promised to optimise overheads.[8][16] Return on capital employed is only 6.32%, which is low for a business that looks this strong.[24]

Why return on capital looks low, and what that does and does not mean

Return on capital employed (ROCE) is operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. DLF's reading is 6.32% and its return on equity is 9.50%.[24] Both are far under the 15% level the scoring framework expects from a compounder. Two things pull the figure down. First, the balance sheet carries Rs 22,947 crore of investments, mostly the stake in DCCDL (Rs 20,481 crore of joint venture and associate investments at March 2026), whose profit arrives as share-of-profit rather than operating profit.[24][17] Second, 'other current liabilities' of Rs 23,396 crore sit on the books, which we read as mostly customer payments received in advance for homes not yet recognised, so assets look large while profit is still waiting.[17]

The low reading is therefore partly an accounting effect, not only a weak business. Even so, it is a fact: shareholders today earn single-digit returns on book value, and the case for owning DLF depends on those returns rising when completed projects are recognised from FY28.

Where DLF stands against listed peers

Among large listed developers, DLF is the biggest by market value at Rs 1,66,093 crore, against Rs 1,00,778 crore for Lodha, Rs 68,115 crore for Phoenix Mills and Rs 61,554 crore for Prestige.[24][26][29][28] It trades at 36.1 times trailing earnings, near the middle of the peer range from 22.1 to 54.0 times, while its return on capital is the lowest in the group.[24][25][26][27][28][29] Chapter 3 sets out the matrix in full. The reasons investors accept a low return are DLF's rental arm and its land bank, which no peer matches in the Delhi region.

The market backdrop and the interest-rate setting

Anarock reports that housing sales in the top seven cities rose 3% year on year in July to September 2026 to about 1,00,200 units, that prices rose 7%, and that the Delhi region held 18 months of unsold inventory.[31] The Reserve Bank of India repo rate stands at 5.50% as of 27 October 2026, which keeps mortgage costs steady.[30] Demand is therefore stable, not booming. DLF's super-luxury customers are less rate-sensitive than mass buyers, but the volume of its sales depends on the timing of its own launches more than on the market.

A developer-landlord cannot be judged on revenue growth and a price-to-earnings ratio alone, because its cash and its reported profit move at different times. We chose five measures that govern this business. Pre-sales show whether customers still want what DLF builds. Collections and cash show whether bookings turn into money. Rental income and occupancy measure the steady half. Embedded margin shows whether new launches are profitable. Reported revenue conversion shows how soon that profit reaches shareholders.[1]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management guidance can be checked against what happened. Four statements made in 2024 and 2025 can be tested today. DLF met its FY26 bookings guidance, it achieved zero gross debt, and it raised the dividend. It missed or slipped on overhead control, on the timing of several launches and on the pace of rental growth.[15][8][9][16]

On the FY26 pre-sales target, an analyst on the October 2025 call cited guidance of Rs 200 to 210 billion and management said it would work to that commitment. The company reported Rs 20,143 crore, at the low end of the range.[15][9] On debt, the Q3 FY26 results stated that the goal of zero gross debt had been achieved ahead of the estimated timeline, and the Q4 press release confirmed zero gross debt in the development business at year end.[13][12][9] On shareholder returns, the dividend rose 33% to Rs 8 a share, Rs 1,980 crore in total, and was paid in the September quarter.[9][1]

The misses matter just as much. In October 2024, DLF's presentation listed 'optimizing organizational overheads' as a priority, yet staff cost rose from Rs 592 crore to Rs 640 crore and other expenses rose from Rs 1,161 crore to Rs 1,257 crore in FY26.[16][8] In October 2025 management named Goa, Arbour senior living, Privana, Hamilton 2, a Westpark phase and Panchkula as launches for the next five and a half quarters; one year on, the senior living project (Aureva) launched in September 2026 and the Goa residential project is held up in litigation.[15][6][1]

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter puts five years of numbers next to each other, then applies the first of the three underwriting layers. All figures are consolidated and in rupees crore unless stated. The first thing to see is that sales grew only moderately: from Rs 5,717 crore in FY22 to Rs 8,194 crore in FY26, a rise of 12.9% a year over the last three years, which is joint-lowest in the peer group. Net profit rose faster, from Rs 1,500 crore to Rs 4,415 crore, while operating margin fell.[24] Profit grew faster than sales for two reasons. DLF now shows a large share of profit from DCCDL and other joint ventures, and it earns more other income, such as interest and valuation gains.[8][24]

The second thing to see is cash. Cash from operations rose from Rs 2,539 crore in FY24 to Rs 5,235 crore in FY25 and Rs 6,347 crore in FY26.[24] That is a sound number, but the audited cash flow statement shows where it comes from. In FY26, operating profit before working capital changes was only Rs 1,534 crore, and an increase of Rs 6,251 crore in other liabilities, which we read as customer advances, explains almost all of the Rs 6,347 crore of operating cash flow.[17] Customers paying ahead of construction create cash now and a delivery obligation later.

The third thing is debt. Borrowings fell from Rs 4,103 crore in March 2025 to Rs 306 crore in March 2026, and to Rs 314 crore in September 2026.[24] Interest cost fell from Rs 397 crore in FY25 to Rs 93 crore over the last twelve months.[24] DLF is, for practical purposes, a company without bank debt. Most of the group's debt now sits in DCCDL, which is not consolidated.[1]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above has a weak point. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, builds the strongest case against the idea, and states the single claim on which the whole idea depends and when we will know it.

Weakest assumption behind each estimate

The FY28 earnings estimate (Rs 22.5) rests on the assumption that 20% of the Rs 12,850 crore Arbour-cluster margin is recognised by March 2028. The evidence that would show it is wrong is a delay in occupancy certificates for Arbour towers, which management itself ties to 'sometime next year'. A stoppage of construction on air-quality grounds would also count. Management says construction outflows may stay low in the third quarter. The reasons are labour migration and Delhi's pollution restrictions, which have typically lasted 30 to 40 days in Gurugram.[2] We will know more at the Q3 FY27 results in January 2027, when construction spending and completion dates are updated.

The target multiple of 34.2 assumes that investors keep paying today's price for next year's earnings. The weak point is that today's multiple already includes a recovery in profit. If estimates fall again, the multiple on the lower estimate rises. The stock must then fall to restore the old ratio. We will know when consensus FY28 EPS stops falling, which the monthly estimate data show.[24]

The FY27 bookings guidance of Rs 20,000 crore is not our estimate, but our verdict depends on it. The weak point is the second half: it must produce Rs 15,543 crore against Rs 4,386 crore in the second half of FY26.[6][5][1][9][12] The main reason is dependence on a few launches. The evidence that would show it is wrong is third-quarter bookings below Rs 5,000 crore, about two-thirds of the Rs 7,771 crore a quarter that the second half needs (Rs 15,543 crore over two quarters). We would know in January 2027.

Red-team case: the strongest argument against buying

The case against is that DLF is an expensive stock with falling estimates and no earnings-led profit growth until FY28. The stock trades at 36.1 times trailing earnings, in line with peers, but its return on capital of 6.32% is the lowest in the group.[24][25][26][27][28][29] Revenue fell 23% in the September quarter, operating profit fell from Rs 284 crore to Rs 23 crore, and the quarterly profit was kept up by a Rs 738 crore valuation gain on an investment that management itself calls one-time.[1][2][24] Dividends of Rs 1,980 crore were paid while revenue and operating profit are falling and the reported rise in profit rests on a one-time gain.

The bear also argues that the cash is not the shareholders' cash. Rs 12,270 crore of the Rs 15,084 crore of net cash sits in RERA accounts reserved for construction, and the Rs 23,396 crore of other current liabilities is largely owed to customers in homes still to be built.[1][17] Management concedes that delivery is the issue, and the FY27 to FY28 turn in revenue is described with 'hopefully' language on the Q2 call.[2] Lastly, foreign institutional holding has fallen from 16.66% in September 2024 to 11.43% in September 2026. Domestic institutions have bought instead. So the price has been held up by domestic flows, not by overseas buying.[24][23]

We take the red-team case seriously. It does not turn the limit-order plan into a rejection. No red line is crossed, the balance sheet is clean and the stop is close. It does set the size: a TIER_3 position capped at 3.5% of the portfolio, built in two halves.
