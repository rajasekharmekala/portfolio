1. What the company does

Marksans Pharma Limited, listed on the NSE as MARKSANS and on the BSE as 524404, is a Mumbai-based maker of finished-dose generic medicines: tablets, capsules, liquids, ointments and sachets.[1] In FY26 about 80 percent of its sales were over-the-counter (OTC) products, which a customer can buy without a prescription, and 20 percent were prescription (Rx) products.[1] Most of the OTC work is store-brand manufacturing: the company makes the product and a retailer or pharmacy chain sells it under its own name. CARE Ratings puts these retail and pharmacy customers at about 85 percent of OTC revenue, with the rest sold under Marksans' own labels made at its New York plant.[2]

The customers are large retail and pharmacy chains, on contracts of about three years in which prices are fixed for the term.[2] That fixed price matters for the profit margin, because a rise in the cost of a raw material, a solvent or a shipping container during the term is absorbed by the company unless the contract is reopened. The group reports one business segment, pharmaceuticals, but splits revenue by customer location. In FY26 the United States and North America supplied ₹1,533.5 crore of ₹2,950.9 crore, or 52.0 percent; Europe and the UK ₹1,015.0 crore (34.4 percent); Australia and New Zealand ₹303.1 crore (10.3 percent). And the rest of the world ₹99.4 crore (3.4 percent).[3] Two customers each supplied 10 percent or more of group revenue, together ₹679.1 crore or 23.0 percent. In FY25 no customer reached that level. The filing does not name them.[3]

By therapy, pain management is the largest line at ₹1,310.8 crore, or 44.4 percent of FY26 revenue, followed by cough and cold at ₹460.9 crore and gastrointestinal products at ₹384.0 crore.[1] CARE treats this as a weakness, because one therapy area carries about 45 percent of sales.[2] It counts more than 350 approved product licences and over 200 products in development, which is how the company plans to widen the range over time.[2]

Three markets, three different businesses

The United States business is almost all OTC. Management said it was historically about 95 percent OTC and 5 percent Rx and may move toward 85 and 15.[4] It is run through Time-Cap Laboratories, Inc., a wholly owned subsidiary bought in 2015 with a plant at Farmingdale, New York.[1] In August 2025 the managing director said about 35 to 40 percent of US revenue is made in the US and the rest in India.[5] The company describes its US business as about $175 million and now aims at $300 million.[1][4]

The UK business runs through Marksans Pharma (UK) Ltd and two older purchases: Bell, Sons and Co. (Druggists), bought in 2007, which sells OTC products, and Relonchem, bought in 2008, which sells prescription medicines. Management puts the UK split at about 55 percent Rx and 45 percent OTC.[1][4] UK revenue was hurt by price pressure in early FY26 and then recovered: the UK business without QliniQ grew 53.1 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[2][1]

Europe is new. In June 2026 the company completed the purchase of QliniQ B.V. in the Netherlands, and in July 2026 it agreed to buy ABCnow GmbH in Germany for €892,384 in cash; ABCnow's turnover at 31 December 2025 was €227,233.69.[6][7] Management expects European revenue of about ₹180 crore this year and an Rx share of 80 to 85 percent, a higher mix of prescription products than the US business.[4] Ireland and Canada are new entities with no revenue yet. Management expects German revenue in the third quarter of FY27 and Irish revenue next year.[1][4]

Plants, capacity and where the product is made

The company has four manufacturing units: two in Goa, one at Farmingdale, New York, and one at Southport in the UK, plus four research centres.[1][2] The company lists total capacity of 26 billion units a year. The second Goa plant was bought from Teva Pharma India in 2023 and has scalable capacity of 8 billion units. The stated plan is to double Indian capacity to 16 billion units a year.[1][8] Management says 65 to 70 percent of sales are made in India.[4] Where a product is made matters for two reasons. Plants in India give the company a cost advantage against rivals that make everything in the US or Europe. But Indian-made goods shipped to the US are the part exposed to any future US duty on imported generics, which Chapter 2 sizes. Management said it may need another unit in India within a year or two, so more capital spending could follow.[4]

Who owns and runs the company

The promoter family holds 43.87 percent, in five names led by Mark Saldanha, the founder, chairman and managing director. None of those shares is pledged (Chapter 3).[9][1] OrbiMed Asia IV (Mauritius), a foreign venture fund, held 39,050,881 shares, or 8.62 percent, in the September 2026 filing.[9] Related-party items in FY26 are small: ₹1.07 crore of rent paid to the chairman and ₹13.3 crore of pay for five key managers, of which ₹7.7 crore went to the chairman.[3] What this means for you: the owner-managers are paid modestly and own nearly half the company, but one financial investor with 8.6 percent could become a large seller. A block sale could push the price down for days whatever the business news.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business is that it is a trusted, low-cost supplier to retailers who want a store-brand partner, and that it has grown every year since FY17 while keeping net cash. The case against is that the position rests on fixed-price contracts with a few large customers, that margins move by several points within a year, and that over half of sales go to one country whose trade policy is in flux. Chapter 2B turns the tests into scores.

One figure frames everything else. The gross margin is sales minus the direct cost of making the goods, shown as a share of sales. It was 56.7 percent in FY26 and 59.1 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[10][1] EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a company's operating profit before those four items. Quarterly EBITDA margins in the last five quarters ran 16.1, 20.1, 21.3, 22.8 and 25.3 percent.[1] That nine-point swing shows how much depends on input costs and product mix.

Evidence for the moat: scale in store brands and a record of renewal

The moat is a position, not a patent. OTC revenue grew from ₹445 crore in FY17 to ₹2,361 crore in FY26, about 5.3 times, a compound rate of 20.4 percent a year.[1] Management says the number of items supplied to each retail customer is rising, which is how a store-brand supplier grows: if the company supplies 10 items today, a customer may add 10 more.[4] The company also says some larger competitors are struggling, which it treats as good news.[4] Two rating agencies back the credit story: CARE rates the bank facilities AA- with a stable outlook, and India Ratings revised its AA- outlook to positive in June 2026.[2]

No market-share figure is published, the customers are not named, and CARE lists non-renewal of private-label contracts among the risks.[2] Switching a supplier means new approvals, which protects the incumbent, but fixed prices stop the company passing on cost increases. So we treat the moat as real but narrow.

Margin: what repeats and what came from cheap stock

In the June 2026 quarter the gross margin rose to 59.1 percent because of product mix, lower-cost materials already sitting in inventory, and a favourable exchange rate. The chief executive said the comfortable level is 55 to 56 percent, and that the stock of cheap materials is being used up.[4] CARE also wrote that the elevated June-quarter margin was primarily due to an inventory gain and that it expects about 20 to 21 percent in the medium term.[2] Company guidance (the outlook a company gives for its own results) for FY27 is revenue growth of 15 to 20 percent and an EBITDA margin of 20 to 21 percent. In August 2026 management said 21 to 22 percent by year end is achievable.[10][4] Our central case uses 21.0 percent for the three remaining quarters of FY27, which is below the 22.8 percent of the March quarter and well below 25.3 percent (Chapter 5).

The June 2025 quarter showed a 16.1 percent EBITDA margin after UK price pressure and tariff uncertainty.[1][2][5] A repeat is possible if raw-material costs jump, and management named higher solvent, freight and raw-material costs from the West Asia conflict as a headwind.[4][10]

The US regulator's record: three plants, no serious action found

Three of the four plants sell into the US and are inspected by the US FDA: the original Goa plant, the second Goa plant bought from Teva, and the New York plant. The inspection history is clean on the serious outcomes. The original Goa plant was inspected from 9 to 17 April 2024. The press reported a Form 483 with five observations on 18 April, and on 20 August 2024 the company announced that the FDA had closed the inspection as Voluntary Action Indicated.[11][12] The second Goa plant was inspected from 3 to 7 November 2025 with zero Form 483 observations.[13] The New York plant was inspected from 16 to 24 April 2025 with one observation reported by the press, and the company received the Establishment Inspection Report on 30 June 2025.[14][15] The company's letters do not state the New York classification. The PharmaCompass list, a secondary source, shows Voluntary Action Indicated for the April 2025 inspection and No Action Indicated for the November 2025 Goa inspection.[16]

US tariff exposure on generics

The legal position today is favourable. Proclamation 11020 of 2 April 2026 put 100 percent Section 232 duties on patented drugs and their ingredients but excludes generics, and CARE says the generic segment remains exempt.[18][2] On 22 July 2026 the President posted a plan for generics on social media: no duty for two years from 1 August 2026, 100 percent from August 2028 and 200 percent from 2029. It is not a proclamation, order or rule, so it cannot be enforced.[18] At the August call the chairman said he would not put much weight on the 200 percent figure, and that building more US capacity is always an option.[4]

Exposure in numbers (our estimate): the US and North America supplied 52.0 percent of FY26 revenue, ₹1,533.5 crore.[3] If 35 to 40 percent of US revenue is made in the US, as management said in August 2025, then 60 to 65 percent, or about ₹920 to ₹997 crore, is imported, which is 31 to 34 percent of group revenue.[5] A duty is charged on the import value, which is lower than the US selling price, so that revenue figure is an upper bound. Each 10 percent of duty the company could not pass on would cost at most ₹90 to ₹100 crore a year. Management said in May 2026 that it built extra inventory the year before to stay ahead of any tariff zone.[10] The plan would start in August 2028, after the 12-month horizon of our value, so it bears on the multiple and the 24-month cross-check and not on the central value in Chapter 5. Five questions stay separate: whether the plan is only announced or enacted, whether OTC products would count as generic drugs (not verified), where the goods are made, who bears the duty after pass-through, and when the market would price it in. The 31 to 34 percent is an upper-bound estimate of imported US revenue, not a share of dutiable exports and not a deduction from profit.

Acquisitions and capital spending

The group has made cash acquisitions over two decades. It acquired Bell, Sons (2007) and Relonchem (2008) in the UK, Time-Cap Laboratories in the US (2015), Access Healthcare in Dubai (2022), the Goa plant from Teva Pharma India (completed April 2023), QliniQ in the Netherlands (June 2026) and ABCnow in Germany (July 2026).[1][8][6][7] The Teva plant price was not published, but about ₹125 crore had been invested in it by March 2024, including the purchase and expansion.[19] QliniQ is reported at 7.5 million (currency not legible in our copy) and ABCnow at €892,384, small against ₹1,058 crore of cash.[6][7][1] Management said larger deals are possible, with nothing agreed.[4]

Capital expenditure (money spent on plants and equipment) was ₹208.8 crore in FY24, ₹172.9 crore in FY25 and ₹130.5 crore in FY26, and commitments not yet provided for were ₹53.9 crore at 31 March 2026.[20][3] Management called the major capex cycle complete in May 2026.[10] The payoff is slow. The second Goa plant earned about ₹50 crore of revenue in FY24 and, in August 2026, management said it was at ₹50-plus crore against a projection of ₹80 crore, about 60 to 65 percent of plan. The call does not say whether those figures are per quarter or per year.[19][4] CARE also lists integration risk.[2]

The weak spots: working capital, receivables and governance

Working capital is the money tied up in inventory and customer bills less supplier credit. Inventory was ₹972.3 crore at 31 March 2026, up 15.0 percent in a year, and trade receivables were ₹644.6 crore, up 19.4 percent against revenue growth of 12.5 percent.[3] The company set aside ₹21.9 crore for expected credit losses in FY26, against ₹8.3 crore in FY25 and ₹5.2 crore in FY24.[3][20] The company measures its working-capital cycle at 138 days for FY26; CARE measures 181 days, up from 163, and attributes the rise to inventory built for new launches.[10][2] In the June 2026 quarter the company's measure fell to about 132 days from 159, and management confirmed that inventory was being unwound, which helps cash now and takes away the cheap stock later.[1][4]

On governance, the picture is clean on the main tests: no pledge, no contingent liabilities at 31 March 2026 or 2025, and an auditor's report with no qualification.[9][3] One item deserves a note: the auditor reported that, for one accounting software, the audit-trail feature that logs changes was not enabled at database level, as explained in note 52(m). The report is not modified for it.[3]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks two questions: does the company break a hard red line, and which archetype does it fit. Three terms help here. A base is a period when a stock moves sideways and builds a floor, a breakout is a rise above the top of that range, and asymmetry is the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss. A stock in Stage 2 is in a rising trend and a stock in Stage 4 is in a falling one. The scoring file defines the red lines. For India they are: "Debt-to-Equity >= 1.20x (non-financial. And "Stage 4 breakdown (Close < falling SMA50 < falling SMA200) with no base formation (Test C not passing)". A drug maker is a mature, non-order-book business, so we apply the stricter 0.40 limb of the cash line, and no relief clause is used. For the pledge line we test the rule that pledge is higher than the quarter before while the close is below a falling 200-day average. An average is falling when it is strictly below its level 63 sessions earlier. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Weakest assumption in the EPS estimate: that margins settle at 21 percent

The central case assumes the three quarters after June earn a 21.0 percent EBITDA margin, which is 4.3 points below the June quarter. Weakest assumption in the cash view: that stock keeps turning into cash

The three-year cash ratio of 0.80 depends on FY26's 1.09.
