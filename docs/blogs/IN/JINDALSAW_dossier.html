1. What the company does

In plain words: Jindal SAW turns steel and iron into pipes for water, oil and gas. Its cash is tied up in stock, customer bills and group credit.

Jindal SAW Limited, the flagship of the P.R. Jindal group, makes pipes. It sells large welded steel pipes (longitudinal and helical), ductile iron pipes, seamless and stainless steel tubes, and iron ore pellets from its own mine. Capacity is about 2.45 million tonnes a year of pipes (0.55 longitudinal, 0.84 helical, 0.71 ductile iron and 0.40 seamless and stainless) and 1.65 million tonnes of pellets, spread over nine sites in India, with a ductile iron plant in Abu Dhabi and coating and finishing units in the United States. [7] [1] Customers are oil and gas companies, state water schemes and the contractors who build them, and export buyers, mostly in the Middle East and Latin America. [2]

The company makes money on the gap between what it charges for a pipe and what the steel plate, coil, pig iron and coking coal cost, less the cost of running mills that are only partly full. Management put oil and gas at about one quarter of revenue. [2] Water pipes, mainly ductile iron, are the other large block, and domestic water demand is tied to the Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM), the central programme that pays for rural tap water. Contractors under that programme have been paid slowly, so the company's pipe orders exist but are delivered late. [8] In the September 2026 quarter the sales mix leaned to low-margin domestic water pipes, which management said were in oversupply. [3] Higher cost pellets, a weak Abu Dhabi plant and a seamless line without its API licence for five months added to the squeeze. Volumes were not the problem: pipe sales were 7.32 lakh tonnes in H1 FY27 against 6.19 lakh a year earlier, so price and mix fell, not tonnes. [2]

Cash is tied up in three places. Inventory was ₹4,539.77 crore and trade receivables ₹2,885.71 crore on 30 September 2026 against trade payables of ₹2,391.19 crore. [1] Much of the raw material is bought from group companies on credit that a bank backs with a usance letter of credit (an LC that pays the supplier later, at a stated date). That credit is booked as trade payables, so it is not in the borrowings line. The ₹1,397.73 crore of such acceptances at March 2026 is counted in our debt readings in Chapter 3. [8] The third is the Middle East: a six lakh tonne job-work order, and the exports behind it, have not shipped since March. [2] [3]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • LSAW and HSAW are longitudinal and helical submerged-arc welded steel pipes: large-diameter pipe made by rolling plate and welding it along a straight or spiral seam.
  • Ductile iron pipe is a strong cast-iron pipe used for water supply; it is made from pig iron and coking coal rather than rolled steel plate.
  • Seamless pipe is pipe made without a weld, used where pressure is high. An API monogram is the American Petroleum Institute licence needed to sell such pipe to many oil and gas buyers.
  • Job work means the customer supplies the steel and the company only makes the pipe, so the order value is the conversion fee and the tonnes look large against the money.
  • Usance letter of credit is a bank promise to pay a supplier at a later date; the supplier's invoice then shows as an acceptance within trade payables. Buyers' credit is a short-term foreign-currency bank loan used to pay for imports; it sits in borrowings.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

In plain words: A rated, scaled business with a falling margin, an investment that depends on a lawsuit, and heavy buying from group companies.

The business has real strengths and one clear problem. The strengths are scale, a dominant domestic position in welded and ductile iron pipe, an order book that covers most of the next year, and a balance sheet with room. The problem is profit: the group earned a 6.9 percent EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) margin in the September 2026 quarter against 11.3 percent a year earlier, on 5.6 percent more revenue. [2] [1] This chapter weighs the two.

Where the strength is

CARE Ratings describes a "dominant position in the domestic steel-pipe manufacturing sector" run by an experienced promoter group, and reaffirmed CARE AA with a stable outlook on long-term facilities and CARE A1+ on short-term facilities on 28 September 2026. [7] Brickwork Ratings also reaffirmed BWR AA with a stable outlook on the ₹500 crore debenture issue in October 2026. [13] No rating action followed the results.

The confirmed order book is about US$1,135 million (about ₹10,700 crore) for pipes and pellets, with about 17.26 lakh tonnes of pipes; letters of intent for a further US$575 million, later US$625 million, are mostly oil and gas buyers outside the Middle East. [2] [3] Management said the chance of those letters not converting is "very, very low", with production from February or March once steel arrives.[3]

Debt is falling. Consolidated net institutional debt (bank debt after cash, excluding acceptances) was ₹2,174 crore at September 2026, ₹2,473 crore at June and ₹2,528 crore at March. [3] [4] The seamless line's API licence, suspended from January 2026 until mid-June, was reinstated in June 2026. [4] [7] And management says Saudi Arabia's new anti-dumping duty on Indian ductile pipe exports (16 to 30 percent) does not apply, because the company sells there through its Abu Dhabi subsidiary. [3]

Where the weakness is

Profit to owners fell 69.03 percent to ₹47.04 crore from ₹151.89 crore. [14] [13] Profit after tax for all holders, ₹35.28 crore, was made of ₹14.42 crore of profit before tax plus a ₹20.86 crore tax credit; ₹26.67 crore of earlier-year tax claims were decided in the company's favour. [1] The EBITDA margin fell for four reasons management named together: Middle East disruption, higher freight and raw material cost, weak domestic water demand, and an Abu Dhabi plant at about half its usual use. [3] Management itself said the weakness was "a combination" and not "a single adverse factor", which means one fix will not restore it.

A second weakness is a legal case that sits inside the accounts. Jindal ITF Limited, a subsidiary, won an arbitral award of ₹1,891.08 crore against NTPC, a single judge set it aside in January 2025, and the appeal bench split on 2 September 2026. A third judge will hear it on 22 January 2027. [2] [1] The parent carries ₹1,708.80 crore of investment in that subsidiary, which is 13.5 percent of its standalone net worth of ₹12,644.09 crore, and the auditors drew attention to it without qualifying their report. [1] The parent's stake in the subsidiary fell from 76.09 percent to 56.42 percent in FY26. [8] If the award is lost, a write-down is possible; management says its counsel sees an "extremely strong case".[1]

Third, the group buys a great deal from related companies. In FY26 the standalone company bought ₹3,380.88 crore of goods and services from related parties, 24.4 percent of its ₹13,874.57 crore of expenses. [8] [1] The group owed related parties ₹1,694.14 crore in trade payables at March 2026 against ₹684.72 crore a year earlier, and the standalone company paid ₹44.23 crore of interest on purchases to one such supplier in FY26. [8] CARE links the rise in acceptances to a change in raw-material buying. [7] These dealings are disclosed in the related-party note, but they tie the company's cash and credit to the group.
