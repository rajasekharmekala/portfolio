1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Operating Cycles

Core Investor Question: Where did this business come from, and how has it behaved through its own bad years before you trust the good ones?

Start with the company before the numbers got big. Stallion’s own website says it began operations in 1992 and set up its first stand-alone debulking and bottling facility at Panvel, near Mumbai, in 1998. A North India site at Manesar, Gurgaon followed in 2002 [13]. The listed company is younger than that history. It was incorporated as Stallion India Fluorochemicals Private Limited in Mumbai on 5 September 2002 [5]. The website says the 1992 business was acquired by the company on a slump-sale basis in 2023 [13]. A slump sale transfers a running business as a going concern, so the operating record came across with the business rather than starting from zero.

What the company sells is refrigerant gas and the services around it. The RHP says the company has been the Honeywell distributor for HFC refrigerant gases since 2006 [5]. The FY25 annual report’s milestone list records the steps after that: HFO-1234yf supplies to Indian auto makers, a partnership with Daikin for HFC-32 sourcing, isobutane supplies from the Khalapur plant, and a new facility at Ghiloth, Rajasthan [4]. The list is not dated in the extract I read, so treat it as a sequence rather than a timetable.

The growth path, in audited numbers

Revenue from operations grew from ₹233.24 crore in FY24 to ₹377.45 crore in FY25, a rise of 61.8%, and then to ₹430.68 crore in FY26, up 14.1% [4][1]. What the rights issue changed

The biggest structural change of the last year is not in the income statement. The board authorised a rights issue on 23 January 2026, which the annual report puts at about ₹363.92 crore gross [2]. Net proceeds in the cash flow statement were ₹336.02 crore [23]. Equity capital and reserves together rose from ₹300.84 crore at March 2025 to ₹680.60 crore at March 2026 [1]. Cash and bank balances stood at about ₹433.67 crore at March 2026, against ₹75.01 crore a year earlier [1]. The company now holds a large treasury beside its operating business, and that changes how you should read returns on capital in chapter 6.

The rights issue also changes the arithmetic of the company’s returns. Equity more than doubled in one year, so a profit that looked high on the old base looks lower on the new one. Be careful with the cash figure as well. Cash and bank balances rose by about ₹359 crore in the year, but about ₹205 crore of that went into fixed deposits, which the cash-flow statement shows as an investing outflow [23]. The money is real. It is also a choice about where to park it, and chapter 6 works through the returns on the larger base.

Measured against the closing equity of March 2026, the same ₹43.84 crore of FY26 profit is a return of about 6.4%. Measured against the equity a year earlier, it would have been about 14.6%. The profit did not shrink. The base grew, and the question for chapter 6 is whether the new money earns enough to lift the return back [1].

Operating cycles, not one-off years

The RHP gives the cleanest long history. Stallion’s EBITDA margin on the RHP table was 17.71% in FY22, fell to 6.90% in FY23, and recovered to 11.30% in FY24. The PAT margin followed the same path, from 11.33% to 4.31% and then 6.26% [7]. Those are the company’s own trough numbers. They carry more weight than any single good year.

The quarterly series shows the same behaviour at a finer grain [14]. The operating profit margin was 16.89% in the June 2024 quarter, fell to 2.86% in the September 2024 quarter, and was back at 16.81% by December 2024. From then until June 2026 it ran between 11.16% and 18.15%. It fell to 2.20% in the September 2026 quarter [14]. Two of the three weakest quarters in the series are September quarters. Earlier management calls noted sharp refrigerant seasonality—roughly 45% to 50% of annual demand falls between February and May, with up to tenfold peak-to-trough monthly swings—which structurally magnifies off-season margin vulnerability.

September 2026 sales were ₹136.03 crore, up 28.87% on a year earlier, yet operating profit (excluding other income) was only ₹2.99 crore [14]. Filings from 13 to 24 November 2026—including the Q2 FY27 results, press release, and 19 November earnings call transcript—show two distinct causes behind the drop. First, Stallion booked a ₹9.51 crore non-cash equity-settled ESOP charge under Ind AS 102 (covering a two-quarter catch-up on 15,86,505 options granted at ₹10 face-value exercise price with one-year vesting, with roughly ₹4.7 crore per quarter continuing in Q3 and Q4 FY27). Second, underlying spreads compressed sharply: raw-material expenses jumped 38.95% year on year to ₹116.79 crore (85.9% of sales) amid 10% to 20% Gulf input inflation, over 20% freight escalation, and intense industry-wide import selling during the final 2024–2026 HFC quota baseline year. Even stripping out the ₹9.51 crore ESOP provision and excluding ₹3.34 crore of other income, core operating profit was ₹12.50 crore (a 9.19% margin)—down roughly 557 basis points from 14.76% a year earlier and 896 basis points from 18.15% in June 2026.

Two terms in the margin table need a plain reading. EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, so it measures the operating business before financing and accounting charges. Profit after tax is what remains for shareholders after all of them. In FY23 both margins fell together, which shows that the operating squeeze reached the bottom line rather than being absorbed by financing or tax [7].

Why one bad year hit so hard

A company with several product lines can absorb a bad year in one of them. Stallion in FY22 could not. Refrigerant gases were 95.89% of product sales that year. The share was 83.63% in FY23 and 81.85% in FY24, and it was 87.34% in the six months to September 2024 [25]. Even at its lowest point in the RHP series, the refrigerant line was more than four-fifths of product sales. With so little in other lines, a squeeze on the refrigerant spread went straight through to the profit line. The company has since commissioned its Khalapur helium unit (September 2026) and is building the Bhilwara R-32 plant. Whether that is enough diversification is the question for chapter 2.

The product share can mislead on its own. The refrigerant share of product sales fell from 95.89% in FY22 to 81.85% in FY24 [25], which looks like diversification. Over the following periods the import cost share rose from 70.23% of revenue in FY23 to 85.00% in the six months to September 2024 [6]. The line got smaller as a share of sales, but the imported input became a larger share of revenue. Adding product lines did not remove exposure to imported raw material.

What the history does not show

The history here is short. The RHP margin table starts in FY22, the quarterly series starts in June 2024, and the years before the 2023 acquisition are a company timeline rather than audited figures. Before the acquisition the business operated under Stallion Enterprises, and the sources I checked do not give its margins for those years. So the FY22 and FY23 figures are the earliest margins I could verify. A longer cycle may exist, but until a filing shows it, treat any claim about one as unverified.

One promise you can check

In the October 2025 analyst call, management said it had promised FY26 turnover of ₹430 crore and profit after tax of ₹40 crore [10]. The audited figures came in at ₹430.68 crore of revenue and ₹43.84 crore of profit after tax [1]. That is a kept promise, and it deserves credit. Chapter 5 takes the later promises one at a time. Most of the plant commissioning dates have now slipped repeatedly into FY28.

R-32: the product that carries the company

R-32 is a refrigerant used in air conditioners. The RHP says the company’s revenue depends on it, in plain terms, and that any fall in demand for it could hurt sales, earnings and cash flow [6]. That is the single biggest concentration in the business. The company’s plan is to make some of it rather than only buy and blend it. The August 2026 call put the planned Bhilwara R-32 plant at 10,000 metric tons, and management described the plant as the first major backward integration [8][2].

The plant has a cash-flow effect that management has repeatedly revised. In August 2026, management said the earlier FY27 contribution of about ₹250 crore would “change a little bit” [9]. By the 19 November 2026 Q2 FY27 earnings call, management confirmed that Bhilwara R-32 trials will conclude by March 2027, phased production starts April 2027, and 100% stabilization is expected by May 2027—reducing FY27 R-32 revenue contribution to zero [36]. For FY28, management now projects 7,000 tonnes of output contributing ₹350 to ₹400 crore (alongside ₹600 crore at full utilization in an illustrative ₹1,200 crore steady-state mix) [36]. Crucially, Bhilwara Phase 1 excludes upstream anhydrous hydrofluoric acid (AHF) manufacturing, leaving Stallion reliant on external AHF purchases without long-term contracts [6][36].

Who the margin belongs to

The product map matters most for margin, and management refined its illustrative mix on the 19 November 2026 call [36]. On a hypothetical ₹1,200 crore turnover, management projected ₹600 crore from Bhilwara R-32 at a 22% PAT margin, ₹200 crore from HFO blends and helium at 15% PAT, and ₹400 crore from the regular refrigerant business at 10% PAT (lifting to 15% only if post-2028 quotas restrict imports) [36]. That is lower than the August 2026 remark that newer lines would run at 24% PAT [9], and all three newer pillars remain unproven in audited accounts while core refrigerant gases still carry the business today [25].

Put the two sources of margin side by side. The core business is still doing the heavy lifting today, and in Q2 FY27 its operating spread compressed sharply even before non-cash ESOP accounting [36]. Whether backward integration lifts consolidated margins depends on Bhilwara starting smoothly and competing profitably against larger integrated domestic producers.

Backward-integrated rivals and the domestic R-32 capacity surge

A crucial blind spot in reading Stallion solely through its own filings is what India’s backward-integrated fluorochemical majors—SRF, Gujarat Fluorochemicals (GFL), and Navin Fluorine (NFIL)—delivered in the exact same Q2 FY27 (July–September 2026) quarter [42][43][44]. While Stallion’s core operating margin (ex-ESOP and ex-other income) fell to 9.19% (2.20% reported) [14][36], Navin Fluorine grew Q2 FY27 revenue 57% year on year to ₹1,191 crore and profit 81% to ₹269 crore at a 34.2% EBITDA margin, commencing commercial R-32 output at Surat (15,000 MTPA HFC capacity) and extending its HFO supply contract with Solstice Advanced Materials (~$590 million) through December 2032 [42]. Gujarat Fluorochemicals grew Q2 FY27 revenue 45.5% to ₹1,760 crore—with fluorochemicals up 135% year on year on R-32—at a 26.9% EBITDA margin [43]. SRF grew Q2 FY27 revenue 35% to ₹4,904 crore, earning ₹527.8 crore of Chemicals EBIT (25.4% margin) while building a 20,000 MTPA fourth-generation refrigerant unit, a 30,000 MTPA AHF plant, and over 65,000 MTPA of HFC capacity in Odisha [44].

The divergence exposes a structural disadvantage. On the 19 November 2026 call, Stallion’s own CEO acknowledged that India’s domestic R-32 demand is roughly 20,000 tonnes per year while coming domestic installed capacity reaches about 80,000 tonnes—four times domestic demand [36]. Integrated incumbents already own upstream AHF capacity, decades of fluorine process engineering, and global export channels [42][43][44]. Stallion enters late, without captive AHF in Bhilwara Phase 1, and holds only a non-exclusive Honeywell distribution relationship while HFO patents approach expiry—meaning larger rivals can compete directly across both R-32 and next-generation HFOs [36].

How the business works, end to end

The chain is simple to describe. The company buys its raw material, much of it imported, then debulks, blends, bottles and sells refrigerant gases to air-conditioning and refrigeration customers. The planned plants move one step further up the chain. Instead of buying and blending R-32 and HFO, the company would make them. Each step up should earn more per kilogram if it works—provided external AHF input costs and domestic R-32 oversupply do not compress the manufacturing spread. That is the thesis behind the capital spending examined in chapter 3.

What the filings do not split

No filed number gives the revenue of the helium project, of R-32, or of the distribution business on its own. The audited revenue line is one figure, and the RHP gives broad percentages. Even after Khalapur helium started on 21 September 2026, Q2 FY27 filings still report a single reportable segment (manufacture of industrial gases) with no product-level margin split [36][37]. Use management’s plant-level projections strictly as targets to verify against future filings.

Helium: the second product family, and a different supply chain

The Khalapur facility is the company’s helium project, with an installed capacity of 1,200 metric tons a year. Commercial operations commenced on 21 September 2026 (recorded as completed 29 September 2026 in CARE Ratings’ monitoring report), moving the project from construction into early ramp-up [36][37]. Liquid helium is sourced under a partnership with Sharjah Oxygen Company in Dubai, which sources from RAS Gases & Oilfields in Qatar [1]. On the August 2026 call, management forecast a two-to-three-year helium shortfall [8], guided to roughly 20% utilization of the 1,200 metric tons in year one rather than 50%, and noted helium rates near ₹4,000 a kilogram [9]. Capacity is not output, and the November 2026 disclosures provided no initial revenue or volume run-rate [36].

Helium is a different kind of business from refrigerant gas. In October 2025 management explained that helium is not manufactured by anyone. It is recovered as a by-product, then refined and liquefied, and the company’s role is in processing and handling [11]. That makes the helium project a logistics and purification business whose inputs depend on a few producing countries, which is the point the next chapter tests.

The helium supply link runs through a partnership with Sharjah Oxygen Company in Dubai, which sources liquid helium from RAS Gases & Oilfields in Qatar. The annual report calls it a long-term strategic partnership [1]. The contract terms are not in the sources, so I cannot say how long it runs or what share of supply it covers. That is the first thing to ask about any helium revenue number.

Customers and the cost of concentration

Ten customers accounted for 77.32% of revenue in the six months to September 2024, against 72.88% in FY22 [6]. The top two customers alone were 35.34% of revenue in FY24 [6]. Concentration of that size is not unusual in industrial supply, but it changes what a lost order means. A single large customer taking volume elsewhere moves the whole revenue line, not a slice of it.

The top-ten share has risen in every period the RHP reports, from 72.88% in FY22 to 74.77% in FY23, 75.69% in FY24 and 77.32% in the six months to September 2024 [6]. That can be normal for an industrial supplier, but it means the business is more exposed to its largest buyers each year. That is the direction to watch.

The arithmetic of the top two is blunt. In the six months to September 2024 the top two customers were 30.66% of revenue and the top ten 77.32% [6]. That leaves about 23% of revenue for every other buyer combined. A decision by one of the top two to cut volume or move a contract would reach about a third of the company’s sales in one move. The sources I checked do not give the length of those relationships.

Put the chapter in one sentence. The company sells mainly refrigerant gas, buys most of its input from abroad, depends on a small group of buyers, has just commissioned a helium unit, and is building a delayed, non-AHF-integrated R-32 plant into a domestic market where integrated giants are expanding capacity to four times local demand.

Core Investor Question: When the profit is reported, how much of it arrives as cash, and what has to stay true in the supply chain for that to continue?

Profit is an opinion until the cash arrives. For a refrigerant business the cash story runs through three places: the raw material bought from abroad, the inventory held to meet orders, and the credit given to customers. This chapter follows each one through the FY26 audited figures and the RHP’s disclosures, and it tests two bear points against the filings.

Where the raw material comes from

The raw-material side is the largest and least secure part of the chain. The RHP says import costs were 85.00% of revenue from operations in the six months to September 2024, up from 70.23% in FY23 [6]. China supplied 92.97% of those imports in the same period [6]. 4. The Regulatory Clock, Substitutes & Helium Supply

Core Investor Question: Which rules and supply chains decide how long this company's main products stay the main products?

Refrigerant gas is regulated before it is sold. The rules that shape this company’s product mix are not set in a boardroom. They sit in an international treaty, and the treaty decides how fast the company’s main molecules must shrink. This chapter reads the treaty as a supply rule for this business, then tests the helium supply chain, which has its own limits.

The Montreal Protocol and the Kigali Amendment

The Montreal Protocol is the treaty that phased out the ozone-depleting gases. The Kigali Amendment extended the framework to hydrofluorocarbons, the HFCs that replaced the older gases. The UNEP summary puts it plainly: “Adopted in 2016, the Amendment commits countries to reducing HFC production and consumption with differentiated timelines for developed and developing nations.” [17] Developed countries began their reductions in 2019 [17]. HFCs do not deplete the ozone layer, but they are potent greenhouse gases, and that is why they now sit under a timetable.

Where India sits in the schedule

India is in Group 2 of the HFC annex, with Bahrain, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Oman, Pakistan, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates [16]. For Group 2 the base level is the average HFC consumption for 2024 to 2026 plus 65% of the HCFC baseline. The freeze takes effect on 1 January 2028 [16]. A freeze caps consumption at the base level, so the national ceiling is set by the years that are happening now.

The treaty summary does not say how India will share its national ceiling among companies, and no formal allocation rule has been notified yet (expected in 2027). On the 19 November 2026 earnings call, management confirmed that the 2024–2026 baseline window is already distorting the market: importers are aggressively maximizing volumes before the baseline closes to lock in 20-year entitlements, creating severe selling pressure that hit Q2 FY27 spreads [36]. Management asserted that Stallion expects to receive “more than 50% of the country quota” and lift regular-business PAT margins from 10% to 15% after 2028—an unverified forward expectation until the government publishes company-level allocations [36].

R-32 is an HFC. The FY25 annual report records a partnership with Daikin for HFC-32 sourcing [4]. So the company’s largest product, and the planned Bhilwara R-32 plant, both sit inside the freeze rather than outside it [2][9].

Trade remedies: DGTR’s five-year anti-dumping renewal on Chinese R-32

A second regulatory layer omitted from the initial baseline analysis is India’s trade-remedy shield on Chinese R-32 [41]. India originally imposed anti-dumping duty (ADD) on Chinese HFC Component R-32 in December 2021 (Notification No. 75/2021-Customs (ADD)) [41]. On 18 September 2026, following a sunset review initiated on an application by SRF Limited and supported by Gujarat Fluorochemicals (10,000 MTPA R-32) and Navin Fluorine (9,000 MTPA at review [41]; 15,000 MTPA commercialized in November 2026 [42]), India’s Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR) issued Final Findings in Case No. AD (SSR)-11/2025 recommending continuation of anti-dumping duty on Chinese R-32 imports for another five years (subject to Ministry of Finance customs notification) [41].

This ruling cuts asymmetrically across Stallion’s transition. While Bhilwara remains uncommissioned through Q1 FY28, Stallion is an importer reliant on China for over 90% of imported inputs—meaning continued ADD elevates landed costs while domestic producers SRF, GFL, and Navin Fluorine enjoy protected realizations [6][41]. Conversely, once Bhilwara begins producing in April–May 2027, prolonged ADD could support domestic R-32 pricing, though Stallion must still compete against roughly 80,000 tonnes of coming Indian R-32 capacity (against ~20,000 tonnes of domestic demand) without captive AHF backward integration [36][41].

The substitutes: HFOs and blends

The company is hedging the rule with lower-warming molecules. It supplies HFO-1234yf to Indian auto makers [4], and the redesigned Mambattu facility will blend HFO with R-32 once completed in January 2027 / Q1 FY28 [36][37]. However, on the 19 November 2026 call management confirmed that Bhilwara currently has no HFO manufacturing capability, Environmental Clearance for the proposed ₹200 crore Hurda HFO plant is still pending, funding remains undecided, and construction will not begin until three to five months after Bhilwara R-32 stabilizes—pushing any HFO build into late 2027 at the earliest [36].

That stretches the timeline directly against the 1 January 2028 HFC freeze. Management estimated India’s current HFO demand at 500 to 600 tonnes, rising above 2,500 tonnes after 2028 [36]. Yet backward-integrated rivals are already executing commercial HFO lines: Navin Fluorine extended its Solstice Advanced Materials HFO contract (~$590 million) through December 2032 [42], and SRF is building a 20,000 MTPA fourth-generation refrigerant unit in Odisha [44]. Because Stallion’s Honeywell distribution relationship is non-exclusive and HFO patents are approaching expiry, Stallion does not hold an exclusive moat over India’s HFO transition [36].

On the August call, management said the company’s R-32 imports were over 4,000 tons before it began making R-32 itself, and that its own requirement would be roughly 40 to 50% of production [9]. That is a spoken figure, and I did not find it in a filing. It matters because it shows how much of the company’s refrigerant volume depends on what it can make itself, which is the point of the Bhilwara plant.

What to watch in the next filings

Four things will tell you more than any call. First, the Ministry of Finance customs notification following DGTR’s 18 September 2026 five-year R-32 ADD recommendation, because it directly shapes import spreads until Bhilwara stabilizes [41]. Second, the national HFC quota allocation when notified in 2027, to test management’s “over 50% country quota” claim [36]. Third, Environmental Clearance and funding terms for the Bhilwara HFO plant [36]. Fourth, quarterly Khalapur helium volumes, now that the 1,200 MTPA facility commenced operations on 21 September 2026 [36][37].

Keep the dates and the numbers in one place as you read. A regulatory date tells you when a product line must change. A capex line tells you whether the company can change in time. A volume line tells you whether the new plant is earning. The three together are the test of whether the substitute story is a plan or only a slide.

The helium project is the company’s other new line, and its supply rests on partners rather than on owned resources.
