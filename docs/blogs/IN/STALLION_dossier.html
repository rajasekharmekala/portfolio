1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Operating Cycles

Core Investor Question: Where did this business come from, and how has it behaved through its own bad years before you trust the good ones?

Start with the company before the numbers got big. Stallion’s own website says it began operations in 1992 and set up its first stand-alone debulking and bottling facility at Panvel, near Mumbai, in 1998. A North India site at Manesar, Gurgaon followed in 2002 [13]. The listed company is younger than that history. It was incorporated as Stallion India Fluorochemicals Private Limited in Mumbai on 5 September 2002 [5]. The website says the 1992 business was acquired by the company on a slump-sale basis in 2023 [13]. A slump sale transfers a running business as a going concern, so the operating record came across with the business rather than starting from zero.

What the company sells is refrigerant gas and the services around it. The RHP says the company has been the Honeywell distributor for HFC refrigerant gases since 2006 [5]. The FY25 annual report’s milestone list records the steps after that: HFO-1234yf supplies to Indian auto makers, a partnership with Daikin for HFC-32 sourcing, isobutane supplies from the Khalapur plant, and a new facility at Ghiloth, Rajasthan [4]. The list is not dated in the extract I read, so treat it as a sequence rather than a timetable.

The growth path, in audited numbers

Revenue from operations grew from ₹233.24 crore in FY24 to ₹377.45 crore in FY25, a rise of 61.8%, and then to ₹430.68 crore in FY26, up 14.1% [4][1]. Profit moved faster than revenue over the same two years. The table puts the two side by side. What the rights issue changed

The biggest structural change of the last year is not in the income statement. The board authorised a rights issue on 23 January 2026, which the annual report puts at about ₹363.92 crore gross [2]. Net proceeds in the cash flow statement were ₹336.02 crore [23]. Equity capital and reserves together rose from ₹300.84 crore at March 2025 to ₹680.60 crore at March 2026 [1]. Cash and bank balances stood at about ₹433.67 crore at March 2026, against ₹75.01 crore a year earlier [1]. The company now holds a large treasury beside its operating business, and that changes how you should read returns on capital in chapter 6.

The rights issue also changes the arithmetic of the company’s returns. Equity more than doubled in one year, so a profit that looked high on the old base looks lower on the new one. Be careful with the cash figure as well. Cash and bank balances rose by about ₹359 crore in the year, but about ₹205 crore of that went into fixed deposits, which the cash-flow statement shows as an investing outflow [23]. The money is real. It is also a choice about where to park it, and chapter 6 works through the returns on the larger base.

Measured against the closing equity of March 2026, the same ₹43.84 crore of FY26 profit is a return of about 6.4%. Measured against the equity a year earlier, it would have been about 14.6%. The profit did not shrink. The base grew, and the question for chapter 6 is whether the new money earns enough to lift the return back [1].

Operating cycles, not one-off years

The RHP gives the cleanest long history. Stallion’s EBITDA margin on the RHP table was 17.71% in FY22, fell to 6.90% in FY23, and recovered to 11.30% in FY24. The PAT margin followed the same path, from 11.33% to 4.31% and then 6.26% [7]. Those are the company’s own trough numbers. They carry more weight than any single good year.

The quarterly series shows the same behaviour at a finer grain [14]. The operating profit margin was 16.89% in the June 2024 quarter, fell to 2.86% in the September 2024 quarter, and was back at 16.81% by December 2024. From then until June 2026 it ran between 11.16% and 18.15%. It fell to 2.20% in the September 2026 quarter [14]. Two of the three weakest quarters in the series are September quarters. The sources I checked do not say whether that is seasonal or something else, so I leave it open.

September 2026 sales were ₹136.03 crore, up 28.87% on a year earlier, yet operating profit was only ₹2.99 crore [14]. Revenue growth and profit can split apart in this business for a simple reason. A refrigerant gas earns a spread over the cost of the imported raw material, and when that spread narrows, extra volume does not rescue the margin. Chapter 3 shows how much of the cost base sits inside that spread.

Two terms in the margin table need a plain reading. EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, so it measures the operating business before financing and accounting charges. Profit after tax is what remains for shareholders after all of them. In FY23 both margins fell together, which shows that the operating squeeze reached the bottom line rather than being absorbed by financing or tax [7].

Why one bad year hit so hard

A company with several product lines can absorb a bad year in one of them. Stallion in FY22 could not. Refrigerant gases were 95.89% of product sales that year. The share was 83.63% in FY23 and 81.85% in FY24, and it was 87.34% in the six months to September 2024 [25]. Even at its lowest point in the RHP series, the refrigerant line was more than four-fifths of product sales. With so little in other lines, a squeeze on the refrigerant spread went straight through to the profit line. The company has since added helium, specialty gases and the planned R-32 plant. Whether that is enough diversification is the question for chapter 2.

The product share can mislead on its own. The refrigerant share of product sales fell from 95.89% in FY22 to 81.85% in FY24 [25], which looks like diversification. Over the following periods the import cost share rose from 70.23% of revenue in FY23 to 85.00% in the six months to September 2024 [6]. The line got smaller as a share of sales, but the imported input became a larger share of revenue. Adding product lines did not remove exposure to imported raw material.

What the history does not show

The history here is short. The RHP margin table starts in FY22, the quarterly series starts in June 2024, and the years before the 2023 acquisition are a company timeline rather than audited figures. Before the acquisition the business operated under Stallion Enterprises, and the sources I checked do not give its margins for those years. So the FY22 and FY23 figures are the earliest margins I could verify. A longer cycle may exist, but until a filing shows it, treat any claim about one as unverified.

One promise you can check

In the October 2025 analyst call, management said it had promised FY26 turnover of ₹430 crore and profit after tax of ₹40 crore [10]. The audited figures came in at ₹430.68 crore of revenue and ₹43.84 crore of profit after tax [1]. That is a kept promise, and it deserves credit. Chapter 5 takes the later promises one at a time. Most of them have dates that have not yet arrived, so the test is still open.

NARRATOR’S REFLECTION

Revenue up 62% in one year and profit above the promised ₹40 crore in FY26 look like the whole story. The FY23 trough and the two September dips are the other half. Write both down. A history that includes a halved margin tells you what a bad year looks like in this business, and that is more useful than the best year.

Notice the other thing the history shows. The operating business sits inside a company that is younger than its own brand, and its treasury now holds hundreds of crores in cash. That is a different balance sheet from the one that produced the 2023 trough, so the old base rate is a guide rather than a law.

Who the margin belongs to

The product map matters most for margin, and management gave its own split on the August call. About half the business is still core business, which management described as lower margin. The other half is the newer business, which it said runs at about a 24% PAT margin [9]. An analyst put the conventional business at about 10% PAT, and management answered yes [9]. The company’s case is that the newer half will lift the average as it grows. That is a forecast for plants that are not yet running, and the filings will show whether the mix moves when the plants do.

Put the two sources of margin side by side. The core business is about half the total and runs at a lower margin, while the newer half is management’s claim of about 24%. If the newer half is about half the business today, the average margin depends on whether that half grows and on whether the plants arrive. Refrigerant gas was 81.85% of product sales in FY24 [25], so the core line is still doing most of the work today.

How the business works, end to end

The chain is simple to describe. The company buys its raw material, much of it imported, then debulks, blends, bottles and sells refrigerant gases to air-conditioning and refrigeration customers. The planned plants move one step further up the chain. Instead of buying and blending R-32 and HFO, the company would make them. Each step up should earn more per kilogram if it works. That is the thesis behind the capital spending examined in chapter 3.

What the filings do not split

No filed number gives the revenue of the helium project, of R-32, or of the distribution business on its own. The audited revenue line is one figure, and the RHP gives broad percentages. For the new lines, the only numbers are management’s, and they describe plants that are not yet running. Use them as targets, and check each one against the next quarterly result.

Helium: the second product family, and a different supply chain

The Khalapur facility is the company’s helium project. Management described an installed capacity of 1,200 metric tons a year and said it expected commercial operations to begin in the coming quarter, as of August 2026 [9]. Liquid helium is sourced under a partnership with Sharjah Oxygen Company in Dubai, which sources from RAS Gases & Oilfields in Qatar [1]. On the same call, asked about the outlook, management said: “The next two three years Helium outlook is shortfall and higher …” [8]. That is management’s own forecast, and chapter 4 sets it against independent data. On the same call management said it expected the plant to be used at about 20% of its 1,200 metric tons next year, not 50%, and that the ramp would be gradual [9]. It agreed with an analyst that the helium rate had risen sharply and was now roughly stable near ₹4,000 a kilogram [9]. Capacity is not output, and the sources give no date for full use.

Helium is a different kind of business from refrigerant gas. In October 2025 management explained that helium is not manufactured by anyone. It is recovered as a by-product, then refined and liquefied, and the company’s role is in processing and handling [11]. That makes the helium project a logistics and purification business whose inputs depend on a few producing countries, which is the point the next chapter tests.

The helium supply link runs through a partnership with Sharjah Oxygen Company in Dubai, which sources liquid helium from RAS Gases & Oilfields in Qatar. The annual report calls it a long-term strategic partnership [1]. The contract terms are not in the sources, so I cannot say how long it runs or what share of supply it covers. That is the first thing to ask about any helium revenue number.

Customers and the cost of concentration

Ten customers accounted for 77.32% of revenue in the six months to September 2024, against 72.88% in FY22 [6]. The top two customers alone were 35.34% of revenue in FY24 [6]. Concentration of that size is not unusual in industrial supply, but it changes what a lost order means. A single large customer taking volume elsewhere moves the whole revenue line, not a slice of it.

The top-ten share has risen in every period the RHP reports, from 72.88% in FY22 to 74.77% in FY23, 75.69% in FY24 and 77.32% in the six months to September 2024 [6]. That can be normal for an industrial supplier, but it means the business is more exposed to its largest buyers each year. That is the direction to watch.

The arithmetic of the top two is blunt. In the six months to September 2024 the top two customers were 30.66% of revenue and the top ten 77.32% [6]. That leaves about 23% of revenue for every other buyer combined. A decision by one of the top two to cut volume or move a contract would reach about a third of the company’s sales in one move. The sources I checked do not give the length of those relationships.

Put the chapter in one sentence. The company sells mainly refrigerant gas, buys most of its input from abroad, depends on a small group of buyers, and is building plants to make its main product rather than only buy it. Each feature is in the filings, and each one means the margin history matters more than the revenue growth.

NARRATOR’S REFLECTION

Ask yourself which part of this business you would own if the company disappeared tomorrow. The answer is R-32 distribution and blending, a helium project still being commissioned, and a customer list that is heavily concentrated. Each of those is a real asset, and each is a different risk. The mix matters more than the total, so keep the product map in view as you read the margin chapters.

A caution about the sources. Management describes the business in broad strokes, and the RHP gives the cleanest percentages. Where the two disagree, I have used the filed document and labelled the management remark as a remark.

3. Cash Conversion, Working Capital & the Raw-Material Chain

Core Investor Question: When the profit is reported, how much of it arrives as cash, and what has to stay true in the supply chain for that to continue?

Profit is an opinion until the cash arrives. For a refrigerant business the cash story runs through three places: the raw material bought from abroad, the inventory held to meet orders, and the credit given to customers. This chapter follows each one through the FY26 audited figures and the RHP’s disclosures, and it tests two bear points against the filings.

Where the raw material comes from

The raw-material side is the largest and least secure part of the chain. The RHP says import costs were 85.00% of revenue from operations in the six months to September 2024, up from 70.23% in FY23 [6]. China supplied 92.97% of those imports in the same period [6]. The RHP is direct about what follows: “We do not have long term supply contracts with our raw material suppliers, and therefore, we are subject to the risk of increases in the costs of raw materials.” [6]

On the August 2026 call, an analyst asked about sourcing, and management’s answer shows why the contract gap matters. Management said: “Now to have anything to have value has to be iron-clad. And iron-clad will mean time-bound supply after which the penalties start enhancing. So it’s not in the best interest of the company.” [9] It also expected a glut of AHF supply by 2028, which it called a buyer’s market [9]. Both are management’s views. The sources contain no supply contract behind either one.

The inventory credit is the line to watch. The ₹23.96 crore credit to cost came from holding more finished inventory at the end of the year than at the start [1]. The cost sits on the balance sheet until that inventory is sold, so the margin benefits in the year the inventory is built and the cost lands later. On the August call, an analyst asked how much of the quarter’s EBITDA came from inventory gains. Management replied: “It’s a mix, 50:50.” [9] That is an answer about one quarter, not the full year, and no filing gives the split.

Put a number on the raw-material exposure. The FY26 waterfall has materials at about ₹85.70 of every ₹100 of revenue, and profit after tax at about ₹10.18 [1]. So a rise of 1% in material cost alone, before any pass-through to customers, takes about ₹0.86 of profit out of every ₹100 of revenue. That is roughly 8% of the year’s profit from a single percentage point. With no long-term contract, the company carries that sensitivity itself.

Working capital: customers paid faster, inventory stayed heavy

Trade receivables fell from ₹105.54 crore at March 2025 to ₹77.53 crore at March 2026 [1]. Inventory at March 2026 was ₹126.63 crore, and the audited change in inventories shows it rose by about ₹24 crore over the year [1]. The ratio series shows the effect in days. Debtor days fell from 102 to 66, while inventory days edged up from 128 to 134 [24]. Days payable fell from 27 to 19, so the company now pays its suppliers faster than it did a year earlier [24].

Put the days into money, roughly. FY26 revenue of ₹430.68 crore works out to about ₹1.18 crore of sales a day. The 36-day fall in debtor days is therefore worth roughly ₹42 crore of cash, released once. That is a real gain, but it does not repeat every year, so the next gain has to come from sales or margin [1][24].

4. The Regulatory Clock, Substitutes & Helium Supply

Core Investor Question: Which rules and supply chains decide how long this company's main products stay the main products?

Refrigerant gas is regulated before it is sold. The rules that shape this company’s product mix are not set in a boardroom. They sit in an international treaty, and the treaty decides how fast the company’s main molecules must shrink. This chapter reads the treaty as a supply rule for this business, then tests the helium supply chain, which has its own limits.

The Montreal Protocol and the Kigali Amendment

The Montreal Protocol is the treaty that phased out the ozone-depleting gases. The Kigali Amendment extended the framework to hydrofluorocarbons, the HFCs that replaced the older gases. The UNEP summary puts it plainly: “Adopted in 2016, the Amendment commits countries to reducing HFC production and consumption with differentiated timelines for developed and developing nations.” [17] Developed countries began their reductions in 2019 [17]. HFCs do not deplete the ozone layer, but they are potent greenhouse gases, and that is why they now sit under a timetable.

Where India sits in the schedule

India is in Group 2 of the HFC annex, with Bahrain, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Oman, Pakistan, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates [16]. For Group 2 the base level is the average HFC consumption for 2024 to 2026 plus 65% of the HCFC baseline. The freeze takes effect on 1 January 2028 [16]. A freeze caps consumption at the base level, so the national ceiling is set by the years that are happening now.

The treaty summary does not say how India will share its national ceiling among companies, and the sources I checked do not say either. That allocation is the part of the rule that would matter most to this business, and it is the one thing I could not verify. Ask for it before you assume anything about the company’s refrigerant volume after 2028.

R-32 is an HFC. The FY25 annual report records a partnership with Daikin for HFC-32 sourcing [4]. So the company’s largest product, and the planned Bhilwara R-32 plant, both sit inside the freeze rather than outside it [2][9].

The substitutes: HFOs and blends

The company is hedging the rule with lower-warming molecules. It supplies HFO-1234yf to Indian auto makers [4]. Its HFO plant at Bhilwara is planned under a memorandum of understanding with the state, at about ₹200 crore with construction from 2027 [2]. The Mambattu plant blends HFO with the company’s own R-32, and management says it will replace HFO blends the company now imports [9].

The hedge is real, but it is a different business. HFO products have their own chemistry, customers and input chains, and the sources do not give margins for them separately. Management says the HFO plant will raise revenue, EBITDA and profit significantly once it runs [9]. Test that against the filings when they arrive, not against the call.

On the August call, management said the company’s R-32 imports were over 4,000 tons before it began making R-32 itself, and that its own requirement would be roughly 40 to 50% of production [9]. That is a spoken figure, and I did not find it in a filing. It matters because it shows how much of the company’s refrigerant volume depends on what it can make itself, which is the point of the Bhilwara plant.

What to watch in the next filings

Three things will tell you more than any call. First, the national allocation of the India ceiling, once it is public, because it decides how much refrigerant volume the company can sell after 2028. Second, the HFO plant’s capex and construction start in the annual report, because the two figures in the sources need to converge. Third, the helium volume in the quarterly results, because management’s 20% utilisation view is testable one quarter at a time.

Keep the dates and the numbers in one place as you read. A regulatory date tells you when a product line must change. A capex line tells you whether the company can change in time. A volume line tells you whether the new plant is earning. The three together are the test of whether the substitute story is a plan or only a slide.

The case for owning it

The strongest bull argument is that management has delivered on its last set of numbers. FY26 revenue was ₹430.7 crore and profit after tax ₹43.8 crore, and operating cash flow of ₹39.4 crore met the ₹40 crore level the company had pointed to [1][3].
