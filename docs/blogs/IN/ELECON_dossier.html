1. What the company does

Elecon Engineering Company Limited makes industrial gearboxes and the machines that move bulk material such as coal, ore and cement. It was founded in 1951 to make elevators and conveyors, became a public company in 1962 and set up its gear division in 1976.[14] Its main plant at Vallabh Vidyanagar in Gujarat covers about 335,000 square meters.[4] It also runs assembly and repair centres in the UK, Sweden, the USA and the Netherlands.[4][12]

The buyers are plants that run heavy machinery for years: steel mills, power stations, cement works, sugar mills and ports. Power, steel and cement are the three largest industries for the gear division.[3] A gearbox sits between a motor and a machine and changes speed and force. It is a small part of a large project, but the plant stops if it fails, so customers buy on reliability and delivery time as much as price.

About 53 percent of gear sales are catalogue products, made to a standard design and sold from a price list. The other 47 percent are engineered products made to a customer's design.[3] The rating agency ICRA says gear orders typically run two to six months from order to delivery, and that orders are fixed-price, so a rise in the price of steel hits profit before it can be passed on.[14] For catalogue products management says it resets the price list monthly instead of locking prices back to back, and that this business is about half of company revenue.[3]

The second division builds bulk material handling machines: stackers, reclaimers, wagon tippers, feeders and conveyors for steel, power, cement, fertiliser and port customers.[4][3] ICRA notes that the company exited engineering-procurement-construction (EPC) contracts, in which it once built whole systems, and now sells equipment and service, which ICRA says reduced the money tied up in working capital — cash tied up in inventory and customer bills minus supplier credit —, the cash locked in stock and unpaid customer bills.[14] The division earned ₹151 crore of revenue in the September 2026 quarter against ₹488 crore for gears.[4]

About 27 percent of first-half revenue came from overseas, up from 23 percent a year earlier, through exports and four overseas plants in Sweden, the Netherlands, the UK and the USA.[4] The company has no borrowings. At 30 September 2026 it held ₹125 crore of cash, ₹172 crore of other bank balances and ₹687 crore of current investments, which is ₹984 crore on our addition, against ₹361 crore of lease liabilities.[4] Management describes net cash of about ₹700 crore.[3]

How one order becomes profit

A customer places an order, the company buys steel and components, machines and assembles the gearbox, tests it and ships it. Profit is the gap between the agreed price and the cost of steel, labour and machine time. Management says it has raised prices and improved the product mix: the September 2026 quarter gross margin, which is sales less the cost of materials as a share of sales, was 46.1 percent against 43.1 percent a year earlier.[4][3]

Two consequences follow. First, a gearbox order converts to revenue quickly, so the order book is a short-range gauge and not a multi-year backlog; only about ₹160 crore of the gear book runs beyond the current year.[6] Second, fixed machine cost is spread over volume, so profit rises faster than sales when the plants are busy and falls faster when they are not. Management puts plant use near 60 percent.[3] That is why chapter 2B scores order cover and margin together.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ROCE is return on capital employed: operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business.   • Cash flow from operations is the cash a business earns after tax and before spending on plant; free cash flow (FCF) is that cash less capital expenditure, the spending on plant and equipment.   • Guidance is what management says it expects to deliver. The consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who cover a stock.
  • RSI is the relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has moved. ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price swing, which we use to measure chart distances. The 20-day, 50-day and 200-day moving averages are the average closing price over the last 20, 50 and 200 sessions.
  • Stage 2 is a rising trend above rising averages, and Stage 4 is a falling trend below falling averages. 2. Why the business is strong or weak

The gear franchise is strong where it is hard to copy and weaker where it depends on public spending. ICRA puts Elecon's share of the Indian industrial gearbox market at 38 to 40 percent, and the company says it has one of the widest product ranges in India and is the only company in India able to build complex gearboxes for the Indian Navy.[14][4] The weaker part is that a large share of orders comes through public-sector buyers, whose capital budgets move with government funding.[6]

Competitive position: what is proven and what is claimed

Proven by third parties: a 38 to 40 percent domestic share, an ICRA rating of AA with a positive outlook, and an interest cover of 21 times for FY26.[14][15] Proven by filings: no borrowings, ₹984 crore of liquid assets and cash conversion near 1.0 over three years, which chapter 3 tests.[12][4] Claimed by management: a moat in marine, high-speed and coal-mill gearboxes, and a plan to reach ₹5,000 crore of revenue by FY30 with half from overseas.[3] The claims are plausible for a 75-year-old company with an in-house foundry, but they are statements about the future, and chapter 2B shows management has missed its own revenue targets.

In material handling the picture is mixed. TRF, a former rival, has closed its material handling operations, according to management, and the competitors named on the call are L&T, TKIL, TAKRAF and AUMUND.[3] The division won ₹253 crore of orders in the September quarter, up 32.5 percent, but its margin was 20.2 percent against 25.7 percent a year earlier because of project mix and delayed customer clearances.[4] What this means for an owner: the gear franchise supports the earnings, and material handling adds growth but also execution risk.

Demand: steel, cement and power

Management says industry research puts growth in the Indian heavy-duty gearbox market at about 8 percent a year through FY30.[3] The ministry of steel puts national steel capacity at about 220 million tonnes in FY26 and expects 300 million tonnes by 2030, and finished steel consumption rose 8.3 percent in April to June 2026 while crude steel output rose 3.0 percent.[18][19] Management also cites an 'anticipated threefold expansion' in steel capacity, which we could not reconcile with the ministry's 36 percent rise, so we use the ministry figure.[3][18] ICRA projects cement volumes up 6 to 7 percent in FY27, with 85 to 90 million tonnes of new capacity in FY26 and FY27.[20] For power, which is the largest gear industry, we found no free external capacity source and rely on management and ICRA.[3][14] What this means for an owner: end demand is growing at mid to high single digits, which supports gear sales near 10 percent a year and not 20 percent, and that matches our central case.

Pricing power and steel cost

Pricing power showed in the September 2026 quarter: gear EBIT margin rose 290 basis points to 22.1 percent, helped by a price rise, a better mix and a bunching of export shipments into the quarter.[4][3] A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. Management says export orders planned for the June quarter shipped in September, which explains part of the spike. Steel remains the swing factor: management says domestic project work slowed because of steel price volatility and a weak monsoon, and ICRA expects higher steel prices to keep margins under pressure in the near term.[3][14]

Catalogue products, about half of revenue, are priced from a monthly list, and management says back-to-back pricing, where a selling price is matched by a locked purchase price, does not happen in that business.[3] Because most gear orders deliver within two to six months, the lag between a steel price move and the selling price is short, but it is not zero.[14] What this means for an owner: the September margin is partly a timing gain, so we do not extend it into every quarter.

Where the strength is thin

Domestic activity has been softer than overseas. Management calls domestic demand stable but describes near-term softness, and attributes it to steel volatility, a slow monsoon and clearance delays; it says orders are firm and some finished goods are waiting for delivery.[3] Gear order intake in the September quarter was ₹493 crore against ₹497 crore a year earlier, so the 37 percent rise in the gear order book reflects slow conversion of orders into sales as well as new demand.[4] Plant use near 60 percent means capacity is available, but management says some machines are bottlenecks that it is debottlenecking.[3]

Two accounting items deserve plain mention. In FY26 the company wrote off ₹101.77 crore of goodwill as a non-cash impairment, and it re-marked its stake in Eimco Elecon, which produced a gain in June 2025 and a fair-value loss through other comprehensive income.[12] Neither is cash, but they explain why FY26 reported profit does not match operating profit, and chapter 3 shows both readings. What this means for an owner: the business is strong enough to be worth the work of watching, and its weak points are execution and timing, which chapter 2B scores.
