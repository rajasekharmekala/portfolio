HCL Technologies (NSE: HCLTECH) is an Indian company that builds, runs and repairs computer systems for large clients in the United States and Europe.[1][15] Our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: do not buy at the 27 October close of ₹1,420.00, and consider buying only if the price falls to ₹1,230 to ₹1,270 and a fresh chart check passes, with an exit at ₹1,133.36.[36] The business is strong, with ₹24,503 crore of net cash.[1] But the shares rose 16% in two weeks, so the reward is only 0.50 for each 1 of risk today and 2.14 at ₹1,270.[3][36] The idea is wrong if growth stalls below the 2.5% to 4% guided for FY27, if the EBIT margin falls below 17.5%, or if the price falls below ₹1,133.36[1][3].

1. What the company does

HCL Technologies Limited, called HCLTech here, sells technology work to big organisations. A bank, a car maker or a hospital group hires it to build software, run data centres and networks, design products and keep its systems secure. In the year to March 2026 it earned revenue of ₹130,144 crore and, in the quarter to September 2026, ₹35,620 crore.[8][1] It employed 230,339 people at the end of September 2026 and works in more than 60 countries.[1] Its shares trade on the National Stock Exchange under the symbol HCLTECH, and the promoter group, led by the Nadar family, owns 60.88% of them.[17]

The company describes three businesses. IT and Business Services is the largest, at 75.6% of revenue in the September quarter. It covers application work, cloud, cybersecurity, digital workplace and network services, much of it under multi-year contracts.[1] Engineering and R&D Services, 16.8% of revenue, does engineering and product-design work for manufacturers, chip makers and carmakers.[1][3] HCLSoftware, 7.9% of revenue, sells its own products, such as the BigFix device-management tool and the HCL Commerce shopping platform, as licences and subscriptions.[1] The first two together are called Services and made 92.4% of revenue.[1]

Customers are spread across industries and countries. Financial services is 22.4% of Services revenue, manufacturing 18.3%, technology 14.3%, life sciences and healthcare 13.8%, telecom and media 12.0%, retail and consumer goods 10.5% and public services 8.8%.[1] The United States supplies 56.1%, Europe 26.9%, the rest of the world 13.6% and India 3.4%.[1] No single client dominates: the five largest were 11.4% of revenue over the last twelve months, the ten largest 18.4% and the twenty largest 27.4%.[1] The company counts 23 clients who each pay it more than $100 million a year and 291 who each pay more than $10 million.[1]

The money works like this. Most of the cost is people. Employee costs were 55.6% of revenue in the September quarter and outside contractors 15.0%.[1] What is left after all operating costs is the EBIT margin, 18.2% in the quarter, or 19.0% before an 84 basis point restructuring charge.[1][3] Revenue per employee was $65.4 thousand a year, up 2.4% on the year before, which is the company's measure of how much more work each person now handles with the help of AI tools.[1] Clients pay in dollars, euros and pounds while most staff are paid in rupees, so a falling rupee lifts profit. The average rate was 95.72 rupees to the dollar in the quarter against 87.63 a year earlier.[1]

That exchange rate explains a gap you will see throughout this report. Revenue in rupees grew 11.5% on the year, but revenue in dollars grew only 2.2% and revenue in constant currency, which strips out currency moves, only 2.6%.[1] The honest growth rate is the last one. Growth is also uneven by segment: Services grew 4.0% in constant currency while HCLSoftware revenue fell 11.2%, because fewer multi-year licences came up for renewal. A multi-year licence books 60% to 70% of its value on day one, so software revenue swings from quarter to quarter.[1][3]

How a services contract turns into profit

A services contract is a promise to deliver work over several years. HCLTech books the full value of newly signed contracts as total contract value, or TCV. TCV was $3,258 million in the September quarter, up 27% on the year, and $5,665 million in the first half of FY27 against $4,381 million a year earlier.[1][11][12][6] Revenue arrives later, as the work is done. The company says a very large digital workplace contract signed in early July will add no revenue in the December or March quarters and will start to count from April 2027.[3][7] That delay matters to the shares, because the market sees the signing today and the revenue a year later.

Pricing is changing. Management calls it a well-known fact that AI-driven price deflation of 2% to 3% a year is now part of the industry, because clients expect AI tools to lower the cost of routine work.[9] HCLTech answers in two ways. It sells new AI-led work, which it calls Advanced AI, and it uses AI inside its own delivery to keep margins up. Advanced AI revenue was $219 million in the quarter, up 78.0% in a year, and the company says the services part of this work earns margins at least 7% to 10% higher than traditional services.[1][3]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Constant currency is revenue growth with exchange-rate moves taken out, so it shows real demand.
  • EBIT margin is operating profit before interest and tax, divided by revenue.
  • TCV, total contract value, is the full dollar value of newly signed contracts. It is not revenue yet.
  • ARR is annual recurring revenue, the yearly value of software subscriptions and support contracts that are active on the last day of a quarter.
  • Attrition is the share of staff who left over the last twelve months.
  • Restructuring cost is a one-off charge for reshaping teams or offices; HCLTech reported 84 basis points of it in the September quarter. One basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point.
  • RSI is the relative strength index, a gauge from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has moved. A reading above 70 is read as stretched.
  • ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price swing of a share, used here to measure distances and to place stops.
  • ROCE is return on capital employed, the operating profit earned on the money invested in the business. ROIC is return on invested capital, a similar measure that the company reports itself.
  • EPS is earnings per share, the profit attributable to each share. FCF is free cash flow, the cash left after the money spent on equipment and software.
  • Free cash flow is the cash from operations minus capital spending. Cash flow from operations is the cash that core business activity brings in, after tax.
  • Operating margin is the share of revenue left after operating costs. This report mostly uses the EBIT margin, which is the same idea for HCLTech.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' equity. Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by profit per share, the number of years of profit the price pays for.
  • Guidance is management's official forecast for the year. Book-to-bill is new orders won divided by revenue billed. HCLTech does not publish it, so we use deal wins divided by revenue as a proxy.
  • Stage 2 is the rising uptrend phase of a stock's cycle, and Stage 4 is the falling downtrend phase. Stage 3 is the top or pullback between them.
  • Base is a flat resting range in a share price, and a pivot is the price level that would end that rest. The report's tests ask how far the price is from them.
  • Asymmetry is the possible gain divided by the possible loss. The reward-to-risk ratio is the same measure. The entry zone is the price range where we would place buy orders.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence of a good business is what it earns on the money put into it. HCLTech reports a return on invested capital, or ROIC (return on invested capital, the profit earned on each rupee tied up in the business), of 41.2% over the last twelve months, up 261 basis points in a year. The Services business earned 49.4% and HCLSoftware 19.2%.[1] Screener.in, a data site, shows return on capital employed of 30.4% and return on equity of 23.8%, and the five-year range for capital employed returns is 25% to 32%.[29] That is a steady record through a period when revenue growth in constant currency fell from 4.8% in the December 2025 quarter to 2.4%, 2.6% and 2.6% in the three quarters since.[1]

Scale and relationships are the second strength. The company has added 33 clients paying over $10 million a year in twelve months, and the count of clients paying over $100 million rose by one to 23.[1] It reached Premier partner status with Anthropic and Advanced Partner status with OpenAI in 2026, and it builds AI computing racks with NVIDIA's specialists.[1][3] Management says its AI Force platform is used in 92 client accounts and holds more than 900 software agents, so some of its work is now a product sold with services, not only people billed by the hour.[7][3] The third strength is the balance sheet: net cash of ₹24,503 crore, borrowings of ₹954 crore, a top domestic rating from ICRA and A- ratings from S&P and Fitch.[1][19][20]

The weaknesses are just as clear. Growth is slow. Constant-currency revenue growth was 2.6% in each of the last two quarters, and FY27 guidance opened in April at 1% to 4%, was held in July and was narrowed to 2.5% to 4% in October.[1][8][2] Part of the October raise is bought growth: acquisitions add 87 basis points to the full-year figure, and management says the organic midpoint is broadly unchanged.[3] Management has named budget cuts at three of its twenty largest clients, two in telecom and one a manufacturer, and in April it put the cost of two of them at close to 50 basis points of FY27 growth.[3][9]

The software business is a second weak spot. Reported HCLSoftware revenue fell 11.2% in constant currency, and about 15% on an organic basis, while recurring revenue rose only 2.6% before counting the Jaspersoft acquisition.[1][3] Engineering and R&D Services made 15.2% EBIT margin, 231 basis points lower than a year ago.[1] Life sciences and telecom revenue each fell 2.1%, and Europe fell 0.3%.[1] Peers are in the same squeeze: Infosys cut its FY27 growth guidance to 1% to 2% on 23 October and cited price pressure and a higher client ask for productivity.[23]

What would make the position stronger or weaker

The business gets stronger if three things hold at once. Services growth must stay near 4%, software must return to growth in reported revenue as multi-year renewals come back in FY28 and FY29, and the new AI work must keep earning the higher margin the company describes.[3] It gets weaker if clients keep cutting discretionary projects or if AI-driven price cuts outrun the new work. The United States is 56.1% of Services revenue, so American policy matters. On 8 October the US Labor Department suspended new green-card labour certification filings from HCL and other large Indian IT firms.[1][27] HCLTech says it filed very few such applications and expects no material effect on talent plans or client work.[3] One analyst estimate puts the industry margin cost at 10 to 30 basis points over twelve months, rising if the suspension lasts for years.[28]

The wider market gives little help. Gartner forecasts world spending on IT services to grow 4.4% in 2026 and 4.9% in 2027, against about 15% a year for software.[26] HCLTech's Services growth of 4.0% in constant currency is close to the services market, so the company is keeping pace and not taking share quickly.[1][26] The fast growth in IT spending is in AI computers and software, where HCLTech earns through its Advanced AI work and its software arm.

For a reader deciding whether to own the shares, the key point is that the business quality is high but the growth is modest and the price has already moved. The next chapter scores the operating measures that govern an IT services company and checks whether management delivered what it promised.

3. The numbers, in plain words

The numbers describe a business that turns almost 12 rupees of every 100 earned into free cash, pays most of it out and keeps a large cash pile. Revenue was ₹138,052 crore over the twelve months to September 2026, up from ₹101,456 crore in FY23.[14][1][8] Currency explains much of that rise. From FY23 to FY26, revenue in dollars grew 16.5% and revenue in rupees 28.3%, so about two-fifths of the rupee gain is currency.[14][8] Rupee revenue grew 11.2% in FY26 and 11.5% in the September quarter, yet constant-currency growth was 3.9% and 2.6%.[8][1]

Profit tells a similar story. EBIT was ₹22,397 crore in FY26 and ₹24,201 crore over the last twelve months, both before a one-time labour-code charge of ₹956 crore.[8][1] The EBIT margin has stayed between 17.2% and 18.3% for four years, which is the sign of a mature contract business. Net income was ₹16,642 crore in FY26 after the one-time charge and ₹17,361 crore before it.[8][16] The labour-code charge is a catch-up cost from India's new labour laws and will not repeat. Restructuring costs have recurred, though: 65 basis points of revenue in FY26, then 62 and 84 basis points in the first two quarters of FY27.[8][6][1]

Customer concentration is low and cyclicality is moderate. The twenty largest clients make up 27.4% of revenue, and no industry is above 22.4% of Services revenue.[1] Growth in constant currency slowed from 6.0% in the March 2024 quarter to 2.4% in March 2026 as clients cut discretionary projects.[14][8] The company does not depend on borrowed money. Borrowings were ₹954 crore against equity of ₹76,126 crore on 30 September 2026, and lease liabilities were ₹5,059 crore.[1]

The cash story has two parts. Over three years the company collected far more cash than it reported as profit, at a ratio of 1.30, as the first estimate below shows. Over the last twelve months the ratio fell to 0.95 because of a tax payment under a bilateral pricing agreement and a payment into a new employee gratuity trust.[1][3] Net cash fell by ₹8,785 crore in the first half of FY27, from ₹33,288 crore to ₹24,503 crore, as the second table shows. That is not a warning on its own, but it is the number to follow in the next two results.[1][8] Shareholders were paid generously. The board declared an interim dividend of ₹12 a share on 12 October, with a record date of 16 October and payment on 26 October. That brings the last twelve months to ₹60 a share, which is 88.6% of net income, after 93.5% a year earlier.[4][3][40]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report has a weak point. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each one, says what evidence would show it is wrong and gives the date it will be known. It then argues the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, with numbers, and names the single claim on which the verdict depends.

Profit per share: the margin and tax assumptions

The weakest assumption in the profit forecast is that the EBIT margin holds at 18.6% in the second half of FY27 and 18.3% in FY28 while the tax rate returns to 25%. The September quarter margin was lifted by gains from currency of 23 basis points and by cost savings from Project Ascend, which the company credits with 73 basis points.[3] A stronger rupee would reverse the currency gain. The rupee averaged 95.72 to the dollar in the quarter, 9.2% weaker than the 87.63 of a year before.[1] Evidence that the assumption is wrong would be a December-quarter margin below 17.5%, the bottom of the guided band. It will be known when the Q3 FY27 results are published, which was 12 January last year and has not been announced for this year.

Value per share: the 20 times multiple

The weakest assumption in the base value is that investors keep paying 20 times profit. Three peers pay 13.9 to 16.3 times. Infosys, at 14.4, cut its FY27 guidance in October, and Wipro, at 13.9, guided its next quarter to between a fall of 1.5% and a rise of 0.5%.[31][32][33][23][24] HCLTech's premium rests on its higher growth in Services and its software business. If the market treated it like TCS, at 16.3 times our FY28 profit, the value would be ₹1,273, about the same as the top of the zone, so a buyer at ₹1,270 would have almost no upside and the reward-to-risk ratio would be 0.02. Even at 17.2 times the ratio at ₹1,270 would be only 0.54. Evidence would be a sector-wide fall in multiples after the December-quarter results of the large peers. The next peer reads are in January 2027, and the dates have not been announced.

Free cash flow: the one-offs roll off

The weakest assumption in the cash estimate is that the two one-time items, a tax payment under a bilateral pricing agreement and a payment into a gratuity trust, are indeed one-off. Management says free cash flow will return to 100% of net income or more as they leave the twelve-month window.[3] If the ratio stays near 87%, the reverse DCF stays at 8.5% a year and the shares stay fairly priced, not cheap. The first read is the half-year cash flow statement in the Q3 FY27 results, and the full-year audited statement follows in April 2027.

The red-team case against our own conclusion

The strongest case against buying even in the zone is this. Growth in constant currency was 2.6% in each of the last two quarters, and acquisitions added $35.6 million in September, about one point of growth, so organic growth was nearer 1.6%.[1][3] AI price cuts of 2% to 3% a year are, on management's own account, a known cost.[9] If growth fell to about 1% in FY28 and the margin slipped to 17.0%, the bear case in the scenario table, which allows for the US labour-certification suspension, profit per share would be about ₹70 and the stock would trade nearer 16.3 times, or ₹1,141. We accept that case as the main risk. The sizing is set to match it. At 3.5% of the portfolio and a stop 10.8% below the entry, the planned loss at the stop is about 0.38% of the portfolio, which is below the 0.50% risk budget for this tier.[36]
