Before you read

Electrosteel Castings makes ductile iron pipes, the iron water mains that carry drinking water and sewage.[4] Contractors building government water schemes, such as the Jal Jeevan Mission, are its main buyers.[5] This report rejects the stock today for two reasons: its price is in a long downtrend that fails our recovery test, and its scorecard of business measures is 2.5 out of 5.0 against a floor of 3.0.[37][38] The company has little debt and its owners raised their stake by 4.4 points in the September quarter, yet sales volume fell 27 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[2][15] We would be wrong if state payments reach pipe orders soon and the September results, due near mid-November, show a profit margin above 9.5 percent on rising volume[17].

1. What the company does

Electrosteel Castings Limited is India's pioneer maker of ductile iron (DI) pipes. A DI pipe is a spun iron tube, tougher than old cast iron, that carries drinking water, sewage and irrigation water under pressure. The company says its pipes last 70 to 90 years, and it is the largest exporter of DI pipes from India, selling to more than 130 countries.[4] It also makes DI fittings (the bends and joints that connect pipes), cast iron pipes, pig iron, coke, sponge iron, ferro alloys, cement and power, mostly for its own use.[4]

The company is built as one chain. It runs its own blast furnaces, coke ovens, a sinter plant and a power plant, so iron ore and coking coal go in at one end and finished pipe comes out at the other.[4][9] Pipe capacity is 9 lakh tonnes a year (a lakh is 100,000), and total finished-goods capacity is about 10 lakh tonnes across seven plants: three in West Bengal, two in the south, and two valve plants in Italy and Turkey.[4] The chain lowers cost when the plants run full. It also means the fixed costs stay when they do not, which matters a great deal in this report.

Who pays? Most domestic pipe goes to engineering contractors such as L&T, Megha Engineering and NCC, and to state water departments, who lay pipe for projects that governments fund.[4] Exports were about 22 percent of revenue in the latest presentation.[4] Management says DI pipes are about 85 percent of the business today and that new lines (valves, industrial paints) could cut that to about 55 percent in four to five years.[5] So the company's sales follow government water spending, and the cash arrives only when states pay their contractors.

How does it make money? It sells pipe by weight. In the June 2026 quarter the net price was about ₹55,000 a tonne, up from about ₹50,500 in the March quarter, and revenue was about ₹62,000 a tonne.[5] Management says 60 to 70 percent of the product cost depends on iron ore and coal prices, so margins move with raw materials as well as demand.[8] Profit per tonne is small compared with the price, so the number of tonnes sold, and therefore plant use, decides the year.

Three newer lines matter for the story. Valves: the company bought T.I.S. Services of Italy in 2025 for about €11.5 million, at 7 to 8 times operating profit, and wants to double valve revenue in four years.[4][5] Industrial paints: management plans ₹250 to ₹300 crore of spending for ₹800 to ₹1,000 crore of annual sales in five years.[5] Oman: in September 2026 it completed the purchase of 70 percent of its Omani sales agent, Arabian Water Tech, for about OMR 21,000, to secure control of its own distribution.[36]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business has real strengths. It is the pioneer of DI pipe in India, it owns its raw-material chain, and even in the weakest year its gross margin stayed near 46 to 49 percent, which Crisil called intact.[4][10] Its balance sheet is conservative: net debt was ₹649 crore against net worth of ₹5,924 crore at March 2026, a ratio of 0.11 times, after a ₹1,109 crore cut in net debt during FY26.[4] Crisil rates it AA, but moved the outlook to Negative in February 2026 because volumes and prices fell.[10] India Ratings also rates it AA, with a Stable outlook in the May 2026 presentation.[7]

The weakness is just as clear. Pipe demand rests on government water spending, and the Jal Jeevan Mission paused. Central funds drawn by states fell from ₹69,886 crore in FY24 to ₹22,541 crore in FY25 and ₹1,562 crore in FY26, then recovered to ₹10,344 crore so far in FY27.[4] Company sales volume dropped from 7.81 lakh tonnes in FY25 to 5.84 lakh tonnes in FY26, and to 1.20 lakh tonnes in the June 2026 quarter, down 27 percent from a year earlier.[4][5] Management said it believes FY26 was the worst year for DI pipes in India since 1994, and that this is the bottom.[5]

Because plant costs are fixed, lost volume cut profit far more than it cut sales. Crisil reports that pipe-plant use fell from about 90 percent in FY25 to about 60 percent in the first half of FY26, and operating profit per tonne fell from about ₹15,000 to about ₹8,700.[10] The annual report shows DI pipe output of 5,36,336 tonnes in FY26 against 7,32,004 tonnes the year before, so the 9 lakh tonne capacity ran at roughly 60 percent.[9]

Competition has not eased. Jindal Saw, which also makes DI pipe, said on 19 October 2026 that rising coking coal costs squeezed its DI pipe margins, that lower plant use added to the squeeze, and that its Ductile business may take some more time to recover.[19] Its report from 16 October showed an EBITDA margin of 5.8 percent against 10.7 percent a year earlier.[20] Industry commentary also points to delayed state payments to contractors.[28]

Why payment timing matters more than the budget

The central government approved JJM 2.0 on 10 March 2026 with a total outlay of ₹8.69 lakh crore, and the central share rose from ₹2.08 lakh crore to ₹3.59 lakh crore.[29][2] The FY27 central allocation is ₹67,670 crore, against a revised ₹17,000 crore for FY26.[9] That is a large number, but the money moves in steps. By 6 August 2026 the Centre had released ₹6,150 crore to states, and the company's 7 August deck puts central sanctions at ₹10,344 crore.[29][4]

The Centre releases its share only after a state shows its own matching money, and money sent to a state does not always reach the contractor who bought the pipe.[28] The company's chief executive estimated that ₹30,000 to ₹35,000 crore is owed to contractors across the sector, and described pipe demand as having hit a pause button.[28] Pipe makers are paid by contractors, so a late state payment becomes a late pipe order.

Input costs: iron ore, coking coal, scrap and energy

Iron ore for the plants comes from Odisha, Jharkhand and Karnataka, coke is made in the company's own ovens, and coking coal is imported mainly from Australia.[9] On 26 October 2026 premium low-volatility hard coking coal was assessed at $266.70 a tonne free on board Australia, and the India delivered price for the same grade on a net forward basis was $292.00 a tonne.[27] Management told investors in May 2026 that coking coal, freight and energy costs were rising, with energy cost in India up almost 70 to 80 percent.[6]

This is a squeeze with two sides. Input costs are rising while the domestic selling price is soft, and the company has said pricing is a result of both demand and raw-material moves.[8] The annual report names iron ore and coke as the main raw materials and publishes no separate scrap price or scrap cost, so we treat scrap as not published and do not model it.[9]

The Electrosteel Steels history and legacy claims

Electrosteel Castings promoted a steel company, Electrosteel Steels Limited (ESL), that went through a corporate insolvency resolution process. Vedanta's resolution plan was approved on 17 April 2018, with ₹5,320 crore paid up front and about 90 percent of the shares going to Vedanta, and Vedanta took control on 4 June 2018.[30][31] The pipe company kept a small shareholding in ESL, and old links remain on its books.[3]

Note 4 of the June 2026 results says the company still holds 19,796,000 ESL shares, of which 17,334,999 were pledged to ESL's lenders. The lenders' attempt to invoke that pledge was set aside by the Calcutta High Court, and the company's request to release it is pending.[3] A lender also took symbolic possession of company land at Elavur, worth ₹29,493.58 lakh, that had been mortgaged for an ESL loan, and an appeals tribunal asked the company to deposit 50 percent of the demand, or ₹29,355.04 lakh (about ₹294 crore, or about ₹4.7 a share). The company has challenged that order in the Madras High Court, and none of our scenarios includes this cash outflow.[3] The Supreme Court on 6 January 2026 confirmed the company is not a guarantor for ESL's facilities, so no insolvency case can be started against it, but it also held that the ESL plan did not wipe out claims against third-party security providers, and the company is considering a review application.[3]

A separate item is the Parbatpur coal block, cancelled in earlier years. The company has claimed ₹154,944.48 lakh in compensation, and had received about ₹98 crore by August 2026.[3][5] Auditors gave a qualified conclusion on both matters because the effects cannot be worked out yet.[3] These are not red-line items under our rules, but they are why the Layer 1 page treats governance as a watch item and not a clean pass.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management guidance is the second half of Layer 2. We compare what was promised in May 2025 and May 2026 with what the filings later showed.[8][6] The pattern is that capital spending and debt cuts were delivered, but volume and margin promises were cut each time, usually within one to three quarters.

In May 2025, management guided FY26 pipe volume of 8.3 to 8.5 lakh tonnes, a medium-term operating margin of 15 to 18 percent, and an upturn from the second quarter of FY26.[8] Actual FY26 sales were 5.84 lakh tonnes of all products, DI pipe output was 5,36,336 tonnes, and the FY26 EBITDA margin was 9.4 percent.[4][9] In May 2026, management guided about 7 lakh tonnes of pipe dispatch and a 13 to 14 percent margin for FY27. By 7 August 2026 the volume guide was about 5.75 lakh tonnes and the margin guide was 12 to 13 percent for the third and fourth quarters only.[6][5]

On capital, the plan to lift capacity from 6.8 lakh to 10 lakh tonnes was largely delivered: finished-goods capacity reached 10,11,000 tonnes, and the company said ₹500 crore of a ₹700 crore plan had been spent by March 2025.[4][8] The timing was poor, since the new capacity opened into a demand pause. Debt was cut well: net debt fell ₹1,109 crore in FY26.[4] Against that, the paints plan grew from a ₹600 crore revenue target in the May 2026 call to ₹800 to ₹1,000 crore in August, and spending rose from ₹100 crore to ₹250 to ₹300 crore.[5]

Governance moves are mixed. The promoters raised their stake from 46.21 percent in December 2025 to 54.53 percent in September 2026, with open market buying at about ₹71 to ₹73 a share in the last week of September.[11][14][16] Of that rise, 4.4 points came in the September quarter alone (50.13 to 54.53 percent). The 5.00 percent total in the promoters' filing includes purchases back to March 2026.[13][15] Foreign funds moved the other way: their holding fell from 15.73 percent at March 2026 to 12.80 percent in June and 8.57 percent in September.[11] Management also said a share buyback is on its mind, but gave no plan.[5] The chief financial officer changed in August 2026, days after the June results, when Rajesh Daga replaced Ashutosh Agarwal.[32][5] Auditors still qualify the results for the coal block and the ESL matters.[3]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of numbers show a boom and a bust. Revenue rose from ₹5,281 crore in FY22 to ₹7,478 crore in FY24 and then fell to ₹5,918 crore in FY26, with the trailing twelve months at ₹5,786 crore.[1][9] Operating profit peaked at ₹1,204 crore in FY24, which was a 16 percent margin, and was ₹359 crore in FY26, a 6 percent margin on the Screener definition that excludes other income.[1] Profit after tax fell from ₹740 crore in FY24 to ₹161 crore in FY26, which includes a ₹38 crore one-time labour-code provision.[1][2][32]

Cash tells a different story. Cash from operations was ₹1,147 crore in FY26, up from ₹480 crore, because stock and unpaid bills were released while profit fell.[1][9] That inflow cut net debt to ₹649 crore, but it cannot repeat, since it came from shrinking working capital. At June 2026 net debt had already risen to ₹876 crore on gross debt of ₹1,658 crore.[5] The three-year ratio of cash from operations to profit is 1.51 (derived: ₹2,430 crore of cash over ₹1,611 crore of profit for FY24 to FY26), which clears the red line but flatters the trend.[1]

Returns on capital collapsed with profit. Return on capital employed (ROCE), the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses, was 16 percent in FY24 and 5 percent in FY26, and the company's return on equity was 3.04 percent in the latest Screener reading.[1] Stock days are the other warning: 276 days of inventory at March 2026, up from 221 two years earlier, and a cash conversion cycle of 278 days.[1] Inventory of ₹2,398 crore is about 40 percent of a year's sales (derived: ₹2,398 crore over ₹5,918 crore).[4]

The most recent quarter is the June 2026 quarter. The September 2026 results have not been filed, and the company has not yet named a board date. Its trading window has been closed since 1 October, and last year the September board meeting was on 10 November.[17][18] In the June quarter, revenue was ₹1,426 crore, down 8.5 percent from a year earlier, EBITDA including other income was ₹139 crore (9.5 percent margin), and profit after tax was ₹48 crore.[4][2] Management said it expects the September quarter to be about the same, and that it will not do worse than June.[5]

The range

In the bull case (FY27 earnings of ₹4.20) book value would be about ₹99.1. In the bear case (earnings of ₹2.20) about ₹97.1.

If the number is off

Our price target uses book value times a multiple. A ₹2 error in book value moves the base target by about ₹1.50, so this input is not the one that decides the outcome.

Risk budgets are counted in basis points, and one basis point is one hundredth of one percent. A tier is a conviction grade that sets the budget and a size cap; TIER_0 means no conviction and no money. 'Let winners run' means we do not sell a rising stock just because it looks high.
