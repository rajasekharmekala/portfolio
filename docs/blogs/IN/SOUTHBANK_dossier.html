South Indian Bank is a private bank founded in 1929 in Kerala, with 959 branches and ₹1.10 lakh crore of loans, about a quarter of them lent against gold.[3][4] On 15 October 2026 it reported its best quarterly profit on record, ₹426.13 crore, up 21.28%, with bad loans down to 1.24%.[3] We rate it a real turnaround that the market has already paid for in part: the price of ₹54.64 is 5.35 ATR (average true range, the typical daily swing) above support, and about 9 times trailing earnings against a three-year average of 5.55 times.[1][20] The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, with limit orders at ₹46.11 to ₹47.96 and a stop at ₹45.14.[1] We are wrong if gold prices fall hard, if cheaper deposits stop helping the margin, or if the new chief executive disrupts the plan[29][10][27].

1. What the company does

A bank is a business that borrows money from many people and lends it to others at a higher price. South Indian Bank was founded in 1929 in Thrissur, Kerala, and was the first scheduled bank among the state's private banks.[16] It has no promoter, which means no single family or group controls it. On 30 September 2026 foreign funds held 25.57% of its shares, Indian institutions 16.78% and the public 57.65%.[17] The network had 959 branches, 1,277 ATMs and cash recycling machines, 9,485 employees and about 8.2 million customers across 26 states and four union territories, and 98.7% of transactions ran on digital channels.[4][5]

The bank earns money from the gap between what it charges borrowers and what it pays depositors. In rupees that gap is called net interest income (NII). In the September 2026 quarter it was ₹1,116 crore, up 37.95% on a year earlier.[3] Divided by the assets that earn interest, the gap becomes the net interest margin (NIM). South Indian Bank's NIM was 3.33%, against 2.80% a year earlier and 3.23% in the June quarter.[3][4] A smaller part of income comes from fees, trading and recoveries. Non-interest income was ₹468 crore, about 30% of total income: ₹198 crore of core fees, ₹80 crore of treasury and foreign-exchange profit, and ₹190 crore of other items.[4] The CFO said that ₹190 crore includes ₹73 crore recovered from loans already written off in the books and about ₹30 crore to ₹35 crore of insurance-selling commission.[11]

Who pays the bank? Borrowers on a few books. Gross advances were ₹1,09,517 crore on 30 September 2026, up 18.67% in a year, or about 20% after adding back a ₹1,163 crore technical write-off made last year.[3][5] Corporate loans were 39% of the book and the other 61% was retail, agriculture and small-business lending, which the bank calls RAM.[5] Gold loans are the fastest-growing part: ₹26,603 crore, up 41.17%, which is 24.3% of all loans. The CEO put the split at about ₹15,000 crore retail and ₹10,000 crore agriculture, at a blended yield close to 10%.[3][11] A gold loan is a loan against jewellery kept with the bank. It is repaid with interest, and if the borrower fails to pay, the bank sells the gold.

Who funds the bank? Depositors. Deposits were ₹1,37,279 crore. Of that, ₹43,310 crore, or 31.55%, was current and savings account (CASA) money, which is cheap because the bank pays little or nothing on it.[4][7] Retail term deposits were ₹86,043 crore, and large “bulk” deposits were ₹7,926 crore, up from ₹1,511 crore three months earlier.[7] Non-resident Indian deposits were ₹38,203 crore.[3] The cost of funds fell to 4.42% from 4.74% a year earlier, which is the main reason the margin widened.[7]

Loan prices adjust at different speeds. About 43% of loans are linked to an external benchmark that moves with the Reserve Bank's repo rate or with treasury bill yields, 5% to the bank's own cost-of-funds rate, and 47% carry fixed rates.[11] The repo rate, which is the rate at which the Reserve Bank lends to banks, rose 25 basis points to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, when the Monetary Policy Committee also changed its stance to calibrated tightening.[32] The CEO said the benchmark-linked loans reprice the next day and that new gold loans are written at the new rate, while deposits reprice only as they mature.[10] That is why a rate rise tends to help this bank's margin first, and why the cost of deposits is the number to watch next.

A bank cannot be read like a factory. For a factory, the ratio of operating cash flow to profit shows whether profit turns into cash, and debt-to-equity, which is total borrowings divided by shareholder equity, shows how much it borrows. For a bank, deposits are the raw material it lends out, so debt-to-equity and interest cover are not meaningful, and operating cash flow swings with every rupee of new loans and deposits. Screener reports cash from operations of ₹5,165 crore, minus ₹7,261 crore, ₹7,075 crore, minus ₹2,353 crore and ₹3,907 crore for FY22 to FY26, while profit rose from ₹45 crore to ₹1,456 crore.[17] We therefore replace those tests with return on equity, return on assets, bad-loan ratios, capital ratios, margin, deposit mix and credit cost, which is how the Reserve Bank and the rating agencies read a bank.[2][16]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Net interest income (NII): interest earned on loans and investments minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings.
  • Net interest margin (NIM): NII as a share of the assets that earn interest; a rising NIM means the bank keeps more of each rupee lent.
  • CASA: current and savings account deposits, the cheapest funding a bank has.
  • Gross non-performing assets and net non-performing assets (GNPA and NNPA): loans that have stopped paying for 90 days, before and after the bank sets aside money for them.
  • Provision coverage ratio (PCR): the share of bad loans already covered by money set aside, shown with and without loans written off in the books.
  • Technical write-off: a loan removed from the books for accounting purposes while the bank keeps trying to recover it; recoveries come back as income.
  • Credit cost: the yearly charge for bad loans as a share of loans; the bank reports its own measure, and we also compute provisions divided by average advances.
  • Capital adequacy ratio (CRAR) and CET1: the bank's own capital as a share of its risk-weighted loans; CET1 counts only the highest-quality capital. The Reserve Bank's minimum is 11.50%.[7]
  • Return on equity (ROE) and return on assets (ROA): yearly profit as a share of shareholders' money and of total assets.
  • Price-to-book (P/B): the share price divided by net worth per share; a bank at 1.0 costs the same as its accounting value.
  • Special mention accounts (SMA): loans paying late but not yet bad; SMA 1 and 2 are 31 to 90 days late.
  • Expected credit loss (ECL): new Reserve Bank accounting from 1 April 2027 that makes banks set aside money for losses they expect, not only those that have happened.[33]
  • EBLR: external benchmark linked lending rate, a loan rate tied to the repo rate or to treasury bill yields.
  • RAM: the bank's label for retail, agriculture and MSME (small-business) loans, 61% of its book.[5]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Three things make the business strong. First, bad loans have been cleaned up. Gross bad loans fell from 6.97% in March 2021 to 1.24% in September 2026, and net bad loans from 4.71% to 0.09%, which the chief executive and ICICI Securities describe as the lowest net bad-loan ratio among banks.[6][10][24] The bank has also set aside money against 92.42% of its bad loans, or 97.84% if loans written off in the books are counted, and new slippages (loans turning bad in the quarter) were only ₹103 crore.[3][6] Second, capital is ample. The capital adequacy ratio is 19.40% against a minimum of 11.50%, and the whole Tier 1 ratio of 18.71% is common equity.[7] Third, the funding is cheap and sticky: CASA is 31.55% of deposits, the cost of deposits fell to 5.00% from 5.41%, and CARE says retail term deposits are about 96% of term deposits.[4][7][16]

Four things make it weak. Returns are modest. Return on assets was 1.08% and return on equity 14.00% in the September quarter, and the cost-to-income ratio, which is running costs divided by income, was 53.7%, an improvement from 59.6% a year earlier but still above the 50% level that management itself aims for within two years.[5][10] The loan margin of 3.33% is below Karur Vysya Bank (4.34%), Tamilnad Mercantile Bank (4.29% in the June quarter), City Union Bank (3.87%) and CSB Bank (3.81%), and level with Federal Bank (3.32%).[37][36][38][39][40] The loan mix is not changing as fast as promised: corporate loans were 39% of the book against 40% a year ago, and an analyst on the October call noted that corporate and gold supplied about 80% of the quarter's loan growth.[5][11] Geography is the third weakness: Kerala is 30% of advances and 63% of deposits, and south India is 63% of advances.[16] The fourth is the leadership change, covered below.

How does it compare with its neighbours? On net bad loans South Indian Bank is the best of the six banks we checked, at 0.09% against 0.14% at Tamilnad Mercantile Bank, 0.17% at Federal Bank, 0.18% at Karur Vysya Bank, 0.33% at CSB Bank and 0.48% at City Union Bank.[35][36][40][37][39][38] On gross bad loans it is in the middle: 1.24% against 0.61% at Tamilnad Mercantile, 0.69% at Karur Vysya, 1.32% at Federal, 1.44% at City Union and 1.58% at CSB.[35][36][37][40][38][39] On return on equity, the aggregator's trailing figure of 13.9% sits just under the group median of 14.1% and far below Karur Vysya at 21.0%.[35][37] On price it is cheap against Federal Bank (2.15 times book value) and City Union Bank (2.01), and level with Tamilnad Mercantile (1.19) despite earning about half its return on assets.[40][38][36] The question for the rest of this book is whether that discount is a mispricing or a fair charge for a short track record, a modest return on assets and a new chief executive.

Who owns the shares, and why that matters

The bank has no promoter, so its price is set by funds and the public. That mix has changed fast. Foreign funds held 17.9% in September 2025 and 25.57% a year later, mutual funds went from 10.01% to 15.13%, and the public share fell from 70.16% to 57.65%.[17][19] Three mutual funds hold 13.64% between them: Kotak Multicap Fund 6.05%, Bandhan Small Cap Fund 4.69% and SBI Banking and Financial Services Fund 2.90%.[19] Promoter pledge, which is shares a controlling owner has borrowed against, is not applicable and shows 0.0% in every quarter.[19]

The re-rating of the stock has therefore been bought by institutions, and the next buyer or seller will also be an institution. That works in the bull case while earnings keep rising. It is a risk in the bear case, because a few funds hold a large block and a fund that decides the story has played out can sell a lot of stock into a thin tape: only 12.5 million shares traded on 28 October, 0.56 times the 20-day average.[1] The stock is also up about 82% from ₹29.99, the price on 6 October 2025 in Trendlyne's report list, which means much of the recovery is already in the price.[22][1]

The leadership change

P R Seshadri, the managing director and chief executive for about two years and nine months, did not seek a second term, and his term ended on 30 September 2026.[12][13][27] The Reserve Bank approved Mahesh Muralidhar Pai, aged 50, a chief general manager at Canara Bank who led its digital banking and set up its gold loan vertical, for three years from 1 October 2026.[27][26] ICICI Securities downgraded the stock to Hold on 9 July because of the transition and cut its target from ₹56 to ₹45, and the stock fell 8.33% that day; it upgraded to Buy on 17 October with a target of ₹63 after the Q2 results.[26][23][22]

The new chief executive's first message was continuity: “no change in the strategy”, with tweaks, and a stated wish to strengthen the existing system rather than change it.[11] He did change two things. He talked about taking gold loans to 35% of the book as a first phase, where his predecessor had said the bank did not want gold above 29% to 30%, and he plans a credit card business built on the bank's own technology by the first quarter of FY28.[11][29][28] ICICI Securities names churn in senior personnel as the main risk, and one chief general manager who headed branch banking and liabilities took voluntary retirement shortly before the downgrade.[23][26] Chapter 2B tests whether management delivered what it promised before the change, which is the best evidence we have on the quality of the people who remain.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management guidance is its forecast for the year. A bank that guides low and beats every quarter is easy to praise and hard to price, because the beats stop being informative. This bank's managers have mostly done what they said they would do. In May 2025 the then chief executive said growth would be “north of 12%” and return on assets about 1%, and he hoped to beat the growth number by three or four points.[14] Loans grew 14.5% in FY26, or 15.8% before the technical write-off (adding it back), and return on assets was 1.03%.[13][8]

The two clear misses are small and instructive. In October 2025 he expected the margin to recover to over 3% by the end of FY26, from 2.8%, and the fourth quarter printed 2.95%, five basis points short; the margin crossed 3% one quarter later, at 3.23%.[30][4] And the loan mix has not moved as promised: the aim has been to cut corporate loans, yet they were 39% of the book in September against 40% a year ago, and the new chief executive agreed on the call that this is a concern, saying the shift in segments is “not happening”.[5][11] The other signal is about the future. The old ceiling for gold loans was 29% to 30% of the loan book, as Mint reported in May 2026 when gold was about 24%, and the new chief executive talks of 35% as a first phase.[29][11] A ceiling that moves in five months is a change of risk appetite, which Chapter 6 weighs.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter gives the numbers first and the judgement afterwards. Layer 1 compares where each number stands with the direction it is moving, because a bank that is improving fast can be a better buy than a steady one at the same price. The numbers come from the bank's filings and investor presentation for the recent periods and from the aggregators Screener and Trendlyne for the multi-year tables.[4][8][17][18] Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares, and it appears in the tables below as a per-share figure.

How the money is made, step by step. The bank earns 8.55% on its loans and pays 5.00% on its deposits, and after funding costs on everything it keeps a net interest margin of 3.33% on its earning assets.[7][3] It adds fees, trading profit and recoveries, which are about 30% of total income, and subtracts staff and branch costs, which are 53.7% of income.[4][5] It then sets aside ₹159 crore for bad loans and other provisions, pays tax at about 25.7% (₹147 crore on ₹573 crore) and is left with ₹426 crore.[4] Return on assets is 1.08%. The bank's assets of ₹1,56,188 crore are about 12.9 times its net worth of ₹12,076 crore, so a return on assets of 1.08% becomes a return on equity of about 14%.[4] The net worth figure is capital of ₹262 crore plus reserves of ₹11,814 crore.[4]

Cyclicality works through three channels. The rate cycle: 43% of loans reprice with the repo rate or treasury bills, and deposits reprice only as they mature, so the margin moves up quickly when rates rise and falls quickly when they fall; the former chief executive said in July 2026 that when rates fell the bank was among the most affected, because it passes a repo change on within a day.[11][12] The gold cycle: gold futures were near $4,150 an ounce in October 2026, inside a range of $4,091 to $4,259, and the former chief executive said in October 2025 that the speed of the rise worried him; a fall in the price would hit the value of the collateral behind a quarter of the loans.[34][30][3] The credit cycle: the legacy book is mostly cleaned up, but the loans written since October 2020 under new underwriting standards have kept clean, but they have not met a full downturn.[16]

Three views pull in different directions.

The first is that most of the re-rating is done. ICICI Securities' target rose from ₹45 to ₹63 in three months, and its own note says the multiple went from about 0.8 to about 1.15 times book value.[26][23] The trailing price-to-earnings ratio is about 9.0 against a three-year average of 5.55 times, so a buyer today is paying for a quality that the bank has shown for only about two years.[20][1] Our base value uses 8.0 times earnings and 1.20 times book value, which is below today's trailing multiple, because the multiple is the largest single risk. The second is that the margin gain came from cheaper funding and not from higher loan yields. The cost of deposits fell 41 basis points over the year while the yield on advances was unchanged at 8.55% against 8.56%.[7] The chief executive says deposits will not reprice much in the next two quarters, because they are renewing at rates below last year's, and that every repo move helps the 43% of loans linked to external benchmarks.[10] That supports a margin of 3.36% to 3.38%. The third is that management's return-on-assets target is the real upside and we do not pay for it. The bank aims for 1.20% to 1.25% within a year and 1.25% to 1.30% in the medium term, against 1.08% today.[10] Our FY28 estimate implies 1.11%, in line with ICICI Securities' 1.1%, because costs are rising with new hiring and branches and credit cost is likely to rise from its trough.[23] Each 0.1 point of return on assets is about ₹5.50 of value, so reaching 1.25% is worth about ₹8 a share. That is the bull case, and it is why the verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT and not REJECTED. Net, the stock is not obviously cheap, because the reported discount depends on the cost of equity, and it is not obviously dear, because earnings are rising about 17% a year. The plan is a limit order near support, where the reward-to-risk is high enough to pay for being wrong, and not a purchase at ₹54.64, where it is below 1 to 1.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A red team is the part of the process that argues against the conclusion. Its job is to build the strongest honest case that we are wrong, to find the weakest assumption behind each estimate, and to name the single claim on which everything rests. This chapter does that for the 12-month value of ₹62, the earnings estimates of ₹6.67 and ₹7.58 per share, the implied-return reading, and the capital estimate.

The red-team bear case

The bear case starts with the books that grew fastest. Gold loans are 24.3% of advances, up 41% in a year, and the new chief executive wants them at 35% where his predecessor wanted a ceiling of 29% to 30%.[3][11][29] A gold loan is sized to the value of the gold, so part of the growth follows the gold price and would reverse with it. The bank's average loan-to-value was 57.18% in March and 65.25% in June, and its own former chief executive said in October 2025 that high loan-to-value on some loans worried him, because gold fell from $1,800 to $1,200 an ounce in 2011 to 2012.[13][12][30] On our arithmetic, a loan written at the 75% ceiling for large loans reaches the full value of the gold behind it after a 25% fall, and the average loan at 65.25% crosses 100% only after a fall of about 35%. The Reserve Bank's new rules also cap consumption loans at ₹2.5 lakh and force the bank to ask for more margin when prices fall, which is workable in a mild fall and hard in a fast one.[31][29] The bank does not publish the September loan-to-value, so we cannot size the exposure better than this.

The second argument is that the margin gain is borrowed. The cost of deposits fell from 5.41% to 5.00% while loan yields stayed at 8.55%, and bulk deposits rose from ₹1,511 crore to ₹7,926 crore in one quarter, which supplied more than half of the ₹11,462 crore rise in deposits.[7] The chief executive said bulk rates were lower than retail rates because foreign-currency deposit liquidity had risen, and that the bank had taken the opportunity.[11] That is a fair use of cheap money, and it is also money that can leave or reprice faster than a savings account. CASA fell 143 basis points to 31.55% and the chief executive said some of the new current account money came from IPO flows and might not stay.[7][10] The repo rate has just gone up 25 basis points and the Reserve Bank has changed its stance to calibrated tightening, which means deposit costs are more likely to rise than fall from here.[32]

The third is earnings quality and the mix. 7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule behind it is the technical routing rule. The stock passes every red line, qualifies for the TACTICAL_BREAKOUT archetype with a Layer 2 score of 4.0 out of 5.0, and is a Stage 2 leader. The archetype describes the trend and leadership profile; NO_MOMENTUM in the gate is a timing result about price and volume, not a verdict on the business. The close is 5.35 ATR above support, in the NO_BUY band of the support-distance ladder, and reward-to-risk is 0.77 at the close against the 2.5 floor for a tactical name.[2][1] So we do not buy at ₹54.64. We place limit orders inside the entry zone of ₹46.11 to ₹47.96 instead, where reward-to-risk is 4.98 at the top of the zone. The decision would change if the close fell below ₹45.14, which is the stop. It would also change if Q3 FY27 results showed profit below ₹430 crore, a margin below 3.25%, or credit cost above 0.6%, or if a senior executive left.

The entry zone has a stated reason. Its floor is the 20-day swing low of ₹46.11. Its top, ₹47.96, is today's 50-day moving average, which sits 0.95 ATR above the swing low and so falls in the STANDARD band of the ladder, within 1.2 ATR of support.[1][2] Measured from the ledger's own anchor, the 63-day low of ₹44.26, the floor is 0.95 ATR away and the top 1.91 ATR, which is beyond the 1.6 ATR limit for buying at market; that is why every order is a limit order inside the zone and none is a market order, as the ladder allows for the NO_BUY band. The zone is measured on the 28 October bar; because the 50-day average is rising by about ₹0.08 a day, we refresh the zone from the ledger at each review. The stop of ₹45.14 is the swing low minus half an ATR (₹0.97), which is the tactical rule for a stock in a pullback. It sits ₹0.88 above the 63-day low of ₹44.26 and ₹1.54 above the 200-day average of ₹43.60.[1][2] For comparison, the core-book rule (minimum of the swing low and the 50-day average, minus one ATR) would give ₹44.17; we use the tactical stop because the bucket is tactical.

The size is set by risk, not by conviction alone. The conviction tier is TIER_3. It carries a 50 basis point risk budget and a range of 2.0% to 3.5% of net worth, and we chose it because return on equity is under 15%, the chief executive is new and the stock is up about 82% in about a year, even though the Layer 1 red lines and Layer 2 results pass.[2] The stop is 5.9% below the top of the zone, so the risk-budget formula gives 8.5% of net worth, and the tier cap cuts that to 3.5%. A split order helps: half at ₹47.96 and half at ₹46.11 gives a blended entry near ₹47.04, a stop distance of 4.0% and reward-to-risk of about 7.9 on the base value. The planned loss at the stop depends on which orders fill. If the whole 3.5% were bought at ₹47.96, the loss at the stop would be 3.5% times 5.9%, about 0.21% of net worth; this is the single-order reference case that the table below uses. If only the top half fills (1.75% of net worth), the loss at the stop is about 0.10%. If only the lower half fills (1.75% at ₹46.11, a 2.1% stop distance), it is about 0.04%. If both halves fill (3.5% at a blended ₹47.04, a 4.0% stop distance), it is about 0.14%, which is the planned maximum under the half-and-half plan. All are well inside the 0.50% budget. That assumes orderly trading: a gap from ₹47.96 straight to the ₹39 bear value would lose 18.7% of the position, or about 0.65% of net worth at a 3.5% weight, which is above the budget. A close-based stop does not remove that risk, and we accept it by keeping the size at or below the cap. If a hard gap-loss cap of 0.50% were wanted, the weight for this illustration would be about 2.7% (0.50% divided by 18.7%); we do not adopt that cap, and the 0.50% budget governs planned, orderly stop execution only.

The let-winners-run rule applies after entry. We do not sell because the RSI passes 70 or because the weight passes 15%. We trail the stop to the rising 21-day average or 50-day average while the earnings trend and estimate revisions hold, and the appreciated weight may run to 22%.[2] We review any position at each quarterly result against the Layer 2 scorecard. A second KPI moving to FAIL would change the verdict from hold to review, and so would gross bad loans above 2.0% or a margin below 3.20%.
