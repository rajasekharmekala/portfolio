1. What the company does

360 ONE WAM Limited, listed on the NSE as 360ONE, was founded in 2008 as IIFL Wealth and is now one of India's largest independent wealth and asset managers[32]. At 30 September 2026 it managed Rs 8,99,481 crore of client assets (about Rs 9 lakh crore). Of that, Rs 3,53,382 crore sits in assets that pay it a yearly fee, which the company calls ARR assets, short for annual recurring revenue assets. The other Rs 5,46,099 crore is held in transactional, brokerage or syndicated products, where it earns when clients trade or buy[1]. Think of the firm as a private bank for rich families: it advises on how to invest, runs funds for them, lends against their shares, and arranges deals.

Clients pay in two ways. The first is a recurring fee, a small percentage of the assets each year. The company reports this fee as retention, measured in basis points, where 100 basis points is 1%. In the September 2026 quarter the blended retention was 79 basis points, but only 67 basis points once the lumpy profit-share income called carry is removed[2]. Carry is a share of a fund's profit that the manager keeps when the fund exits investments above an agreed return. The second way is transactional: brokerage on trades and deals, which brought in Rs 190 crore in Q2 FY27, down 9.1% from a year earlier[1]. In Q2 FY27, 75% of revenue from operations was recurring fees, 4% was carry and 21% was transactional income[1].

The company also lends. Its non-bank lender, 360 ONE Prime, had a loan book of about Rs 13,000 crore across 1,000-plus clients in June 2026, up 67.8% in a year[3]. It stood at Rs 10,628 crore in December 2025[7]. Rating agency CARE says 94% of the book is loans against securities and 6% is loans against property[17]. Because borrowings fund this book and the investments the firm holds before selling them on to clients, the usual debt-to-equity ratio is not a useful test here: for a lender, interest is the cost of making the product, not a sign of distress. We therefore judge leverage by the ratio of borrowings to tangible net worth, by capital adequacy and by bad loans, and we explain the substitute tests in Chapter 3.

Two features shape every number in this report. The first is concentration: clients with more than Rs 10 crore each number just over 4,100 and hold about 95% of the wealth assets, so the firm depends on a few thousand families[2]. The second is cycle: asset-based fees rise and fall with markets, deal income depends on IPOs and block trades, and the company's own investments can swing profit by tens of crores a quarter. In Q2 FY27, other income was minus Rs 44 crore because of market-linked losses, against plus Rs 47 crore in Q1[1][3].

Unit economics: what a rupee of client assets earns

The best way to see the economics is the yield on each type of asset. In FY26, assets in the advisory and portfolio product called 360 ONE Plus earned 0.30% a year, assets in distribution that pay trail fees earned 0.65%, alternate funds earned 1.19% and mutual funds earned 0.46%[5]. Lending income came to 5.54% of the average loan book, which is much higher because a loan puts the firm's own capital to work[5]. Management told investors that new large advisory mandates earn about 35 to 40 basis points, and the very largest earn 20 to 25 basis points[2]. That matters: as the mix moves toward big advisory accounts, the blended yield will drift down unless carry, lending and alternates fill the gap.

On costs, the company now prefers the ratio of cost to operating income, which was 51.5% in Q2 FY27 against 54.3% in Q1[2]. Operating profit before tax was Rs 434 crore on revenue from operations of Rs 896 crore, which is an operating margin of 48.4%, up from 45.7% in Q1 and 47.6% a year earlier[1][31]. Employee cost is the big line: Rs 342 crore in Q2, up 19.9% in a year, including about Rs 9 crore of one-off restructuring[2]. People are the product in this business, so employee cost rises with growth and is the main lever the firm has to pull for better returns.

Customer concentration and cyclicality

Concentration shows up in three places. Clients with more than Rs 10 crore each hold about 95% of wealth assets (ex-custody)[2]. Wealth revenue from the UHNI segment was Rs 2,018 crore of Rs 2,284 crore in FY26, or 88%[5][31]. And in the loan book, the twenty largest loans are about 44% of the book, which is about 148% of tangible net worth on CARE's count at June 2026[17]. A few large clients leaving, or a few large loans going bad, would move the results more than they would at a bank with millions of customers.

Cyclicality comes from three sources. Markets move the value of fee-earning assets, so a 10% fall in equity markets lowers recurring revenue after a lag. Deal income needs IPOs, block trades and mergers. Management says the transactional line is now mostly brokerage and expects it to grow in the high single digits to low double digits, slower than recurring revenue[2]. Finally, the firm's own investments, held to meet fund commitments and to sell on to clients, are marked to market each quarter. About Rs 110 to 120 crore of the Q2 FY27 mark-down came from the listing of NSE, which the firm's funds held[2]. The next chapter asks whether the franchise is strong enough to ride through those swings.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • AUM: assets under management, the total client money the firm looks after or advises on.
  • ARR assets: client assets that pay a yearly fee; the rest of the assets pay only when clients trade or buy.
  • Retention (basis points): yearly revenue as a share of average assets; 79 basis points is 0.79%.
  • Carry: a fund manager's share of fund profit above a hurdle, paid when investments are sold; it is lumpy.
  • TBR: the company's label for transactional, brokerage and syndicated income, which is not a yearly fee.
  • NBFC: a non-bank finance company regulated by the Reserve Bank of India; 360 ONE Prime is one[27].
  • Tangible net worth: shareholders' funds after taking out goodwill and other intangible assets.
  • Gearing: borrowings divided by tangible net worth; it replaces debt-to-equity for this lender.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position is strong on franchise and weaker on recent returns. Management describes the moat as three things sitting in one room: people, product and platform[2]. The evidence for stickiness is in the client data. About 81% of wealth assets come from clients who have been with the firm for five years or more[5]. Average assets per family rise with the age of the relationship, from Rs 21 crore for families aged 0 to 3 years to Rs 25 crore at 3 to 5 years and Rs 68 crore beyond five years, about three times the youngest group[5][2]. Client attrition was 1.9% in FY26 with 0.8% of assets lost, and management says attrition in the September 2026 quarter was below half a percent of clients and below a quarter percent of assets[5][2].

The franchise is also growing its client list. Clients with more than Rs 10 crore rose from 1,825 in FY21 to 3,777 in FY26 and to over 4,100 by September 2026[5][2]. The company counts about 4,200 newer families today against 1,800 to 1,900 five years ago, and says the mix of net flows in Q2 FY27 was 50% from deeper wallet share and 50% from new mandates, against a history of 65% to 70% new[2]. Net new assets are the cleanest sign of a growing moat. Fee-earning assets grew 23.2% to Rs 2,46,828 crore in FY25 and 26.4% to Rs 3,11,940 crore in FY26[13][5]. In FY26, flows into fee-earning assets excluding the UBS India business transfer were Rs 35,199 crore, or 14% of opening assets[5].

Scale in alternates is a second strength. The group's alternative funds hold Rs 58,139 crore, up 27.9% in a year, and the firm calls itself the leader in private credit among performing-credit managers[2][5]. Its largest older fund, P1A, raised about Rs 1,000 crore, has already returned about Rs 1,500 crore and is now worth about Rs 3,000 to 3,500 crore, with two holdings, NSE and CIBIL[2]. That fund shows what the model can earn, and also how redemptions can hit assets: management expects it to be returned over 6 to 18 months[2].

The weaker side has four parts. First, returns have fallen. Return on tangible equity was 30.1% in FY24, 24.3% in FY25, 19.3% in FY26 and 16.9% in Q2 FY27[5][1]. Part of the fall is arithmetic, because the firm raised Rs 2,472.48 crore from share issues in FY25 and a further Rs 1,583.85 crore arrived from UBS in October 2026[15][24]. Second, costs have risen faster than revenue: the cost-to-income ratio was 49.9% in FY26 against 45.9% in FY25[5]. Third, a large share of statutory income comes from investment gains. Net gains on fair value changes were Rs 958.88 crore in FY26, about 21% of total income of Rs 4,477.26 crore[15][31]. Fourth, the franchise rests on relationship managers. The firm had 92 team leaders and 163 relationship managers at March 2026, and CARE lists attrition at employee and client level as a key rating risk[5][17].

Competition, regulation and governance

Management does not expect new digital and private-equity-backed rivals to cut margins sharply. It says the big reset in industry margins happened in 2021 and 2022 and that rivals will differ on people, product and platform, not on price[2]. We treat that as informed but self-interested. Newer players win on technology and talent offers, and the call itself describes a constant war for talent, which shows up as employee cost growth of 19.9% in Q2[2].

Regulation is a standing risk. CARE names regulatory action in wealth management and changes in commissions or margin rules as risks, and a fall in assets after client or employee attrition as a rating trigger[17]. Both ICRA and CARE reaffirmed the A1+ rating on its commercial paper[29][17]. The Reserve Bank of India regulates 360 ONE Prime under its scale-based framework for non-bank lenders[27], and SEBI is adding new product classes such as the specialised investment fund, which 360 ONE has already used with its Dyna-SIF[28][2]. The company reported small penalties in FY26: Rs 25 lakh from MCX Clearing Corporation, Rs 1 lakh from NSE, and a Rs 14.625 lakh settlement with SEBI after one of its private equity funds breached an AIF rule, which the notice recorded as settled in full[15].

The largest governance item is tax. The Income Tax Department searched the company and a subsidiary in the March 2025 quarter. On 21 April 2026 it issued an order with a demand of Rs 336.14 crore, which the company has disclosed as a contingent liability and says it will appeal; the auditor drew attention to the matter without qualifying its opinion[15][6]. The sum is about 28% of FY26 profit, so it is material if lost, and it is why we treat governance as a watch item even though no pledge or fraud finding exists[31]. 2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A wealth manager with a loan book is judged by five numbers, not by the usual factory metrics. We chose them because each one governs a different way the business can go wrong: the flow of new assets, the yield on those assets, the cost of running the franchise, the loss of clients, and the safety of the loan book and its funding. Each is scored PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). The scorecard qualifies at 3.0 out of 5.0 or more with no more than one FAIL, and it fails with two FAILs or a lower score[30].

The scorecard is followed by a check of what management promised one to two years ago against what it delivered. A franchise can have a good score today and still deserve less trust if its leaders often miss their own targets, so the second table matters as much as the first.

3. The numbers, in plain words

The numbers for a wealth firm with a lender inside it need a careful reading. Statutory total income includes interest from loans, fees, and gains on the firm's own investments. Finance cost is the price of the money it borrows. The company's own presentation nets some of these lines, so its 'total revenue' of Rs 3,144 crore in FY26 differs from the statutory total income of Rs 4,477.26 crore[5][15]. We use the statutory and Screener figures for the five-year trend and the company figures for the latest quarters, and we say which is which.

Over five years the direction is clearly up. Statutory income grew from Rs 2,072 crore in FY22 to Rs 4,470 crore in FY26 on Screener's count (the audited FY26 total income is Rs 4,477.26 crore; Screener shows Rs 7 crore of other income on a separate line, which closes the gap), and profit grew from Rs 578 crore to Rs 1,216 crore; the three-year growth rates are 30% for sales and 23% for profit[32]. Growth has slowed lately. Profit after tax was Rs 302 crore in Q2 FY27 against Rs 316 crore a year earlier, down 4.4%, while operating profit before tax grew 19.6% to Rs 434 crore[1][31]. The gap is other income, which swung Rs 95 crore, from plus Rs 51 crore to minus Rs 44 crore. Operating profit before tax rose Rs 71 crore, other income fell Rs 95 crore, and profit before tax fell Rs 22 crore, from Rs 413 crore to Rs 391 crore. So the core business grew and the investments dragged[1][31].

The balance sheet grew faster than equity. Borrowings rose from Rs 5,808 crore in FY22 to Rs 15,931 crore in FY26 and Rs 17,341 crore in September 2026, while equity rose from Rs 3,024 crore to Rs 9,836 crore and Rs 10,361 crore[32]. Borrowing funds three things: the loan book, investments held for clients, and acquisitions such as B&K Securities, which cost Rs 1,032.70 crore net of cash in FY26[15]. Because a lender's profit is made by borrowing and lending, we show borrowings next to equity but do not apply a debt-to-equity limit; we use gearing, the lender's capital ratio, bad loans and interest cover instead.

Cash flow needs the same care. The statement of cash flow from operations treats every rupee lent to a client as an operating outflow, so a lender that grows its loan book shows negative operating cash even when profit is high. We show the arithmetic anyway. Over FY24 to FY26 audited operating cash was minus Rs 5,853.34 crore against profit of Rs 3,035.68 crore, a ratio of -1.93. We explain below why that number is not a fair test for this firm. We then show what a fairer test gives, and why even the fairer test is weak[15][16][31]. For the same reason, free cash flow (FCF), which is cash from operations less money spent on equipment and software, is not a useful measure for this firm.

The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, with no order today. The rule that decides it is the technical routing rule. The plan has three steps. Step one is a ledger re-run after any close. Step two is a full gate test: no order is placed until a fresh ledger shows a passing momentum gate, meaning Test C, A or B. The entry zone, the stop and the target are rebuilt on that fresh ledger, and an order is allowed only if reward-to-risk at the top of the new entry zone is at least 2.0 to 1.
