1. What the company does

Sambhv Steel Tubes turns iron ore and coal into steel pipes. It does the whole job at one site near Raipur in Chhattisgarh. The ore and coal come from Chhattisgarh, within 200 to 250 kilometres of the plant. The company makes sponge iron (iron ore with the oxygen taken out), melts it into steel slabs, rolls the slabs into hot-rolled (HR) coil, and then forms the coil into pipes[2]. Management calls itself the only integrated maker of this kind in India[2]. The rating agency Crisil lists the upstream capacity as 2.8 lakh tonnes a year of sponge iron, 3.6 lakh of slabs and 4.5 lakh of HR coil, behind 3.5 lakh tonnes of ERW pipe capacity and 1.16 lakh of stainless coil[11]. A lakh is 100,000. In plain terms, most of the steel in a Sambhv pipe was made by Sambhv, so the company earns a margin at each step instead of paying one to a coil supplier.

Who pays the company? Mostly builders, fabricators and pipe makers, through traders. In July 2025 management said the network had 43 distributors and more than 700 dealers[6]. The black pipes and hollow sections go into construction frames, water lines, farm equipment and general engineering. The galvanised (zinc-coated) coils and pipes go to roofing, solar mounting and plumbing. Stainless steel is different. The company sells stainless coils, not pipes, and 28 small pipe makers now sell pipes under the Sambhv brand next to their own[2]. That model puts the company's stainless coil in front of 800 to 900 small regional pipe plants that it could not reach directly[2].

The money mechanics are simple. Revenue is tonnes sold times price per tonne. Profit depends on the gap between the price of the finished product and the cost of ore, coal, power and labour. The company's yardstick for that gap is EBITDA per tonne. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, which is profit before financing costs, tax and the wear on equipment. It was ₹5,321 a tonne in FY25 and ₹6,964 in FY26[7]. It was ₹9,355 in the June 2026 quarter[1]. Stainless coil earns roughly twice the company average per tonne: management quotes ₹15,000 to ₹16,000 a tonne for stainless against ₹7,500 to ₹8,500 for the whole company[2]. That is why the mix matters more than the volume. The table below shows what the company sold and what each product fetched.

Where the plants are and what is being built

Today there are two plants, at Sarora (also called Tilda) and Kuthrel, both near Raipur. Installed capacity is 3.5 lakh tonnes of ERW pipe, 1.16 lakh tonnes of galvanised coil and 1.16 lakh tonnes of stainless coil[17]. The stainless coil capacity doubled from 58,000 to 1,16,000 tonnes after a cold-rolling expansion at Kuthrel, and the Consent to Operate came in the June 2026 quarter[1]. The big build is at Kesda, a new site. Phase 1 adds 3.6 lakh tonnes of stainless capacity with a 25 MW power plant. The company puts the cost at ₹810 crore plus ₹125 crore for power, about ₹935 crore in all (management rounds this to ₹930 crore), and the first trial runs are targeted for the March 2027 quarter[1][11]. Smaller projects add 1.5 lakh tonnes of ERW capacity by a method called direct forming, a 30 MW power plant at Sarora and 8 MW of rooftop solar[1]. The stated aim is more than 2 million tonnes of finished capacity by 2030, against 0.68 million today[1].

Who owns the company and how it came to market

The promoter group, led by the Goyal family, owns 56.11% of the shares, and none of them are pledged to lenders[14][9]. Suresh Kumar Goyal is chairman and Vikas Kumar Goyal is managing director and CEO[2]. The company sold shares to the public between 25 and 27 June 2025 and raised ₹440 crore, of which ₹390 crore was set aside to repay borrowings[16]. The shares listed on 2 July 2025[6]. The repayment was done: the debt-to-equity ratio fell from 1.08 to 0.36 in the year to March 2026[7]. That ratio is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, so 0.36 means 36 paise of debt for each rupee of equity. The ₹390 crore is the reason a company that looked stretched in March 2025 looks safe in March 2026. The next chapters ask whether it stays that way once the Kesda bill arrives.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ERW pipe: electric resistance welded pipe, made by rolling steel strip into a tube and welding the seam. It is the cheapest common way to make steel tubes.
  • Hollow section: a square or rectangular steel tube used as a beam or column in building frames.
  • HR coil and CR coil: hot-rolled coil is steel rolled while hot. Cold-rolled coil is hot-rolled coil rolled again at room temperature to make it thinner and smoother.
  • GP: pre-galvanised, meaning coated with zinc before it is formed into pipe.
  • Lakh and crore: Indian counting units. One lakh is 100,000. One crore is 10 million, or 100 lakh.
  • QCO: quality control order, a rule that bars imports of steel without Indian quality marks. It has been relaxed for stainless steel, which lets more imports in.
  • Warrant: a right to buy a new share at a fixed price later. The buyer pays part now and the rest when the right is used.
  • Safeguard duty: an extra import tax on some flat steel products, set at 11.5% in October 2026 under a declining three-year schedule.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Is this a strong business? It is a good one with a thin moat, and the honest answer depends on which half of it you look at. The strong half is cost. Because Sambhv makes its own steel from ore found close to its plant, it can sell black pipe at the same price as the big brands, or 1% to 2% below, and still earn a margin[2]. A pipe maker that buys coil has to pay the coil maker's profit. Sambhv does not. The proof is in the earnings per tonne. EBITDA per tonne rose from ₹5,321 to ₹6,964 between FY25 and FY26, and it reached ₹9,355 in the June 2026 quarter[7][1]. Value-added products, which are pipes, galvanised goods and stainless coil, grew from 80.05% of revenue in FY24 to 92.66% in FY26[7].

The weak half is price. Steel is a commodity, and the company is a price taker for the coil that sets its pipe prices. The proof is the fall in EBITDA per tonne from about ₹6,100 in the September 2025 quarter to about ₹5,200 in the December quarter, when hot-rolled coil prices fell 6% and stainless imports were allowed in for three months[4]. Crisil puts the December figure at ₹5,245[11]. Two quarters later the figure was ₹9,355. A number that swings from 5,245 to 9,355 in two quarters is telling you that the cycle, not the company alone, sets it. Management says as much: it expects pipe prices to ease another 3% to 5% this year and stainless margins to soften as imports return[2].

So the moat is a cost position, not a brand. Cost positions hold while raw material is cheap and protection lasts. The government's safeguard duty on flat steel imports is 11.5% in October 2026 and is part of a declining three-year measure[24]. Finished steel imports fell 32% in FY26 after the duty arrived, according to the rating agency ICRA[23]. Later data show imports up 29.5% in April to August 2026, and the Steel Ministry declined on 7 October 2026 to add new curbs[24]. Management told investors that it does not expect a sharp price drop when the duty ends, because Chinese output is also being cut[2]. That is a judgment about 2028, and nobody can verify it today.

The strengths, with the evidence

First, integration. The coil is made in-house for pipe up to 5 inches across, and bought-in coil covers the sizes above that. Management says pipes up to 7 inches are 80% of the industry's tonnage[2]. The new Kesda plant will make coil for the larger sizes too. Second, a power plant of 25 MW already runs on waste heat and by-product fuel[6], and the company switched its gas supply from LPG to piped natural gas in January 2026, which kept fuel supply and cost steady during the war-related disruption[2]. Third, stainless steel is a real niche. The company makes narrow-width cold-rolled coil, 330 mm wide, in thin gauges of 0.4 to 2 mm, and it has qualified for a government production-linked incentive that management says should return about 13% of the revenue from that line until 2030[2]. About 90% of its stainless output is the 200 series, which uses little nickel, so it is less exposed to nickel prices[2]. Fourth, quality approvals are widening. In the June 2026 quarter the company won product approvals from Engineers India Limited and Chennai Metro Rail, which open government and institutional sales[2].

The weaknesses, with the evidence

One site is a concentration risk. Between 1 and 15 May 2026 the company shut 15 MW of its 25 MW power plant, 300 tonnes a day of its 800 tonnes a day kiln capacity and 3 of its 9 furnaces for maintenance[20]. It said it held enough stock to cover the gap. That worked, but it shows how much sits in one place. Pipe is also a crowded trade. APL Apollo, a leading branded maker, earns a 31.8% return on capital and trades at 48.6 times earnings, so Sambhv competes with a much larger rival[30]. Hi-Tech Pipes, a smaller ERW maker, earned an operating margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, of 3.8% and a return on capital of 9.77%[31]. That is a guide to what plain ERW pipe earns, and it is why the integrated margin of 11% to 13% is worth protecting.

A third weakness is the stainless sales model. The company sells coil to 28 small pipe makers and plans to lift supply to them from about 1,200 tonnes to a target of 2,500 tonnes by the end of the year[2]. Small buyers carry more credit risk than large ones, and their demand depends on the price gap with imports. The Kesda plant will add 3.6 lakh tonnes of stainless capacity, which is more than three times today's 1.16 lakh[2]. Filling that capacity means finding an extra 20,000 to 22,000 tonnes a month of buyers, on management's figure, in a market it describes as unorganised and import-driven[2].

Ownership, governance and what lenders think

Governance looks clean on the records we could fetch. The auditor, S S Kothari Mehta and Co., gave an unmodified opinion on the FY26 accounts and on internal financial controls[7], and its limited review of the June 2026 quarter found nothing that caused it to doubt the numbers[22]. The only contingent liability is an income tax demand of ₹1.93 million, about ₹0.19 crore[7]. The IPO money was spent as the offer document said, with no deviation reported by the monitoring agency[16]. Four relatives of directors are paid under related-party approvals of ₹2 million to ₹4 million each, and the company buys fixed assets from promoter-group firms such as Ganpati Sponge Iron[18]. The amounts are small, but they are the kind of link a minority holder should watch. An assistant vice-president in the CEO's office and investor relations resigned in May 2026[21]. The number of shareholders fell from 1,30,812 to 69,951 in a year, which suggests many early retail holders sold[9].

Lenders are comfortable. Crisil assigned an A+ rating with a stable outlook in August 2026. CARE and Acuite rated the company A in 2025[11][12][13]. The Crisil letter names two risks: the cyclical price of steel and the large Kesda capex programme[11]. It also names a downgrade trigger that we will use later: net debt more than 3 times EBITDA[11]. Net debt is borrowings minus cash. CARE named a trigger of gearing above 1 time and a working-capital cycle above 60 days[12].

The industry the company sells into

ICRA expects steel demand to grow 9% to 10% in FY27, up from 7.6% in FY26, because government spending on roads, railways and other projects has risen about 25%[23]. Management quotes a pipe and tube market that grows from 12.3 million tonnes in 2024 to 18.5 million in 2029[6]. That is about 8.5% a year on our arithmetic, and it is a company-cited figure we could not trace to an independent source. ICRA also warns that margins for steelmakers will stay about flat in FY27 as coking coal and iron ore get dearer, and that industry debt will rise to about 3 times operating profit[23]. The demand backdrop is therefore supportive. The profit backdrop is stable at best. That split is why this report treats volume growth as likely and the margin as the open question.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Chapter 2 asked whether the business is strong. This chapter asks two narrower questions. Which few numbers run this particular business, and did management do what it said it would do? Generic ratios miss the point for a steel pipe maker. The business lives on the profit it makes per tonne, on the share of its output that is value-added, on how full the plants are, on how much cash the cycle ties up, and on whether a very large build finishes on time and on budget. We chose five measures for those reasons. A measure scores PASS if it clears a stated bar, WATCH if it is mixed, and FAIL if it breaks the bar. The house rule is that four of five must pass.

Each bar is written down before the reading, so the score cannot be bent to fit a view. The first three measures pass clearly. The last two are mixed, and they are the two that decide whether the Kesda build adds value or adds strain.

Here is how to read each measure. EBITDA per tonne is profit per tonne of steel sold. A rise means the company earns more per unit. A fall means steel prices have squeezed it. The value-added share is the part of revenue that comes from pipes, galvanised goods and stainless coil. It rises when the company sells fewer raw intermediate goods. Plant use is the share of capacity that is running. A pipe plant at 70% is normal. A plant above 90% has no room to grow without new equipment.

Working capital is the cash tied up between buying ore and being paid for pipe, which is stock and customer credit less what suppliers have lent. A short cycle is good. A short cycle built on supplier credit is less safe. The last measure asks a plain question. Will the new plant start on time, and can the company pay for it? We score that with the rating agency's view next to management's.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 tests the numbers against fixed hurdles, then asks how fast each number is moving. A hurdle checks the level. A trend checks the direction. A company can pass on level and still fail on direction, and the reverse. Two chart terms come into the table. Stage 2 is the rising phase of a stock, when the price sits above a rising 50-day and 200-day average. Stage 4 is the falling phase, when the price is below falling averages, and it is a red line for rejection. The other new term is asymmetry, which is the reward if we are right divided by the loss if we are wrong, and a reward-to-risk ratio is the same idea. The scoring file sets the floor at 3.5 to 1 for a tactical momentum name. We cannot anchor the target on a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, which is the share price divided by earnings per share, because the shares have traded for only about fifteen months. We anchor it on a forecast of earnings per share and a multiple taken from peers, as Chapter 5 shows.

5. What could happen next

Chapter 5 asks what the market already expects, and what we expect. We use EPS, which stands for earnings per share, meaning profit divided by the number of shares. The first tool is a reverse DCF. A normal discounted cash flow model starts with growth and finds a price. A reverse one starts with the price and finds the growth. At ₹172.47, with a 12% required return and a final value of 20 times earnings in year ten, the market is pricing in profit growth of 12.16% a year for ten years. The starting profit is the ledger's twelve-month figure of ₹167.4 crore, taken as market value divided by the trailing price-to-earnings ratio[25]. Compounded, that is about ₹528 crore of annual profit in ten years, 3.2 times today's. The ledger could not compute a free cash flow (FCF) yield. FCF is cash from operations less spending on plant, and the group's was minus ₹75 crore in FY26 and minus ₹100 crore in FY25[9]. A reverse DCF built on profit alone therefore flatters a company that is spending more than it earns in cash.

Is 12.2% a year realistic? Over four years the company grew profit 18.7% a year, from ₹72 crore to ₹143 crore[10]. Our own FY28 estimate is ₹227 crore, which is 26% a year from FY26. Management wants to triple the finished capacity to over 2 million tonnes by 2030[1]. If that is built and filled, ₹528 crore of profit by FY36 is plausible, and the implied growth is not demanding. The price is not asking for a miracle. That finding matters for the verdict, because it suggests the problem is the price of the entry more than a demanding growth assumption, as Chapter 7 shows. It is a limited finding. The reverse DCF is built on profit and not on cash, and the build will absorb cash, so it cannot show that the business itself is cheap.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 attacks our own estimates. Every number in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption, and each assumption has a weakest link. The red-team method is to build the strongest honest case against the conclusions. We do not edit the conclusions here. We show where they break.

Our conclusion is that the stock is rejected at ₹172.47 because the payoff is 0.64 to 1. The conclusion holds up under most errors in our estimates, because even the bull price of ₹291.20 gives only 2.5 to 1 against the stop. Fatal flaw 1: the steel spread collapses

The central case needs EBITDA per tonne of ₹7,500 to ₹8,000 in FY28. The December 2025 quarter showed ₹5,245 when hot-rolled coil fell 6% and stainless steel imports were let in for three months[11][4]. Several forces could repeat that. The safeguard duty is on a declining three-year path. The government has declined to add new curbs, and imports are up 29.5% in April to August 2026[24]. A trade case on hot-rolled steel from China, Japan and Russia is under way, but its outcome is unknown[24]. Management itself says the quality-control rule on stainless imports has been relaxed[2]. If EBITDA per tonne went to ₹6,000, FY28 EBITDA would be about ₹330 to ₹340 crore and the bear price of ₹90.25 would follow. We would see it in the Q2 FY27 and Q3 FY27 results, due in November 2026 and February 2027.

Fatal flaw 2: Kesda is late, over budget or hard to fill

Management targets trial runs in the March 2027 quarter. Crisil sees a start from the December 2027 quarter, three quarters later, and calls the project about 30% done in August 2026[2][11]. There are ₹612 crore of equipment orders still to pay for[7]. The plant adds 3.6 lakh tonnes of stainless coil to a base of 1.16 lakh, and the market is 70,000 to 80,000 tonnes a month of imports and unorganised supply that must be won over[2]. Management plans ₹1,000 to ₹1,150 crore of debt by March 2027, which is 0.78 to 0.90 times equity on our sum[2]. It also says the plant needs ₹200 crore of extra working capital in FY28 and ₹300 crore in FY29[2]. Those amounts come after March 2027, and we have not modelled how they are funded, so debt could rise further. Interest expense would also be on the profit and loss account from the day of start. We would see delay in the Q3 FY27 call in February 2027 and in the first-production announcement due by March 2027.

Fatal flaw 3: the supplier credit that funds growth reverses

Trade payables are ₹554 crore, 1.5 times reported borrowings, and they include bank acceptances that are in substance borrowing[7]. They rose ₹229 crore in FY26, which is more than the ₹212 crore of operating cash[7]. If lenders cut acceptance limits, or suppliers shorten terms, the company would need ₹100 to ₹200 crore of new cash. The rating agencies track this. CARE names a working-capital cycle above 60 days and gearing above 1 time as negative triggers[12]. Crisil names net debt above 3 times EBITDA[11]. Neither is close today, and bank limit use was about 40% over the 12 months to July 2026[11]. The flaw is that the buffer is being spent on equipment while the payables base keeps growing faster than sales. The next Crisil review and the annual report for FY27 would show it.
