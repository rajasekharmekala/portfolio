1. What the company does

Sambhv Steel Tubes Limited, listed as SAMBHV, turns iron ore and coal into steel pipes. It was incorporated in 2017 as Sambhv Sponge Power Private Limited and is based in Raipur, Chhattisgarh[11][1]. It runs two plants, at Sarora and at Kuthrel, with room for 350,000 tonnes a year of ERW pipes (electric resistance welded, made by rolling steel strip into a tube and welding the seam), 116,000 tonnes of pre-galvanised coils and 116,000 tonnes of stainless steel coils[14]. Upstream it has 280,000 tonnes of sponge iron, 360,000 tonnes of steel blooms and slabs and 450,000 tonnes of hot-rolled coil capacity, plus a 25 megawatt power plant[11][22].

The chain works like a bakery that also mills its own flour. Sponge iron is iron ore with the oxygen removed. It is melted into blooms and slabs, which are rolled hot into narrow coils, which are cut and welded into pipes or coated with zinc[4]. Making the early steps in-house lets the company earn a margin at each step and not pay a mill for it. The seed note called the integration 100 percent. Crisil, the rating agency, says the lines are “mostly integrated, backed by upstream capacities of 4.5 lakh TPA, 3.6 lakh TPA and 2.8 lakh TPA” for hot-rolled coil, blooms and slabs, and sponge iron[11]. Our reading, not Crisil's: those 450,000, 360,000 and 280,000 tonnes do not match one-to-one the 350,000 tonnes of pipe and the 116,000 tonnes each of galvanised and stainless coil, so integration cannot be 100 percent by capacity alone. We use Crisil's wording.

Who pays is a distributor network. The company sells mainly through more than 47 distributors in 15 states and one union territory, who in turn serve more than 1,000 dealers[1]. The pipes go on to builders, fabricators and infrastructure buyers[4]. Sales volume was 396,731 tonnes in the year to March 2026, up from 290,581 tonnes a year earlier, and 122,662 tonnes in the September 2026 quarter, up 24 percent on the same quarter a year before[14]. Revenue in FY26 was ₹2,413 crore, up 60 percent[1].

Money is made on the spread, which is the gap between what the company charges for finished pipe and what its steel and fuel cost. The company gives the spread as operating profit per tonne. That figure was ₹6,964 over FY26, ₹8,555 in the March 2026 quarter and ₹9,355 in the June 2026 quarter[5][4]. In the December 2025 quarter Crisil puts it at ₹5,245, after a sharp fall in hot-rolled coil prices[11]. A business whose margin moves that much in a year is a cyclical business, and chapter 3 and chapter 6 treat it that way.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization): the profit from running the plants before interest, tax and the yearly write-down of equipment. This report calls it operating profit when it can.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed): yearly operating profit after tax divided by the money tied up in the business. Above 15 percent is the repository's floor for a compounder.
  • EPS (earnings per share): profit after tax divided by the number of shares.
  • FCF is free cash flow: cash from operations minus the cash spent on plant. It is negative while a company builds.
  • RSI (relative strength index): a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has risen or fallen over 14 sessions; 30 is called oversold and 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range): the average distance a stock travels in one session over 14 sessions. Here it is ₹7.82, about 5 percent of the price.
  • Stage 2 is the markup phase, when the price is above a rising 50-day average that is above the 200-day average; Stage 4 is the markdown phase, when the price is below a falling 50-day average that is below the 200-day average.
  • The 20-day moving average is the average close of the last 20 sessions; the 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the same idea over 50 and 200 sessions. They are the usual lines traders use to judge trend.
  • A base is a stretch where a price moves sideways before its next move. A pivot is the level that ends a base, and a breakout is a close above it.
  • The entry zone is the price range in which we would buy. Asymmetry is the same idea as reward-to-risk: the gain to the target compared with the loss to the stop.
  • Operating margin is operating profit divided by sales, and net margin is profit after tax divided by sales.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' equity. Net debt is borrowings less cash and bank deposits.
  • The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by profit per share over twelve months. Guidance is the forecast a company gives its investors. Dilution is the fall in each owner's share when a company issues new shares.
  • Working capital: the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company owes suppliers. Limit order: an instruction to buy only at a stated price or lower. Retest — a pullback that tests prior support —: a return of the price to a level it has already bounced from.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

This chapter asks why the business earns what it earns and what could take it away. Sambhv has a real cost position and a better product mix than a year ago, and both depend on a steel spread whose operating profit per tonne rose about 78 percent in two quarters. The sections below cover the integration, the margin, the customers and the weak spots.

The integration claim, tested against the filings

The integration is real but not complete. Crisil lists the upstream capacities and calls the pipe and stainless lines mostly integrated, and it adds that the plants sit in Chhattisgarh near iron ore and coal[11]. On the August call management put iron ore and coal within 200 to 250 kilometres of the plants[6]. In FY26 the company still bought ₹16.34 crore of goods for resale, so a small part of what it sells is traded and not made[1].

The proof of an integration benefit would be a better margin than comparable pipe makers that buy their coil. The two public comparisons support it. Hi-Tech Pipes reported revenue up 79 percent to ₹1,413 crore in the June 2026 quarter but profit after tax of only ₹20.04 crore, about 1.4 percent of sales[31]. Sambhv made ₹56.6 crore on ₹732 crore, a 7.7 percent margin[3]. The caution is that the gap is partly a cycle effect: when coil prices fall, a company that makes its own coil carries stock bought dear, and when they rise it carries stock bought cheap. The March 2026 and June 2026 quarters were the second kind[7]. What this means for an owner: the cost advantage is real, and the recent margin is flattered by timing. We do not treat ₹9,355 a tonne as normal.

Pricing power and what moves the margin

Pipe prices follow hot-rolled coil prices, and management says it has no way to split margin by product because every plant feeds the next[7]. It also says it gets the same price as the big brands or 1 to 2 percent less for black pipe, which is the price of a smaller brand[6]. APL Apollo, the largest listed peer, raised its gross profit per tonne by ₹1,000 from the previous quarter by pricing ₹100 to ₹200 a tonne above steel increases, which shows what a brand can do and what Sambhv has not shown[30].

The margin record is the clearest evidence. Operating profit was ₹727 million in the June 2025 quarter, then ₹603 million, ₹511 million, ₹922 million and ₹952 million in the four quarters since[4]. Per tonne, the last four quarters were about ₹6,100 for September 2025 (our arithmetic: ₹603 million over the 98,768 tonnes sold), ₹5,245 for December 2025 (Crisil), and ₹8,555 for March 2026 and ₹9,355 for June 2026 on the company's own basis; ₹952 million over the 107,771 tonnes sold in June would be about ₹8,834[4][11][5][14]. Management's own guidance is ₹7,500 to ₹8,500 a tonne for the September quarter and for FY27 as a whole, and it expects MS pipe prices to settle another 3 to 5 percent lower this year[6]. So even management expects margin to slip from the June level. Stainless coil is the part with the better margin, ₹15,000 to ₹16,000 a tonne against a company average near ₹7,000, and 90 percent of that output is the 200 series, which uses little nickel[6].

Customers, channel and what can go wrong

The company does not publish a customer concentration figure. Its sales go through distributors and, for stainless coil, to pipe makers that sell under a shared brand: 28 partners at August 2026, up from 10 a year earlier, taking about 1,200 tonnes against a target of 2,500 tonnes by the end of the year[6]. That channel is new and unproven at scale. Stainless coil prices depend on import rules and shipping costs, and management said war-related shipping delays were helping prices[6].

Three weak spots deserve a name. First, the margin cycle described above. Second, the large build programme: capital commitments not yet provided for were ₹612 crore at March 2026 against equity of ₹1,055 crore[1]. Third, the power plant maintenance shutdown from 1 to 15 May 2026 took 15 of the 25 megawatts of power, 3 of the 9 furnaces and 300 of 800 tonnes a day of kiln capacity offline, which shows how much output depends on a few units[22]. None of these is a red line. Each one reappears in the scorecard and in chapter 6.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report rests on assumptions, and the weakest assumption in each one is named below with the evidence that would show it wrong and the date it will be known. The ratio of reward to risk is most sensitive to three of them: the spread, the multiple and the plant. A paper ratio of 2.73 at the zone top shrinks fast when any of them moves, as the table after the sections shows.

Weakest assumption of the central earnings: that the spread holds near ₹7,000 a tonne

Our base case uses ₹7,000 of operating profit a tonne for the legacy business in FY28. The last five quarters were about ₹7,843, ₹6,100, ₹5,245, ₹8,555 and ₹9,355, so ₹7,000 is below the average of the latest four[10][4][11][5]. The spread depends on hot-rolled coil prices, which Crisil says fell sharply in the December 2025 quarter[11]. Management itself expects MS pipe prices 3 to 5 percent lower this year[6]. What would show it wrong: operating profit per tonne below ₹7,000 for two quarters in a row. It is known with the September results and again with the December quarter, due around late January or early February 2027 (not announced; the December 2025 quarter was approved on 31 January 2026[36]). If the legacy spread is ₹6,000 a tonne, ₹1,000 lower, the base value falls to ₹149.51, 2.9 percent below the zone top, and the ratio is -0.53.

Weakest assumption of the margin: that stock timing gains do not reverse

Management said the March 2026 margin was helped because stock bought at the bottom of the price cycle was used up after prices rose, and it expected raw material prices to fall in the rainy season[7]. A company that holds a month or two of stock earns more when prices rise and less when they fall. The June 2026 per-tonne profit of ₹9,355 is above the ₹7,500 to ₹8,500 that management guides for the next quarter and the year, which is an admission that part of it will not repeat[6]. The evidence that would show it wrong is a September-quarter operating profit under ₹860 million, which is ₹7,000 a tonne on the 122,662 tonnes sold in the quarter (our arithmetic; the company's own per-tonne basis uses a different tonnage, so its figure for the same profit would differ by a few percent). If margin falls to ₹7,000 and stays, FY27 profit per share is about ₹6.4 and the price asks for more growth than the ₹7.39 base supports.

Weakest assumption of the multiple: that 24 times holds in a weak market

The base multiple of 24 is our judgment: about 10 percent below our own median of 26.6 times since November 2025, and well above the 17.1 and 17.2 times of Jindal Stainless and Hi-Tech Pipes[34][33]. The multiple ranged from 70.9 times in July 2025, on thin profits, to a low of 20.7 times on 7 August 2026. The Nifty 50 is in a downtrend with only 10.2 percent of its stocks above their 50-day average, and the stock is a leader against it. A multiple of 22 gives a value of ₹162.52 and a ratio of 1.00 at the zone top; 20 gives ₹147.74 and a ratio of -0.73. The ratio falls to the 2.5 floor at 23.74 times. What would show it wrong: a close under the 50-day average of ₹142.94 with the Nifty still falling, or a price-to-earnings ratio under 22 on trailing profit while profit rises.
