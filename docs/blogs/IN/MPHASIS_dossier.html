Mphasis is an Indian IT services company that builds and runs software for banks and insurers, and it earns about 85 percent of its revenue in the Americas.[5][4] Our verdict is REJECTED, because its owner, Blackstone, has pledged all of its 30.53 percent stake to lenders, and our rule rejects any pledge of 20 percent or more.[7] The business is sound, with a 22 percent return on capital and net cash, but our central value of ₹3,052 is only 16.6 percent above the ₹2,618.40 close, while the stop is 19.7 percent below.[10][48] We would be wrong if the pledge falls below 20 percent and the price drops near ₹2,374, or if the 5 November results beat our estimates.[41] Status: PUBLISHED_WITH_GATE_EXCEPTIONS.

1. What the company does

Mphasis writes and maintains software for large clients, moves old computer systems onto newer platforms, and runs some back-office work such as claims handling and customer support. In the year to March 2026 it earned revenue of ₹15,880 crore (₹158,796 million), and 84 percent of that came from the Americas.[5] Clients pay in three ways. In the June 2026 quarter, 46.5 percent of revenue was billed by the hour or day (time and materials), 44.3 percent was fixed price for a defined result, and 9.2 percent was charged per unit of output.[4] Fixed price work has grown from 30 percent of revenue in the June 2024 quarter, which means the company now carries more of the risk if a project runs over.[4]

Who pays? Mostly financial firms. Banking and financial services supplied 52.7 percent of revenue in the June 2026 quarter, insurance 14.9 percent, technology, media and telecom 18.2 percent, logistics and transport 4.2 percent, and other industries 10.0 percent.[4] Application services (building, modernising and running business software) were 75.5 percent of revenue, business process services 15.7 percent and infrastructure services 8.8 percent.[4] The largest client is 13 percent of trailing twelve-month revenue, the top five are 40 percent and the top ten are 56 percent.[4] About 98.6 percent of revenue now comes from clients that deal with Mphasis directly, rather than through a reseller.[18]

The business sells people's time, so staff numbers and use of that time drive profit. Mphasis had 32,097 employees at the end of June 2026, of whom 27,784 were billable, and 60.9 percent of revenue came from staff working at the client's site.[4] Utilisation, the share of available billable time that is actually billed, was 81 percent, down from a peak of 87.7 percent in the September 2025 quarter.[4] Revenue is earned in dollars but most costs are in rupees. The average rate was ₹94.82 per dollar in the June 2026 quarter against ₹85.39 a year earlier, which is why reported revenue rose 17.5 percent while dollar revenue rose 7.7 percent.[1][4] The company hedges about 80 percent of its exposure for the next four quarters, so a weaker rupee reaches profit slowly, and hedge losses took ₹83 crore off June-quarter revenue.[24][4]

Management's plan is to move from selling effort to selling outcomes on its own AI platforms. NeoIP is the engine for modernising old systems, and Mphasis Tria, launched at the analyst day on 27 May 2026, is a platform meant to turn company data into governed decisions.[46] Management says AI-led work is 70 percent of its deal pipeline and 63 percent of the June-quarter new contract value.[3][1] A broker note from the analyst day says the company aims to lift subscription and recurring revenue from a low single-digit share to 20 to 30 percent over the medium term.[26] None of that revenue is reported separately yet, so it is a plan and not a result.

The company also buys small businesses and customer contracts. In FY26 it paid ₹432 crore for acquisitions.[5] In 2026 it added Theory and Practice, a decision-software firm, for CAD 10 million upfront plus up to CAD 20 million later,[43] the outsourcing operations of OKIN for up to ₹52 crore,[45] and a customer contract from Red Oak Technologies for up to US$28 million.[44] For these customer deals management pays 1.0 to 1.2 times the contract's annual revenue and expects the acquired work to earn a margin close to the company average after the upfront cost is expensed.[3][47]

Who owns Mphasis

Blackstone, the private equity firm, bought 60.4 percent of Mphasis from Hewlett Packard Enterprise in September 2016.[15] Its holding company, BCP Topco IX Pte. Ltd., is the only promoter and held 5,82,99,642 shares, or 30.53 percent of the company, at 30 September 2026.[7] The promoter stake was 55.45 percent in March 2024, fell to 40.35 percent by June 2024 after an earlier sale, and fell again to 30.64 percent when Blackstone sold 1.80 crore shares (9.46 percent of the company) on 18 November 2025.[10][17] Mutual funds hold 31.12 percent, insurers 14.28 percent and foreign portfolio investors about 19.4 percent.[9]

The point for an investor is that the largest owner is a seller, not a buyer, and that since May 2026 every one of its shares is pledged to nine lenders. Chapter 3 treats that as a red line under the portfolio rules, and chapter 5 prices what it could mean.[7][14]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Total contract value (TCV): the full signed value of a new contract over its life. It is a forward indicator, not revenue.
  • Constant currency: growth with exchange-rate moves removed, so a weaker rupee does not flatter the number.
  • EBIT margin: operating profit after depreciation, as a share of revenue. Mphasis targets 14.75 to 15.75 percent.
  • Days sales outstanding (DSO): how many days of sales are owed by clients and not yet collected. Higher means slower cash.
  • Utilisation: the share of billable staff time that is actually billed to clients.
  • Promoter pledge: shares that the controlling owner has handed to lenders as security for a loan. If the loan goes wrong, lenders can sell the shares.
  • Reverse DCF: a calculation that starts from today's price and solves for the yearly growth in cash flow that price needs for ten years.
  • Basis point: one hundredth of a percentage point, so 60 basis points is 0.6 percent.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Mphasis is a mid-sized IT services company with a strong grip on US banking. ICRA, the rating agency, notes that its customers include the top 15 US banks, mortgage lenders and large insurers.[25] Direct revenue from banking and financial services has grown at a compound quarterly rate of more than 3.5 percent over eight quarters, which management calls the strongest among its peers.[3] That is a real strength, but it is narrow. It rests on a handful of long relationships, and the top ten clients supply 56 percent of revenue.[4]

The growth engine is new contract value. FY26 brought record net new TCV of US$2.12 billion, up 68 percent, and the June 2026 quarter added US$461 million, the fifth straight quarter above US$400 million.[18][3] The pipeline of deals under discussion rose 8 percent in the quarter and 28 percent in a year, and large deals now include one worth more than US$100 million.[3][2] The link from contract value to revenue is looser than before: the correlation (R-squared) fell to 0.75 from above 0.9, which management puts down to short, quick-start deals.[2][3] The test is whether those signings show up as revenue in the next three quarters.

Financial strength is a clear positive. ICRA reaffirmed [ICRA]AA+ (Stable) in February 2026, and reports a net cash position, total debt of 0.8 times operating profit and interest cover of 17.2 times.[25] Return on capital employed was 22.1 percent and return on equity 18.5 percent.[10] Mphasis paid out 64 percent of FY26 profit as dividends, ₹62 a share.[10][18] Few mid-sized IT firms combine that payout with net cash.

Four weaknesses matter. First, margins sit at the bottom of the guided band: EBIT margin was 14.8 percent against a band of 14.75 to 15.75 percent, and gross margin fell 140 basis points in one quarter.[1][2] Second, pricing power is limited. ICRA says competition restricts pricing flexibility,[25] and management says long-term run contracts are highly susceptible to disruption from AI productivity claims.[3] Third, cash conversion has weakened: cash from operations was ₹1,253 crore on profit of ₹1,863 crore in FY26, and days of sales outstanding rose from 84 to 95 in a year.[5][2] Fourth, tax disputes are large: the company contests ₹3,465 crore of direct tax and transfer pricing claims and has furnished ₹1,138 crore of bank guarantees, with no provision made.[5]

The sector around it: demand is slow and the market fears AI

Large Indian IT firms grew only 0.6 to 7.2 percent in constant currency in the September 2026 quarter. TCS grew 2.8 percent, Infosys 1.5 percent, HCLTech 2.6 percent, Wipro 0.6 percent, LTM 5.1 percent and Tech Mahindra 7.2 percent.[37] Infosys cut its FY27 growth forecast to 1 to 2 percent from 1.5 to 3 percent on 23 October 2026.[36] Against that, Mphasis grew 8.3 percent in constant currency in the June quarter and its guidance, which is the company's own published forecast, is high single-digit to low double-digit growth for FY27.[1][2] The two numbers are not like-for-like in timing (June against September quarters), but the gap is wide enough to count.

The market's worry is that AI tools shrink the hours that IT firms bill. Over two years the Nifty IT index fell 24 percent, Accenture fell 33 percent and chip stocks rose 110 percent, and Accenture alone fell 18 percent in one day in June 2026.[37] Five of the six largest Indian IT names trade below their own five-year median price-to-earnings multiple (the share price divided by earnings per share), yet the group is up 13 percent in a month.[37] Mphasis has risen with it: ₹2,465 on 13 October (a gain of 5.18 percent that day) and ₹2,618.40 on 27 October.[38][40] A rebound inside a sector under pressure can reverse quickly, and the next test for the group is its own result on 5 November.[41]

Ownership, tax disputes and what the moat is worth

The moat is moderate. Mphasis has deep bank relationships, domain data and fixed-price delivery skill, but the services it sells are bought on price and can be rebuilt by rivals, and it has not yet shown platform revenue in the accounts. Management itself concedes that clients will not return to the discretionary spending of three to five years ago.[3]

Two governance items need weighing. The first is the sponsor's pledge, covered in chapter 3. The second is the tax contingency: ₹3,465 crore of direct tax and transfer pricing claims (₹2,545 crore a year earlier) equals 32 percent of shareholders' equity, and ICRA lists it as a key monitorable.[5][25] The auditor, B S R & Co. LLP, signed the FY26 accounts on 29 April 2026.[5] We found no auditor qualification, but a loss on the tax cases would hit profit directly.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of numbers show a good business that stopped compounding quickly. Revenue rose from ₹11,961 crore in FY22 to ₹15,880 crore in FY26, but the path was uneven: growth of 23.0 percent in FY22 and 15.4 percent in FY23, a fall of 3.8 percent in FY24, then 7.2 percent and 11.6 percent.[10] The three-year compound growth rate is 4.8 percent for sales and 4.4 percent for profit, measured from the FY23 peak. The five-year rates are 10 percent and 9 percent.[10] In dollars, FY26 revenue grew 6.9 percent to US$1,796 million, which is why management's constant-currency figure was 6.7 percent.[4][18]

Profit followed revenue. The company's own EBIT margin has stayed near 15 percent for years, 15.3 percent in FY26 and 14.8 percent in the June 2026 quarter, while Screener's operating profit line (which sits before depreciation) shows 18 to 19 percent.[18][1][10] Profit after tax was ₹1,863 crore in FY26. Earnings per share (EPS) were ₹97.61 in FY26 on Screener's basis (the annual report shows basic ₹97.82) and ₹100.22 on the trailing twelve months, and finance costs rose from ₹161 crore in FY24 to ₹204 crore in FY26 as short-term borrowing grew.[10][6][5]

Cash is where the picture weakens. Cash from operations (the cash a business makes from running itself, before investing and financing) fell from ₹2,180 crore in FY24 to ₹1,905 crore in FY25 and ₹1,253 crore in FY26. The gap is mostly working capital, which is the cash tied up in unpaid client bills and prepaid costs. Receivables rose by ₹905 crore in FY26 and other assets by ₹800 crore, which management links in part to the cost of acquiring customer contracts, and tax paid was ₹700 crore.[5][3] Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after paying for equipment and software, and it was about ₹935 crore in FY26 after ₹319 crore of capital spending.[5] Even so, the three-year total, FY24 to FY26, is 1.04 times profit, because FY24 and FY25 were strong.[6][5]

The balance sheet is liquid and lightly geared. At June 2026 Mphasis held ₹3,359 crore of cash, bank balances and investments against borrowings of ₹1,970 crore, leaving net cash of about ₹1,389 crore before leases and ₹561 crore after ₹828 crore of lease liabilities.[4] Borrowings were ₹1,793 crore at March 2026 against ₹1,116 crore a year earlier, mostly short term, and goodwill from acquisitions stood at ₹4,768 crore, 27 percent of total assets.[5] Debt-to-equity, which is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, was 0.17 at March 2026, and ICRA calls the position net debt negative, which means cash and investments exceed borrowings.[5][25]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks three things. Does the company clear the portfolio's red lines? How do its levels compare with its trajectory? Which archetype fits? The red lines for Indian companies are four. The first is debt-to-equity of 1.20 or more, or interest cover below 2. The second is a promoter pledge of 20 percent or more of the promoter's holding, or a rising pledge into a falling stock. The third is three-year cash-to-profit below 0.25 (0.40 for mature companies) without a turnaround. The fourth is a literal Stage 4 downtrend, which is the confirmed downtrend phase of the Weinstein stage scale (Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend).[48] The asymmetry floor compares the possible gain with the possible loss, as (target minus entry) divided by (entry minus stop), a ratio also called reward-to-risk. The third archetype is for a breakout, a move above a flat price range on rising volume, in a stock already in a confirmed uptrend, and the portfolio calls it TACTICAL_BREAKOUT.

Mphasis clears every red-line test but one. The failing red line is the pledge. It also fails the asymmetry floor, which is a price test and not a red line. BCP Topco IX Pte. Ltd. pledged all of its 5,82,99,642 shares on 15 May 2026, which is 30.53 percent of the company. The pledge secures a term loan of up to US$550 million under a facility dated 12 May 2026. The September 2026 filing shows the pledge still in place.[7][14][13] The filing says the loan refinances a US$1.1 billion facility from 2021. The security for that older facility was an indirect pledge higher up the ownership chain. The company calls the new loan a routine refinancing of acquisition debt.[12][15] Nine lenders replaced 35. The pledge carries negative lien and non-disposal covenants, which stop the sponsor selling the shares without the lenders' consent.[14][13]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest one under each estimate. It says what evidence would show it wrong and when we will know. It also gives the strongest honest case against our own verdict, because a rejection that nobody has tried to break is only an opinion.

EPS estimates: the weakest assumption is that margin stays at 15.0 to 15.4 percent

We hold EBIT margin flat to slightly up. The earn-out cost continues, and utilisation, at 81 percent, has not yet turned. If utilisation recovers faster, margin could reach the top of the band. Each 0.5 point is worth about ₹2.7 of FY27 EPS. If the Insurance programme and the June-quarter gross margin (26.9 percent, down 140 basis points) persist, margin could slip under the 14.75 percent floor. FY27 EPS would then be ₹2 to ₹3 below ours.[1][3][4] The evidence arrives on 5 November 2026 with the September-quarter result. It arrives again with the December quarter. Mphasis reported that quarter on 22 January 2026, so we expect it in late January 2027, but the date is not announced.[41][24]

Central value: the weakest assumption is a multiple of 25 times

The multiple is a judgement, not a fact. Mphasis's own series ranges from 21.1 to 38.1 times over three years. The large peers are moving fast: the group gained about 13 percent in a month.[11][37] If the sector re-rates, 29.5 times is not unreasonable, and our central value would be ₹3,602. If AI-deflation fears return, a drop to 22 times would leave only 2.6 percent upside. At the series low of 21.1 times, unchanged FY28 EPS of ₹122.1 would be worth about ₹2,576, which is 1.6 percent below the close, so reaching our earnings estimate alone does not guarantee a gain. We will know in days, not quarters, because the multiple moves with the sector every session. The 5 November result is the company-specific test.[37][41]

The pledge reading: the strongest case against our rejection

The red-team case for buying runs as follows. The pledge refinances an older and larger loan. The company is net cash and rated AA+. The loan-to-value is about 34 percent. Institutions hold 65 percent of the shares, and their holding rose from 55.6 percent to 65.3 percent in a year. A holder of 1 to 3 percent of the portfolio, with a stop at ₹2,102.72, would risk about 0.2 to 0.6 percent of the portfolio. The stock leads its sector, with relative strength of 17.44 percent over 63 sessions. The price-to-earnings of 25.9 is 12 percent under its three-year median.[15][25][9][48][10][11]

Why do we still reject it? The portfolio rule is deliberately binary, and the pledge is five times the line. Even a benign pledge removes something a minority holder cannot replace. That is the sponsor's ability to sell in the open with no lender's veto. It also adds a tail risk that is hard to size, because the lenders' margin-call terms and the loan tenor are not published. The facts that argue for buying are already in the price, since the reverse DCF needs 10 to 17 percent growth a year. And with the pledge ignored, reward-to-risk is 0.84 at the close and 1.25 at the zone top, against a floor of 2.5.[48]

Probabilities: 20, 50 and 30 are our judgement

The weights are not measured from data. Large peers have mostly missed growth this year, so the bull case is hard. Mphasis has beaten its own revenue guidance, so the bear case is not the central case. Cash conversion is deteriorating, so the bear is more likely than the bull. A 10-point shift from bear to bull would lift the expected value to ₹3,125. A 10-point shift from bull to bear would lower it to ₹2,778. The verdict is the same either way.[37][18]
