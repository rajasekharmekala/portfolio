Authum Investment & Infrastructure (NSE: AIIL) is a Mumbai finance company that mostly invests its own ₹14,728 crore of net worth in listed Indian shares[13]. At ₹571.90 the stock is in an uptrend, but our verdict is REJECTED[51]. Four tests fail: return on capital is 13 percent against 15, cash conversion is 0.49 against 0.70, the gain-to-loss ratio is 1.27 against 3.5, and only one of five business measures passes[6][12][2]. Profit follows the stock market, and the shares cost 3.3 times book value against 0.2 to 1.7 times for peers[44][45][46][47]. We would be wrong if markets keep rising and the new loans stay clean, and the results due by 14 November 2026 will start to show that[40].

1. What the company does

Authum Investment & Infrastructure Limited is listed on the NSE as AIIL and on the BSE as 539177. It is a non-bank finance company, which means a lender registered with the Reserve Bank of India that does not take deposits from the public. The company was set up in 1982, was taken over by Mrs. Alpana Sanjay Dangi in FY20, and is run from Mumbai with more than 25 branches and more than 450 staff. The promoter group holds 68.81 percent of the shares and none of them are pledged to lenders[14][21].

The company does three jobs with money. First, it invests its own capital in listed and unlisted companies and in a few control positions. Second, it lends, mainly through a loan book it built after buying the loan assets of two failed Reliance-group lenders. Third, since June 2025 it owns 88.37 percent of an asset reconstruction company (ARC), a firm that buys bad loans from banks and tries to recover more than it paid[14][17]. The first job produces most of the profit. The other two are young and still small.

The investment job has no customers in the usual sense. The payer is the stock market. Of the ₹2,653.89 crore of total income in FY26, ₹1,586.49 crore (60 percent) came from gains on investments valued at current market prices, ₹551.95 crore (21 percent) from interest, ₹307.11 crore (12 percent) from other operating income that includes recoveries, ₹67.54 crore from selling pens made by a newly bought subsidiary, ₹45.42 crore from dividends, ₹35.29 crore from fees, ₹15.00 crore from rent and ₹45.08 crore from other income[1]. Interest and dividends are the steady part. Gains on shares are the swinging part.

The gains on investments are not all cash. The FY26 cash-flow statement removes ₹964.45 crore of unrealised price gains and ₹491.89 crore of gains from turning loans bought from other lenders into shares, and leaves ₹130.15 crore from actual sales[2]. The company's own slides show profit booked on exits of ₹155.7 crore in FY26, against ₹1,797.0 crore in FY25[14]. So in FY26 about 8 percent of the ₹1,929 crore profit was locked in by a sale, and the rest was a paper gain, a swap gain or a recovery.

The credit job started with a bargain. Authum bought Reliance Commercial Finance (now Open Elite Developers Limited) through a bank-led resolution that became effective on 14 October 2022, bought the financial assets of Reliance Home Finance on 29 March 2023, and in May 2024 the tribunal approved moving the lending business into Authum itself[32]. Most of that acquired book was already bad and was written down to zero, so recoveries flow straight to profit. The acquired book fell from ₹1,036.8 crore in March 2023 to ₹275.2 crore in March 2026, while the fresh loan book, which includes the ARC, grew to ₹3,928.1 crore[13].

How the business is funded

Three sources of money sit behind the assets. Net worth, the shareholders' stake, was ₹14,727.8 crore on 31 March 2026 and has barely moved in a year, from ₹14,689.3 crore[13]. Preference shares and loans from the promoter company Mentor Capital Limited came to ₹2,122.4 crore of preference shares and ₹540.5 crore of inter-corporate deposits, which are loans between companies and not public deposits. Third-party credit lines were only ₹494.8 crore[13]. The audited balance sheet shows borrowings of ₹1,116.41 crore, subordinated liabilities of ₹2,122.35 crore and debt securities of ₹63.80 crore[3]. The preference shares carry a 0.01 percent annual dividend and are redeemed after 15 years at a premium that gives the holder 6.5 percent a year. The inter-corporate deposits carry 10 percent a year[30][10].

This funding mix matters for two reasons. It costs common shareholders little in cash dividends while the promoter keeps supplying it, though the real cost is about 6.5 to 10 percent a year (chapter 2). It also ties the company to one related party for most of its borrowed money, a point we return to in chapters 2B and 6.

What else the company owns

Besides the main portfolio, Authum holds control stakes in Prataap Snacks (43.66 percent, about ₹790 crore invested) and Nitco (about 46.8 percent, acquired by converting debt into shares)[14]. It bought BIC Cello (India), now Primetek Pens, in November 2025[34]. In 2026 it committed about ₹350 crore to buy real-estate assets of Wind World (India) under an insolvency plan approved on 27 July 2026, and on 9 October 2026 it turned a ₹100 crore deposit into 25 percent of Vibhav Energy, a company that reported no turnover in FY26[23][24]. It also took over SpiceJet shares by enforcing a pledge in February 2026[22]. In July 2026 a tribunal approved its plan for Creatoz Builders, a real-estate company[27]. None of these is core, but together they show a company that acts on distressed and special situations, and that is where both its edge and its risk sit.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • NBFC: a non-bank finance company registered with the Reserve Bank of India. It lends and invests but does not take deposits from the public.
  • Net worth: what belongs to shareholders after all debts are subtracted. It is also called book value.
  • Fair value or mark-to-market: valuing an asset at today's market price instead of its purchase cost.
  • Other comprehensive income (OCI): price changes on shares that go straight into net worth without passing through the profit line. The company's FY26 loss of ₹1,985 crore here explains why net worth stood still while profit was ₹1,929 crore.
  • ARC: an asset reconstruction company that buys bad loans from banks and tries to recover more than it paid. It pays partly in security receipts, which are paper claims on the recoveries.
  • Preference shares (NCRPS): shares that rank ahead of ordinary shares for repayment but pay a fixed small dividend and are redeemed at a set date. Here they are held by the promoter company.
  • Sensitised net worth: the credit rating agency CRISIL's stress view of net worth, which cuts 30 percent of the investment book's value before counting it.
  • Flow investments and strategic investments: flow means stakes under 20 percent held for returns. Strategic means control stakes above 20 percent.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest fact about the business is its capital. Net worth grew from ₹3,416.4 crore in March 2023 to ₹14,727.8 crore in March 2026, a 4.3-fold rise, and borrowings are small relative to that figure: debt-equity was 0.23 times in the company's own measure[13][6]. CRISIL first rated the company A-/Stable in July 2024 and upgraded it to A/Stable in October 2025[33]. It also reported a capital adequacy ratio of 54.2 percent in March 2025, far above what a regulator demands[32]. The company also has a record of profitable exits: in FY25 it booked ₹1,797.0 crore of profit on sales, and in FY26 its best exit returned 14.8 times the money invested[14].

The weakest fact is that this capital is mostly invested in the same market that drives the share price. CRISIL measured 79 percent of the March 2025 balance sheet as investments, almost 90 percent of them listed shares, and said treasury gains have been the main contributor to profit and can be volatile[32]. The FY26 figures show what that means in practice. Profit after tax was ₹1,929.35 crore, but total comprehensive income, which adds the price changes that bypass the profit line, was a loss of ₹55.77 crore[1]. A holding company that earns its profit on the way up and gives it back through reserves on the way down has a net worth that behaves like a mutual fund, not like a compounding business.

Competitive position is therefore a question of skill, not of moat. The company describes its edge as market expertise, a research team and proprietary capital[14]. Those are real but they are people and judgment, and they do not stop a rival from buying the same shares. In the credit job the edge is different and more concrete: the group took over two distressed lenders through resolution processes and now owns collection teams, agencies and branches that most new lenders lack. That edge is real in recoveries and unproven in fresh lending, where the book is under two years old.

Customer concentration does not apply in the normal sense, but portfolio concentration does. The company says its listed holdings are about 60 percent of the investment portfolio at market value, strategic stakes about 19 percent, and the rest unlisted shares, mutual funds and similar items, with the largest single holding near 10 percent of the portfolio[14]. The note on financial instruments gives a harder view: of ₹15,472.61 crore of investments, ₹10,115.44 crore (65 percent) is listed and priced on the exchange, ₹2,387.75 crore (15 percent) is security receipts valued by model, and ₹2,152.89 crore (14 percent) is unlisted shares[5]. About one rupee in three of the book therefore has no daily market price. Data aggregators that track only holdings above the 1 percent disclosure line list 32 holdings in July 2026, with the top five making up 76 percent of that disclosed value, and 30 holdings worth ₹8,445 crore at the June 2026 close[42][43]. Those figures are partial, so we use them only as a pointer to concentration.

Cyclicality is the dominant fact. Quarterly profit was ₹1,940 crore in September 2023, ₹1,763 crore in March 2025, ₹161 crore in December 2025 and ₹58 crore in March 2026, then ₹1,108 crore in June 2026[44]. The equity holdings lost value through the second half of FY26: the fair-value line lost ₹1,345 crore in the September quarter, ₹637 crore in the December quarter and ₹1,514 crore in the March quarter, then gained ₹1,748 crore in the June 2026 quarter[20][19]. Anyone buying today buys a reading of the last quarter, which is at the high end of the range.

Where the company is strong

Strength one is flexible funding from a related party. The promoter's preference shares pay a cash dividend of 0.01 percent a year, but they are redeemed at a premium that gives the holder about 6.5 percent a year, so 6.5 percent is the real cost. The inter-corporate deposits cost 10 percent a year. Against that, interest income was ₹551.95 crore on an average loan book of about ₹3,264 crore, about 17 percent in our arithmetic, though that interest also includes interest on deposits and investments. So the funding is affordable if the loans perform. It lets the group fund new loans and deals without going to banks[30][10][1][13]. Strength two is the exit record, including the 14.80 times return on the best FY26 exit[14]. Strength three is the rating: CRISIL's upgrade to A/Stable rests on net worth, capital adequacy and a liquidity cushion of cash and bank balance of ₹604 crore against ₹220 crore of debt due in six months at 31 August 2025[32].

Where the company is weak

Weakness one is earnings that rest on price moves and recoveries. Weakness two is a thin stream of non-market income: our estimate in chapter 3 is about ₹480 crore a year after tax, a quarter of recent profit. Weakness three is history that the company has bought along with its assets. The SEBI order of 22 August 2024 fined Reliance Commercial Finance ₹25 crore and barred it from the securities market for five years. That order is under appeal with a hearing listed for 22 September 2026[31][19]. The Enforcement Directorate has opened an inquiry into transactions of the two acquired lenders from before the takeover, and the CBI has questioned whole-time director Mr. Amit Dangi and the advisor Mr. Sanjay Dangi in a ₹57.47 crore bank-fraud case that concerns Reliance Commercial Finance[19][37][38]. The company says the inquiry has no effect on operations. We cannot size the exposure from the filings, so we treat it as an open item.

Weakness four is the quality of the books in a few places. The auditors noted that properties with a gross carrying value of ₹127.48 crore are held in the name of promoter company Mentor Capital and ₹56.30 crore of land in the name of Mrs. Alpana Dangi[8]. The reported earnings per share in the annual report is ₹61.61 for FY26 because it appears not to be restated for the January 2026 bonus issue of 67,93,80,400 shares. On the enlarged share count it is ₹22.72[1][19][44]. The annual report gives return on capital employed as 13.10 percent on page 12 and 15.71 percent on page 173[6][7]. None of these items changes solvency, but they lower our confidence in the filings.

Why the market pays a premium anyway

The shares trade at 3.3 times March 2026 book value and 23 times trailing earnings[44]. The company's own slides put its market value at about ₹47,500 crore on 10 November 2025 and ₹43,000 crore on 15 May 2026[16][14]. Today's ₹48,567 crore is above both. The market is paying for three things: the exit record, the promoter's reputation as a capital-markets investor, and a hope that lending and recoveries turn a trading profit into a steady one. Two peers with similar structures, Bajaj Holdings and Tata Investment, trade at 1.7 and 1.06 times book value[45][46]. The premium is the thing to test, and chapters 3 and 5 do so.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's promises over the last two years fall into three groups: deals and platforms, financial outcomes, and controls. On deals the record is good. The company said in May 2025 that the ISARC purchase would close by 30 June 2025 and it closed on 17 June 2025, with about ₹313 crore invested against a stated minimum of about ₹312.5 crore[18][17][14]. It said it would harvest recoveries from the acquired book in the first year and then grow fresh lending, and the acquired book fell from ₹398.4 crore to ₹275.2 crore while the fresh book rose from ₹1,553.8 crore to ₹3,928.1 crore[18][13].

On products and controls the record is weaker. In May 2025 the slides listed the launch of an alternative asset fund with the company's own money invested alongside. A year later the slides say the asset management company exists, the strategy is being worked on and credit funds are being evaluated[18][14]. Fee and commission income fell from ₹75.07 crore to ₹35.29 crore[1], even though third-party servicing assets passed ₹2,000 crore. The resolution plan for Vas Infrastructure, announced in April 2025 with Authum as successful applicant, was rejected by the tribunal on 7 July 2026[26]. The company states there is no impact on operations.

On disclosure the record is mixed. The annual report shows two different figures for return on capital employed (ROCE), which is profit before interest and tax divided by the capital the business uses, an earnings per share (EPS) figure, which is the company's profit divided across its shares, that is not adjusted for the bonus issue, and a prior-period restatement at the subsidiary Open Elite Developers because a deferred tax adjustment was inadvertently not accounted for[6][7][1][19]. The Chief Financial Officer resigned on 1 June 2026, and since 25 August 2026 the interim Chief Financial Officer is whole-time director Mr. Amit Dangi, who was questioned by the CBI in March 2026 about the pre-acquisition fraud case[29][28][37]. The board has six independent directors out of nine[11], which is the right number on paper, but it is hard for the audit committee to oversee a finance function led by a director who is also an executive.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter shows five years of the company's accounts and then applies the first underwriting layer, which sets floors on solvency, governance and quality. Two of the usual yardsticks do not work for this company, so we explain them first. The operating margin in the market-data feed is 92.41 percent[51], but that is only because the main costs of a holding company are small: there is no factory, no raw material and no sales force, so almost every rupee of revenue is left over. It tells you nothing about skill. Debt-to-equity, the ratio of borrowings to shareholders' money, is waived for lenders because lending is financed by borrowing. We still compute it, because a company with promoter-funded preference shares of ₹2,122 crore deserves a look at how stretched it is.

The second yardstick is cash conversion, meaning cash flow from operations divided by profit after tax. For a company that buys and sells shares, the cash flow statement counts purchases of shares under investing, not operating, activity, so the ratio can swing for reasons unrelated to quality. We still measure it, show the working and test the hard line honestly, because the rule exists to catch profit that is not backed by cash and that is exactly the risk here. Every figure is consolidated, meaning the parent plus its subsidiaries, in ₹ crore, from the audited annual reports unless a note says otherwise.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 has two parts. The first part is four hard red lines. Failing any one sends a stock to REJECTED outright. They are borrowing or interest-cover distress, a pledge of promoter shares of 20 percent or more, three-year cash conversion below 0.40 without a verified turnaround, and a Stage 4 breakdown. Stage 4 is the Weinstein stage of a confirmed downtrend. In that stage the price has formed no base, which means a sideways stretch of trading where selling dries up. Stage 2 is the stage of a confirmed uptrend. The second part is the floors. They are softer tests of quality and payoff: return on capital of at least 15 percent, debt-equity below 0.70, cash conversion of at least 0.70, a Stage 2 trend, and a payoff ratio of at least 3.5. A stock that misses the floors can still qualify as a turnaround. It must pass the red lines, show at least two improving signals, be cheap and clear the payoff ratio of 3.5. A target can come from forward earnings times a typical price-to-earnings ratio. It can also come from a measured move. A measured move takes the height of a price pattern and adds it to the price where the stock breaks out upward. A breakout is a close above the top of that pattern. Our variant perception

The price and the screen treat AIIL as a compounding financial platform. At 23 times trailing earnings and 3.3 times book value it is priced above Bajaj Holdings, which owns 30 percent stakes in two of India's largest listed groups, and far above Tata Investment[44][45][46]. The implied assumption is that the ₹2,095 crore trailing profit is a base that grows from here.

Our view differs in three ways. First, the quality of the base: only about a quarter of FY26 profit is income that does not move with the stock market, and 92 percent of the latest quarter's pre-tax profit came from the investment segment[1][19]. Second, the funding: borrowed money has risen 3.4 times in a year, almost all of it from the promoter, who sold ₹1,307 crore of shares in July 2025 at about ₹2,254 a share before the bonus and then supplied ₹2,500 crore of deposits, ₹2,050 crore of which became preference shares[44][35][36][32]. Third, the legacy: enforcement matters at the acquired lenders are open and unsized.

Where the market may be right is also clear. One good quarter, June 2026, added ₹2,591 crore to net worth, which is 17.6 percent of the March figure[19][13]. If markets keep rising and the fresh loan book proves clean, book value could compound faster than we assume in the base case. The market's own reaction is a clue: on the day the strong June quarter was announced, 20 July 2026, the shares fell 3.34 percent to ₹551.95[49]. So the view is not that the shares are a sell. It is that today's price already pays for the good outcome, and the evidence for it so far is one quarter.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is REJECTED. The rule is the Layer 1 floor in the portfolio rules. A pipeline stock must pass the hard red lines. It must then pass the compounder floors or qualify as a turnaround. For a tactical trade it must also offer a gain-to-loss ratio of at least 3.5. The rules call this asymmetry, or reward-to-risk. It is the potential gain divided by the potential loss, which is (target less price) over (price less stop). AIIL passes the four red lines. It fails the return-on-capital floor, at 13.1 percent against 15. It fails the cash-conversion floor, at 0.49 against 0.70. It fails the payoff floor, at 1.27 against 3.5. Its Layer 2 score is 1 of 5 against the 4 of 5 required. The change in the verdict would come from the first trigger below, not from the chart.
