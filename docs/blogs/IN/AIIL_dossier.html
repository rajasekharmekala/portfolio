1. What the company does

Authum is a holding company registered with the Reserve Bank of India as a non-banking finance company (NBFC). In plain terms it behaves like a family investment office with a lending arm. It buys shares in listed and unlisted companies, lends money against assets, and since June 2025 it also runs a company that buys bad loans from banks[11][10]. The business began in 1982 and its current form dates from the promoters' purchase in FY2020, when the balance sheet was small and book value (net worth) was only ₹420 crore[11].

Money comes from three places. The first is the investment book, which held ₹13,117 crore at 31 March 2026: about ₹10,694 crore of "flow" stakes below 20% in mid- and large-cap companies and ₹2,423 crore of "strategic" control positions such as Prataap Snacks (43.66%, about ₹790 crore invested)[11]. Listed equities are about 60% of the investment portfolio at market value and the largest single holding is about 10%[11]. The second is the credit book of ₹4,364 crore, of which ₹3,928 crore is fresh lending built since FY24 and the rest is the legacy portfolio bought from Reliance Commercial Finance and Reliance Home Finance[11]. The third is the asset reconstruction company, India SME ARC (ISARC), where Authum owns 88.37% and has built about ₹693 crore of assets under management[11][20].

Profit comes mostly from the first and third sources, and it arrives in two forms. Realised profit is cash earned when a stake is sold or a loan is recovered. Mark-to-market profit is the paper gain when a holding is revalued to its market price. In FY26 the net gain on fair value was ₹1,586 crore, of which ₹622 crore was realised and ₹964 crore was unrealised[5]. Interest income was ₹552 crore and dividends only ₹45 crore[2]. This split is the key to reading the company: the profit line follows the stock market and the timing of recoveries, while book value is the steadier yardstick of what the owners have.

Because of this, the usual factory-style ratios do not fit. We judge Authum on price-to-book (P/B), on how much of profit is realised, on the total comprehensive income that includes revaluation of shares held through other comprehensive income, and on a normalised cash flow that strips out non-cash gains. The company is funded mainly by owners' money and, since FY26, by ₹2,122 crore of preference shares and ₹541 crore of inter-corporate deposits from a promoter company, so the lenders are mostly the promoters themselves[11][13].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Book value (net worth): assets minus liabilities, the owners' share. Authum's was ₹14,728 crore at 31 March 2026, or ₹173 a share[11][1].
  • Price-to-book (P/B): share price divided by book value per share. At ₹485 the stock is at about 2.8 times[1].
  • Mark-to-market gain: paper profit from revaluing an asset to its current price, before any sale.
  • Total comprehensive income: profit plus gains and losses booked directly to equity. It is the closest accounting measure of how much richer the owners became in a year.
  • Security receipt (SR): a note issued by an asset reconstruction company when it buys bad loans; its value rises as the loans are recovered.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength is capital. Capital to risk assets (CRAR) was 69.30% against the 15% regulatory minimum, and the Tier I part alone was 54.75%[6]. Borrowings of ₹3,598 crore sit against equity of ₹14,728 crore, a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.24 times[1]. Crisil upgraded the rating to A/Stable in October 2025 on the strength of this capital position and because the company was growing non-investment businesses[13]. A balance sheet this unlevered gives the company room to buy distressed assets when sellers need cash.

The second strength is deal sourcing. Exited investments earned about ₹1,797 crore of profit in FY25 and a further ₹156 crore in FY26, with the best FY26 exit returning 14.8 times invested money[10]. Over three years to March 2025 Crisil counted ₹4,838 crore of realised gains from the treasury book and called it the main contributor to net profit[13]. The company also converted unsustainable loans into equity, for example the Nitco debt in FY25. That conversion was booked as ₹1,037.81 crore of gain[4]. That is a real skill, but the record also shows how lumpy it is: realised exit profit fell from ₹1,797 crore to ₹156 crore in one year[10].

The weakness is earnings quality. Profit after tax of ₹1,929 crore in FY26 compares with an operating profit before working-capital changes of ₹849 crore, and that figure is before ₹158 crore of cash interest[2][4]. Security receipts alone added ₹1,044 crore of fair-value gain in FY26, against ₹13 crore a year earlier[5]. Other comprehensive income was a loss of ₹1,985 crore, so total comprehensive income was minus ₹56 crore and book value was almost flat at ₹14,728 crore against ₹14,689 crore a year earlier[2][11]. The first quarter of FY27 reversed the picture: profit was ₹1,108 crore and other comprehensive income was +₹1,483 crore, for total comprehensive income of ₹2,591 crore[14]. One quarter moved book value by about 18%, which tells you how closely the owners' wealth tracks the stock market.

The third weakness is dependence on related parties and open regulatory items. Crisil notes that the 68.8% promoter group, Alpana and Sanjay Dangi, funds the company and that ₹2,500 crore of promoter inter-corporate deposits became preference shares in October 2025[13]. A subsidiary, Open Elite Developers (formerly Reliance Commercial Finance), carries a SEBI penalty of ₹25 crore and a five-year restraint from the securities market, under appeal with a stay, and its net worth is fully eroded with accumulated losses of ₹897.98 crore[9]. The Income Tax Department searched the company from 20 to 25 July 2026, and the company says there was no material impact while it awaits the final outcome[21][22]. There is also a pending income tax demand of ₹136.11 crore that the company disputes[8]. None of these is a proven loss, but each one narrows the margin for error at a 2.8 times book price.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

For an investment-holding NBFC the useful operating indicators differ from a factory's. We score five. They are earnings quality, book-value compounding, fresh credit and asset quality, capital and leverage, and governance. Each is PASS (1.0), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). The company qualifies at 3.0 out of 5.0 or more with at most one FAIL[35]. The thresholds for the first two indicators are our own, and the table states them so you can disagree.

Earnings quality matters most here. We define it as normalised owner cash flow divided by reported profit after tax. Owner cash flow starts from FY26 operating profit before working-capital changes, which was ₹849.17 crore. We deduct cash interest of ₹157.81 crore and cash tax of ₹9.78 crore. That leaves ₹681.58 crore[4]. Against profit after tax of ₹1,929.35 crore, the ratio is 35.3%[2]. We set PASS at 60% or more, WATCH from 30% to 60% and FAIL below 30%. This is a WATCH, and a close one.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Authum is a financial company. The usual hard rules on debt-to-equity and cash conversion do not apply as red lines. We use return on equity (ROE), the net NPA ratio, the capital ratio and price-to-book instead. We still show the three-year cash conversion for completeness[35]. Return on capital employed (ROCE) as Screener computes it is 13.3%, and ROE is 12.8%[1]. The company's own ROCE was 15.71% for FY26, against 28.04% for FY25[10]. Both are below the 15% compounder floor. Both have fallen from the FY24 and FY25 ROE levels of 61% and 34%[1].

Four filed years show a company that is up, down and up again. Revenue was ₹539 crore in FY23. It was ₹2,593 crore in FY24 and ₹4,580 crore in FY25. It fell to ₹2,614 crore in FY26. The twelve months to June 2026 brought ₹2,863 crore[1]. Profit after tax followed the same path: ₹4,304 crore, ₹4,285 crore, ₹4,241 crore and ₹1,929 crore, then ₹2,095 crore for the latest twelve months[1]. The company's FY23 income statement shows ₹4,285.9 crore of exceptional income[11]. Crisil describes one-time exceptional income from the acquisition of Reliance Commercial Finance and of assets of Reliance Home Finance (Crisil spells it Housing Finance)[13]. Crisil also says FY24 profit included ₹1,790 crore of write-backs and ₹269 crore of recoveries from the legacy portfolio[13]. Three-year sales growth is 69.3% a year (FY23 to FY26), but the credit business is shown only from FY24 onwards[11]. We do not quote a three-year profit growth rate, because the FY23 figure conflicts with the net worth series. Consolidated FY23 profit of ₹4,304 crore does not fit net worth, which rose only from ₹3,121.3 crore at FY22 to ₹3,416.4 crore[11]. Screener's ROE for that year is 2%. Screener's standalone series shows profit of ₹240 crore in FY23 and ₹2,920 crore in FY24, against ₹4,304 crore and ₹4,285 crore consolidated[28]. One possible cause is that the exceptional income arose in Reliance Commercial Finance, which became a subsidiary on 14 October 2022[13]. We did not find in the filings we read how that income passed through net worth, so the cause is not confirmed. On consolidated numbers the three-year profit change is minus 23.5% a year. On standalone numbers it is about plus 100% a year. Neither is a fair guide.

Cash flow tells the same story. Cash from operations was ₹245 crore, ₹3,291 crore, ₹326 crore and ₹1,455 crore. Over FY24 to FY26 it totals ₹5,072 crore, against profit of ₹10,455 crore. That is a cash-to-profit ratio of 0.49[1]. The ratio is a noisy guide for a company that revalues investments through profit. FY26's ₹1,455 crore included about ₹652 crore of higher payables and other liabilities, which is not earnings[4]. We do not apply the 0.25 hard line to an NBFC. The normalised figure in the estimate that follows is the one we use for valuation.

Quarterly profit shows why one quarter should never anchor a decision. Profit was ₹943 crore in June 2025 and ₹767 crore in September 2025. It was ₹161 crore in December 2025 and ₹58 crore in March 2026. It was ₹1,108 crore in June 2026[1]. The financing margin, which is financing profit divided by revenue, went from 94% to 89% to 60% to 44% and back to 85%[1]. Two of the last five quarters (₹161 crore and ₹58 crore) fell below the roughly ₹170 crore a quarter that our normalised figure implies. One quarter (₹1,108 crore) was more than six times it.

The second question is whether analysts are raising or cutting their numbers.

Our variant perception

The popular view is that Authum is a compounding machine. The evidence for it is real: ROE of 61% and 34% in FY24 and FY25, exits at up to 14.8 times invested money, a fresh credit book up to ₹3,928 crore and a Crisil upgrade[1][10][11][13]. The stock reflected this when it closed near ₹572 on 16 October, about 3.3 times the audited March book value[23][1].

Our view differs in one respect: the quality of the profit, not the amount. Total comprehensive income was ₹4,368 crore in FY25 and minus ₹56 crore in FY26. Normalised owner cash flow in FY26 was about ₹682 crore[2][4]. At a ₹41,196 crore market value that is a 1.65% cash yield. The market is paying for cheap asset purchases and for rising markets. We think the skill is real. We do not think it is scarce enough to justify more than about 2.4 times book. That is where the stock trades on our June book estimate.

The strongest evidence for the other side is the Q1 FY27 result: profit of ₹1,108 crore and total comprehensive income of ₹2,591 crore[14]. If September also shows a book value near or above ₹205 and owner cash flow for the half-year of at least ₹500 crore, the case for 2.4 times book strengthens. The ₹500 crore figure is our hurdle, about one and a half times the ₹341 crore a half-year implied by our normalised base.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

The call is only as good as its weakest assumptions. Four matter: that book value will not keep rising fast enough to justify the multiple, that no consensus coverage exists to cross-check us, that the governance items stay unproven, and that our own probability weights are right. The red-team bear case below argues against our rejection, then the bull-side stress is shown in the table.

Weakest assumption: the multiple compresses to 2.4 times and no further

We assumed the market keeps paying today's 2.4 times June book. Peers trade at 0.17 to 1.66 times[29][30][31][32]. As a stress test, suppose Authum moved to 2.0 times on a ₹210 book and nothing else changed. The value would be ₹420, which is 13.43% below the close. Keeping our 30/45/25 weights and the other two values, the weighted value would fall to ₹444.97, or 8.28% below the close. The 2.0 times multiple and the weights are our assumptions, not levels Authum has traded at. Evidence of compression would be a fall through ₹457.49 on high volume. We will know more when September results show whether book value rose or fell. The weakness cuts both ways: at 3.0 times on ₹210 the value would be ₹630.

Red-team bear case: the rejection is too cautious

The strongest argument against us is that Authum has earned an average ROE of about 30% over three years (Screener's three-year figure; the yearly row of 61%, 34% and 13% averages about 36%) and turned ₹3,416 crore of net worth into ₹14,728 crore in three years[1][11]. A buyer at 2.4 times June book is paying for a business that did ₹2,591 crore of total comprehensive income in one quarter[14]. As an illustration, if the credit and ARC books reached ₹10,000 crore at a 14% net spread they alone would add ₹1,400 crore of recurring pre-tax profit. We respond that the book needed to be rebuilt after FY26's loss of ₹56 crore of total comprehensive income, that the credit book has no full-cycle loss history, and that the rights-issue commitment to ISARC (40.65 crore shares at ₹10, or ₹406.5 crore, of which ₹101.6 crore of application money was paid in August 2026) has yet to earn a return[2][20].

Governance items could turn from open to closed or from open to costly

Four items are open: the final outcome of the tax search[22], the interim CFO arrangement[18], the SEBI restraint on the subsidiary Open Elite under a stay with 50% of the penalty deposited[9], and the ₹136.11 crore disputed tax demand[8]. If all close without loss, the Layer 2 governance score rises to PASS and the discount we apply disappears. We will know about the tax search when the company informs the exchanges, and about the CFO when a permanent appointment is announced.

The data behind our numbers has gaps

Several sources were unavailable or stale. Q2 FY27 results are not out, so our June 2026 book value is a roll-forward and not a filed figure. The Crisil rationale is dated October 2025. No earnings call transcript was found for FY26 or Q1 FY27, and the Business Standard and NSE pages returned errors. These are recorded in the run notes. The effect is to widen the range on book value, not to change the direction of the analysis.

The verdict is REJECTED. No archetype qualifies at ₹485.15, and the expected return is about zero. The rule is the archetype test in the scoring file. A compounder needs ROE of 15% or more, and we have 12.8%. A turnaround needs a cheap price, and we do not have one. A tactical breakout needs the momentum gate to pass, and it returns NO_MOMENTUM[35][34]. This is not a claim that the company is unsound. Capital is strong, the net NPA ratio is 0.74%, and Layer 2 scores 3.5 out of 5.0. The verdict becomes WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT if the triggers below are met.
