Before you read

Transformers & Rectifiers (India) Ltd makes large power transformers and reactors that move electricity across the grid, and it sells mostly to utilities and industry.[1] The business is growing: sales rose from ₹1,291 crore in FY24 to ₹2,507 crore in FY26, and the order book hit a record ₹6,630 crore on 30 June 2026.[2][10] This report still rejects the stock for the shortlist today, because the company has turned very little of its profit into cash (₹81 crore of cash from operations against ₹535 crore of profit over three years) and because the share price sits below its falling 50-day and 200-day averages.[4][5][17] The conclusion would be wrong if the results due on 13 October 2026 show cash flow turning clearly positive and the price closes back above both averages.[29]

What the company does

Think of the power grid as a road network for electricity. Power stations produce electricity at one voltage, long-distance lines carry it at a much higher voltage to lose less on the way, and local networks bring it back down for homes and factories. A transformer is the device that changes the voltage at each step. This company builds the big ones: single-phase power transformers up to 500 MVA and 1,200 kV class, furnace transformers for steel plants, rectifier and distribution transformers, series and shunt reactors, and mobile substations.[19]

The customers are mainly state and central power utilities, plus industrial buyers. It sells on a business-to-business basis and wins work by bidding on tenders.[19] In October 2026 it announced two such wins: a Gujarat Energy Transmission Corporation (GETCO) order for 80 MVAR and 125 MVAR shunt reactors and 500 MVA auto transformers, to be delivered over 24 to 30 months, and a Damodar Valley Corporation (DVC) order for 500 MVA and 200 MVA auto transformers, to be delivered over 15 to 28 months.[14][8] Media reported each order as falling in a ₹100 crore to ₹500 crore band.[15] An earlier letter of intent from APTRANSCO (14 August 2026) was reported in the same band, with 13-month delivery.[16] The exact value of each contract was not published in the sources read for this report, so it is N/A here.[14] The transcript note that prompted this research said "~₹500 cr order"; that figure is the top of one band, not a confirmed contract value.[24]

The money mechanics are simple but slow. A utility awards a contract. The company buys steel, copper and other materials, builds the unit over many months, ships it and is paid in stages. This means revenue lags orders by one to two years, and the company must fund materials and unfinished work in between. The company said the current order book can be executed over the next 18 to 24 months.[13] In FY26 the factories made 33,000 MVA of equipment, up from 29,118 MVA in FY25.[19]

The company is expanding. It is spending about ₹150 crore on its Changodar plant, plans backward-integration projects worth ₹900 crore to ₹1,000 crore (making more of its own components), and is building three new factories at Chiyada.[11] It said the Changodar work lowered output in the June 2026 quarter and should finish by August 2026.[10] Whether it did finish is N/A until the 13 October results.

Glossary of terms used in this report

MVA
Mega-volt-ampere, the size rating of a transformer. A 500 MVA unit is very large.
Unexecuted order book
Contracts won but not yet delivered and billed. It is the work already sold.
Book-to-bill
New orders received in a period divided by revenue billed in the same period. Above 1 means the order book is growing.
ROCE
Return on capital employed: operating profit after tax relative to the money tied up in the business. Higher is better; our floor is 15%.
CFO
Cash from operations: cash the business generated from running itself, before buying equipment.
PAT
Profit after tax, the accounting profit left for owners.
OPM / EBITDA margin
Operating profit as a share of sales. The two names differ slightly by how other income and some costs are counted.
DMA
Day moving average: the average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 trading days.
ATR
Average true range: how far the price typically moves in a day, here over 14 days. It is used to measure distance in "normal daily moves".
Stage 2 / Stage 4
Weinstein stages of a share price. Stage 2 is an advance above a rising 200-day average. Stage 4 is a decline below a falling one.
Asymmetry
Possible gain divided by possible loss: (target minus entry) divided by (entry minus stop). We need 3.5 or more for a tactical idea.
P/E
Price divided by profit per share. It shows how many years of current profit the price pays for.

Why the business is strong or weak

The strengths are real and measurable. The first is demand visibility: the order book of ₹6,630 crore is 2.60 times trailing twelve-month sales of ₹2,552 crore (6,630 divided by 2,552), so about two and a half years of the current sales pace is already sold.[10][12][2] The second is order momentum: in the June 2026 quarter the company won ₹2,114 crore of new orders against ₹572.34 crore of revenue, a book-to-bill of 3.69 (2,114 divided by 572.34), and inflow grew 218% on the year.[10] Working backwards, that implies about ₹665 crore of inflow a year earlier (2,114 divided by 3.18), a derived figure, not a reported one. The third is a pipeline of enquiries of about ₹23,000 crore with a historic win ratio of 10% to 15%.[13] Applying that ratio gives roughly ₹2,300 crore to ₹3,450 crore of wins if history repeats, which is our arithmetic and not a company forecast.

The weaknesses are in cash and in margins that have stopped improving. Profit before tax and operating margin rose sharply in FY25 (operating margin from 10% in FY24 to 16%), but FY26 slipped to 15%, and the June 2026 quarter was 16% against 17% a year earlier on Screener's measure.[2][3] The company's own EBITDA margin was 19.16% in that quarter, and Upstox reported 20.5% a year earlier, so the margin is down about 1.3 points on the company's measure.[10][13] Quarterly profit after tax was ₹64.34 crore per the company; Upstox reported ₹61.52 crore against ₹67.35 crore, an 8.66% fall.[10][13] The two profit figures differ in what they count; the report uses both and does not pick one.

The cash record is the central weakness. Cash from operations was ₹29 crore in FY24, ₹157 crore in FY25 and negative ₹105 crore in FY26. Free cash flow (cash from operations less equipment spending) was negative ₹78 crore in FY25 and negative ₹215 crore in FY26.[5] The working-capital figures show why. Customers took 129 days to pay in FY26 against 85 in FY25. The company held stock for 133 days. And it paid its own suppliers in 62 days against 109 days. The cash conversion cycle, the days between paying for materials and collecting from customers, went from 94 days to 200 days.[6] A growing manufacturer that must pre-fund materials and wait for utility payments will often show this pattern. It may reverse as large milestone payments arrive. It is also how profitable-looking growers run short of cash.

Ownership adds a second question. Promoters held about 74.9% until March 2023 and 64.36% from December 2024 to September 2026.[7] Screener flags a 10.6 point fall over three years.[21] Foreign institutions rose to 11.33% in March 2025 and fell to 6.98% by September 2026; domestic institutions fell from 7.22% to 1.19% over the same period.[7] Institutional holders have trimmed their stakes since March 2025.
