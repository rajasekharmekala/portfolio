Before you read

SMS Pharmaceuticals makes the active ingredients inside common medicines, mainly ibuprofen, HIV drugs and diabetes drugs, at two plants in southern India. We reject the stock for now: it passes the safety checks, but none of our three buying cases holds at the ₹475.45 close on 28 October. The business is improving and the trend is strong, yet the latest quarter showed sales up only 5.6% and profit per share down 3.5%, at a price of about 43 times a year of profit. Results come on 4 November 2026, and growth of 12% or more plus a pullback toward ₹420 would reopen the case. We would be wrong if the new factory block starts early or fills fast.

1. What the company does

SMS Pharmaceuticals makes active pharmaceutical ingredients (API) — the chemical that does the medical work inside a tablet — and the intermediates, the half-finished chemicals used to make them. It does not sell medicines to patients. Its customers are other drug makers, such as Lupin, Granules India and Dr. Reddy's, who buy the powder, press it into tablets and sell the tablets under their own names[10]. The company is based in Hyderabad, was set up in 1987 and has been run by the current management since 1989[4]. It is listed on both NSE and BSE and trades under the symbol SMSPHARMA[1]. Years are financial years ending 31 March, so FY26 means April 2025 to March 2026.

Production sits in two plants. The Vizag plant in Andhra Pradesh has 3,000 kilolitres (KL) of reactor volume — the total size of the steel vessels where the chemistry happens — and handles large-volume molecules, including ibuprofen. It houses what the company calls the largest single-block, single-product ibuprofen plant in Asia[29]. The Hyderabad plant has 120 KL and handles small-volume, higher-value molecules. Together that is 3,120 KL of reactor volume[3]. The company says its plants have passed US Food and Drug Administration (USFDA) approval six times at Hyderabad and four times at Vizag[3]. That is ten plant inspections. The December 2025 disclosure counts twelve across all its sites because it includes two audits of its central laboratory[13]. It holds more than 120 Drug Master Files (DMF), the confidential dossiers a drug maker files with a regulator so that its customers can use the ingredient in a regulated market. The annual report says 140 plus, a gap we flag in Notes[3][9].

What does it sell? The company lists more than 55 APIs in 14 treatment areas and more than 800 customers in over 75 countries[3]. Two products carry the volume. Ibuprofen, an everyday pain and inflammation drug, is roughly 20% of revenue[5]. Tenofovir and other anti-retroviral (ARV) drugs for HIV were 28% of revenue in FY26 and 31.5% in the June 2026 quarter[3][10]. The rest is a spread of higher-value molecules: sitagliptin and related diabetes drugs (15% in FY26), triptans for migraine (11%), and smaller lines for ulcers, erectile dysfunction, epilepsy and angina[3]. The company's own product list does not name paracetamol, so that is not part of this story.

Two business models run side by side. The first is sale mode: SMS buys raw materials, makes the API and sells it. The second is job work for large drug groups, where the customer supplies the inputs and SMS earns a conversion fee. Management says it books some ARV volume as job work, which makes quarterly revenue lumpy[8]. The company's slide says 88% of revenue comes from regulated markets such as the US, Europe and Japan[3]. Management put the export share of sales at roughly 70% on the May 2026 call, an off-the-cuff estimate[5]. The two figures measure different things and the sources do not fully reconcile them. On the June 2025 call management said its regulated-market count includes deemed exports, meaning sales in India to formulators who ship the finished tablet abroad[7]. For ibuprofen it gave a split of about 40% regulated markets and 60% domestic and other markets, so the mix differs by product[7].

The company has a long history of reinventing its product list. From 1989 to 2011 it was, by its own account, the world's largest maker of ranitidine, a heartburn drug[3]. Regulators then pulled ranitidine from the US market because of a contamination problem with a chemical called NDMA. The FDA approved a reformulated ranitidine only in November 2025, after a five-year absence, and the approval went to VKT Pharma, a finished-dose company in which SMS owns 34.83%[14][15][9]. That stake matters later, because VKT's profit share lifted SMS's FY26 earnings.

Where the money comes from, per ₹100 of sales

In FY26 the company sold ₹886.87 crore of product. Raw materials and other direct costs took ₹584.04 crore, leaving a gross margin — sales minus the cost of the goods sold, shown as a share of sales — of 34.1%, or about ₹34 of every ₹100[3]. Staff, power, freight and other running costs took about ₹14.8 of every ₹100, which leaves EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) of ₹171.28 crore, or 19.3%[3]. Depreciation (₹39.92 crore) and interest (₹23.08 crore) then take about ₹7 of every ₹100. Tax is about 24.6% of the remaining profit before tax of ₹116.63 crore[3]. After tax, the core business earned ₹87.99 crore, and ₹13.99 crore more came from the VKT Pharma stake, for ₹101.98 crore in total[3].

Notice how thin the cushion is. A 1 percentage point change in the gross margin moves EBITDA by about ₹9 crore, which is about 5% of the year's EBITDA. That sensitivity is why solvent prices, which jumped in March 2026 after the conflict in the Middle East, show up in the margin within a quarter[5].

The partners and related parties

SMS has a 45:55 joint venture with Chemo Iberica of Spain, which owns the intellectual property for certain APIs, while SMS makes them and Chemo sells them in regulated markets[17]. Management calls the diabetes ingredient the model case: SMS was first to market in Europe through this venture[6]. The company also has an associate, VKT Pharma (34.83%), a peptide subsidiary, SMS Peptides (₹8 crore invested in FY26 and a ₹50 crore loan approved in 2026), and dealings with SMS Life Sciences, a separate company in which the promoters hold shares[9][3][5].

These links are disclosed, not hidden. On the May 2026 call management said SMS sold about ₹40 crore of API to VKT, and that SMS buys some intermediates and raw materials from SMS Life Sciences; an analyst on the call put those purchases at about ₹20 crore[5]. A reader should still keep them in mind, because related-party trade is outside the ordinary arm's-length test that independent customers provide.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Start with what SMS has that others lack. It has cost control in a few high-volume molecules, regulatory files that take years to build, and a long-standing set of customers. Backward integration is the clearest advantage. Over roughly 12 to 18 months the company spent close to ₹150 crore to make key intermediates of ibuprofen and other APIs itself, which management says cuts reliance on Chinese suppliers and shortens delivery times[6][3]. The effect showed up in the numbers: the EBITDA margin was 16% in the March 2025 quarter and 20% in the September 2025 quarter, and CARE Ratings (a credit rating agency, called CARE from here on) lists backward integration among the causes of a rise in the full-year margin to 19.78% in FY26 from 18.41% in FY25 on CARE's own measure of operating profit (PBILDT), which differs slightly from the company's 19.3% and 17.8% EBITDA margins[4][10].

The regulatory record is clean for a plant of this size. The USFDA inspected Vizag from 8 to 12 December 2025 and left one minor observation on Form 483. The company says the observation was procedural and unrelated to data integrity. It calls this the fourth inspection of Vizag and the twelfth across all its sites[13][26]. A single minor finding is a good result in a business where a warning letter can freeze exports for a year or more. We could not read the inspection classification itself, because the FDA database pages were not reachable, so we rely on the company's account and on CARE's note that Unit II and Unit VII are approved by the USFDA, Japan's PMDA and European authorities[10].

Now the weaknesses. First, the moat is narrow. Ibuprofen is a commodity: the Executive Director said order lead times on commodity APIs are 'hardly like 4 days or 5 days', so SMS must hold stock to win orders[7]. A research note from March 2025 reported that two competitors have ibuprofen capacity of 1,000 and 700 tonnes a month, against SMS's 500 now and 800 after the current expansion[17][5]. SMS is aiming to be among the largest, not the largest. Prices matter more than market share here: the same note says the average ibuprofen price fell about 7% in FY24 and 10% in the first nine months of FY25[17].

Second, the product mix swings far more than a 'steady' business should allow. CARE records that ARV drugs fell from 40% of revenue in FY22 to 3% in FY23 while diabetes drugs rose from 3% to 38%. That swing took the company from a ₹62 crore profit in FY22 to a ₹7 crore loss in FY23[12][1]. The mix has swung again since: ARV is back at 28% to 31%, and diabetes has dropped to 15%[10][3]. The company is not tied to one product, but it is tied to whichever product is in favour at the time.

Third, working capital is heavy. The company holds a lot of stock: inventory days (the average days of sales sitting in the warehouse) were 260 in FY26, up from 241 in FY25 and 201 in FY24[1]. In June 2025 management promised to reduce inventory in the following year[7]. Inventory rose instead. Standalone inventory grew to ₹350.95 crore from ₹284.73 crore, up 23%[9]. CARE puts the full operating cycle at 178 days in FY26 and expects about 195 days in FY27[10]. The result is that free cash flow (FCF) was negative in FY25 (−₹42 crore) and FY26 (−₹71 crore)[1].

Competition and the policy backdrop

Among listed peers the closest like-for-like names are Aarti Drugs, IOL Chemicals, Granules India (an API maker that CARE also lists as a customer of SMS) and, in quality terms, Neuland Laboratories and Divi's Laboratories. IOL Chemicals describes itself as the largest ibuprofen maker in the world, with a 35% world share[19], which puts SMS's ibuprofen ambitions in context. Chapter 3 compares the group. The policy tailwind is real but modest: the government runs a ₹6,940 crore production-linked incentive scheme for bulk drugs and a ₹3,000 crore scheme for bulk drug parks[23], and a March 2026 list showed APIs for which China supplied 70% or more of India's imports[24]. SMS's own slide claims low dependence on China for raw materials[3].

Ibuprofen prices are the swing factor. One price tracker described global ibuprofen as 'broadly stable to soft' in the June 2026 quarter, with easing feedstock costs[25]. Management, by contrast, said in May 2026 that ibuprofen prices were rising because of the war[5]. We cannot settle that difference with the sources available, and we treat ibuprofen price as an open risk rather than a tailwind.

Pledged shares and who owns the company

The Potluri family and related entities own 68.07% of the company as of September 2026[1]. They also own it more heavily than before: the stake was 64.67% in December 2024 and rose to 68.07% after the family paid ₹114.3 crore for new shares through warrants, a fair sign of conviction[1][9]. Some of those shares are pledged to lenders. At September 2026, 11,466,740 of the promoters' 63,745,010 shares were pledged, which is 17.99% of their holding[16]. A year earlier the pledged share was 34.65%. It fell in the December 2025 quarter and has stayed at 17.99% since[16]. About 74% of the pledged shares sit with two family-linked companies, and the rest with one family member. That is below the portfolio's 20% line and the 25% hard red line, but it is a risk to watch.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

The second half of Layer 2 asks whether management did what it said it would do. We compared three annual guidance statements, the capex plan, and the promise on inventory, using the May 2024, June 2025 and May 2026 calls and the annual reports. Management's record is mixed. It is strong on engineering, funding and compliance and weak on sales guidance, delivery dates and working capital.

On May 31, 2024 management guided to FY25 revenue growth of 20% to 25% and an EBITDA margin of about 20%[8]. The year closed with revenue growth of 10.4% (₹783 crore against ₹709 crore) and an EBITDA margin of 17.8%[1][3]. In June 2025 it guided to FY26 revenue growth of about 20% and an EBITDA margin of 20%[7]. The year closed with revenue growth of 13.3% and a margin of 19.3%[3]. In May 2026 it guided to 15% growth for FY27[5]. The first quarter delivered 5.6%. Two full-year guides in a row were too high on sales, by about 7 to 15 percentage points, and the third is behind pace after one quarter. Management itself calls the FY27 number prudent and says the underlying target for FY28 and FY29 is 20% to 25% growth[5].

The record on funding is better. In May 2024 the company said it would fund capex partly with ₹114.3 crore of warrants issued to the promoters[8]. The annual report confirms that all 90 lakh warrants were converted, the last 50 lakh in September 2025 after the promoters paid the balance of ₹47.63 crore[9]. Backward integration also did what was promised. In June 2025 management said it would improve margins from the second quarter of FY26[7], and the EBITDA margin rose from 16% to 20% between the March 2025 and September 2025 quarters[4].

The two clear misses are delivery dates and inventory. In June 2025 the plan was ₹250 crore of capex over 18 months with revenue early in FY27[7]. By November 2025 it was ₹280 crore, finished by November 2026, with revenue from FY28[6]. CARE now writes physical completion in March 2027 and commercial start in July 2027[10]. On inventory, the same June 2025 call promised that stock would come down[7]. Inventory days rose from 241 to 260[1].

First, the next print is likely to look ordinary. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A report that only argues for its verdict is not finished. This chapter attacks our own numbers. We take each estimate, name its weakest assumption, say what evidence would show it is wrong, and say when that evidence will arrive. Because the verdict is already a rejection, the red team here argues the opposite side too: what would make the stock a good buy at ₹475.45 and why we think that case is thinner than it looks.

The bear case in one paragraph: profit growth has already slowed to 2% to 8% in the latest quarter, a seventh of last year's profit came from a partner company that produced almost nothing in June, cash flow is negative while debt rises 38% in a year, and the share costs about 43 times profit. If the new plant block is late, and the company's own record says that is plausible, the market will pay 28 times a flat ₹10 of earnings, which is ₹280[3][1][10].

Fatal flaw 1: the associate profit may not repeat

VKT Pharma's contribution jumped from ₹1.74 crore in FY25 to ₹13.99 crore in FY26, and ₹11.75 crore of that arrived in the March 2026 quarter, four months after the FDA approved its reformulated ranitidine[3][14]. A product returning to a market after five years can show a burst of initial stocking before sales settle. In June 2026 the contribution was ₹0.71 crore, and consolidated profit rose only 2% while core profit rose 8%[3]. Management says the full-year contribution should be sustainable and strengthen into FY27[5]. Our base case assumes ₹8 crore for FY27, below FY26 but well above the June run rate of about ₹3 crore a year.

What would show us wrong: a September 2026 contribution below ₹2 crore. We would know on 4 November 2026. At zero, FY27 earnings per share would be about ₹0.85 lower than our base[3].

Fatal flaw 2: cash does not follow profit

Profit has risen for three years, but free cash flow has fallen each year from −₹2 crore to −₹42 crore to −₹71 crore, and operating cash flow was 49% of EBITDA in FY26[1]. Inventory consumed about ₹66 crore of cash in FY26 on a standalone basis (₹6,622.41 lakh)[9]. CARE projects operating cycle days rising to about 195 in FY27 and borrowings rising to ₹502.83 crore[10]. CARE's own negative rating trigger is total debt above 2.5 times PBILDT, its measure of operating profit before interest, depreciation and tax. On our base case that ratio is about 2.43 (₹502.83 crore ÷ ₹206.7 crore); in our bear case it is about 2.67[10].

What would show us wrong: the September 2026 half-year cash flow statement, published with the 4 November results, showing operating cash flow above 60% of EBITDA. If inventory days fall below 240 at March 2027, the Layer 2 working-capital KPI would move from FAIL to WATCH[1][10].

Fatal flaw 3: the new capacity may start late or fill slowly

FY28 earnings per share of ₹14.44 relies on the ₹280.78 crore project, of which 43.1% was spent by June 2026[10]. The project adds ibuprofen capacity (500 to 800 tonnes a month) and a block for four or five new APIs[5]. These new products are not yet commercial. Management expects them to contribute meaningfully in FY28 and to scale up in FY29[5]. Running the larger plant as full as today's (about 80%) means selling about 640 tonnes a month instead of about 400, or 60% more volume. A March 2025 research note reported that ibuprofen prices fell about 7% in FY24 and about 10% in the first nine months of FY25, so more volume may mean lower prices[17]. One price tracker says global ibuprofen was 'stable to soft' in June 2026[25].

What would show us wrong: any statement that commissioning moves beyond July 2027, a rise in capital work in progress without capitalisation at March 2027, or a Q4 FY27 stock build ahead of new sales. We would know the first signs at the Q3 results in February 2027 and the full picture at the March 2027 completion date.

Fatal flaw 4: concentration and a history of mix swings

The top five customers took about 53% of FY26 revenue and 50% in Q1 FY27, and the largest customer was 28%[10][3]. ARV drugs were 28.4% of FY26 sales and 31.5% in Q1 FY27, while diabetes drugs fell to 15% of FY26 sales from 24% the year before[10][3][4]. CARE records that the same ARV line was 40% of revenue in FY22 and 3% in FY23[12]. Management also described the ARV business as part sale and part job work, where quarters can swing between the two[8]. A further wrinkle is that management's account of the largest customer has not been consistent. On the November 2025 call it said the 24% figure was the largest treatment area, not a customer; the Q1 FY27 presentation shows the largest customer at 28%, and in May 2026 management said that single customer buys several products[6][3][5].

What would show us wrong: ARV share staying near 30% for three more quarters with stable pricing, and the top-five customer share falling below 50%. We would know from the Q2 and Q3 FY27 presentations.

Fatal flaw 5: the multiple leaves no room

At a P/E of 43.5 the stock trades 23% above its 3-year median of 35.4 and 41% above the middle P/E of its three nearest peers (30.8)[2][18][19][20]. The floor needs a P/E of at least 33.7 on our FY28E earnings. A drop in the multiple of just 5% from the median wipes out the cushion. Aarti Drugs, a similar-sized ingredient maker, trades at 21.3 times earnings after 3-year sales growth of −2%, which shows how little the market pays when growth stalls[18].

What would show us wrong: a P/E that holds above 40 after a flat quarter, which would say the market is paying for the capacity story, not the numbers. We would see that after the 4 November results.

The other side: the best case for buying now

A fair buyer would make four points. Margins are at a record and backward integration is only now reaching its full effect. The pledge problem has eased: 34.65% of promoter shares were pledged a year ago and 17.99% are now[16]. Ibuprofen runs near 80% of capacity, so the new capacity answers demand that already exists[5]. And the chart shows buyers are willing to pay up, with a 63-day return 30.24 points above the Nifty 50[31].

We take those points seriously, and they are why this is a rejection for now, not a permanent no. But each one is already in the price. A record margin is part of why the P/E is 43.5 and not 35.4. The pledge fix removes a risk, but it does not add earnings. Demand for ibuprofen is real, yet a 60% jump in volume sold into a market where prices fell 7% to 10% in a year is not a given[17]. And a strong chart describes the past 63 days. To own the stock at ₹475.45 you need the bull case, which carries a 25% probability in our model. At ₹420 you need only the base case. That is the whole difference between the two prices.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, for now. The rule is that a candidate needs one archetype to qualify. SMS passes the safety checks and the Layer 2 business test (3.0 of 5.0, PASS_WITH_WATCH), but the compounder test fails on ROCE of 13.3%, the turnaround test fails because only one of four trajectory signals is positive and the P/E is a 23% premium, and the tactical test fails because sales and profit growth slowed to 5.6% and 2% and the reward-to-risk ratio is 0.44 to 1. The price that would change the answer is ₹420.33; the event that would change it is the 4 November 2026 results. We do not recommend buying at the current price, or after a spike on results day[31][32].

Layer 3, the entry posture. The price is 1.24 ATR above its support, which sits in the LIMIT_ORDER_REDUCE band (starter size or a limit order only). If the case reopens, we would use only a limit order inside an indicative entry zone of ₹405.00 (the 20-day swing low) to ₹420.33 (the 50-day average). The stop is ₹393.99: the swing low of ₹405.00 minus half an ATR (0.5 × ₹22.03 = ₹11.015). From ₹420.33 the stop is ₹26.34 away, 6.27% below. Reward-to-risk at ₹420.33 is 3.45 to 1 against a base target of ₹511.20; the bull target is ₹731 and the bear target ₹280[31][32].

Position size. As a rejected name the conviction tier is TIER_0 and the size is zero. If it were reopened, we would place it in TIER_3, where the risk budget is 50 basis points of the portfolio: 0.50% divided by a 6.27% stop distance is 7.98%, which the tier cap of 3.5% limits to 3.5% of net worth. That is a small starting position by design, because the stop is tight against a stock whose daily swing is about 4.6% of its price[31][32].

Let winners run. The portfolio's rule is not to trim a rising Stage 2 holding only because the RSI passes 70 or the weight grows past 15%; the weight can run to 22% while the stop follows the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average. That rule applies after a purchase. It does not turn a rejected name into a buy, and we hold no position in SMS today.
