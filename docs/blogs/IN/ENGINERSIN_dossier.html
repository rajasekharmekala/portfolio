1. What the company does

Engineers India Limited is a company whose largest owner is the Government of India, with 51.32 percent of the shares[1][18]. It gets paid to design and manage big oil, gas and chemical plants. It is a Navratna central public sector enterprise, which means a large state-run company that has extra freedom to invest, and it reports to the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas[8]. Think of EIL as the architect and site manager of a refinery. The oil company owns the refinery and pays EIL to plan it, to buy the equipment and to supervise the builders. CARE Ratings notes that 20 of India's 23 petroleum refineries and 10 of its 11 mega petrochemical complexes carry EIL's work[8].

EIL earns money in two ways. In consultancy and engineering, it sells studies, designs and project management, often for a fee. This work needs engineers, not heavy equipment, so it keeps a high share of each rupee of turnover: segment profit was 24 percent of consultancy turnover in the June 2026 quarter[4][6]. In turnkey work, EIL signs a contract to deliver a whole plant. Most of these are on an open-book basis, which means the client pays the real costs plus an agreed margin, so EIL carries less price risk[6]. Turnkey work brings large turnover but thin profit, 7.5 percent of turnkey turnover in the same quarter[6]. The mix therefore sets the profit: more consultancy means more profit from the same sales.

The company reported standalone turnover of ₹3,849 crore for FY26 (one crore is ten million), made of ₹1,782 crore from consultancy and ₹2,067 crore from turnkey work[11]. The order book is the signed work not yet delivered. On 30 June 2026 it stood at ₹14,424 crore, and 73 percent of it was consultancy[6][4]. Mega projects take four to five years to finish and mid-sized ones two to three years, so the order book turns into sales slowly and the quarterly numbers move with project timing[6].

Who pays EIL, and why demand is lumpy

Most of the money comes from state-run oil and gas companies such as Indian Oil, Bharat Petroleum, Hindustan Petroleum and ONGC[4][6]. CARE Ratings says oil and gas made up about 70 to 80 percent of the order book over the last three years[8]. Because these clients decide their spending in large steps, EIL's inflow of new orders arrives in lumps. In the March 2026 quarter alone it booked ₹3,711 crore of new business, against ₹514 crore in the June 2026 quarter[4].

India is still building refining capacity. The installed capacity was 267,116 thousand tonnes a year on 1 April 2026[30], and the government has told Parliament it expects 309.5 million tonnes a year by 2030, up from 258.1 million then[31]. New units, upgrades and petrochemical add-ons all need design and project management, which is EIL's trade. The caution is that oil companies can delay approvals: management said Indian Oil's Paradip Phase 2 still awaits an investment decision[6].

EIL is also trying to widen its range of work beyond oil and gas. Overseas consultancy now carries a large part of the order book, led by a Dangote Group order in Nigeria that management values at more than ₹3,200 crore over about five years[11][5]. Management also points to nuclear studies, coal gasification feasibility work, data centres and institutional buildings[6]. These new lines are early and unproven in size, so we treat them as options, not as part of our central estimate.

Investments outside the core business

EIL owns 26 percent of Ramagundam Fertilizers and Chemicals Limited (RFCL), a urea plant in Telangana, with a total equity investment of ₹491 crore[4][7]. It also holds 4.37 percent of Numaligarh Refinery Limited, for which it invested ₹838.41 crore, and is building a small compressed biogas plant at Kolhapur[4]. RFCL matters most for profit: the joint venture earned ₹42.51 crore for EIL in the June 2026 quarter, against a loss of ₹7.37 crore a year earlier when the plant was shut[6].

These stakes add profit and dividends, but they also use cash that could be paid out to shareholders. When an investor asked about sharing the surplus cash, the Director of Finance said other investment plans are under consideration and that there is no plan to distribute the entire reserves[6]. CARE notes that EIL has no firm investment commitment in the medium term but that a large cash drain would be a point to watch[8].

Who runs the company

The Government of India nominates the chairman and managing director. The previous holder retired on 28 February 2026, and Shri Atul Gupta took charge on 29 June 2026 for a term that runs to 30 September 2029[7]. The FY26 accounts were signed on 21 May 2026 by an officer holding the chairman's post as an additional charge[7]. The company has 2,747 permanent employees[7]. Government ownership gives EIL steady clients and a top credit rating, but it also means the board does not choose the chief executive and cannot easily change strategy.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ROCE (return on capital employed) — the profit a business earns on each rupee of capital it uses; 30.4 percent here[1].
  • EPS (earnings per share) — the company's profit divided by the number of shares; ₹12.30 for FY26 on the consolidated basis[1].
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) — profit before financing and accounting charges, a rough measure of cash profit from operations.
  • Operating margin — operating profit as a share of sales, after the costs of running the business and before other income, interest and tax.
  • Working capital — the cash tied up in day-to-day business: money owed by customers plus stock, less money owed to suppliers and advances received. EIL's is negative, which means customers pay before it spends[1].
  • Cash flow from operations — the cash the business itself generates before investing and financing.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) — cash flow from operations minus spending on plant and equipment.
  • Debt-to-equity — total borrowings divided by shareholders' money.
  • Price-to-earnings — the share price divided by earnings per share; it is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit.
  • Guidance — the company's own published forecast for the coming period.
  • Consensus estimate — the average of professional analysts' forecasts for a number.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong in one way and weak in another. It is strong because it has little debt, a top credit rating, deep experience and clients that cannot easily go elsewhere. It is weak because its profit swings from quarter to quarter, and some recent profit came from one-off gains, not from steady trading. Both halves matter for the price, so this chapter takes each in turn and ties them to the numbers in Chapter 3.

The strengths: track record, clients and a clean balance sheet

EIL's main edge is its record. It has engineered 10 grassroots refineries, holds 58 live patents for process technologies and has a research centre at Gurgaon[8]. State-run oil companies know its staff and its standards, and it has signed frame agreements with Middle East clients[6]. That is hard for a new rival to copy within a few years. It is also why CARE Ratings describes EIL's position in oil and gas consultancy as dominant[8].

The second strength is the balance sheet. On the consolidated figures, borrowings were ₹17 crore on 31 March 2026 against shareholders' money of ₹3,146 crore[1]. CARE counted liquid investments and cash of ₹1,359 crore on the same date[8]. CARE rates the bank facilities CARE AAA with a stable outlook, its top long-term rating, for long- and short-term limits of ₹2,248 crore and long-term limits of ₹121.50 crore[8]. Its working capital is negative at minus 60 days, which means that on balance customer advances and unpaid supplier bills exceed what customers owe it[1].

The third strength is demand that has policy support. The Union Cabinet approved a ₹37,500 crore scheme on 13 May 2026 to encourage coal gasification, and the first application round closed with seven applications, including NTPC and Adani Enterprises[27][28]. EIL says it is bidding for feasibility studies and already works on coal-to-gas projects for NTPC[6]. These projects would use the same process-engineering skills EIL already sells.

The weaknesses: lumpy earnings, thin turnkey profit and one-off gains

Profit does not move in a straight line. Consolidated quarterly net profit was ₹65 crore in June 2025, ₹83 crore in September 2025, ₹347 crore in December 2025, ₹196 crore in March 2026 and ₹158 crore in June 2026[1]. The December 2025 figure is the outlier. Management told analysts that a change order from a client, together with the reversal of a provision for delay penalties, added about ₹226 crore to turnover and ₹213 crore to profit[10]. A provision is money set aside for a possible future cost; when the cost does not happen, the money returns to profit.

The annual report adds a second item. In FY26 the company wrote back ₹3,516.70 lakh (₹35.17 crore) of contract obligations after settling with a client in the consultancy segment, and ₹8,253.93 lakh (₹82.54 crore) in FY25[7]. On the Q1 FY27 call the Director of Finance said the company does not expect any write-back of provisions in the current year[6]. So the FY26 profit of ₹692 crore was helped by gains that will not repeat, and Chapter 3 estimates how much.

Turnkey work is also thin and uncertain. Turnkey turnover fell 33 percent in the June 2026 quarter, from ₹449 crore to ₹302 crore, because large projects were winding down[6]. Management expects it to recover in the third and fourth quarters as new orders reach full execution[6]. That is a forecast, not yet a fact, and Chapter 6 treats it as the hinge of the case.

Government ownership: a safety net and a ceiling

The majority owner is the Government of India, so the promoter pledge is zero percent in every quarter shown for the last three years[18]. That removes a risk that hurts many Indian small and mid-sized companies. It does not remove all risk. CARE lists a material fall in government ownership as a negative rating factor[8]. A stake sale by the government would add shares to the market and could weigh on the price for a while, even though it would not change the business.

The ownership also shapes how management behaves. The company signs a yearly memorandum of understanding with the ministry that sets targets for revenue, return on net worth and exports, and it was rated Very Good for FY25[7]. Dividend policy follows government guidance too: EIL paid ₹5 per share of dividend for FY26, an interim ₹2.5 and a proposed final ₹2.5[11]. Those payments are steady and attractive, but they come with limited freedom to change course.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above rests on a few assumptions that can be wrong. This chapter takes each one, names its weakest point, says what evidence would show it is wrong and when we will know. It then gives the strongest honest bear case and names the single claim on which the whole view hinges.

Weakest assumption in the FY27 estimate: an operating margin of 14%

The estimate needs the second half to earn about the same margin as the first quarter, while turnkey turnover rebuilds. Turnkey work earns 7.5% against 24% for consulting, so a rebuild of turnkey turnover lowers the blended margin even as profit rises[6]. If turnkey turnover rebuilds faster than consulting, the margin could end nearer 12% than 14%.

The evidence will come on 30 October 2026 with the September quarter, and again in February 2027 with the December quarter[25]. We would call the margin wrong if the September quarter shows an operating profit below 12% of turnover without a write-back, because that would leave no room for a 14% year.

Weakest assumption in the target: a multiple of 21.7 times

The 3-year median of 21.7 times was set while reported profit included one-offs, so it may overstate what the market pays for clean profit[3]. The multiple has ranged from 13.7 to 36.4 times, which is a wide band. A fall to 16 times, the bear case, takes the value to ₹166.

We will see how the market prices clean profit at the next two results. If the stock holds above 20 times clean profit after a clean quarter, the median is a fair anchor. If it falls toward 16 times on the same profit, the multiple is the weak link.

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF: how much profit turns into cash

The price looks reasonable if 70% of profit becomes cash and expensive at 38%. Three-year cash conversion is 0.38 and FY26 was 0.46[1]. The company has said that its payment terms are 30 days and that clients pay within 45 days[12], but the FY26 cash flow statement shows receivables and other assets absorbing ₹330 crore of cash[7].

The September 2026 half-year cash flow statement, filed with the results, will show whether conversion is improving. We want half-year operating cash of at least ₹150 crore, which is about half of FY26's ₹319 crore[1].
