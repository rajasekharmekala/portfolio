1. What the company does

CCL Products (India) Limited is listed on the NSE as CCL and on the BSE as 519600. It makes instant coffee, which is coffee brewed in large columns and then dried into powder or granules that dissolve in hot water.[1][12][21] The March 2020 annual report, as quoted by Screener, says the company operates mainly in India, Vietnam and Switzerland. It supplies private-label and business-to-business customers worldwide and also sells a branded consumer line.[1] A private-label customer is a retailer or coffee brand that sells the product under its own name. The reach is more than 110 countries and 1,000 blends.[1]

Two drying methods matter. Spray-dried coffee is made by spraying extract into hot air, which is cheaper. Freeze-dried coffee is frozen and then dried under vacuum, which costs more to build and sells at a higher price. Management puts group capacity at about 77,000 tonnes a year, roughly 40,000 in India and 36,000 to 37,000 in Vietnam.[7] It said use was about 65 percent in FY26 and closer to 70 percent in October 2026, with freeze-dried plants busier than the average.[5][3] The largest Indian site, at Duggirala in Andhra Pradesh, has about 20,000 tonnes a year.[21]

The business model is cost-plus. When a customer signs a contract, CCL buys the green coffee for it at the same time, so a move in the coffee price does not change the profit on that contract.[5][4] Green coffee is about 60 to 70 percent of costs, so the rupee value of sales follows the coffee price while profit per kilogram stays near a set level.[7] Management quotes that profit, earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA), at ₹135 to ₹140 per kilogram.[3][8] Management gives guidance, which is its own published forecast for the year, in the same two terms, volume and EBITDA. The reader's task is therefore to watch volumes and the per-kilogram figure, not the sales line.

The sources of sales are worth stating in figures. Group turnover was ₹4,465.80 crore in FY26, up 43 percent, and ₹1,215.99 crore in Q2 FY27, up 7.8 percent, because green coffee was cheaper.[5][3] The India business was ₹650 crore in FY26, of which ₹440 crore was retail and branded, and in Q2 FY27 India sales were ₹210 crore gross with the brand at about ₹140 crore, up 25 percent. Management maintained FY27 branded guidance of ₹550 to ₹600 crore.[5][3] International branded sales are about ₹30 crore, with Percol in the UK at ₹25 to ₹30 crore, and lose ₹1 to ₹2 crore a year while the brands are built.[3] By our subtraction the export and other business-to-business line was about ₹3,816 crore in FY26 (₹4,465.80 crore less ₹650 crore), which our sources do not report separately. What this means for you: the business is mainly a contract manufacturer of instant coffee, so volume and per-kilogram profit decide value, and the India branded line is the only fast-growing piece that CCL owns outright.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for CCL is a mix of scale, flexibility and a repaired balance sheet. It is a large independent maker of instant coffee for other brands, with a share of the outsourced market that management says is still in single digits.[3] With plants in India and Vietnam and coffee bought from around the world, it can switch sources when one origin turns dear, as the chief executive said on the Q1 FY26 call.[6] Revenue rose from ₹2,071 crore in FY23 to ₹4,457 crore in FY26, a compound rate of 29.1 percent a year. Profit after tax rose from ₹284 crore to ₹388 crore, 11.0 percent a year.[1]

The weak points are the thin margin per rupee of sales, the cash that coffee ties up and the cost of building plants. EBITDA margin on turnover was 17.5 percent in the September 2026 quarter, unchanged from a year earlier, so profit comes from a conversion fee and not from pricing power.[10] Return on capital employed (ROCE), meaning the profit earned on the money the business uses, slid from 23 percent in FY15 to 12 percent in FY24 while new plants were built and debt rose. It recovered to 16 percent in FY26.[1] So the question for this stock is not growth but how much capital the growth needs.

The green coffee price cycle and the pass-through

Green coffee prices swing widely, and the swing reaches CCL through cash, not margin. Management says profit per kilogram does not change with the coffee price, because buying is matched to contracts.[4][5] What does change is the cost of carrying coffee. Inventory days fell from 208 in FY25 to 131 in FY26, the cash conversion cycle fell from 246 to 166 days, and cash from operations jumped from ₹290 crore to ₹858 crore as coffee got cheaper.[1] Gross debt peaked near ₹1,950 crore in December 2024 and was ₹1,268 crore at June 2026 on management's figures, while Screener shows borrowings of ₹1,324 crore at March 2026.[4][1]

The cycle is now turning down. Cofdar's summary of a September 2026 coffee market report (secondary) gives 167.49 US cents a pound for the canephora group, the hardy bean that dominates instant coffee, down 7.3 percent on the month. It says the report points to an estimated global surplus in the 2025/26 coffee year after several years of deficit.[18] The World Bank expects canephora prices to fall 18 percent in 2026 and 3 percent in 2027.[19] Management says the market has been range-bound at about $3,400 to $3,800 a tonne and that the Vietnam harvest in November and December will set the next direction.[3] Lower prices release working capital, which is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills. They bring customers back to longer contracts and push buyers toward freeze-dried products, but they cut sales in rupees, which is why Q2 value growth was 7.8 percent.[5][3]

A price spike is the risk. Vietnam supplies about 40 percent of the world's canephora. A Reuters report of October 2026 says the current crop looks steady but El Nino threatens the 2027/28 crop.[20] If prices rose sharply, margin per kilogram would hold on paper, but inventory and receivables would swell, debt would rise and customers would delay contracts, as management says they did in 2025.[7] What this means for you: coffee prices decide the cash and the volumes, so a rising price is a warning for the balance sheet even though it does not touch the per-kilogram profit.

Vietnam and Brazil capacity, and US and EU trade rules

Competition comes from other soluble coffee makers, notably in Vietnam and Brazil, and from Tata Consumer Products, which runs instant plants in Tamil Nadu and Telangana.[21] India exported 46,954 tonnes of instant coffee in 2025, up 11.56 percent.[21] Larger Brazilian supply was a main bearish factor for coffee prices in September 2026.[18] More supply lowers the green coffee price and the barrier for other soluble makers, so the contest is for the freeze-dried niche where CCL has built a lead.

US trade rules have moved in CCL's favour for now. Before the February 2026 trade deal, Indian coffee carried a 25 percent reciprocal duty in the US. The deal reduced it to zero.[16] On 23 July 2026 the US Trade Representative exempted coffee, including unflavored instant coffee, from the new Section 301 tariffs on forced-labor policies.[15] The exemption names unflavored instant only, and a trade magazine says heavily flavored and packaged coffees face a harder environment.[17] CCL's US share and flavored mix are not in our sources, so the exposure is unquantified. Indian instant exports to the US were 8,392 tonnes in 2025, about 18 percent of the national 46,954 tonnes. That is an industry figure and not CCL's.[16][21]

The EU Deforestation Regulation applies from 30 December 2026 and requires coffee placed on the EU market to be free of recent deforestation. EY's alert says a Commission review due by 30 April 2026 might change the rules. We did not confirm the outcome.[23] The filings we read do not quantify traceability costs. What this means for you: trade policy is a small positive today and a tail risk tomorrow, and the exposure figure that would settle it is not published.

The Vietnam plant, capacity and capital spending

In January 2023 the company announced a $50 million, 6,000-tonne freeze-dried unit in Vietnam, then expected by the December 2024 quarter.[22] On the October 2026 call the chief executive said that from the second quarter of the previous year the whole Vietnam freeze-dried capacity was in play.[3] That puts the plant roughly two quarters behind the original date, though the exact commissioning date is not in our record. The company capitalised about ₹25 to ₹30 crore of interest in FY25 while the plant was not running, and construction in progress fell from ₹501 crore at March 2024 to ₹3 crore at March 2026.[5][1] In November 2025 the new Vietnam capacity was only 15 to 20 percent used, and management expected a full ramp in three to four years.[7]

The next round of spending is nearer than management said earlier. In November 2025 it said the next decision would come at 80 to 85 percent blended use, two to three years away. In February 2026 it expected little capital spending in FY27 and FY28, and in May 2026 it said capacity was fine for two years.[7][8][5] In October 2026 it said freeze-dried plants could be close to full in a year to a year and a half. The last 6,000-tonne addition cost about ₹500 crore and a build takes nine to 12 months. Management wants customers to underwrite 30 to 50 percent of a new plant's volume first, and it will choose between new capacity and a contract-manufacturing tie-up by the fourth quarter of FY27 or the first quarter of FY28.[3] A ₹500 crore build is affordable against net debt of ₹742 crore, which is borrowings less cash held, but it ends the cash-flow holiday of FY26. What this means for you: the capacity decision is the next event that can change the earnings path, and the dates are management's.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

The estimates can be wrong in five places. The table at the end of this chapter changes one input at a time on the central case and shows the value and the ratio at the zone top of ₹908.30. Weakest assumption in the EPS estimate: that profit per kilogram stays flat while volume grows

The central case assumes EBITDA grows as fast as volume. That is what management guides, and what it had achieved for nine straight quarters until the September 2026 quarter, when EBITDA rose 9.5 percent against volume of 10 to 15 percent.[3] Management attributes the gap to marketing, freight and fuel and expects ₹135 to ₹140 to hold.[3] If per-kilogram profit settles at ₹130, the value falls to ₹960.31 and the zone-top ratio to 0.86.

Normalised-earnings stress: the trailing earnings per share of ₹34.58 flatters profit, because the trailing tax rate is about 11 percent against management's 17 percent.[1][7] At a 17 percent tax rate the trailing EPS would be ₹32.27 and the multiple 28.1 times, not 26.2. So the stock is less cheap than the headline multiple says, which is one reason we do not use the 35.4 times median as an anchor.

Weakest assumption in the cash view: that coffee stays cheap and stock days stay low

Management does not promise lower inventory days from here, and the Vietnam harvest season is a buying season. Net debt guidance is about ₹800 crore against ₹742 crore now, and gross debt of ₹1,000 to ₹1,200 crore against ₹1,192 crore now.[3][5] If coffee rose sharply, as the El Nino risk to the 2027/28 crop suggests it could, stock and receivables would swell, debt would head back toward the December 2024 peak and the cash row would slip toward FAIL, though per-kilogram profit would hold on paper.[20][4] A finance-cost stress of ₹12 crore a year, which is 100 basis points on ₹1,200 crore, lowers the value only to ₹1,047.32. Interest is not the main risk. Customers delaying contracts while prices swing is, and it would show first in volume growth.

Freeze-dried plants may be close to full in a year to a year and a half.
