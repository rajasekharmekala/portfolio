1. What the company does

In plain words: Grindwell makes abrasives, ceramics and polymer parts for factories, and a French group controls it. It has a large cash pile and almost no debt.

Grindwell Norton makes tools that grind, cut and polish metal and other materials in factories. Its Norton-brand abrasives are grinding wheels, thin cutting discs, sandpaper belts and discs, and diamond and boron nitride wheels for precision work. Customers are steel mills, foundries, bearing and car-part makers, engineering shops and, through dealers, small workshops. The company says no single industry is more than 15 percent of its abrasives sales.[3] Four plants make abrasives, at Mora near Mumbai, Bengaluru, Nagpur and Baddi, and the company says it makes more than 15,000 different products a year.[3]

The second business is ceramics and plastics. Ceramics and refractories (heat-proof linings) are made from silicon carbide, a hard mineral, for steel plants, sanitaryware makers and armour; the company makes the silicon carbide itself at Tirupati and through a 70 percent Bhutan subsidiary.[3] Plastics means performance polymers: bearings, seals, tubing, single-use bags for drug makers and adhesive tapes used in cars and battery packs.[3] The third piece is Digital Services, an in-house IT centre whose main customer is the Saint-Gobain group itself.[3]

The company makes money on the gap between what it charges and what materials and labour cost. In FY26 the gross margin, which is the share of sales left after materials, was 53.6%, down from 54.6% in FY25 and 55.1% in FY24 on our arithmetic from the filed statements.[1] [3] Staff cost was 11.4% of sales and other expenses 23.6%. Operating profit before depreciation, or EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), was ₹573.65 crore, an 18.7% margin; it was 18.3% in FY25 and 19.3% in FY24.[1] [3] The company adds ₹94.92 crore of other income, mostly interest and gains on its cash, which was 17.1 percent of pre-tax profit in FY26.[1]

It has almost no debt and about ₹1,158 crore of cash, bank deposits and short-term investments, and a French group, Saint-Gobain, controls it and also supplies technology, some of the goods it sells and most of the customers for its IT centre.[1] [3] The next table shows the four segments, then a second table puts every Saint-Gobain flow against the right denominator.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

In plain words: This is a leading, debt-free business with slow abrasives growth and close ties to its parent. Each strength and weakness is weighed with its evidence.

Grindwell Norton is a strong business that is valued as if it were a stronger one. The strengths are a leading position in Indian abrasives, a technology link to a global group, no debt and a high return on the money invested. The weaknesses are slow abrasives growth, margins that have slipped since FY23, dependence on the parent for technology and IT revenue, and a price that already assumes a long run of growth. This chapter weighs each, and Chapter 3 puts numbers on them.

Where the strength is

The company calls itself the market leader in Indian abrasives and says the industry has two major players and many small ones.[3] Its FY26 Board's report says the business achieved a "measurable improvement in market standing against a key competitor in India" while volume growth stayed moderate, but it gives no market-share figure, so we cannot measure the claim.[4] The seed's claim of a market share above 50 percent in bonded abrasives is not in any source we could read, and we do not use it.

Technology comes from Saint-Gobain, which runs research centres in the US and elsewhere; the company's own research spending was only ₹3.06 crore in FY25, or 0.11 percent of revenue.[3] That is a dependence as well as a strength. The royalty of ₹44.53 crore in FY25 is the price of the link.[3]

The balance sheet is the third strength. At 31 March 2026 borrowings were ₹1.01 crore and leases ₹59.70 crore, against ₹1,158.20 crore of cash, bank deposits and current investments.[1] Return on capital employed (ROCE) was 21.0% on our arithmetic, and operating cash flow was 1.17 times profit over three years (Chapter 3).

The fourth is mix. Ceramics and plastics grew faster than abrasives in FY26 (10.3 against 6.3 percent) and in the June 2026 quarter (18.8 against 10.0 percent), and its margin of 21.2 percent in that quarter is well above the abrasives margin of 12.7 percent.[1] [2] The Board's report says polymers grew strongly on car and drug-maker demand and Halol added silicon-carbide ceramic capacity.[4]

Where the weakness is

Abrasives grew sales 6 percent and operating profit 6 percent in FY26, so its segment margin stayed at 12.9 percent; the Board's report says overall volume growth was moderate while demand in core segments stayed healthy.[4] [1] The gross margin fell from 55.1% in FY24 to 53.6% in FY26. Company-wide EBITDA margin recovered from 18.3% to 18.7% only because staff and other costs grew more slowly than sales.[1] [3]

The Board's report says sales and operating profit fell because of price pressure from cheap imports, and the Bhutan subsidiary was subdued by higher power and input costs.[4] Ceramics exports also fell, which the report blames on geopolitics and US tariffs.[4]

The group ties are a weakness as well as a strength. Group companies supplied 23.2% of materials and traded-goods cost in FY25 and were the customer for the whole Digital Services segment, which earned a 26 percent segment margin that depends on group transfer pricing.[3] [1] Trade payables rose from ₹425.28 crore to ₹514.32 crore in FY26, so part of the cash conversion is borrowed from suppliers (Chapter 3).[1]

The cycle: Indian auto and industry, Europe and China

Indian demand is strong. Industrial production grew 8.0 percent in August 2026, manufacturing 9.0 percent and capital goods 16.9 percent.[12] [13] The HSBC manufacturing PMI (a monthly survey where 50 means no change) rose to 55.1 in September from 52.8, though the second-quarter average of 53.8 was the lowest since 2021, and the flash reading for October was 54.9.[14] [15] Passenger vehicle wholesale dispatches rose 20.9 percent in September.[16]

China matters in three ways. First, its 13 percent export VAT rebate on abrasives and ceramics was withdrawn from April 2026, which narrows the price gap with Chinese imports and is cited in broker notes as a margin help.[11] [9] Second, the FY26 Board's report says cheap imports hurt silicon-carbide prices.[4] Third, the company reports nothing by country, so its own China sourcing is not in the record.

Europe and the US show up mainly through the group. The filings give no revenue by country. What they give is foreign-exchange earnings of ₹435.40 crore in FY25 against ₹738.15 crore of outgo, so the company is a net importer.[3] About ₹290.40 crore of that came from group companies on our arithmetic (services ₹157.69 crore plus goods ₹132.71 crore), leaving at most about ₹145 crore, or 5 percent of standalone revenue, from other export customers.[3] A broker expects a gradual recovery in exports to Europe and North America; that is a view, not a filed fact.[9]

Margins, input costs, the dividend and capital spending

Input costs are the main swing factor. Foreign-exchange outflow was ₹738.15 crore in FY25 (it likely includes royalty and dividends paid abroad as well as imports), so a weaker rupee raises costs, and the FY25 report names rupee depreciation as a risk for the polymer business, which is import-intensive.[3] The first-quarter EBITDA margin was 19.55 percent against 18.46 percent a year earlier; one broker attributes the gain to mix and operating leverage and adds that Chinese import costs are elevated.[2] [9]

The dividend is steady and growing. The Board recommended ₹19 a share for FY26 against ₹17 for FY25 and FY24, a cash outflow of ₹210.37 crore, or 50.5 percent of consolidated profit, and the record date was 10 July 2026.[1] [4][3]

Capital spending was ₹118.91 crore in FY26 against ₹94.06 crore in FY25 and ₹172.37 crore in FY24, close to depreciation of ₹105.12 crore.[1] [3] The Board's report names expansion of silicon-carbide ceramics at Halol and a freehold land purchase there as steps for long-term growth, and small renewable-power contributions of up to ₹86 lakh and ₹111 lakh.[4] No capex budget or return target was published; contracted plant commitments were ₹50.68 crore at 31 March 2025.[3]
