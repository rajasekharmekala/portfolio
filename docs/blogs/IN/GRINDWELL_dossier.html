What the company does

Grindwell Norton is an Indian factory supplier. It sells grinding wheels and abrasive belts that shape metal and other hard materials, and it makes ceramic and plastic parts for industry. The company describes itself as a maker of abrasives, ceramics and performance plastics, with a digital services unit that serves its parent group.[13] It employed 2,385 people.[28] Its parent is Compagnie de Saint-Gobain, the French materials group.[1]

The customers are other factories. A summary of the June quarter lists automotive, steel, bearing and general engineering as the core end markets.[6] The company's own report says it expects to benefit from demand in those markets and from newer areas, including electronics, semiconductors, defence and aerospace.[2]

The company reports in three parts. Abrasives is the largest. Ceramics and plastics makes engineered silicon carbide, refractory products and performance polymers. The digital unit runs software and infrastructure work for the group.[2] In FY26, consolidated revenue from operations was 3,073.19 crore rupees, and product sales were 2,788.18 crore of that. A crore is ten million rupees.[2]

Growth differed by part. Abrasives sales rose 6 per cent. Ceramics and plastics sales rose 10 per cent and its operating profit rose 15 per cent. Silicon carbide, one product inside that part, had a hard year because cheap imports pushed prices down, and exports fell because of US tariffs.[2]

Terms used in this report

Each term is defined where it first appears and is repeated here for reference. The list covers the measures and chart words the report uses.

  • return on capital employed (ROCE) is a measure of how much operating profit a business earns on the money tied up in it. A higher figure means the business turns its capital into profit more efficiently.
  • earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit for the year divided by its number of shares. It is the profit available to each share.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is a rough measure of operating profit before financing costs, tax and the wear on plant and intangibles. It is used to compare the core operations of companies.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is the cash a business has left after paying for its running costs and the equipment it buys. It is the cash that can go to owners or lenders.
  • relative strength index (RSI) is a score from 0 to 100 over 14 sessions that shows how fast a price has risen or fallen recently. Above 70 is often called overheated and below 30 oversold.
  • average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions, in rupees. It is a yardstick for how far a price normally moves in a day.
  • Stage 2 is the phase when a stock trades above a rising 50-day average and above a rising 200-day average, which is the uptrend most buyers want.
  • Stage 4 is the phase when a stock breaks down below falling averages, which usually marks a downtrend.
  • base is a sideways pause in price after a fall. A base can be the start of a new uptrend if the price then breaks out.
  • pivot is the price a stock has to clear to confirm that a base is ending.
  • breakout is a close above a pivot, ideally on higher than normal trading volume.
  • Stage 1 is the phase after a decline when a new base forms and selling slows, so the price moves sideways before it can rise.
  • Stage 3 is the topping phase, when the price stalls near its high, slips back and its averages flatten. It often comes before a fall.
  • Core is one of the two buy categories in the rules: a longer-term holding bought for the quality of the business, which needs Stage 1 or Stage 2.
  • Tactical is the other buy category: a shorter-term trade bought on a chart setup, which needs Stage 2 with rising averages.
  • operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales. It shows how much of each rupee of sales is kept after running costs.
  • debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' equity. It measures how much the company relies on lenders.
  • asymmetry is the ratio of the gain to the target to the loss to the stop. A ratio of 3 means the possible gain is three times the possible loss.
  • measured move is a target found by adding the height of a base to its pivot.
  • entry zone is the price range in which a buy would be allowed under the rules.

Why the business is strong or weak

The company's main asset is its brand and the technology behind it. The company says its strength comes from its association with Compagnie de Saint-Gobain and its subsidiaries.[2] In our reading, which is not the company's own wording, a parent that supplies process know-how and customer relationships is hard for a smaller rival to copy quickly.

The market grows, but slowly. Ken Research sizes India's abrasives market at 557 million US dollars in 2025 and expects 6.89 per cent a year growth to 2031.[14] IMARC gives 2.3 billion US dollars for 2025 and 5.83 per cent a year to 2034.[15] Two other publishers put growth at 6.1 per cent and 7.9 per cent a year.[16][17] That is a steady market, not a boom, and the company's growth will track it closely.

Ken Research names Carborundum Universal, 3M India, Wendt India and Klingspor India as the other major players.[14] A separate market listing also names Grindwell Norton among the key players in India's abrasives market.[30] The company's own report says it improved its market standing against a key competitor in India, though overall volume growth was moderate.[2]

Two weak spots show up in the company's own words. Silicon carbide faced price pressure from cheap imports, and exports fell after US tariffs.[2] A strong brand does not protect a product from those price and trade risks, so they are the points to watch. Governance and quality signals are mixed. The company's ESG rating for FY2025-26 was CRISIL ESG 60, and the company said it did not engage the rating agency for that score.[20] Its June quarter figures were reviewed under a limited review by Kalyaniwalla & Mistry LLP, not audited.[7]
