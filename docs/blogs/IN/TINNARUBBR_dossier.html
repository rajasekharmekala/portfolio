Before you read

Tinna turns scrap tyres into rubber products that go into roads, industrial goods, steel cleaning and consumer items, and it earns extra cash by selling tyre recycling credits.[17] The business is good on paper: the audited year to March 2026 (prior period) shows a 22.5% return on capital and a 0.43 times debt-to-equity ratio, and the June 2026 quarter showed a 21.7% operating margin.[6][12][17] We still reject the stock today, because the fresh 9 October 2026 price data fails the trend floor: the price is below a falling 50-day average, the stage is 3, and neither momentum test passes.[1] Two of the five business checks are on watch, so the business score is 3 out of 5 against a floor of 4.[38] The call is wrong if the price closes above about ₹1,023 on rising volume with the 50-day average turning up, or if the next two quarters show margins well above 18% with receivables falling.

What the company does

Tinna collects used tyres and crushes them into reclaimed rubber, crumb and fine rubber powder. It sells these to road builders, industrial makers, steel-abrasive buyers and consumer-product makers.[17] The company reports its FY26 revenue (prior period) as 38% Infrastructure, 30% Industrial, 20% Steel, 8% Consumer and 4% PCMB.[17] The abbreviation PCMB is the company's own; the pages read for this report do not spell it out, so we use it as written.

Money comes from three places. First, customers pay per tonne for recycled rubber products. Second, producers of new tyres must buy recycling credits, and Tinna sells them. The credits brought in about ₹29.3 crore in FY26 (prior period).[12] Third, a 49.42%-owned associate, TP Buildtech, added revenue of ₹19 crore and EBITDA of ₹3 crore in the June 2026 quarter, which is shown separately from Tinna's own sales.[17]

The company says it crushed 44,238 tonnes in India in the June 2026 quarter, up 35% from a year earlier, at 88% utilisation in India and 78% in Oman.[17][18] India crushing capacity is planned to rise from 185,000 tonnes in FY26 to 235,000 tonnes in FY27.[18] CARE puts installed capacity at 200,000 tonnes a year in March 2026, with 15,000 tonnes in Oman and a 24,000-tonne Saudi plant expected around Q1 of FY28.[14]

The road business rests on an Indian Roads Congress guideline that describes a gap-graded road surface made with rubberised bitumen, using crumb rubber from waste tyres.[36] Management says the quarter of April to June is the peak road season and July to September is weak because of the monsoon.[20] we could not read a Ministry of Road Transport circular on this topic (the fetch returned nothing), so the size of any government demand push is N/A in this report.

Who pays matters. CARE says the top ten customers are about 36% of sales, exports are about 8% of sales, and about 54% of raw material purchases are imported.[14] Raw material is above 55% of production cost.[14] That mix means the company depends on a few buyers and on imported feed, so a supply shock abroad hits margins quickly. Management itself says the West Asia conflict is affecting bitumen supply, the Oman plant and the consumer segment.[20]

What this means for you: Tinna is a small industrial recycler with real growth and a policy-linked cash stream, not a pure commodity trader. The next chapter asks whether the business is strong enough to deserve a place on the shortlist.

Glossary for this report

EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation) is the profit from running the business before financing and accounting charges. ROCE (return on capital employed) is operating profit after tax divided by the money tied up in the business; our floor is 15%. EPR (extended producer responsibility) is the rule that makes tyre makers pay for recycling, through tradable certificates that recyclers such as Tinna sell.[37] QIP (qualified institutions placement) is a share sale to large institutions. DMA (day moving average) is the average closing price over that many days, which acts as a trend line. ATR (average true range) is the normal daily price wobble, used to place stops. RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer for price moves: under 30 is stretched down and over 70 is stretched up. CFO (cash flow from operations) is cash earned from the business itself. MRP is the company's abbreviation for a fine rubber powder product; its expansion is not spelled out in the pages read.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate has one assumption that matters most. The test for each, and the date it will be known, follows.

FY27 earnings of ₹39. The weakest assumption is the 18.5% EBITDA margin. Funding the build-out. CARE says about ₹100 crore of equity is planned for FY28, subject to approvals, and the Tirupati plant of about ₹200 crore reaches phase 1 only by Q4 of FY28.[14] A share sale would dilute today's holders, so our per-share targets assume none; at the planned ₹100 crore raise priced near today's ₹885.85, the share count would rise about 6.3%, cutting the ₹39 EPS case to roughly ₹36.6 and the ₹1,310 target to about ₹1,230. Management itself says most of the nearer-term ₹100 crore capex will be funded from internal accruals.[21] The test is any board resolution or exchange filing on fundraising.

Receivables and working capital. The test is the next CARE review and the March 2027 annual numbers: an operating cycle above 90 days or debt above 2.5 times operating profit is a negative trigger for the rating.[14] Management expects blended working capital days to stay near 50, which is below the 64-day Screener cash conversion cycle; the two measures differ in definition.[20][6]

Overseas exposure. The company continues to put money abroad: on 7 October 2026 it put the first CLP 10,000,000 tranche (₹10.04 lakh) into a new Chile subsidiary, with a cap of ₹5 crore; a fifth SAR 102,000 tranche into its Saudi subsidiary; and a second US$400,000 loan (₹3.86 crore) to a South African joint venture.[29][30][31] Each amount is small, but start-up costs in Saudi Arabia and South Africa are expected to weigh on margins.[20]

Trigger to revisit the verdict: a close above about ₹1,023 (the 50-day average) on a volume multiple of at least 1.0, with the 50-day average flat or rising, 63-day relative strength above 0% and the stage back at 2. Re-run the momentum gate before any allocation.

Trigger on the downside: a close below ₹870.2 marks support as broken. A close below ₹838.54 (the 200-day average) would remove the one momentum check that still passes (A2) and moves the stock toward Stage 4; the next level after that is the 52-week low of ₹527.45.[1]

Trigger on the business: the September 2026 quarter result. An EBITDA margin of at least 18% with falling debtor days would move the working-capital check from WATCH to PASS and lift the KPI score to 4 of 5. A margin below 14% or an operating cycle above 90 days would be the rating negative trigger and would deepen the rejection.[14]

Trigger on funding: an announcement of the roughly ₹100 crore equity raise would be treated as dilution; recompute per-share targets on the new share count before any decision.

What this means for you: leave Tinna on the watch list and do not size a position. The business may deserve a second look after the September quarter, but the price has to agree first.
