Apar makes three things. It makes conductors, which are the wires that carry electricity along transmission towers. The company sells to electric utilities and to industrial and data-centre buyers, and about 30 percent of its revenue comes from exports [1].

The first idea to know is the moving average. A 20-day or 50-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 or 50 sessions. Think of it as a trend line that smooths out the daily noise. When the price sits above a rising average, buyers have been in control for a while.

The second idea is the Weinstein stage. Stan Weinstein described four phases a stock moves through, like four seasons. Stage 1 is a base, Stage 2 is the markup when the trend is up, Stage 3 is a top, and Stage 4 is decline. Our hurdle is Stage 2: price above a rising 50-day average and above the 200-day average. A Stage 4 breakdown is an immediate problem.

The third idea is RSI, the relative strength index. It is a speedometer from 0 to 100 that measures how fast the price has moved recently. A reading above 70 means the stock has run hard and is overheated. A reading below 30 means it has fallen hard. Our reading of 54 is in the normal range.

The fourth idea is ATR, the average true range. ATR measures the normal daily wobble of a stock. Here it is about 720 rupees, or 4 percent of the price. We use it as a ruler: distances are measured in ATRs, so a 1 ATR move is a normal day's move, not a shock.

The fifth idea is support. Support is a price level where buyers have stepped in before. We use the 20-day and 50-day averages and the recent swing low. The support ladder in our rule book then asks how many ATRs the price sits above that support. Within half an ATR is the full-size band. Between half and 1.2 ATR is the standard band. Beyond 1.2 ATR is the no-buy band, and the stock is called extended.

The sixth idea is the stop-loss. A stop is an exit price you set in advance, so the decision is made before emotion takes over. Our stop for this stock is below the recent swing low and the averages, with a half-ATR buffer. If the stop is hit, the rule says the position is no longer supported by its trend.

The seventh idea is the asymmetry ratio. It divides the upside to a defensible target by the downside to the stop. A ratio of 3.5 means the possible gain is 3.5 times the possible loss. Our floor for a Tactical Momentum name is 3.5. A ratio below the floor means the reward does not pay for the risk, however good the business is.

The eighth idea is cash conversion, measured as operating cash flow divided by profit after tax (CFO/PAT). Profit is an accounting figure. Operating cash flow is the money that actually came in. If a company reports profit but collects little cash, the profit may not be real. Our hurdle is 0.70 over three years.

What the company does

Chart is listed on the NSE and BSE and describes itself as the world's largest aluminium conductor maker and India's largest exporter of conductors [2]. Its market value at the 9 October close was ₹75,303 crore [2]. Three divisions produce almost all of its revenue, and each has a different customer and a different risk.

Conductors: the largest division

A conductor is the bare metal wire that carries power on a transmission line. The conductor division is the core of the company. Its revenue reached ₹12,712 crore in FY26, up 32.7 percent on the year before [1]. Against consolidated FY26 revenue of ₹22,902 crore [2], that is about 55 percent of the total. The division's order book was ₹10,190 crore at 30 June 2026 [3], and the company says most of it is executable over the next year or so [4].

The product that matters for the investment case is the high-temperature, low-sag conductor, usually called HTLS. An HTLS conductor carries far more current than an older steel-reinforced wire of the same size, and it sags less when hot. The company's own product page describes its ACCC design as an HTLS conductor [5]. For a utility, the appeal is that it can double the capacity of an old line by swapping the wire, without rebuilding the towers. That swap is called reconductoring.

The company groups HTLS with other high-value products. Its FY26 annual report says premium conductors, including HTLS and AL59, make up 45.8 percent of conductor segment revenue and a little over half of the order book [1]. The company does not publish an HTLS-only share, so we cannot isolate HTLS from the group.

Transformer and specialty oils

Transformer oil is the liquid inside large transformers. It cools the windings and stops electrical sparks from causing damage. The company's oil business sells these oils and other specialty oils. The Q1 FY27 call gave the oil division's EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) per kilolitre as ₹25,482, against ₹7,004 a year earlier, but this figure was boosted by inventory gains and a provision of about ₹93 crore that the company booked in the same quarter [6]. Oil volumes fell 13.7 percent in the quarter because of restrictions at the UAE facility and a port closure at Hamriyah [4].

Oil is also an export business. A July 2026 presentation summary says about 42 percent of transformer oil volumes were sold overseas in FY26 [7]. The prior record put this figure above 46 percent. That number does not match the company's stated figure, so we use 42 percent.

Cables

The cable division makes power and telecom cables, plus cables for data centres. Cable revenue in Q1 FY27 was ₹1,838 crore, up 29.5 percent [6]. The pending cable order book at the end of June was ₹1,925 crore [3] [6]. Management said the company had received approvals from Meta, Microsoft and Google to supply cables for their US data centres [8]. The same call said the company had an approval from one of the largest US utilities for a fibre-optic ground wire with 144 fibre counts [6]. These approvals are the basis for the data-centre part of the thesis. They are not yet a large revenue line. Their revenue effect will show up in the cable line over the next few quarters, and the company has not yet published a data-centre revenue figure. Until it does, the approvals support the thesis rather than adding a number to it.

Where the money is made

The company exports 29.77 percent of its FY26 revenue and sells the rest at home [1]. In Q1 FY27 the export mix fell to 27.5 percent from 31.6 percent a year earlier [6]. Exports matter for two reasons. They bring in dollars, and they expose the company to US tariffs. The annual report says the US duty applied to its conductors is the same whatever the country of origin, so the tariff does not disadvantage the company against rivals, though it did slow business for part of FY26 [1]. That statement is the company's view. The chapter on what could happen next tests it.

The company has three reporting lines in its results. Read them in this order: conductor revenue and order book show the core demand; oil EBITDA per kilolitre shows the commodity-sensitive part; cable revenue and order book show the newest growth line. A quarter can look strong because one division had a one-off gain. Look at the three together before you judge the quarter.

Cash conversion: the gate passes, but one year is negative

Cash conversion measures how much of the reported profit arrives as cash from running the business. Think of a shop that books a sale on credit: the profit is on paper until the customer pays. Valuation against the stock's own history

Valuation asks what price you pay for each rupee of earnings. The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) is the usual tool. On the Screener header the stock trades at a P/E of 62.5 times trailing earnings [2]. That is high for any industrial company, and it is the central reason the asymmetry gate fails.

A P/E on its own says little. The rule compares the stock with its own history. Glossary

ATR: average true range, the normal daily move of a stock, used as a ruler for distances. CFO/PAT: operating cash flow divided by profit after tax, a check on whether profit turns into cash. HTLS: high-temperature, low-sag conductor, a wire that carries more current and sags less than an older design. Moving average: the average close over a set number of sessions, shown here as the 20-day and 50-day lines. P/E: price divided by earnings per share. QIP: qualified institutions placement, a share sale to large investors that adds new shares. ROCE: return on capital employed, operating profit divided by the capital used in the business. Stage 2: the markup phase of the Weinstein cycle, a price above a rising 50-day and 200-day average. Support ladder: the rule that maps distance from support, in ATRs, to a buy band.
