Apar Industries makes the conductors, cables and transformer oils that power grids use.[18][15] Our verdict on the 7 shares you hold is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, which means keep them and add none.[2] The business passes every solvency test, but the ₹14,713.00 close on 22 October 2026 already prices in about 19 percent profit growth a year for ten years.[1] We call it a compounder with a partial fit: it passes the quality tests, yet by our estimate 38 percent of last quarter's profit was a one-off oil gain.[3][18] The view fails if results due on 29 October 2026 show quarterly profit, without any oil gain, below about ₹251 crore, the average of the last four quarters, and chapter 7 lists every action, including a sale on any close below ₹12,911.43[20].

1. What the company does

Apar Industries sells three families of products. Its customers are power utilities, construction contractors, railways, equipment makers and exporters. CARE Ratings calls it India's largest conductor maker by FY26 sales. It also calls it India's largest private-sector maker of speciality oils by installed capacity, and an established maker of speciality cables.[18] A conductor is the bare metal wire strung between transmission towers. It is the part of a power line that carries the electricity. Most are aluminium wire around a steel core. About half of the conductor revenue now comes from premium types[3]. One example is a high-temperature low-sag conductor, which carries more power on existing towers. A utility can then upgrade a line without building new towers.

The company earns a processing fee on metal and oil, not a margin on the metal itself. A conductor contract is priced as the metal cost plus a conversion fee. The company generally hedges the metal when it books an order, so the fee is what is left after the metal passes through.[18] That is why we track EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), which is the profit from running the business before financing and accounting charges. We follow it per tonne of conductor and per kilolitre of oil. In FY26 the conductor division earned ₹43,012 of EBITDA per tonne on 2,41,788 tonnes. The oil division earned ₹5,942 per kilolitre on 6,31,985 kilolitres.[5] Sales include the metal price. Revenue can therefore rise when aluminium rises, even if tonnes fall. In Q1 FY27 conductor revenue grew 19.9 percent while tonnes fell 6.7 percent.[3]

The customer list is fairly spread out. The top ten customers were about 19 percent of FY26 sales. They include KEC International, Kalpataru Projects, Larsen & Toubro, Bharat Heavy Electricals, Indian Railways and state transmission utilities. Exports were about 30 percent of consolidated revenue, and the company sells in over 140 countries.[18] Profit is not smooth, because three things move it from quarter to quarter. The first is the timing of customer delivery clearances. The second is the price of aluminium, copper and the oil feedstock. The third is the direction of oil prices. When oil prices rise, the oil division earns a one-off premium on stock bought earlier at lower cost. Management said so in July 2026, and that effect is why the latest quarter looks so strong.[4]

At 31 March 2026 the installed capacity was 5,55,262 tonnes for conductors, 9,34,600 kilolitres for oils and 9,50,656 kilometres for cables.[18] The company is now in a much larger spending cycle. It spent about ₹500 crore on capex (spending on new plants and machines) in FY25 and about ₹740 crore in FY26. It plans about ₹1,500 crore more in FY27.[9][6] In August 2026 it sold 16,88,618 new shares to institutions at ₹14,805 each. The sale raised a net ₹2,472 crore. It lifted the share count by about 4.2 percent, from 4,01,74,235 to about 4,18,62,853.[23][18][15] What this means for an owner: this is a large manufacturer that ties up a lot of cash in stock and customer bills. Its margins depend on product mix and on metal and oil prices. The next chapter asks how much protection that gives it. The table below first puts numbers on each division.

Who the customers are and why demand is rising

Transmission lines are the main driver. Management cited the National Electricity Plan, which expects India's transmission network to grow from about 5 lakh to about 6.5 lakh circuit kilometres by 2032. India added 113,000 megavolt-amperes of transformation capacity in FY26, 30 percent more than in FY25.[6] Every new line needs conductors. Old lines can be upgraded by replacing the wire, which is called reconductoring, and the company says it has done more than 272 such projects.[25] Data centres are the second driver, in India and in the United States. The company reports approvals from the contractors that serve Meta, Microsoft and Google.[4]

Demand also depends on foreign policy. All bare aluminium products, including rods and conductors, face a 50 percent United States duty under Section 232. Management says customers are paying it, because local United States producers pay the same duty on their aluminium.[4] About 70 percent of the United States conductor market is supplied from local production. The company's addressable share is the roughly 30 percent that utilities import.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ROCE (return on capital employed) — the profit a business earns on each rupee of capital it uses; higher means the capital works harder.
  • EPS (earnings per share) — the company's profit divided by the number of shares; it is the profit that belongs to one share.
  • RSI (relative strength index) — a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been moving; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) — the average daily price swing in rupees; this report uses the 14-day figure to measure distances in units of a normal day.
  • free cash flow (FCF) — cash left after running the business and buying plants and machines.
  • 20-day moving average — the average closing price of the last 20 trading days. The 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the same idea over longer spans, and the price often bounces off them.
  • Stage 2 — the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale. Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend, and Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase between them.
  • base — a sideways stretch the price builds before a move, the platform from which a rise starts. The pivot is the top of that stretch. A breakout is a close above it on rising volume, and a retest is the pullback that tests the level from above.
  • stop loss — the price at which the reason for owning the stock is wrong and the position is cut. A limit order fills only at your price or better and never chases. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry (also reward-to-risk) — the gain to a target divided by the loss to the stop, that is (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). The tactical floor is 2.5 to 1.
  • operating margin — operating profit as a share of revenue. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, and price-to-earnings is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit.
  • working capital — the cash tied up in day-to-day operations, mainly stock and money owed by customers, less what is owed to suppliers. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before financing.
  • guidance — the company's own published forecast. A consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts. Dilution is the fall in each share's claim when new shares are issued.
  • book-to-bill — new orders divided by revenue in the same period; above 1 means the order book is growing. Capital expenditure (capex) is spending on plants and equipment.
  • Other terms used here: an order book is signed work not yet delivered. HTLS means a high-temperature low-sag conductor. A QIP is a qualified institutions placement, a share sale to large institutions. LC acceptances are supplier bills guaranteed by a bank, which work like short-term loans. The next chapter uses these terms to judge how strong the business is.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest part of the case is scale and position. The company is India's largest conductor maker. Its conductor order book was ₹10,190 crore at 30 June 2026, and 56.8 percent of it is for export customers. In Q1 FY27 it won new conductor orders of ₹5,245 crore.[3][18] That includes more than ₹2,800 crore from two overseas utilities, one in the United States and one in Europe, with delivery spread over about four years.[3] Those two orders are larger than the whole rise from ₹7,671 crore at 31 March 2026.[5] The order book is worth about 0.77 times the last twelve months of conductor revenue, which means roughly nine months of work is already signed. That gives revenue visibility. It is one of the reasons CARE upgraded the rating to AA on 9 September 2026 from AA- a year earlier.[18][19]

The second strength is product mix. Premium products earn far more per tonne. They were 50.3 percent of conductor revenue in Q1 FY27, against 43.7 percent a year earlier. Conductor EBITDA per tonne rose from ₹36,683 in FY25 to ₹43,012 in FY26.[3][5] Management guides to ₹35,000 to ₹36,000 per tonne over the medium term. That is below what it has recently earned, so the guide looks cautious.[6] The cable division adds a newer strength. It is now approved as a supplier to the data-centre contractors of three large technology companies. That opens copper and medium-voltage cable orders it could not bid for before.[4] CARE also notes moderate customer concentration, with the top ten customers at about 19 percent of FY26 sales.[18]

The weaknesses are real, and they explain why the operating margin (operating profit as a share of revenue) is only 8.4 percent in FY26 on the Screener definition.[25] First, the business ties up a lot of cash. CARE puts the working-capital need at ₹8,520 crore in FY26, about 37 percent of operating income. Suppliers and bank-guaranteed supplier bills fund most of it, and limit use was about 88 percent over the twelve months to May 2026.[18] Second, margins depend on prices the company does not control. The metal premium over the London price cannot be hedged. When aluminium jumped in spring 2026, some customers delayed delivery clearances, and conductor tonnes fell 6.7 percent from a year earlier.[4] Third, conductor revenue in the United States fell 34.0 percent in Q1 FY27 after the Section 232 duty changes, even though management calls the United States a strong growth market.[3][7]

Competition is a fourth pressure. In May 2026 management said domestic players were adding capacity, which brought some pricing pressure. It also said the gap between London and Shanghai aluminium prices, about $330 to $360 a tonne, favours Chinese exporters.[6] We judge the moat to be narrow and built on execution. It rests on product approvals, long customer relationships, scale in aluminium conversion and a spread across three divisions. It is not pricing power. Operating margin was only 6.2 percent in FY22, and the FY26 level of 8.4 percent is below the 9.7 percent of FY24.[25] This is our inference from the margin history, not a company statement.

What this means for an owner: the franchise is good enough for a place on the watch list, and the evidence is filed, not promised. But the margin is thin. A bad quarter of metal timing or oil prices can move profit by a third. The next chapter turns this into five scored measures and checks whether management has done what it said it would.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions. This chapter names the weakest one under each estimate. It says what evidence would show it wrong, and the date on which we would see that evidence. It also states the strongest case against our own view, as a sceptical analyst would write it. Last, it picks the single claim on which the whole conclusion hinges.

Base-case profit: the 8.8 percent margin

The weakest assumption in the FY28 base case is the margin. Our 8.8 percent sits between 8.4 percent in FY26 and 9.7 percent in FY24. It assumes the oil division returns to a normal profit of roughly ₹376 crore a year. It also assumes conductors hold at or above management's range of ₹35,000 to ₹36,000 per tonne.[25][6] Three readings would show it wrong. One is oil profit per kilolitre staying far above ₹5,000 to ₹6,000. Another is oil profit falling far below that range. The third is conductor profit per tonne below ₹35,000. We will see the first evidence when the Q2 FY27 results are filed on 29 October 2026. The management call follows at 3:30 p.m. IST.[20][21] The Q3 results date has not been announced.

Reverse DCF and the earnings base

The reverse DCF assumes trailing profit of ₹1,182 crore is a fair base. If a large part of it is a one-off, the growth demanded is higher. If cash conversion is better than in FY26, the number looks less demanding. The Q2 filing will include the first half-year balance sheet and cash flow statement since March 2026. It will show whether the working-capital build has slowed. It will also show how much of the ₹2,472 crore of net share-sale proceeds has gone to reducing supplier bills, as CARE expects.[18] The proceeds are also why net debt, which is borrowings less cash, will look much better after September 2026. That is a one-time improvement and says little about trading.

Multiples, probabilities and gap risk

The base multiple of 39.6 is the median of the last three years. In that period the stock traded between 24.4 and 65.5 times earnings. Nothing says the next year will resemble the median. The probabilities of 20, 50 and 30 percent are our judgement. We show how the weighted value moves under other weights. The stop has a weakness too, because a daily-close stop does not protect against a gap. If the results on 29 October are poor, the stock could open well below ₹12,911.43. The loss would then be larger than the ₹12,610.99 we quote. In the bear scenario the value of ₹8,410.17 would mean a loss of ₹44,120 on 7 shares. That is 1.02 percent of net worth.

The red-team bear case

A sceptical analyst would make five points. First, the headline profit is the oil gain. CARE itself says the oil margin is not expected to sustain, so Q1 FY27 is a high-water mark, not a run rate.[18] Second, the company sold shares in August at an indicative price 9.99 percent below the previous close, and the 14,805 sale price was 9.95 percent below it. And 56.8 percent of the order book is exports, so the backlog is more exposed to tariffs than the domestic book.[3][4] Fourth, working capital is large and funded by supplier bills. A sharp fall in metal prices could leave customers holding expensive contracts and squeeze cash.[18] Fifth, cable stocks fell on 22 October after KEI's margin miss. That explains at most the last two sessions of Apar's fall, and the larger part of the week's fall has no sourced explanation, which is itself a risk. Apar's cable division is a quarter of its segment revenue.[29][30] The bear case is reasonable, and it is why we do not buy more at this price. It does not justify selling by itself. Every solvency test passes, the order book is large, and the stop limits the loss.

T3 is therefore a deliberate support-based exception to the rule that a stock failing the momentum tests gets no new money at the market.
