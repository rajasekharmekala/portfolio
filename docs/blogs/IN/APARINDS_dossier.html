1. What the company does

Apar Industries Limited is listed on the National Stock Exchange of India (NSE) as APARINDS. It sells three families of products to the companies that build and run the electricity grid. Conductors are the metal lines strung between towers. Transformer and specialty oils are the fluids inside transformers that keep them cool and insulated. Power and telecom cables carry electricity and data inside plants, railways, wind and solar farms, and buildings.[30][16]

In the year to 31 March 2026 the company reported revenue of ₹22,902 crore and profit after tax of ₹977 crore. Conductors brought in ₹12,712 crore of segment revenue, cables ₹6,220 crore, and transformer and specialty oils ₹5,373 crore. Those three segments earned ₹995 crore, ₹595 crore and ₹364 crore before interest and tax. A crore is ten million rupees, so ₹22,902 crore is about ₹229 billion.[1][2]

Margins are thin because this is a processing business. Apar buys aluminium, copper and oil, turns them into finished products, and keeps a small slice. Operating profit was about 8.4 percent of revenue in FY26, which is ₹1,922 crore on ₹22,902 crore.[30] That is why three things drive profit more than anything else: how many tonnes it ships, how much of the mix is high-value product, and the timing of metal and oil price moves.

How conductors make money

A conductor is the bare metal line that carries power from one tower to the next. Most of the market buys ordinary aluminium conductors, and they are sold largely on price. The better business is the premium range. High-temperature low-sag (HTLS) conductors can carry more power on the same towers without drooping, so a utility can raise capacity without buying new land.[18][16] Replacing the wire on an existing line is called reconductoring. Apar says it takes 8 to 12 months, against 3 to 5 years to build a new line, and Apar had finished 272 such projects covering 7,005 circuit kilometres by FY26.[16]

Premium products were 45.82 percent of conductor revenue in FY26 and 50.3 percent in the June 2026 quarter, against 43.7 percent a year earlier.[16][4] Profit per tonne shows why that matters: operating profit per tonne was ₹43,012 in FY26 and ₹53,418 in the June 2026 quarter, while management says ₹35,000 to ₹36,000 is a normal level over the medium term.[7][4] The gap between those numbers is the part of today's profit that may not last.

How oils and cables make money

Transformer oil is a specialty petroleum product. Apar buys feedstock oils, roughly 65 percent on contracts and 35 percent on the spot market, and blends and sells them.[5] Because it keeps oil in stock, its profit per kilolitre swings when oil prices move fast. In FY26 it earned ₹5,943 per kilolitre; in the June 2026 quarter, after a sharp rise in gas-oil prices, it earned ₹25,482.[7][4] The company itself set aside ₹93 crore in that quarter against a later price fall, and the rating agency said the oil gain is not expected to sustain at the same level.[4][11]

Cables are a volume-and-mix business. Revenue grew 25.8 percent in FY26 to ₹6,220 crore, and the operating margin, which is operating profit divided by revenue, was 10.2 percent.[7] Apar says it has now won approvals from three of the largest US data-centre operators, which lets the electrical contractors that serve them buy its cables and opens a market for copper and medium-voltage cables that it could not enter before.[5] It has also built a house-wire and distributor business at home, where sales rose 46 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[5]

Who pays, and where the risk sits

Customers are utilities, transmission developers, contractors, railways and original equipment makers. No customer dominates: the ten largest accounted for about 19 percent of FY26 sales, according to the rating agency.[11] Exports were about 30 percent of FY26 revenue and 27.5 percent in the June 2026 quarter, and the company sells in more than 100 countries.[16][4][17]

The risk sits in three places. The first is the price of metal and oil, which the company hedges but cannot fully control. The second is the United States, where a 50 percent duty applies to aluminium products and US revenue fell 11.1 percent in the June 2026 quarter against a year earlier.[5][4][22] The third is funding: the business runs on supplier credit and bank letters of credit, which makes it sensitive to interest costs and bank limits.[11] Chapter 3 puts numbers on that third point.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Conductor: the bare metal line, usually aluminium, that carries power between towers.
  • HTLS and ACCC: high-temperature low-sag conductors, one type of which uses a carbon-fibre core. They carry more power on existing towers.
  • Reconductoring: replacing the wire on an existing line instead of building a new one.
  • OPGW: optical ground wire, a line that has optical fibre inside it so the same wire protects the line and carries data. This is the product the watchlist seed called an optical power cable.
  • Premium mix: the share of conductor revenue that comes from higher-value products such as HTLS, copper and railway conductors.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) per tonne or per kilolitre: operating profit divided by physical volume. It shows profit on each unit sold.
  • QIP: qualified institutions placement, a way for a listed company to sell new shares to large institutions.
  • LC acceptance: a bank letter of credit that lets the company pay suppliers later. It works like borrowing, but it is not counted as bank debt on the balance sheet.
  • Section 232: the US trade law under which a 50 percent duty applies to imported aluminium, steel and copper products.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Apar is India's largest conductor maker by FY26 sales and its largest private-sector maker of specialty oils by capacity, according to the rating agency and the company.[11][16] Those are real positions, but a position in a low-margin processing industry earns its money from scale, approvals and execution rather than from pricing power. The evidence below tests which of those three the company can show.

What the evidence says is strong

Scale is visible in the numbers. Revenue grew from ₹9,317 crore in FY22 to ₹22,902 crore in FY26, and profit after tax grew from ₹257 crore to ₹977 crore.[30] Three-year growth was 17 percent a year in sales and 16 percent in profit, and the company's capacity grew in step: conductor capacity went from 2,14,438 tonnes in FY24 to 2,77,327 tonnes in FY26, and cable capacity from 6,81,780 km to 9,50,656 km.[16] The rating agency counts a conductor capacity of 5,55,262 tonnes for the same date, which is double the company's own figure, and we did not reconcile the two.[11]

Approvals and know-how are the second strength. The company has a technology tie-up for the ACCC high-temperature conductor, finished 272 reconductoring projects, and in the June 2026 quarter won approval from one of the largest US utilities for 144-fibre optical ground wire and approvals from three data-centre operators for cables.[18][16][5] These approvals take time to win and keep rivals out of specific tenders. They do not stop rivals from bidding on price in ordinary products.

Customer breadth is the third. The ten largest customers were about 19 percent of FY26 sales, and the company is rated CARE AA with a stable outlook, upgraded in September 2026 from CARE AA-minus.[11] The rating agency says that sustained growth in scale, improving profit and a stronger capital position after the share sale drove the upgrade.[11]

What the evidence says is weak

The weak point is how the business is funded. At 31 March 2026 trade payables were about ₹6,856 crore, which is half of the ₹13,711 crore balance sheet, and receivables were ₹5,334 crore.[1] The rating agency says the company relies on letters of credit and creditors, with average use of non-fund-based limits of about 88 percent in the twelve months to May 2026, up from about 75 percent a year before.[11] Overall gearing counting those acceptances was 1.11 times and total outside liabilities to net worth was 1.54 times, against reported borrowings of only 0.16 times equity.[11][2] Both views are correct. The first is the one lenders watch.

Profit also depends on timing. In the June 2026 quarter the oil segment result was ₹331 crore (segment profit before finance cost and tax, from the exchange results), which is close to the ₹364 crore it earned in all of FY26.[6][2] The company explained that it holds oil bought at older, lower prices and sold at higher market prices, and that margins look disproportionately high as a result.[5] A business whose segment profit can move about four times from one quarter to the next on stock timing does not have a stable margin, whatever the long-term demand.

Demand and competition

Demand for the products is long-lived. India's National Electricity Plan for transmission calls for over 1,91,000 circuit kilometres of new lines and 1,270 gigavolt-amperes of transformer capacity between 2022-23 and 2031-32, a transmission investment opportunity of over ₹9,15,000 crore.[21] The same plan lists high-performance conductors among the technologies it wants used. In May 2026 management said transformation capacity added in FY26 was 113,000 MVA, about 10 percent short of the 126,000 MVA planned, which shows that plans slip.[7]

Competition is rising at the plain end of the range. Management said in May 2026 that domestic players were adding capacity and causing some pricing pressure, and that Chinese competition remained severe in parts of the world.[7] In the US, the 50 percent duty on aluminium products was set under Section 232 and the rules were adjusted again in April and June 2026.[23][22] Management says about 70 percent of the US conductor market is met by local production and Apar sells into the other 30 percent that is imported.[5] That is a smaller market than headline US demand suggests, and US revenue fell 11.1 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[4]

Weighing these points: the franchise is real and the demand is long-lived, but the margin is thin, the funding is stretched, and the latest quarter was flattered by timing. That mix explains why the company passes the solvency tests in Chapter 3 yet scores only three of five on the operating tests in Chapter 2B.

One threshold needs a plain explanation. The usual capital-goods yardstick wants an order book of more than 2.0 times annual sales. That suits projects that take years to build. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management gave specific forecasts in May 2025 for the year to March 2026. A company's own published forecast is called guidance. Setting that guidance against the audited and reported results shows whether the team under-promises and over-delivers, or the other way round. The record is good on margins and weaker on building capacity on time.[8]

On margins the team beat its own targets. It promised ₹30,000 or more per tonne in conductors and delivered ₹43,012. It promised ₹5,000 to ₹6,000 per kilolitre in oils and delivered ₹5,943. It promised cable growth of 25 percent at a 10 to 12 percent margin and delivered 25.8 percent at 10.2 percent.[8][7] On volumes it fell a little short: conductor volume grew 8.6 percent against a promised 10 percent, and oil volume was flat against a promised 6 to 8 percent.[8][7]

On building capacity it fell behind. In May 2025 it planned ₹1,300 crore of capex through FY26 and into the first quarter of FY27. In February 2026 it said it was on plan, with more than ₹500 crore done out of ₹1,400 crore and all facilities running by the middle of FY27. 3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter does the two jobs that Layer 1, the solvency and chart gates, sets. First it shows what the accounts say about size, profit, cash and debt over five years. Then it runs the hard gates: the India red lines for debt, interest cover, pledge and cash conversion, followed by the technical and reward-to-risk floors for a tactical holding. The sources are the audited annual reports, the quarterly company filings and the rating agency report, with Screener.in used for the five-year tables and shareholding.[1][3][4][11][30]

A few terms are used in the numbers. The 50-day and 200-day averages are the average closing price over the last 50 or 200 trading days, and Chapter 4 explains them. The operating margin is operating profit divided by revenue. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is profit before interest and tax divided by the money invested in the business. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing in rupees. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share. Net debt is borrowings less cash. We state both the company's own ratio and the aggregator's where they differ, because definitions differ, and we say which one drives each gate.

A few chart terms appear in the gates. Stage 2 is the rising phase, and Stage 4 is the falling phase. A breakout is a move above a price the stock could not clear before. A measured move is a target found by adding the height of a price shelf to the level where the stock breaks out. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the gain if the target is hit divided by the loss if the stop is hit. A ratio of 3.5 means three and a half rupees of gain for each rupee at risk.

The headline is that Apar clears every India red line and the compounder floors on reported numbers, but fails the two tactical tests: the chart is in Stage 3 and reward-to-risk is below the floor. The detail follows, starting with five years of the accounts.

The 20-day moving average, the 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the average closing price over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. 5. What could happen next

Chapter 5 turns from the past to the future in three steps. It first asks what growth today's price already assumes. It then looks at how analysts are changing their estimates. Last, it sets out three scenarios for the next twelve months with a probability and a number for each.

The first step uses a Reverse DCF, which means a discounted-cash-flow model run backwards. Instead of forecasting growth and computing a price, it takes today's price and solves for the growth that would justify it. At the close of ₹16,033.00, using a 12 percent yearly discount rate, a 20 times exit multiple and trailing profit of ₹1,228.66 crore as the starting point, the ledger solves for about 19.97 percent profit growth a year for ten years.[38] The ledger has no trailing free cash flow (FCF) figure, so the model uses profit.

Is that plausible? Profit growing at 20 percent a year for ten years takes ₹1,229 crore to about ₹7,589 crore, which is 6.2 times today's profit (our arithmetic on the ledger inputs). At the ledger's net margin — profit divided by revenue — of 4.84 percent, that needs revenue of about ₹1.57 lakh crore, or 6.4 times today's ₹24,389 crore. If the margin rose to the 7.1 percent of the June 2026 quarter, revenue of about ₹1.07 lakh crore, or 4.4 times today's, would do. Ten-year sales growth has been 16 percent a year, which compounds to 4.4 times over ten years.[30][4] So the price needs both the 16 percent sales growth to continue for a decade and a lasting rise in the margin. The margin part is the one the oil gain makes uncertain. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a weak joint. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each number, builds the strongest bear case against our own conclusion, and picks the single claim that decides the outcome. It also says when the answer will be known, so that you can check us.

The weakest assumption behind each estimate

For the central case, the weakest assumption is that the profit after the oil gain runs near ₹300 crore a quarter. Consensus FY27 earnings per share of ₹340.44 implies about ₹300 to ₹320 crore a quarter for the three remaining quarters, depending on the share count used (our arithmetic), and the PL Capital estimate for the September quarter is ₹321 crore.[20][19] Evidence that this is wrong would be September quarter profit well below ₹280 crore, an operating margin below 8 percent, or another oil-price provision. The answer comes on 29 October 2026, when the board meets to approve the September and half-year results.[15]

For the bull case, the weakest assumption is that conductor volume returns above 57,000 tonnes, the level of a year ago. In the June 2026 quarter, customers held back manufacturing clearances because metal prices jumped, and management says they will resume as prices fall.[5] If September volume is still near 53,279 tonnes, that explanation is in doubt. For the bear case, the weakest assumption is that the stock finds a floor at ₹13,282. A market in a downtrend and a stock that fell 11 percent without a company-specific reason could overshoot. The red-team section shows how far.

Red-team bear case: why the next year could be worse than we say

The strongest case against holding is a valuation reset. On our normalised profit of ₹278 crore to ₹317 crore a quarter, run-rate earnings per share are ₹265 to ₹303. At the multiples of Polycab (39.2), KEI (43.0) and R R Kabel (44.2), that gives ₹10,388 to ₹13,393, which is 35 percent to 16 percent below today's close. A one-variable test is gentler but still negative: hold the central case's implied multiple of 46.3 times and replace FY28 profit per share of ₹376.05 with ₹303, and the price is ₹14,037.56, which is 12.45 percent below the close. That is an unprotected valuation outcome, not the realised result of the stopped position: an exact fill at the stop would lose 2.82 percent, but a gap through the stop can bypass that fill.[33][34][35] Apar has traded at a premium to those names, but a premium for a lower cash-conversion record and a stretched funding structure is hard to defend if the oil gain fades.

Three more points add weight. The business ran on letter-of-credit limits that were 88 percent used, and the share sale partly repairs that; if the use creeps back up while the plants are being built, the ratings gain may reverse.[11] FY26 spending of ₹736.66 crore was 57 percent of the ₹1,300 crore a year earlier planned through FY26 and the first quarter of FY27, which suggests delivery risk on the new ₹1,500 crore programme.[8][1][7] And the US, where duties of 50 percent apply and local makers supply about 70 percent of conductors, is not a reliable growth engine.[5][22] At ₹11,000 the 7 shares would lose about ₹35,000 against today's value, which is 0.77 percent of net worth. The stop is meant to prevent most of that, but only if the stock does not gap down through it.

If it does not, the bear case becomes the main expectation. We will know on 29 October 2026, when the board approves the September quarter and half-year results, and the half-year statements will also show cash flow and use of working capital.[15] Look at four numbers: the operating margin against 8.0 percent, conductor volume against 53,279 tonnes, oil profit per kilolitre against ₹10,000, and half-year cash from operations against half-year profit.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict label is KEEP_COMPOUNDING, but the instruction is narrower than the label: a conditional defensive hold with no add. Two questions are kept apart. The first is whether Apar qualifies for a new tactical or breakout purchase. It does not. The TACTICAL_BREAKOUT archetype needs a Stage 2 chart and reward-to-risk of at least 3.5, Apar has Stage 3 and 3.07, and the momentum gate says NO_MOMENTUM. The second question is how to manage the 7 shares already owned. The taxonomy has no label for 'hold without adding', so KEEP_COMPOUNDING is the nearest one, and its strict test is not met. That test asks for passed gates, at least 3 of 4 operating KPIs healthy and a chart in Stage 2. The hold is conditional: it lapses at the stop or at the results triggers below.

Why not add? The entry zone is the price band where buying is planned, and for Apar it is ₹15,945.00 to ₹16,033.00. Any add would be a limit order, which fills only at your price or better. The ladder band is the rule that says how large a first purchase may be at a given distance from support. The band here is FULL_SIZE, the closest one, because the close is 0.12 ATR above support, and the RSI of 35.16 means there is no chase problem. But the ladder only says where buying is cheap if every other test passes, and here they do not. The probability-weighted value is 3.8 percent above the close, below the 12 percent required return. One variable shows how fragile that is: if FY28 profit per share reaches only ₹303 and the multiple stays at the central case's 46.3 times, the price is ₹14,037.56, which is 12.45 percent below the close. And results arrive thirteen days after the close, with analysts expecting profit to fall from ₹467 crore to about ₹320 crore.[19][15]

Why not trim or replace? Two labels cover selling. TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING means selling 30 to 50 percent of a holding to bank gains, and its rule needs an extended price, an RSI above 75, a bull target hit, or a weight above the hard cap of 15 percent. REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER means selling to swap into a better stock, and its rule needs a broken structure, two failed KPIs, or a broken thesis. None applies: no close has been below the stop, no KPI failed outright, the rating was upgraded, and the red lines are clear. A below-hurdle expected return and failed gates are reasons not to add. The retention rules do not turn them into a sell, because those rules test for a broken structure, failed KPIs and a broken thesis.

The reasons to hold until the stop or the results are about the future price, not about what was paid. First, the stop is 2.8 percent below the close, so if the chart breaks the further loss is about 0.07 percent of net worth. Second, the outcome that hurts most, a valuation fall, is mostly decided by the results thirteen days away, and the triggers below cut the holding by three sevenths at once if the numbers disappoint. Third, selling today would give up the central and bull outcomes, +8.7 and +20.2 percent with a combined 70 percent weight, to avoid a loss that the stop already limits. The honest risk is a gap: the stress above is unprotected, and a gap through the stop at the results could cost more than the planned 2.8 percent. A reader who cannot accept that event risk can trim to 4 shares before the results, which has the same effect as the results trigger with certainty. This holding is also the first to fund a shortlist challenger that clears every gate.

The size follows the risk-unit rule, which is the Layer 3 sizing step. A tier is the size bracket the rules give a stock according to its quality. A name that fails the stage and reward-to-risk tests cannot be in the top tiers, and a name with clear red lines, a rating upgrade and 3 of 5 KPIs cannot be in the lowest tier. TIER_3 is the middle-to-lower bracket and allows 2.0 to 3.5 percent of net worth, and the current 2.45 percent is inside it. The rule would allow up to 3.5 percent, which is about 9 shares at today's price, so there is room for 2 more, but only once the add trigger further down has been met.
