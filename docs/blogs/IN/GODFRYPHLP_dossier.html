1. What the company does

Godfrey Phillips India makes and sells cigarettes. It is the flagship company of the K K Modi Group, is run from Delhi, and is listed on the NSE (symbol GODFRYPHLP) and the BSE (500163)[18]. It has about 1,100 employees, more than 1,100 distributors and a field force of more than 9,000 people who service shops[18]. Its own cigarette brands include Four Square, Stellar, Cavanders and North Pole[8]. It also makes Marlboro, under exclusive procurement and supply agreements with Philip Morris International, which one trade report describes as manufacture under licence[18][26]. The cigarette plants are at Rabale near Mumbai and at Guldhar in Ghaziabad, which is owned by a wholly owned subsidiary, International Tobacco Company, and a leaf-processing plant is at Ongole[9].

The first thing to understand is who pays and how much of the payment is tax. A smoker pays for a stick, the company sells to distributors, and most of what the buyer pays goes to the government. In FY26 the company's gross sales value, which includes all indirect taxes, was ₹18,379 crore, but its net revenue was ₹6,391 crore, so it kept about 35 paise of each rupee (my arithmetic)[18]. In the June 2026 quarter, the first full quarter under the new tax rates, gross sales value was ₹5,676 crore and net revenue was ₹1,206 crore, so the company kept about 21 paise of each rupee, against 36 paise a year earlier[18]. The difference is excise duty, a tax charged on each stick made: ₹2,614 crore in the June 2026 quarter against ₹327 crore a year before[18].

The second thing is how the money is made on each stick. The company sold 2,001 million domestic cigarette sticks a month in FY26, up 20% on FY25, and 1,866 million a month in the June 2026 quarter[18][20]. Dividing its earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA, profit before the accounting and finance adjustments) of ₹1,584 crore by its twelve months of volume gives about 66 paise per stick in FY26 and about 33 paise in the June 2026 quarter (my arithmetic; the figure also carries exports and other items, so it is a guide and not an audited unit measure)[18]. At about 22 billion sticks a year, one paisa a stick is worth about ₹22 crore of annual operating profit, so small moves in pricing or tax pass-through decide the year (my arithmetic).

The third thing is the mix. In FY26 domestic tobacco was 77% of net sales, international 22% and other products 1%; in the June 2026 quarter the split was 92%, 7% and 1%, because domestic sales carry the new excise and the leaf exports do not[20][18]. Here the company's 'net sales' means sales before the excise deduction and after GST (₹9,082 crore in FY26 and ₹3,806 crore in the June 2026 quarter), which is not the 'net revenue' used elsewhere in this book[20][18]. International business is mostly unmanufactured tobacco, which means leaf sold to other manufacturers: exports were ₹2,014 crore in FY26, of which leaf was ₹1,945 crore, cigarettes ₹50 crore and cut tobacco ₹19 crore[8]. Confectionery (Funda Goli candies) and distribution of Ferrero products are small: Ferrero revenue rose from ₹22 crore to ₹51 crore[18][8]. The company also manufactures Marlboro for Philip Morris. The filings net many of the Marlboro flows against purchases (₹4,859.39 crore in FY26), and the company does not publish a Marlboro volume or the royalty rate[13].

The fourth thing is the treasury, which is a second business inside the first. The group holds investments of ₹3,181 crore at book value, of which mutual funds are about ₹2,292 crore, a stake carried at ₹759 crore in a Modi-group company, and the rest other holdings, while cash and bank balances are only about ₹33 crore[14][10]. The company also owns 24.80% of Philip Morris India Trading Private Limited, which distributes Marlboro, and took ₹283 crore of that company's profit into FY26 results and ₹258 crore in dividends[15][10][13]. These items add earnings that do not come from selling cigarettes, which matters for how far the profit can be trusted (Chapter 3).

  • Gross sales value and net revenue. Gross sales value includes every indirect tax; net revenue is what is left after excise duty and GST, and is the figure Screener calls sales[18][1].
  • Unmanufactured tobacco is tobacco leaf the company buys from farmers, processes and exports without turning it into cigarettes[8].
  • Associate is a company in which Godfrey Phillips holds 20% to 50% and takes a share of profit without controlling it; Philip Morris India Trading is one[15].
  • Illicit trade means cigarettes sold without paying the full tax; the annual report puts it at about 26.1% of the market[8].
  • Reward-to-risk (the same idea as asymmetry) is the gain to the target for each rupee that would be lost if the stop is hit; the house floor for a Core name is 2.0 to 1[36].
  • Test C is the house recovery test: a share that has fallen must close at or above its 20-day average, with rising relative strength or a positive two-week return, to count as turning up[36].
  • Per-stick EBITDA is company EBITDA divided by domestic cigarette sticks sold in the period; it is a working measure built from published figures[18]. The next chapter uses it to judge the competitive position.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong where it matters in good times and exposed where it matters now. In good times it grew domestic volume from 930 million sticks a month in FY22 to 2,001 million in FY26, more than doubling in four years, against a market where ITC, the leader, has an estimated 75% to 80%, Godfrey is second with about 12% and VST Industries has about 8% (the market shares are from a secondary source)[18][27]. Return on capital employed (ROCE, the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses) rose from 17% in FY22 to 30% in FY26[1]. Direct retail coverage grew 21% in FY26 and the company says its reach is about 15 lakh outlets[20].

The weakness is that the product is controlled by tax. Legal cigarettes are about 10% of tobacco consumption in India yet pay nearly 80% of tobacco tax, and the annual report notes that illicit cigarettes are about 26.1% of the cigarette market and about one-third of legal volume[8]. When the government raises tax sharply, the company has two bad choices: pass it on and lose volume to cheaper or illegal sticks, or absorb it and lose margin. In the June 2026 quarter it did a bit of both: volume fell only 2%, but gross profit fell 29.3% and EBITDA 46.2%[18].

Brand and distribution are real, but pricing power is the open question. The evidence is in the margin: the June 2026 operating margin on net revenue was 15% against 23% a year before, and the leader's cigarette segment result fell 35% in its first full quarter, so both leading firms lost profit, not only Godfrey[2][34]. The detail follows in five parts: the tax reset, volume and price, the Philip Morris relationship, illicit trade, and the investment book.

The tax reset in numbers

From 1 February 2026 the compensation cess on cigarettes was set to nil while GST and excise duty were raised together[8]. GST on tobacco moved from 28% to 40%[24]. Excise duty is now charged per 1,000 sticks by stick length: ₹2,100 for filter cigarettes up to 65 mm, ₹4,000 for 65 to 70 mm, ₹5,400 for 70 to 75 mm and up to ₹8,500 for other categories[23][22]. One secondary source puts the range at ₹2,050 to ₹8,500, so the bottom of the range differs by ₹50 between sources[30][24]. The annual report does not give the company's mix by stick length, so we cannot compute its own average duty per stick.

The effect on reported numbers is large and easy to misread. Excise charged was ₹2,614 crore in the June 2026 quarter against ₹327 crore a year earlier, a rise of about 700%, against 73.5% for ITC and 421% for VST[18][27]. Gross revenue, before the excise deduction, rose 110.7% to ₹3,820 crore, which is why some headlines say revenue doubled; net revenue after excise fell 18.8% to ₹1,206 crore[18][25]. Screener's sales line is net revenue, so the watchlist seed's note that three-year sales growth is 'excise-inflated' is wrong for that line: the 22% a year is not a tax effect, it reflects higher volume (1,295 to 2,001 million sticks a month between FY24 and FY26) and pricing[2][37]. The market understood the reset at once: both Godfrey and ITC fell 21% over two days when it was announced[24].

Volume, price and who bears the tax

Management says it chose a 'balanced pricing strategy' so that the consumer impact is 'phased and not in one go'[18][19]. The result in the June 2026 quarter was a volume fall of only 2%, to 1,866 million sticks a month, which compares well with an estimated 5% fall for ITC and a 14% fall for VST[18][27][29]. The price paid for that was profit: gross profit margin on gross sales value fell from 15.3% to 7.8%, and per-stick EBITDA fell from about 59 paise to about 33 paise (my arithmetic)[18]. In plain words, Godfrey kept its smokers by keeping its margin low.

That is a sound choice only if prices catch up with the tax over the next two or three quarters. VST's September 2026 quarter shows the risk: its volume was 562 million sticks a month against 695 million a year earlier (down 19%), and its EBITDA margin fell to 17.9% from 23.4%[28]. ITC's own price rises of 22% to 23% are described in its dossier, with a further rise needed to restore profit per stick[34]. If Godfrey's June volume was helped by shops stocking up or by dispatches ahead of price rises, the September quarter will show it, and that result arrives on 5 November 2026[3][7].

The Philip Morris relationship

Philip Morris is both a partner and an owner. Philip Morris Global Brands Inc. holds 39,151,425 shares, 25.10% of the company, and is listed in the promoter group, next to the Modi group's K K Modi Investment and Financial Services (29.85%)[15]. The company also owns 24.80% of Philip Morris India Trading Private Limited, which gave it ₹283.12 crore of share of profit in FY26 (₹208.97 crore in FY25) and ₹258.41 crore of dividends (₹193.44 crore)[10][13]. It pays Philip Morris Products S.A. a technical services fee and royalty of ₹21.37 crore (₹18.04 crore in FY25)[13]. That royalty is small, but the rate and the term of the agreement are not published, so I cannot test how secure the licence is (searched in the annual report and the earnings presentation and not found).

The scale of the relationship is the real exposure. Standalone sales of goods to Philip Morris India Trading were ₹2,746.70 crore in FY26, up 93% from ₹1,421.31 crore, and sales to Philip Morris group companies abroad were ₹1,362.20 crore, against ₹1,447.02 crore[13]. Together that is ₹4,108.90 crore, or about 45% of standalone revenue of ₹9,082.46 crore including excise (my arithmetic)[13][12]. The note also says purchases of goods were reduced by ₹4,859.39 crore because of sales of Marlboro made by the company, so the gross Marlboro flow is not visible in the headline revenue[13]. A partner that is also a 25% owner makes the arrangement sticky, but it also means a large part of the revenue depends on one customer group's decisions.

Illicit trade and downtrading

Illicit trade is the risk the company names itself. The annual report says the revised indirect tax structure 'has inadvertently led to growth of illicit tobacco trade' and that the problem needs action by policymakers and enforcement agencies[9][8]. The report puts the annual revenue loss to the government from illicit cigarettes at about ₹23,000 crore[8]. When the legal price rises about 20% to 25%, as it has for ITC, the gap to the illegal price widens and smokers who cannot afford the legal stick can leave the legal market altogether[34].

Downtrading is the second path: a smoker switches to a shorter or cheaper stick that carries a lower excise slab, which cuts the company's revenue per stick even if volume holds. The company does not publish the length mix, so this risk is not measurable from the filings I read. An industry forecast quoted by a secondary source has the legal market contracting by 6% to 8% in FY27 and ITC's volume falling about 6%[27]. Godfrey's June volume of -2% is therefore better than that forecast, but the forecast applies to the full year.

The investment book and what it does to returns

A large investment book lowers ROCE and raises the quality question. Screener's headline ROCE is 30.2% and ROE (return on equity, profit divided by shareholders' money) is 23.4% for the group, and 34.9% and 27.0% for the parent alone[2][4]. Investments of ₹3,299 crore sit inside the capital base that ROCE divides by[1]. If I take the investments out, FY26 operating profit after depreciation of ₹1,464 crore (₹1,585 crore less ₹121 crore) is earned on about ₹3,147 crore (equity of ₹6,212 crore plus borrowings of ₹234 crore, less ₹3,299 crore of investments), which is about 46% before tax (my arithmetic)[1][10]. So the cash book does not hide a weak operating return; it dilutes a very strong one.

The earnings from that book are lower in quality than operating profit. Consolidated other income was ₹204.79 crore in FY26, and ₹153.65 crore of it (75%) is fair-value gains on investments, which are changes in market value and not interest received[10][14]. Add the ₹283.12 crore share of associate profit and the two items together are ₹436.77 crore, or 22.5% of FY26 pre-tax profit of ₹1,939.86 crore (my arithmetic)[10]. That is why I score earnings quality as a WATCH in Chapter 2B. One holding needs a closer look: an investment in K K Modi Investment and Financial Services is carried at ₹758.74 crore, against ₹764.52 crore a year earlier, so it is an existing balance and not new spending. That private company is also the largest promoter shareholder in Godfrey Phillips (29.85%)[14][15]. It is unlisted, so its carrying value is an estimate, and the link is circular, because that company's worth depends partly on Godfrey's own share price (my inference). The next chapter scores the operating measures.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means comparing what management said it would do with what happened. The company does not publish numeric targets for volume or margin, so I tested the written priorities and the dividend it declared, using the Q4 FY25, Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27 presentations and the FY26 accounts[21][20][19]. The record is good on growth and payouts up to February 2026, and weak on the one promise that mattered after it: that phasing prices would protect profit.

Two items deserve a sentence each. In May 2026 the CEO wrote that the tax increase would make 'the next year challenging' and that price increases would be phased[20]. 3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter reads five years of accounts. I used consolidated figures, which include the subsidiaries and the share of profit from the Philip Morris associate, because the group's profit is what a shareholder owns. The main source is Screener's tables, cross-checked to the annual reports[1][10]. Revenue here means net revenue, after excise duty and GST. That keeps years comparable, because excise duty was ₹2,614 crore in the June 2026 quarter against ₹327 crore a year earlier[18].

Operating mechanics matter for how to read the tables. The company buys leaf from farmers, holds it for a long time to age it, and pays excise duty when cigarettes leave the factory, so it needs a lot of working capital. Inventory days, the number of days of sales sitting in stock, were 233 in FY26[1]. When the tax per stick rises, the cash tied up in duty-paid stock and in amounts owed by distributors rises too, which is why cash flow can lag profit in a tax-increase year. Cyclicality is policy-driven and concentration is high on one customer group: Philip Morris entities were about 45% of standalone revenue in FY26[13][12].

Profit per stick is the unit-economics anchor. FY25 earned about 59 paise of EBITDA a stick, FY26 earned 66 paise, and the June 2026 quarter 33 paise (my arithmetic on company figures)[18]. Volume rose every year from FY22 to FY26, so the filings I read contain no volume downturn to study except the tax reset itself, and they do not show margins after earlier tax changes. That gap is one reason I do not accept the recovery in the base case as certain.

Static levels and trajectory point in different directions. The levels in the balance sheet are excellent: almost no debt, ROCE of 30% and an AA+ credit rating[1][8]. The trajectory since February 2026 is poor: operating margin fell from 23% to 15%, profit fell 44%, and cash conversion has been weak for three years[2][18]. Layer 1 tests the levels against the red lines and the trajectory against the archetypes. It ends the chapter with a table that shows which test fails.

First, trailing profit is the wrong anchor: it contains the March 2026 quarter at ₹521 crore and two other pre-reset quarters, while the one post-reset quarter earned ₹198 crore, so profit that repeats the June quarter gives a P/E of about 34, not 19.9[2][35]. Third, about 45% of revenue depends on Philip Morris entities, and the terms are not published, so a change in that relationship would not show in any ratio until it had happened[13].

Where could we be too bearish? If prices rise faster than tax, per-stick profit could return to the old range within two or three quarters and the share could re-rate toward its median. The Q4 FY26 figure of about 96 paise a stick shows that the business can earn a lot per stick in a high-price period. A second possibility is that Godfrey's strong volume (down only 2% in a quarter in which VST's fell 14%) is a durable share gain, which would lift the multiple the market will pay. Our bull case allows both, at a 25% probability[18][29].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest one for each, says what evidence would show it wrong and when that evidence arrives, then gives the red-team bear case, which is the strongest argument against my own conclusion, and the single claim on which the verdict hinges. The verdict is REJECTED, and the arguments below include the ones that would reopen it.

Weakest assumption in the earnings estimate

The weakest input is profit per stick of ₹0.55 in FY28. It assumes prices catch up with the tax by about 22 paise from the June level of 33 paise. The evidence for catch-up is indirect: ITC took price rises of 22% to 23%, and the June quarter was only the first full quarter of the new rates[34][18]. The evidence against is VST's September quarter, in which volume fell 19% and the margin 5.5 points[28]. The Q2 FY27 filing on 5 November 2026 is the first direct test: our base needs EBITDA of about ₹205 crore (38 paise a stick) on volume of about 1,800 million a month[3][7]. If profit per stick reaches only 45 paise in FY28, base EPS falls to about ₹66 and the base value to about ₹1,315, and the weights would move toward the bear case.

Weakest assumption in the target multiple

The base multiple of 19.9 is the current trailing P/E, which is itself computed on profit that is about to fall. If the market looks through the dip and pays the 3-year median on recovering earnings, the share is worth more; if it keeps cutting the multiple as profit falls, the share is worth less. The 25.4 median was set in a period of 30% annual profit growth that has ended, and the 3-year low is 13.2[5]. The evidence that would settle it is the multiple at which ITC trades after its own Q2 result on 2 November 2026[34]. If the market cut the multiple to 16.8, ITC's level today, the base value would be ₹1,301 (my arithmetic), which is 25% below the close. That is a valuation risk on its own, because earnings would still match the base case.

Weakest assumption in the probability weights

The 25/45/30 split is a judgment. Its weak point is that it treats the bull case as less likely than the bear because only one quarter of post-reset data exists, and that quarter was better than the peers on volume. If the September quarter confirms a volume decline of 5% or less and the profit per stick rises above 40 paise, the weights would shift toward the bull case, but they would have to reach about 35% bull and 20% bear, with the base at 45%, before the weighted value reached the price. The weights change after every quarter, and the next change is the 5 November 2026 result. The reverse risk exists too: with weights of 35% bull and 20% bear the weighted value would be ₹1,738, about the price, so the verdict rests on the weights together with the two red lines and not on the weights alone.

The red-team bear case

The strongest case against owning the shares is that the headline numbers describe a business that no longer exists. Profit per stick has halved, trailing profit still includes three pre-reset quarters, and cash conversion has been poor for three years while receivables rose 76% in FY26[18][1][10]. About 45% of revenue comes from Philip Morris entities under terms that are not published, so pricing power with this customer group cannot be verified, and weak bargaining power is a risk and not a finding[13]. Illicit trade is, by the company's own report, about 26% of the cigarette market, and the higher tax widens the gap[8]. A further tax rise is a policy risk with no price level that protects against it. Promoter directors were paid ₹158.70 crore in FY26, and the company carries an existing ₹758.74 crore investment in a promoter-group company. Those are governance risks, not proof of motive, and they weigh more now that profit fell 44% in the June 2026 quarter after rising 42% in FY26[16][14][1][18]. If all of this is right, the shares fall to the ₹977 bear value at ITC's multiple on lower earnings, with a tail near ₹768 at the 3-year low multiple of 13.2, and the 2.9% dividend yield will not compensate.
