Minda Corporation makes locks, wiring, dashboard displays and cast metal parts for Indian two-wheelers, trucks and cars, and it sold ₹6,185 crore of them in the year to March 2026.[2][12] It is financially sound: debt is modest, operating cash was 1.79 times profit over three years, and Crisil rates its debt AA.[1][11][13] But the June 2026 profit of ₹206 crore includes a one-off ₹106 crore gain, its return on capital is a low 12.7 percent, and the shares, at ₹629.75, are 18 percent below their 52-week high.[2][1][38] We rate it WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: buy only between ₹580 and ₹596, after the 3 November 2026 results, and drop the idea below ₹557.03.[30][38][39] We are wrong if the September-quarter margin falls below 10.8 percent, or if the shares cannot be had below about ₹603.

1. What the company does

Minda Corporation Limited is the flagship company of the Spark Minda group. It was set up in 1985, and its offices are in Noida and Delhi.[2][13] It sells parts to vehicle makers, which the industry calls original equipment manufacturers (OEMs): an OEM is the company whose badge is on the scooter, truck or car. In the June 2026 quarter, two- and three-wheelers made up 46 percent of its sales, commercial vehicles 27 percent, passenger vehicles 19 percent and the replacement market 8 percent. India was 89 percent of sales.[2] The stock therefore moves with Indian two-wheeler and truck demand, and with the success of its smaller push into car parts.

Think of a scooter as a few hundred parts. Minda supplies the ones a rider touches or reads: the ignition lock and key, the wiring that carries current around the vehicle, the digital instrument panel and some of the aluminium castings. By product, wiring harnesses were 32 percent of June 2026 quarter sales, vehicle access (locks, latches, door handles, immobilisers) 25 percent, instrument clusters 16 percent, die casting 15 percent, and a mixed group (sensors, electric-vehicle chargers and motors, sunroofs) 12 percent.[2] The company reports two verticals. How the company earns money

A vehicle maker picks suppliers years before a model goes on sale. After a win, Minda builds tools and capacity, then supplies the part for the life of the model. Revenue grows in three ways: more vehicles, more parts per vehicle, and dearer parts as features become fancier. Colour screens now replacing basic two-wheeler instrument panels cost three to four times as much, management said in August 2025.[7] Management calls the total rupee value of Minda parts in one vehicle its kit value. Crisil says the kit value in an electric two-wheeler has risen from ₹15,000–20,000 to over ₹30,000–35,000 after the Flash Electronics deal.[13]

Raw materials are the biggest cost. Of each ₹100 of June 2026 quarter sales, ₹63.9 went on raw materials, ₹15.1 on staff and ₹9.5 on other costs, which left ₹11.5 of EBITDA. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization; it is the profit from selling parts before finance and accounting charges.[2] Copper, zinc and aluminium rose 30 to 40 percent during FY26, and most customer contracts pass such rises on at cost, with no markup and a delay of one or two quarters.[4][3] That is why a commodity spike lowers the margin percentage even when rupee profit is protected.

The group around the listed company: Flash, VAST and the joint ventures

The listed company's accounts show only part of the Minda picture. Group revenue in FY26, counting Flash Electronics and Minda VAST, was ₹9,016 crore against ₹6,185 crore of reported revenue.[2] Flash Electronics makes electric-vehicle motors, motor controllers and power parts. Minda owns 49 percent, so it shows only its share of Flash's profit, which management put at about ₹70 crore in FY26 (49 percent of Flash's ₹137 crore is ₹67 crore), below the operating line. Management gave Furukawa Minda (17.5 percent) as about ₹8 crore and Minda VAST as about ₹5 crore. These rounded figures add to ₹83 crore, against a reported total of ₹81 crore, or 23 percent of FY26 profit; the other small ventures and the rounding explain the gap of about ₹2 crore, which is our inference, as the split is not published.[4][2] This share is real but arrives as cash only when the partners pay dividends, which is why Chapter 3 tests profit and cash separately.

From 1 April 2026 the company consolidates Minda VAST, which makes door handles, locks and access systems for cars and is more than 90 percent a passenger-vehicle business.[4][3] It also has newer ventures: a 60 percent joint venture with Toyodenso of Japan for switches, a 49 percent venture with Turntide Technologies of the UK for motors and controllers, and a 50 percent venture for sunroofs.[4][2] Each needs money before it earns any. The company invested ₹63 crore in group companies in the June quarter alone.[17]

Plain-English glossary for this report

These are the terms this report leans on most, in everyday words, so that a reader new to the sector can check any later sentence against them.

  • OEM: original equipment manufacturer, the vehicle maker that buys the parts.
  • Kit value: the rupee value of all of one supplier's parts in a single vehicle.
  • EBITDA margin: EBITDA divided by revenue; the share of each rupee of sales left after the costs of making and selling.
  • Associate or joint venture: a company Minda part-owns; its profit share is counted in Minda's profit but arrives as cash only when a dividend is paid.
  • SOP (start of production): the date a new part first ships to the customer in volume.
  • Lifetime order book: the total value a customer is expected to buy over a part's life, usually four to five years. It is not revenue today.
  • Pass-through: raising selling prices to recover a higher raw-material cost, usually after a delay and with no markup.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when rivals cannot easily take its customers or its profit. For a parts maker, the test is whether the vehicle makers keep re-awarding it models, and whether it earns a fair return after paying for plants. On the first test, Minda scores well. Crisil calls it one of the largest Indian makers of security systems for two-wheelers and a leading maker of wiring harnesses for two-wheelers, three-wheelers and commercial vehicles, and rates its debt Crisil AA/Stable, upgraded from AA- in April 2026.[13] The company reports a lifetime order book of more than ₹10,000 crore won in FY26, about 1.6 times its annual revenue, with about 20 percent from exports.[4][8] It also has more than 1,000 engineers and 335 patent filings.[2]

Growth shows the position. Revenue grew 22.3 percent in FY26 while ACMA, the auto-parts trade body, reports that vehicle production grew about 12 percent in two-wheelers, 13 percent in commercial vehicles and 10 percent in passenger vehicles.[12][24] Management's own rule is to grow at least 50 percent faster than the industry.[4] In the June 2026 quarter the industry grew about 22 percent and Minda grew 33.2 percent, or about 24.2 percent without VAST.[3][2] The lead is real, but it was smaller in the latest quarter than a year earlier, when Minda grew 16 percent against an industry rise of about 2 percent.[7] Part of that is the harder comparison and part is faster industry growth.

The profit level is the weaker side. The EBITDA margin was 9.9 percent in FY22 and 11.7 percent in FY26, a climb of about 0.45 points a year.[2] A margin near 11.5 percent leaves little room. Raw materials are about 63 percent of sales, so a 1 percent unrecovered cost rise takes about 0.6 points off the margin. Crisil expects the margin to stay near 11.5 percent over the medium term because vehicle makers have strong bargaining power.[13] The company's own aim is 12.5 percent by FY30.[4]

Return on capital: why the reported figure is low

The ROCE (return on capital employed) is profit before interest and tax divided by the money tied up in the business: in plain terms, how many rupees of profit each rupee of capital earns. Screener shows 12.7 percent, down from 16 percent in FY23 and 15 percent in FY24.[1] The company shows 23.1 percent for FY26, up from 20.0 percent in FY25 and FY24.[8][9] The gap has one cause. The company's figure excludes its investments in associates and the profit from them. The reported figure keeps in the capital the roughly ₹1,400 crore invested in associates in FY25, mostly the 49 percent stake in Flash (₹1,372 crore, according to India Ratings as quoted by EduInvesting), but leaves Flash's profit out of the numerator.[8][11][29]

Neither number is wrong, but they answer different questions. Our rules test the reported figure, and a rupee spent on Flash is a rupee spent. Flash earned a profit of ₹137 crore in FY26, so Minda's 49 percent share, about ₹67–70 crore, is about 5 percent on a ₹1,400 crore outlay.[2][4][11] That is below the 12 percent hurdle used in this portfolio's reverse DCF, so for now the stake dilutes the group's return. Chapter 3 shows how the return looks when the associate share is counted. What this means for you: the operating business earns well, but the group's headline return waits for Flash and the new ventures to grow into the capital they were given.

Where the position is thick, and where it is thin

A moat is whatever makes it hard for a rival to take the business. Minda's is built from four things. A vehicle maker will not swap a lock or a wiring harness without years of testing, so a supplier on a platform tends to stay. The group is spread across products, with no vertical above a third of sales. It has in-house engineering. And it is localising: its own division now supplies 18–19 percent of the connectors in its harnesses, up from about 5 percent three to four years earlier.[4][7]

Four weak spots matter more. Customers hold the bargaining power, and the top three bought about 35 percent of FY26 first-nine-month revenue.[13] The profit pool is thin and commodity-linked. About 46 percent of sales come from two- and three-wheelers, which are cyclical.[2] And about 23 percent of FY26 profit was the share of associates, which is not cash.[2] Put together, the evidence says: a well-financed supplier with a good, defensible position, earning a fair but not outstanding return, in a thin-margin business whose profit growth needs steady cost pass-through. That fits the turnaround test of Chapter 3 better than a classic compounder, and Chapter 2B measures it on the operating numbers that matter.

Layer 1 asks two separate questions. The first is about levels: is the company good enough today on the measures our rules name? The second is about trajectory: are those measures getting better or worse? The rules set hard red lines: borrowings of 1.20 times equity or more, interest cover below 2.0, a promoter share pledge of 20 to 25 percent or more, three-year operating cash below 0.25 of profit, or a Stage 4 breakdown.[40] Stage 4 is the falling phase of a share-price cycle: the price is below a falling 50-day average, which is itself below a falling 200-day average. Stage 2 is the rising phase, the opposite of that. Minda clears every red line. Our rules also set a floor for the reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry: the gain to the central target divided by the loss to the stop, or (target minus price) over (price minus stop). Each archetype has its own floor. A COMPOUNDER needs a return on capital of at least 15 percent, borrowings below 0.70 times equity and three-year cash conversion of at least 0.70, with a reward-to-risk of at least 2.0. A TURNAROUND_VALUE_INFLECTION fits a company whose trailing level misses the compounder floor but whose direction is turning up, with a reward-to-risk of at least 2.5. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions, and the weakest one decides how wrong the estimate can be. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, builds the strongest honest case against our verdict, names the single claim that carries the verdict, and says when we will know. A forecast that cannot say how it could fail is not a forecast.

Weakest assumption behind the earnings: that the margin climbs from 11.3 percent to 11.6 percent

Our FY27 profit per share of ₹17.9 needs an EBITDA margin of about 11.3 percent in September and 11.5 to 11.6 percent after that, an FY27 average of 11.5 percent. The evidence for it is the company's repeated guidance of 11.5 to 12 percent, the pass-through contracts, and the fact that the margin has been between 11.3 and 11.9 percent for five straight quarters.[3][2] The evidence against it is that the June 2026 margin fell 47 basis points from March, that management says commodity pass-through lags by one or two quarters, and that minimum wages rose in Haryana and Uttar Pradesh from April 2026.[3][4]

What would show we are wrong: a September-quarter margin below 10.8 percent, or a cut to the guidance of 11.5 to 12 percent. A cut that leaves guidance above 11.0 percent drops the plan for re-underwriting, and a cut below 11.0 percent makes the verdict REJECTED. When we will know: on 3 November 2026, when the board approves the September results and management holds the investor meet at 4 p.m.[30] We use one set of lines for that margin everywhere in this report. At 11.3 percent or more with guidance repeated, the plan may continue. Between 10.8 and 11.3 percent, we keep waiting. Below 10.8 percent, we drop the plan and re-underwrite. Below 10.5 percent, the verdict becomes REJECTED. The 10.8 line is 50 basis points under our model and 70 under the guided floor.

Weakest assumption behind the value: that the market still pays 36 times profit

The central value multiplies profit per share by 36. Nothing in the filings fixes that number. It is a judgement between today's 38.1 and the 29 to 34 times of Pricol and Suprajit, well under the stock's own 47.8 median.[31][34][36] Funds own a fair stake, with mutual funds at 14.78 percent and foreign investors at 9.36 percent, and the market is in a downtrend with 19 percent of stocks above their 50-day lines.[23][38] If the multiple drops to 30 times, the value on our central profit is ₹598, which is level with the top of the zone and gives no gain.

What would show we are wrong: a P/E below 34 times on a good result, or a sell-off that takes the whole auto group lower. When we will know: continuously, with the first read on the price reaction after 3 November. A stock that rises on a good print is being re-rated; one that falls on a good print is being sold for other reasons, and our multiple is too high.

Weakest assumption behind the associate income: that Minda's share of Flash's profit stays near ₹70 crore

Associates contributed ₹81 crore of FY26 profit, 23 percent of the total, and we model about ₹81 crore for FY27. The June 2026 quarter was ₹18 crore against ₹31 crore in March. Management says Flash's lower profit came from excess depreciation and that the Furukawa dip is temporary, and it expects Flash to grow 20 to 24 percent with a 16 to 17 percent margin.[3] Flash's margin of 15.4 percent was below that range, and its profit margin was 6.6 percent against 9.9 percent in March.[2]

What would show we are wrong: Flash's EBITDA margin staying below 15 percent for two more quarters, or associate profit below ₹15 crore in September. When we will know: 3 November for the September quarter, then early February 2027.

Weakest assumption behind the stop and the size: that ₹557.03 will hold

The stop is one ATR under the 63-day low. It limits the loss only if the price trades down through it in an orderly way. A results gap can jump over it, and the bear value of ₹476 is 24.4 percent under the close. A position of 3.5 percent that fell to ₹476 from a ₹596 fill would lose about 0.7 percent of the portfolio, more than the 0.5 percent risk budget of this tier.[39][40] The promoter warrants carry a conversion price of ₹550, close to the stop, so a fall through ₹550 would also test whether the promoters still convert.[14]

What would show we are wrong: a close below ₹557.03, or a gap-down open through it. When we will know: on any session. This is why the plan is to place no order before the results, and to cancel any unfilled order if the margin test fails.

Our own bear case: the strongest honest argument against buying at all

The bear case is that this is an average-quality supplier at an average multiple in a market that is going down. The stock trades at 37.4 times Screener's trailing profit, in line with Lumax and Suprajit, for an 11.7 percent margin and a reported return on capital of 12.7 percent that has not risen in three years.[1][33][36] About a quarter of FY26 profit is the share of associates, not cash.[2] The Flash stake earns about 5 percent on cost, against a 12 percent hurdle.[11][4][2] The standalone accounts show related-party loans on both sides, a ₹67.1 crore tax demand with no provision, and a debt covenant breach that the bank regularised.[11] The quarter that lifted the headline profit 216 percent was a one-off, and underlying profit rose about 54 percent.[2] Margin guidance of 11.5 to 12 percent leaves little room, because the June quarter was 11.46 percent. A single promoter-held entity holds the warrants that fill the funding gap.[14] And the Vision 2030 target needs 19 to 20 percent growth a year for four years.[4]

There is also an honest case that our wait is too cautious. Two-wheeler demand is strong, estimates are rising, and the 3 November results could be good enough to lift the stock 10 percent before any limit order, an order that fills only at your price or better, can fill.[26][38] If that happens we miss an entry and must decide whether to chase it. We accept that cost, because the same results could send the stock under ₹557. Our answer to the bear case is price: at ₹580 to ₹596 the central gain is 20 to 24 percent and the loss to the stop is 4 to 7 percent.
