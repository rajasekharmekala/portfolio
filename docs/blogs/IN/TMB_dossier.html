Tamilnad Mercantile Bank (TMB) is a private bank, founded in 1921 in Thoothukudi, that lends mostly against gold, farm and small-business collateral.[10][3] Its Q2 FY27 results came out on 26 October 2026, as profit rose 42.09% to ₹451.14 crore and bad loans fell to 0.61%.[29][6][7] We rate it a high-quality lender, but profit should grow far more slowly next year, because the loan margin has peaked and costs and provisions will rise.[11] The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: the price of ₹811.85 is 1.36 ATR (average true range, the typical daily swing) above support, so we plan limit orders at ₹770.30 to ₹794.67 and a stop at ₹739.84.[1] We are wrong if gold prices fall hard, if the Enforcement Directorate bonus-share case goes against the bank, or if loan growth outside gold stalls, and the chapters below test each risk[17][9].

1. What the company does

A bank is a business that borrows money from many people and lends it to others at a higher price. Tamilnad Mercantile Bank was incorporated in 1921 and has its head office in Thoothukudi.[10] It has no promoter, which means no single family or group controls it; about 91% of its shares sit with the public.[16] On 30 September 2026 it had 631 branches, of which 460 were in Tamil Nadu and 171 in other states.[9] Its borrowers are mostly households, farmers, traders and small firms in South India.

The bank earns money from the gap between what it charges borrowers and what it pays depositors. That gap, in rupees, is called net interest income (NII). In the September 2026 quarter it was ₹807.17 crore, up 35.17% on a year earlier.[6] Divided by the assets that earn interest, the gap becomes the net interest margin (NIM). TMB's NIM was 4.19% in the quarter, against 3.83% a year earlier.[8] For comparison, South Indian Bank reported 3.33% and CSB Bank 3.81% for the same quarter.[24][25] A smaller income comes from fees and trading. Non-interest income was ₹232.24 crore, about 22% of net income (NII plus non-interest income), and insurance commission was only about ₹9 crore in the half year.[6][11]

Who pays the bank? Borrowers on four main books. Gold loans are the largest: ₹29,280 crore, which is 48.24% of all loans, at a yield of 10.69%.[5] The call gives an average ticket of ₹2.66 lakh and 'about 12 lakh' customers, a rounded figure; ₹29,280 crore at that ticket implies about 11 lakh.[11] A gold loan is a loan against gold ornaments kept with the bank. It is repaid with interest, and if the borrower fails to pay, the bank sells the gold. Farm loans are 41.35% of advances, and most of them are gold loans, because the bank counts gold loans taken by farmers as farm credit. Small-business (MSME) loans are 28.45%, retail loans such as home and vehicle loans are 24.53%, and a mixed book the bank calls Others is 5.67%.[5]

Who funds the bank? Depositors. Total deposits were ₹69,546 crore on 30 September 2026. Only ₹17,629 crore of that, or 25.35%, was current and savings account (CASA) money, which is cheap because the bank pays little or nothing on it. The other 74.65% was term deposits, which pay a fixed rate, and about 19% of all deposits were large 'bulk' deposits.[3][4][11] India Ratings says the funding is still granular, with retail customers supplying 83.3% of deposits in the June 2026 quarter.[17] The bank also borrowed about ₹1,000 crore from refinance lines at 7.5% in the quarter, which costs more than the average deposit.[11]

Loan prices adjust with market rates. About 95% of the loan book floats: 52% is linked to the repo rate (the rate at which the Reserve Bank lends to banks) and 43% to the bank's own cost-of-funds benchmark (MCLR).[11] The repo rate has just risen by 25 basis points to 5.5%, and the chief executive said the whole repo-linked portfolio moves to the new benchmark on each customer's reset day, over a period of about three months.[11] Deposits reprice only as they mature. That is why the margin tends to widen when rates rise and narrow when they fall, and why the latest rise is a tailwind that the bank's own margin guide does not count on.

A bank cannot be read like a factory. For a factory, the ratio of operating cash flow to profit shows whether profit turns into cash, and debt-to-equity shows how much it borrows. For a bank, deposits are the raw material that it lends out, so debt-to-equity is not meaningful, and operating cash flow swings with every rupee of new loans and deposits. The ledger shows trailing operating cash flow of about minus ₹14,569 crore for TMB, which only reflects the loan book growing faster than deposits and not a cash shortage.[1] We therefore replace those two tests with return on equity, return on assets, bad-loan ratios, capital ratios, margin, deposit mix, cost-to-income and credit cost, which is how the Reserve Bank and the rating agencies read a bank.[2][17]

Plain-English glossary for this report

This glossary defines the terms the rest of the report relies on, so that you can follow the bank's numbers without a finance background. Each term is explained again where it first matters, but the plain meaning is gathered here for reference.

  • Net interest income (NII): interest earned on loans and investments minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings.
  • Net interest margin (NIM): NII as a share of the assets that earn interest; a rising NIM means the bank keeps more of each rupee lent.
  • CASA: current and savings account deposits, the cheapest funding a bank has.
  • Gross NPA / net NPA (GNPA (gross non-performing assets) / NNPA (net non-performing assets)): loans that have stopped paying for 90 days, before and after the bank sets aside money for them.
  • Provision coverage ratio (PCR): the share of bad loans the bank has already covered by setting aside money.
  • Capital adequacy ratio (CRAR) and CET1: the bank's own capital as a share of its risk-weighted loans; CET1 counts only the highest-quality capital. The Reserve Bank's minimum CRAR is 11.50%.[13]
  • Return on equity (ROE) and return on assets (ROA): yearly profit as a share of shareholders' money and of total assets.
  • Price-to-book (P/B): the share price divided by the net worth per share; a bank trading at 1.0 costs the same as its accounting value.
  • Special mention accounts (SMA): loans that are paying late but are not yet bad; SMA 1 and 2 are 31 to 90 days late.
  • Credit cost: the yearly charge for bad loans as a share of loans.
  • Cost-to-income ratio: running costs divided by net income; lower is more efficient.
  • Loan-to-value (LTV): the loan amount as a share of the value of the gold or property pledged.
  • Expected credit loss (ECL): new Reserve Bank accounting from 1 April 2027 that makes banks set aside money for losses they expect, not only those that have happened.[8]
  • RAM: the bank's label for retail, agriculture and MSME loans, which are 94.33% of its book.[4] The next chapter uses these terms to judge how strong the business is.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Three things make the business strong. First, the loans are well secured. Gross bad loans fell from 1.01% a year ago to 0.61%, net bad loans from 0.26% to 0.14%, and unsecured lending is only 0.20% of advances.[7][5] The bank has also set aside money against 96.61% of bad loans when technical write-offs (loans already written off in the books but still pursued) are counted, and 77.49% when they are not.[8][31] Second, the bank is very well capitalised. Its capital adequacy ratio is 32.39% against a minimum of 11.50%, and the highest-quality part (CET1) is 31.36%.[10][13] Third, it gets more done with each person: profit per employee was ₹34.66 lakh a year, up 36.45%, and the cost-to-income ratio fell to 38.28% from 43.81% a year earlier.[3][8]

Four things make it weak. Geography is the first. Tamil Nadu supplies 80.9% of advances and 79.5% of deposits, so one state's weather, politics and economy drive the bank.[17] The bank's own annual report lists this as a weakness.[13] Gold is the second. A 48.24% share in one collateral type means a sharp fall in the gold price would hit the collateral behind almost half the book.[5] Funding is the third. CASA slipped to 25.35% of deposits, down 201 basis points in a year, because term deposits grew 28.96% while CASA grew 16.26%.[3][4] Scale and the age of the turnaround are the fourth: the bank is small, with about ₹12,856 crore of market value, and its fast growth is only about two years old, as the present chief executive took charge on 29 August 2024.[21][14]

How does this compare with its neighbours? On bad loans TMB is the best of the six South Indian listed banks we checked, with gross bad loans of 0.61% against 0.69% at Karur Vysya Bank, 1.24% at South Indian Bank, 1.44% at City Union Bank, 1.58% at CSB Bank and 2.29% at DCB Bank.[21][22][24][23][25][26] On return on equity it trails Karur Vysya (21.0% on the aggregator's trailing basis, against 14.7% for TMB) and leads City Union (13.3%), South Indian (13.9%), CSB (14.1%) and DCB (12.1%).[21][22][23][24][25][26] On price, it is the cheap one among the high-quality names: 8.14 times earnings and 1.19 times book value, against 11.58 and 2.43 at Karur Vysya.[21][22] The question for the rest of this book is whether that discount is a mispricing or a fair charge for concentration, gold exposure and legal risk.

The legal and ownership overhang

TMB has no promoter, and its register holds an unusual item. The shareholding pattern shows 8.77% of shares in a 'court cases' bucket and 22.43% held by overseas corporate bodies, mostly legacy foreign holders from share transfers in 2007 and 2008.[16][17] The Enforcement Directorate (ED) issued two show-cause notices. The first, about the share transfers, led to a ₹17 crore penalty that the appellate tribunal cut to ₹3.40 crore in July 2026.[17][9] The second, about the 2016 bonus issue, proposes a penalty of ₹1,037 crore and is undecided; management says the offence, if proved, is chargeable at ₹2.00 lakh, which the bank has fully provided.[9] A ₹1,037 crore penalty would be about 8% of the bank's market value and about 10% of its net worth, which the bank reports as ₹10,769 crore.[6][21] Neither the bank nor the ED has appealed the July order so far.[11]

India Ratings gave the bank an 'IND AA-' issuer rating with a stable outlook on 5 October 2026 and named these disputes, the bank's small scale and the Tamil Nadu concentration as constraints. It listed a fall in CET1 below 15%, loss of senior management, or a material effect from the legal disputes as triggers for a downgrade.[17] A rating one notch below the top private banks is one reason the market charges a discount. The risk is a low-probability event with a large size, so Chapter 6 treats it as part of the red-team case.

Management and the one-person risk

The Reserve Bank approved Salee Sukumaran Nair as managing director and chief executive on 16 August 2024 for three years from the day he took charge, which was 29 August 2024.[18][14] The bank has since hired other senior executives from the State Bank of India and Union Bank of India, including a chief risk officer, head of credit and chief financial officer.[17] Almost all of the improvement in growth, bad loans and efficiency has happened in his tenure, and the term ends in August 2027. Any change in leadership before or at that date is a risk we cannot quantify. The risk is not about his skill. It is about timing and dependence. A bank that has changed this much under one leader has not yet shown that the new habits survive without him. We found no statement on an extension. 3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter gives the numbers first and the judgement afterwards. Layer 1 compares where each number stands with the direction it is moving, because a bank that is improving fast can be a better buy than a steady one at the same price. The numbers come from the bank's filings for the recent periods and from the aggregator Trendlyne for the five-year tables.[15][10]

How the money is made, step by step. A bank starts with the interest on loans, a yield of 10.32% in Q2. It subtracts what it pays on deposits (5.85%) and borrowings. It keeps a net interest margin of 4.19% on its earning assets.[8] It then adds fee income and subtracts staff and branch costs, which are 38.28% of net income. It sets aside money for bad loans, a credit cost of 0.09%. It pays tax at about 26%. The result is a return on assets of 2.18%.[8][6][3] Multiply that by the leverage the bank runs. Its assets are about eight times its equity (₹85,951 crore of assets over ₹10,769 crore of net worth). Return on equity is then 16.92% on the quarter's annualised profit.[6][3] A return on assets above 2% is unusual for an Indian bank. The gold loan book earns 2.89% on assets, which is why the whole bank can.[5]

Concentration is where the risk sits. Tamil Nadu is 80.9% of advances, gold loans are 48.24% of advances, and gold loans are 2.7 times the bank's net worth (₹29,280 crore against ₹10,769 crore).[17][5][6] Those loans are safe so long as the gold is worth more than the loan. The bank's own sensitivity table shows loan-to-value rising from 62.35% to 77.94% if gold falls 20% and to 83.13% if it falls 25%, with 3.13% and 11.13% of gold loans, respectively, becoming larger than the gold behind them.[5] On the 25% case that is about ₹3,260 crore of loans, equal to roughly 30% of net worth, that would be under-collateralised on paper; recovery would also depend on how fast the gold could be auctioned.[5][6]

Cyclicality works through three channels. The rate cycle: 52% of loans are linked to the repo rate and 43% to the bank's own benchmark, so the margin moves with the repo rate while deposits reprice only as they mature.[11] The gold cycle: a rising gold price lifts collateral values and loan sizes, and a falling price does the opposite; the chief executive said on the call that gold crossed USD 4,000 an ounce the day before.[11] The credit cycle: unsecured lending is only 0.20% of advances, so a consumer credit downturn would barely touch the book, but a Tamil Nadu farm or small-business downturn would.[5][17]

Our variant perception

A variant perception is the specific point where our view differs from what the price implies, and the reason for the difference. There are two, and they pull in opposite directions. The first is that we think the market is too pessimistic about the level of returns. At 1.19 times book value the price implies a return on equity of 12.8%; we estimate 14.3% for the next two years, based on the guided margin of 4.10%, a cost-to-income ratio near 42.5% and a credit cost of 0.30%, more than three times today's 0.09%. The second is that we are well below the watchlist seed on growth. The seed quoted advances up 29.32% and profit up 42.09%, and an investor who extends those rates would pay far more. We do not. Management guides to 22% plus for the year, which implies about 7.3% advances growth in the second half against 13.7% in the first; to a margin that is 9 basis points lower; and to a cost-to-income ratio 4 to 6 points higher.[11][13][4] Our FY27 earnings per share is ₹108 and FY28 is ₹112, which is only 3% higher and slightly below a latest-quarter run-rate of ₹114 a year (₹28.49 x 4).[6] In other words the quarter that the seed celebrates is close to our FY28 estimate. We may be too cautious on one input, the margin. Our FY27 and FY28 estimates use the guided 4.10%, which is 9 basis points below the Q2 print. We do not use that as the base because the guide, the funding mix and the Q1 to Q2 fall of 10 basis points all point the other way.

Net, the stock is not obviously cheap, because the reported discount depends on the cost of equity and on the ED case. It is fairly priced for a bank that keeps earning 13%, and cheap if it earns about 14% and the ED penalty stays at the ₹2.00 lakh the bank has provided. That is why the plan is a limit order at support, where the asymmetry is high enough to pay for being wrong, not a purchase at ₹811.85, where it is about 2.75 to 1 on our base target and falls to about 1.5 to 1 if both multiples stay at today's levels.[1]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A red team is the part of the process that argues against the conclusion. Its job is to build the strongest honest case that we are wrong, to find the weakest assumption behind each estimate, and to name the single claim on which everything rests. This chapter does that for the 12-month value of ₹1,010, the earnings estimates of ₹108 and ₹112 per share, the implied-return reading, and the capital estimate.

The red-team bear case

The bear case starts with the thing the bank is proud of. Gold loans are 48.24% of advances at a yield of 10.69% and a return on assets of 2.89%, in a quarter when management says gold crossed USD 4,000 an ounce the day before the call.[5][11] The bank grew gold loans from ₹21,022 crore to ₹29,280 crore in a year; because a gold loan is sized to the value of the gold, part of that growth follows the gold price and would reverse with it. If gold falls 25%, 11.13% of the gold book, about ₹3,260 crore or 30% of net worth, would be larger than the gold behind it, and the bank would have to auction collateral into a falling market.[5][6] The record return on equity of 16.92% is a result of the best conditions for exactly this book.

The second bear argument is that the margin has already turned. NIM was 4.29% in Q1 and 4.19% in Q2, the cost of deposits rose 17 basis points in a quarter, CASA is losing share, the bank borrowed ₹1,000 crore at 7.5% from refinance lines, and it raised loan yields by 22 basis points in a quarter to hold the margin.[8][11] Yields can be pushed up only so far on a book that is half gold, with South Indian Bank growing gold loans 41% and CSB Bank 36% in the same quarter, so competition for the same borrowers is intense.[24][25] The chief executive's reply is that the 4.10% guide is 'always a minimum', that the repo rate has just risen by 25 basis points, and that foreign-currency deposits are plentiful, so deposit rates may not rise quickly.[11] That is a fair rebuttal, and it is why the margin is a WATCH and not a FAIL. But a guide below the latest print still says that the best is behind, and we have chosen to believe the guide in the base case.

The third is the legal case. The ED's second notice proposes a penalty of ₹1,037 crore, about 8% of market value and 10% of net worth, and adjudication is pending with no date.[9][21][6] The bank says the offence is chargeable at ₹2.00 lakh and has provided that amount, which is legal advice and not a ruling. The 22.43% of shares held by overseas corporate bodies and the 8.77% in the court-cases bucket are also an overhang: if the disputes clear, a large block of shares could come to market.[16] Finally, the stock has no analyst coverage and mutual funds hold 0.66% of it, so it has few natural buyers when it falls.[20][16]

Weakest assumption in each estimate

For the FY27 earnings per share of ₹108, the weakest assumption is the provision line: ₹40 crore a quarter, against ₹53.93 crore in Q1 and ₹33.42 crore in Q2.[6] The bank has fully provided for SMA 1 and 2 accounts early, which pulls forward some of the cost of the new expected credit loss rules, so the second half could be lighter or heavier than our guess. The evidence that would show us wrong is a Q3 net profit below ₹400 crore or above ₹450 crore, and we will know when Q3 FY27 results are published; the date is not announced, but the bank reported Q1 on 27 July and Q2 on 26 October, so we expect late January 2027.[12][29]

For the FY28 earnings per share of ₹112, there are two weak assumptions. The first is loan growth: we hold it at about 15%, the second-half FY27 pace, and the 22% guide implies a second half near 7% a half, so growth could re-accelerate or stall. Either would still leave a very large buffer, so the error would not change the decision. We will know from the March 2027 annual disclosures.
