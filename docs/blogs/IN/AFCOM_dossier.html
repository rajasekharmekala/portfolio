1. What the company does

Afcom Holdings Limited is a cargo airline based in Chennai. Capt. Deepak Parasuraman started the company in 2013, and it listed on the SME platform of BSE Limited on 9 August 2024.[1] SME stands for small and medium enterprise, and the platform is a separate board of the exchange for small companies. The IPO sold 68,36,400 new shares at ₹108 each, a ₹10 face value plus a ₹98 premium.[1] Its BSE scrip code is 544224. It flies freight only, and it carries no passengers.

Customers are freight forwarders, which are firms that book cargo space for exporters and importers. A forwarder pays Afcom to move a shipment from one airport to another, a model the company calls airport-to-airport carriage.[1] The goods include electronics, medicines, seafood and general freight. A sales agent, the TTK Group, brings in business from forwarders such as DHL, Kuehne+Nagel, DSV, DB Schenker and Geodis.[6] Afcom also sells space through airline tie-ups, including Etihad, Turkish Airlines, SriLankan and Virgin Atlantic.[6]

Revenue comes in two ways. On scheduled flights Afcom sells space by weight. On charters one customer pays for the whole aircraft. In the year to March 2026, 1,129 of 1,923 trips were charters, which is 58.7 percent.[1] Management said that on many domestic charters only one leg carries cargo, because the client pays for the round trip.[5] The company reports a yield, meaning the revenue it earns per kilogram carried, of US$2.54, against a direct cost of US$1.58 per kilogram.[1] The gap of 96 US cents per kilogram is what pays for rent on the aircraft, head office costs, interest and tax. More than 60 percent of billing is in US dollars, and management says all fuel cost increases are passed to customers as a surcharge.[5]

Afcom does not own its aircraft. It rents them under dry leases of 6 to 8 years from specialist lessors, among them Flight Lease 737 Limited and Vman Aviation Services IFSC Private Limited.[8] A dry lease gives Afcom the bare aircraft. Afcom then pays the rent and also pays for crew, fuel, maintenance, insurance and airport charges.[8] Lessors ask for deposits and advance payments. At 31 March 2026 the balance sheet held ₹140.15 crore of other deposits, much of it with lessors, plus ₹56.27 crore of prepaid aircraft expenses.[1] These deposits matter, because they are cash that left the business and sits with someone else.

The fleet today is three Boeing 737-800 freighters. The third aircraft received its registration certificate from the aviation regulator, the DGCA, in March 2026.[17] It flew only 98.8 hours in the June 2026 quarter, against 625.3 and 442.3 hours on the first two.[6] The network runs from Chennai through hubs in the Maldives, Bangkok and Hanoi to Singapore, Kuala Lumpur, Ho Chi Minh City and Jakarta.[1] A new Navi Mumbai to Dubai World Central route started on 17 August 2026 with three flights a week of 20 tonnes each.[15][14] A long-term jet fuel contract with Hindustan Petroleum took effect on 1 September 2026.[13] On 2 September 2026 Afcom signed a letter of intent with Boeing for up to four 777-8F wide-body freighters. The letter is not a purchase order, because it is subject to final documents.[10][11] In December 2025 Afcom also signed a business agreement with Nauru Air Corporation, now called Naoero Air, to reach the Australia and Pacific region.[21]

Measures used in this report

Four measures run through the numbers, so each gets one plain definition here. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, which means profit before the costs of financing the business and of using its assets. cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before financing and investing, and we shorten it to CFO. free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying the assets it needs. working capital is the money tied up in day-to-day operations, such as bills customers have not yet paid.

Three more measures describe returns and balance-sheet strength. return on capital employed (ROCE) is operating profit divided by the money invested in the business. debt-to-equity is total debt divided by shareholders' money, and for Afcom we show it twice, with and without aircraft lease obligations. capital expenditure is spending on plant and equipment that will last for years; Afcom rents its aircraft, so most of its aircraft spending shows up as deposits and lease payments instead.

Four measures describe value and promises. earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares. price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share, so a reading of 30 means you pay ₹30 for each ₹1 of yearly profit, and we write it as P/E after this point. dilution is the fall in each owner's slice of the company when new shares are issued. guidance is the company's own published forecast for a coming period, and chapter 2B checks it against what happened.

  • Dry lease is a rental of the bare aircraft: the lessor supplies the plane, and the airline supplies crew, fuel, maintenance and insurance.
  • Charter is a flight sold to one customer for a whole aircraft; a scheduled flight sells space to many customers by weight.
  • Freight forwarder is a firm that books cargo space for shippers and is Afcom's direct customer.
  • Yield is the revenue earned per kilogram of cargo carried; cost per kilogram is the direct cost of carrying it.

  • Designated Indian Carrier is a status from the Ministry of Finance that exempts the airline from the 29 percent VAT on jet fuel at Chennai for export flying.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Afcom's strength is a position that few Indian companies hold: it is a dedicated freighter airline with its own air operator permit, a network of transshipment hubs and a fuel cost edge. In February 2026 the Ministry of Finance named it a Designated Indian Carrier. That status removes the 29 percent state VAT on jet fuel at Chennai for export flying, and management puts the saving at 5 to 7 percent of operating cost.[16] The company also reports nil findings in its safety and security audits in FY26, which matters because a grounded aircraft stops a third of the fleet.[1]

The second strength is the unit economics. A direct cost of US$1.58 per kilogram against a yield of US$2.54 leaves a spread of 37.8 percent.[1] Aircraft are also busy: the fleet averaged 19 trips per aircraft per week in FY26, and management quotes peaks of 11.45 and 10.51 flying hours a day on the first two aircraft.[1][5] In the June 2026 quarter the first aircraft ran at 98 percent utilisation and the second at 69 percent.[6] A freighter that earns money only when it flies has a strong reason to keep flying, and Afcom has done that.

The weaknesses are as specific. First, the fleet is small and rented, so one aircraft in the workshop is one third of capacity, and the lessor sets the terms. The placement document itself lists dependence on a few lessors as a risk, and says their names are confidential.[8] Second, a large share of FY26 was charter work lifted by the Gulf conflict. Management's own risk list says charter volumes and the yield premium 'may not recur at FY26 levels' if Gulf hub capacity returns.[1] Third, customers are few. Two customers took 27.2 percent and 17.0 percent of FY26 revenue, and in FY25 one customer took 72.1 percent.[1] Fourth, Afcom competes with the cargo holds of passenger airlines and with other freighter operators, and has no published market share.[1]

The evidence on competitive position is therefore mixed. Costs and utilisation look good, but a rented fleet and a short customer list do not make a lasting advantage. Margins of 40 percent would normally attract rivals. One reason they may last is that new freighters take years to arrive. The reason they may not is that Gulf carriers are returning: in June 2026 Middle East carriers grew 5.6 percent after earlier declines.[25]

Where the demand comes from

Air cargo demand grew 3.4 percent in 2025, and Asia-Pacific carriers grew 8.4 percent, the fastest region.[23] Yields fell 1.5 percent in 2025 but stayed 37.2 percent above 2019, which is why freighters earn well.[23] In June 2026 the industry's own forecast cut 2026 volume growth to 0.2 percent after the Gulf conflict, while expecting yields to rise 6.5 percent and fuel costs to rise by nearly 40 percent.[24] Monthly data since then are mixed: demand rose 8.5 percent in June and 3.9 percent in July, yields fell from June, and several Middle East-linked lanes contracted sharply in July.[25][26]

For Afcom this means the tide is up but uneven. The Chennai hub grew international cargo 12.9 percent in the eleven months to February 2026, against 5.4 percent for India, which suits a Chennai-based carrier.[5] The Gulf disruption helped Afcom in March, April and May 2026, and management said demand in April and May stayed 'better than the average' but 'not at the same level' as March.[5] A fair reading is that part of the 2026 profit rate comes from a shortage that the industry expects to ease.

Quality of the reported numbers

FY26 was Afcom's first year under Ind AS, and the change moves profit around. Under the old rules, FY25 profit after tax was ₹48.42 crore; restated under the new rules it is ₹36.94 crore, which is 23.7 percent lower.[2][1] The new rules also take aircraft rent out of operating cost. Instead, rent shows up as depreciation of the right-of-use asset (₹39.23 crore in FY26) and interest on lease liabilities (₹16.15 crore).[1] So the 40.52 percent EBITDA margin looks higher than an airline's margin under the old rules. If we deduct those two items from EBITDA, the margin is 31.3 percent of revenue, a figure we calculate and that you can verify from the same page.[1]

Three other items deserve a plain mention. The auditor named lease accounting, the provision for return-condition costs (₹9.67 crore) and the preferential issue as key audit matters.[1] Contingent liabilities, meaning disputed claims not yet booked as costs, were ₹6.92 crore, nearly all one income tax dispute.[1] Related-party dealings were small, at ₹4.02 lakh of purchased services and ₹28.36 lakh of other expense with a consulting firm linked to the whole-time director.[1] One governance point is pay: the notice for the 25 September 2026 meeting proposed to double the managing director's monthly pay from ₹5 lakh to up to ₹10 lakh.[1]

This chapter sets out the numbers and then asks the Layer 1 question: how do fixed levels compare with the direction of travel? Afcom reports on a standalone basis, in ₹ lakh. We divide by 100 to show ₹ crore, the unit most Indian readers use. Three ideas recur. A static level is where a measure stands today, such as a return on capital of 22.9 percent. A trajectory is how fast it is moving, such as deposits and advances growing from 25 percent of assets to 43 percent in fifteen months. An archetype is the portfolio's label for the kind of business it is, which sets the bar to clear: compounder, turnaround or tactical momentum.

The price tools appear in this chapter and the next, so each gets one definition. The relative strength index (RSI) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has been moving. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing in rupees. The 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days, and the 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average work the same way over longer spans. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend on the Weinstein scale; Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase between them.

Six more terms describe the trade. A base is a sideways stretch of trading that comes before a larger move, the pivot is the top of that range, and a breakout is a move above the pivot on heavy volume. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, measured from today's price. A measured move is a target that adds the height of a base to its top.

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on future cash. A reverse DCF works backward from today's price and asks how fast cash flow would have to grow, for ten years, to justify it. DCF stands for discounted cash flow, a way to turn future cash into a value today. The ledger uses a 12 percent yearly discount rate and a terminal multiple of 20 times cash flow in year ten.

The ledger says the market is pricing growth of 24.4 percent a year for ten years.[33] The starting point is 'normalised owner's cash flow' of ₹57.2 crore, which is 75 percent of trailing operating cash flow of ₹76.3 crore, because free cash flow is negative at -₹122.9 crore. Growth of 24.4 percent a year for ten years would multiply cash flow almost nine times. Few companies do that, and it is a very high bar for a company with three rented aircraft.

The picture changes if cash flow catches up with profit. Profit is ₹140.05 crore on a trailing basis, and if all of it became owner cash, the price would need about 12.4 percent a year. So the market is paying for a hope that cash conversion improves, and the debate is whether that hope has evidence. There is no consensus estimate to check it against. A consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for a number, and no brokerage covers this stock.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER, in a weak form. The rule is simple: a holding that fails a hard red line, or fails two operating KPIs, is a candidate for replacement.[34] Afcom fails both. Three-year CFO divided by profit is 0.12 against a red line of 0.40, and two of five operating tests fail. The chart is Stage 3 and reward-to-risk is 1.18 against 3.5. The weak form means we do not sell everything today. No stop has closed, the next cash evidence is due within about four weeks, and the loss on the kept shares is limited by the stop. The expected value is thin: +7.5 percent in the scenario table, but -5.98 percent if base-path profit is cut to ₹115 crore, as chapter 5 shows. So the kept shares are not a bet on a positive return. They are a small, paid-for look at the November cash flow statement, and they are an explicit exception to the red line.

June already scores 71.6 percent. The September quarter is the half-year CFO and EBITDA less the June figures, so both are checked separately and not as a six-month blend.
