1. What the company does & day-to-day vertical operations

Afcom is an air-cargo company, not a parcel courier. It flies cargo on set routes and charter trips for freight forwarders, logistics firms, and cargo agents. Those customers arrange shipments for exporters, importers, and others [1][6]. Afcom handles bookings, route plans, airport slots, cargo checks, weight and load plans, flights, and bill collection. Public reports list cargo and flight totals. They do not show customer contract terms, profit by route, or revenue per shipment [1].

The fleet uses Boeing 737-800 freighters held on dry leases. Afcom runs the flights and pays lease, maintenance, and insurance costs. The annual report says FY26 was its first full year of self-operated flying after wet leases. That shift changes how revenue and costs appear, so older years are harder to compare [1]. Three aircraft were in the fleet at year-end. Two more narrow-body aircraft were in the induction pipeline. Four wide-body aircraft were planned for Phase II. Pipeline and planned aircraft are not delivered capacity [1][19].

Scheduled flights give forwarders set lanes and times. Charter flights serve one-off needs for dedicated capacity. Charters helped move cargo delayed by Gulf hub disruption in FY26. Afcom warns that demand may fall as those hubs return to normal [1][5]. The business also faces fuel, currency, aircraft, and maintenance risks. Fuel surcharges and U.S.-dollar revenue offset only part of these costs [1].

The service list includes general cargo, dangerous goods, project cargo, high-value goods, and time-sensitive goods such as medicine and fresh products [6]. These are service types, not separate business lines. Filings do not show that each type is material. They also do not show an owned parcel network. A Dubai unit formed in May 2026 had no sales and had not started work in the Q1 FY27 filing [3].

2. Competitive moat, industry structure & external learning hub

Air freight competes on speed, reliability, route access, and open space. Afcom aims to offer dedicated cargo flights on selected India–Asia and Middle East routes. Forwarders and logistics firms provide the shipments. A small operator may respond fast to charter needs. It has fewer backup routes and less bargaining power than a global network [1][6].

Aircraft access is not a lasting moat because Afcom leases its fleet. Stronger edges could come from approvals, route rights, reliable dispatch, good aircraft use, customer trust, and know-how. These edges are not yet proven. Two customers each made up more than 10% of sales in FY26. Their identities are not public. The company ran 1,129 charter trips, so results are exposed to disruption-linked demand [1].

Blue Dart has express parcel routes, domestic air service, and DHL reach. TCI Express mainly uses road service. These are useful contrasts, but not close peers. Their pickup, hubs, last-mile delivery, and customer mix differ from Afcom's airport-to-airport model [21][22][24][25]. Passenger airline cargo is another option for shippers, but flight times, cargo priority, and cost splits differ. We avoid ranking peers by P/E and compare how they work instead [1][22][26].

Where Afcom can win — and what has to be proven

Afcom can win a lane when forwarders value dedicated lift, the operator secures airport windows and handling, and utilisation spreads lease and crew costs. Its network includes South and Southeast Asia, Maldives hub links and Dubai World Central, with a strategic alliance involving Naoero Air Corporation described in FY26 materials [1]. Filings do not disclose route frequency, load factor, customer retention, contract tenor or revenue by corridor. Network expansion is therefore a possibility, not a proven network effect.

Industry traffic supports a broad need for fast freight, but global cargo growth does not guarantee Afcom’s share or unit economics. IATA, DGCA and Airports Authority of India provide market context, not issuer forecasts [13][14][15][16][17][18]. Afcom’s own annual report is more decision-relevant because it links FY26 charter strength to Middle East capacity withdrawals and warns that the demand may not recur [1].

FY26 shows execution progress: self-operated dry-leased flying, a third aircraft, the Dubai World Central launch, 24,353 tonnes of cargo and revenue from operations up 144.27% [1]. The first full year on the new operating basis changes cost and revenue presentation; growth should not be attributed wholly to organic demand.
