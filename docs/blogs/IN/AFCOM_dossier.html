1. What the company does

Afcom Holdings is an air cargo airline based in Chennai. It does not carry passengers. It flies freight on Boeing 737-800 jets that were converted from passenger planes, and each jet carries roughly 20 to 22 tonnes[5]. The company was formed in 2013, was cleared to run a cargo airline in 2017, received its air operator certificate in December 2024, and listed on the BSE SME platform on 9 August 2024[27][2]. In plain terms, this is a young airline with under two years of flying its own aircraft, so the history you can judge it on is short.

The company does not own its planes. It rents them from aircraft lessors under a dry lease, which means the lessor supplies the bare aircraft and Afcom supplies the crew, fuel, maintenance, insurance and landing fees[2]. Before that it flew on a wet lease, where another operator supplied everything and charged one all-in fee. The wet-lease fee fell from ₹106.0 crore in FY25 to ₹33.5 crore in FY26, while aircraft fuel rose from ₹11.4 crore to ₹116.6 crore[6]. This switch is the main reason revenue jumped, because the airline now books the full freight charge itself[2].

Customers are mostly freight forwarders, the firms that gather parcels from many shippers and buy space on planes[14]. Afcom reaches them through a general sales and service agent. The Nov 2024 call said the agent for India is the TTK group (Taylor Logistics), that it holds exclusivity for India, and that it guarantees 50 percent of each flight's revenue[8]. Afcom earns money in two ways: a price per kilogram on scheduled flights, and a fee per trip on charter flights that a customer books for a whole plane. In FY26 the company made 1,923 trips and 1,129 of them, or 58.7 percent, were charters[2].

Fuel is the largest cost that moves with flying. Management says the extra fuel cost is added to the freight price as a surcharge, so the customer pays it, and that more than 60 percent of billing is in US dollars[7]. Fuel was 20.0 percent of FY26 revenue[6]. The network runs from Chennai to Colombo, the Maldives, Bangkok, Hanoi and Dubai, and since 16 August 2026 includes a scheduled Navi Mumbai to Dubai World Central service three times a week[5][26]. The company plans five narrow-body freighters and has signed a non-binding letter of intent with Boeing for up to four 777-8F wide-body freighters[24].

To fund this plan the company has raised ₹73.83 crore at its public offer in August 2024, about ₹204 crore through a preferential issue of shares and warrants in December 2025, and ₹199.85 crore through a qualified institutions placement in May 2026[2]. Warrants are rights to buy shares later at a fixed price; 11.5 lakh of them are still unconverted, and at ₹647.37 still unpaid on each that is about ₹74.45 crore (our arithmetic)[4][2]. That is the money side of the story, and chapter 3 shows how the cash flows compare.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Afcom's advantage is real but narrow, and it rests on execution rather than on something rivals cannot copy. It was early in India with a stand-alone cargo airline, it holds an air operator certificate, and it was named a Designated Indian Carrier in February 2026. That status gives an exemption from VAT on aviation fuel in Tamil Nadu that the company estimates cuts fuel cost by 5 to 7 percent on qualifying flights[5]. A credit rating agency gave it ACUITE BBB+ with a stable outlook in January 2026 and described its management as experienced and its freighter fleet as expanding[14].

The demand backdrop is favourable. The International Air Transport Association (IATA) reported global air cargo demand up 3.4 percent in 2025 and Asia-Pacific airlines up 8.4 percent, with Asia-Pacific capacity up 7.4 percent[21]. For August 2026, demand rose 4.4 percent while capacity fell 0.1 percent[22]. Tight capacity helps a carrier that is adding planes. The company also says Chennai grew international cargo 12.9 percent in April to February of FY26 against 5.4 percent nationally, which is a company reading of airport data that we could not check against the source[6].

The weaknesses are just as concrete. First, charter work was 58.7 percent of FY26 trips, and the annual report itself says charter volumes and the extra price they earn may not recur at FY26 levels if Gulf hub capacity returns[2]. Second, the fleet sits under dry leases with a limited number of lessors, and a meaningful share of revenue comes from a concentrated set of charter and forwarder relationships[2]. Third, the company's weight-based flights earn 2 to 3 times the per-kilogram yield of charters, so a shift to charters changes the mix of earnings, not just the volume[6].

The cash record is the fourth weakness and the one that matters most for this holding. Audited operating cash flow was ₹35.99 crore in FY26 against EBITDA of ₹238.14 crore, a conversion of about 15 percent, and the annual report blames working capital absorbed by 144 percent revenue growth[2]. The rating agency also noted that receivable days were around 95 in FY25 and that the bank working-capital limits were used at about 96 percent[14]. A cargo airline that grows this fast can be profitable on paper and short of cash in practice, which is why chapter 2B scores cash conversion separately.

Competition comes from belly space on passenger airlines and from other freighter operators[2]. Afcom's reply is route choice: shorter regional lanes where a 737 freighter is cheaper to run, and transshipment through the Maldives, Bangkok and Hanoi[5]. The verdict on competitive position is therefore moderate: strong current economics and a supportive market, but a thin moat, a short track record as an airline, and results that lean on a temporary charter premium.

What this means for an owner

For a shareholder the business is a levered bet on aircraft count and on freight rates. More planes lift revenue almost one for one if the lanes stay profitable, and higher fuel is passed on to customers. The risk is that profit is front-loaded while cash, receivables and lease payments come due later. The upside is real: audited profit is large, the margin is high and the balance sheet now holds more cash than borrowings. The downside is also real: a young airline can lose its charter premium faster than it adds planes. Chapter 2B turns these points into five scored measures. Chapter 3 then puts numbers on the same story, so you can see how much of the profit has become cash.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter shows five years of numbers and then tests them against the Layer 1 rules in the repository. Start with how much history exists. The company listed on 9 August 2024, so the ledger holds 493 daily price bars, about 26 months of trading[1][2]. There is no three-year price record, which means no three-year median price-to-earnings figure exists for it, and we found none published. For accounts, we found five fiscal years of figures: FY22 and FY23 from the offer document, FY24 and FY25 from the FY25 and FY26 annual reports, and FY26 audited[13][3][2]. Only FY26 is a full year as an airline flying its own aircraft on Ind AS accounts. That limits how far trends can be trusted.

Revenue and profit have grown very fast from a small base. Revenue went from ₹84.14 crore in FY23 to ₹583.11 crore in FY26, which is a compound growth rate of about 90.7 percent a year, and profit after tax went from ₹13.59 crore to ₹121.90 crore, about 107.8 percent a year (our arithmetic from filed figures)[13][2]. The scale matters for interpretation: the company earned ₹121.90 crore on assets of ₹934.78 crore, so a few quarters can change the picture a lot. Small bases make growth rates look huge.

The accounting basis changed in FY26, and it flatters some ratios. Under Ind AS the company shows each leased aircraft as an asset and a matching lease liability. Lease rent leaves operating cost and reappears as depreciation and interest, so the EBITDA margin rose to 40.52 percent from 31.70 percent partly because of the new basis[2][7]. The cash flow statement changes too: lease deposits of ₹98.53 crore and right-of-use payments of ₹112.35 crore sit under investing, not operating[2]. Reported operating cash flow therefore looks better than it would if the rent were counted, and the 0.12 reading in the cash test is, if anything, generous. The true picture is no better.

Cash is the weak point. Audited operating cash flow was ₹35.99 crore in FY26 against profit of ₹121.90 crore. Receivables rose by ₹76.73 crore and other current assets by ₹88.70 crore, which absorbed the cash[2]. Operating cash flow minus investing cash flow was a negative ₹170.72 crore in FY26 (our arithmetic), and it was met by share issues and lease financing[2]. After the May 2026 share placement, cash and equivalents were ₹153.3 crore and borrowings only ₹49.4 crore at 30 June 2026[5]. The funding problem is therefore solved for now. The cash-conversion question remains open.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter takes each estimate and names its weakest assumption, the evidence that would show it wrong, and the date we would know. The reasoning is that a good case should be tested where it is thinnest. For Afcom the thin places are the cash record, the permanence of charter earnings and the timing of aircraft, and we add the red-team bear case, which is the strongest honest argument against our own verdict.

Red-team bear case

The bear argument runs like this. Reported profit is high because a Gulf disruption pushed charter demand to Asian operators, and the annual report says the premium may not recur[2]. Audited operating cash flow covers 12 percent of three-year profit[6][2]. The promoter group's own inter-se transfer is priced at ₹800 a share while the 60-day average price was ₹1,450.07[12]. The company said in June that financing for four 777s had closed[7], then signed only a letter of intent in September[24]. The fleet is three planes against a promise of five by March 2025[8][5]. If all of this is read as a pattern, the shares are priced for a perfect run that the record does not support.

We take that case seriously, which is why we do not add. We do not conclude from it that the shares must be sold now, for three reasons. Profit and margins are audited and large. The company has raised ₹479 crore of equity and holds ₹153.3 crore in cash against only ₹49.4 crore of borrowings[5]. And the one test that would confirm the bear case, the half-year cash flow, is due within weeks. The inter-se transfer is between existing promoters and leaves their total holding unchanged[12], so it is not a sale to the market, though a price this far below the market is worth watching.

Weakest assumption in each estimate

Reverse DCF: the weakest assumption is that trailing cash flow is the right starting point. It is distorted by growth in receivables and by deposits to lessors, which pushes the cash basis up and the profit basis down. Evidence that it is wrong would be a half-year operating cash flow well above or below the roughly ₹68.5 crore needed for a rolling ratio of 0.25, which we expect to see in the half-year filing.

Base target: the weakest assumption is 4.5 aircraft-equivalents earning ₹17.1 crore a month. A lower charter share or late aircraft would cut it. Evidence would be the second-quarter fleet utilisation and yield slides, and whether the fourth and fifth aircraft are flying by the end of December 2026, as the chairman told the trade press on 3 September 2026[24].

Probability weights: the weakest assumption is that bull and bear are equally likely at 25 percent. The company has beaten its revenue and margin statements three times and missed its aircraft and wide-body dates several times, which argues for a slightly heavier bear weight; we kept 25 percent because the beats and the misses roughly offset in value terms.

The hinge claim

The claim that decides the whole verdict is that cash conversion has turned. We test it with a rolling ratio over FY25, FY26 and the half-year to 30 September 2026. The ratio must reach 0.25, which at a half-year profit near ₹79 crore needs operating cash flow of about ₹68.5 crore, or 0.86 times that profit. If it holds, our judgment that the turnaround clause is met stands. If it fails, the audited reading of 0.12 is a pattern and the red line stays failed, and we sell. A single quarter can mislead, which is why we wait for the half-year and do not act on Q1 alone. The test is stricter than a true three-year window, because dropping FY24 lowers the ratio. We keep it as a deliberate, conservative exit rule. A narrow miss would not prove that a true window fails, but we would still sell. If the filing is late, the rule in chapter 7 ends the holding on 31 December 2026.
