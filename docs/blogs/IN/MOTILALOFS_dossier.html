Motilal Oswal Financial Services is an Indian group that earns fees from funds, wealth clients, broking and loans, and it invests a pool of its own money[2][3]. We rate it WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: fee profit is growing fast and its credit rating just rose to AA+, but reported profit swings with the market, and at ₹969.00 the reward is only 0.41 for each 1 of risk[3][13][1]. 1. What the company does

Motilal Oswal Financial Services (MOFSL) began in 1987 as a small sub-broking firm. It listed on the stock exchanges in 2007, and its share offer was subscribed 26.4 times[3]. Today it calls itself the largest integrated capital market player among non-bank firms[3]. One group can sell a retail client a broking account, a mutual fund and a home loan. It can also advise a rich family on investments, and help a company raise money from investors. The group says its business partners work from more than 2,500 locations in 550+ cities and towns[2].

The group runs two engines. The first is a set of operating businesses that earn fees and interest. The second is a pool of the group's own money, called the treasury book, which stood at ₹10,482 crore on 30 June 2026[3]. Management puts most operating profit, after dividends, back into that pool. It says the pool has grown 41% a year since 2014 and has earned a 17% yearly return since inception[3]. The pool is mostly invested in the group's own mutual funds and alternative funds, with little direct share holding[9]. Because the pool is valued at market prices, a falling market cuts reported profit even when customers are happy[5].

The group reports two profit numbers. Operating profit after tax (operating PAT) leaves out the treasury book. It was ₹2,360 crore in FY26, which is the year to March 2026, and ₹609 crore in Q1 FY27, which is the quarter to June 2026[3]. Total profit adds the treasury book and other comprehensive income (OCI), the part of market gains and losses that goes straight to equity and not through the income statement. Total profit was ₹2,043 crore in FY26 and ₹1,513 crore in Q1 FY27[3]. The gap between the two numbers is the most important fact about this company, and it returns in chapters 2B, 3 and 5.

Five operating segments produce the operating profit. Asset and private wealth management is now the largest, at about 55% of operating PAT in Q1 FY27[3][9]. Wealth management, which is retail broking, fund distribution and margin lending, comes next. Capital markets, which is institutional equities and investment banking, and housing finance are the smaller two. The next table shows how each segment contributed in the June 2026 quarter.

Who pays the group, and for what

Mutual fund and alternative fund investors pay a yearly fee that is a small share of the money managed. For the asset management and private equity unit, that fee was 0.86% of average assets in Q1 FY27, against 0.81% in Q4 FY25[9]. Retail broking clients pay brokerage on each trade. They also pay a trail commission, a yearly cut of the funds they hold, on third-party funds the group distributes; management says that yield is stable at around 70 basis points, or 0.70%[4]. Borrowers pay interest on margin trade funding, which is a loan to buy shares with those shares held as security. The group's margin book grew 55% in a year to ₹7,800 crore[3].

Companies pay deal fees when the investment bankers help them raise money. Capital markets fee income was ₹309 crore in FY26, up 39%[5]. Home loan borrowers pay interest, and the housing finance unit had ₹6,164 crore of loans at 30 June 2026, with gross non-performing assets (GNPA) of 1.1% and net non-performing assets (NNPA) of 0.6%[3]. Those two ratios measure loans that have stopped paying, before and after safety provisions. The mix of payers matters because some pay every year whatever the market does, and others pay only when markets are busy.

Recurring revenue versus transaction revenue

Management splits revenue into two kinds. Annual recurring revenue (ARR) is money that repeats, such as fund fees, trail commission and loan interest. Transaction-based revenue (TBR) is money that depends on trades and deals, such as brokerage, one-off sales commissions and deal fees. The ARR share was 56% in FY25, 60% in FY26 and 66% in Q1 FY27[6][5][3]. That is the main reason credit agencies upgraded the group. It is also the reason an investor can reasonably pay a higher price than for a pure broker.

Transaction revenue still moves the quarterly numbers. In Q1 FY27 distribution income in wealth management fell about 40% from the previous quarter to ₹63.7 crore[9]. Management said the next three quarters would be far more muted for this kind of income because last year's level was high[4]. This is an honest warning: part of the profit is still tied to market activity.

The treasury book and why it moves the share price

The treasury book is the part of the business that most resembles an investment company inside a financial firm. Management says the long-run return on this book is about 18% a year, with all of it held as collateral for the group's lenders[5]. The book earned ₹904 crore, including OCI, in Q1 FY27 and lost ₹1,054 crore in Q4 FY26[3]. Over FY26 it lost ₹317 crore[3]. Buyers of the stock therefore own two things at once: a fee business that grows steadily and a leveraged market portfolio that does not.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

MOFSL is strong where it has built scale in several businesses at once and weak where each business is still small. In mutual funds its share of industry assets was 2.9% in June 2026, but its share of new money was 4.2%[3]. A share of new money above the share of assets means the group is gaining ground. The group says its net flow share has stayed above its asset share for several periods[4]. In investment banking it ranked second in the share-placement and IPO league tables for Q1 FY27[3].

The best evidence of strength is the profit of the asset management and private equity unit. It rose from ₹141 crore in Q1 FY26 to ₹245 crore in Q1 FY27, a rise of 73%[9][3]. Assets in that unit grew 31% to ₹2.12 lakh crore[3]. Management also points to a product gap that is closing. Only six of its funds are older than three years, and distributors tend to recommend funds only after three years of record. Eight funds will cross that line by March 2027 and sixteen by March 2028[4]. If those funds perform, the flow engine has room to run.

The evidence is weaker on fund performance. At 30 June 2026 the Motilal Oswal Flexi Cap Fund ranked 39th among 39 funds over one year, with a return of -7.8%, and the Midcap Fund ranked 30th among 30, with a return of -10.1%[9]. The mid-cap fund alone held about ₹36,800 crore[9]. Fund inflows follow performance, so a long stretch of weak returns would hurt the unit that now produces most of the profit growth. On the other side, the Focused Fund ranked first and eight of eleven strategies beat their benchmark over three years[9].

Broking is where the rules are changing. SEBI's study released on 20 August 2026 found that 87.7% of individual derivatives traders lost money in FY26, with total losses of ₹91,685 crore[26]. ICRA says the number of active retail traders in futures and options fell 18% to 87.5 lakh in FY26, while overall volumes stay healthy[25]. Reports in August 2026 said SEBI may study removing weekly expiry contracts, which is not a final decision[27]. MOFSL's total market share in cash, derivatives and commodities fell to 7.6% in Q1 FY27 from 9.2% in Q4 FY26, mostly because of commodities[9]. Broking is now only about 36% of wealth management revenue[9], so the group is less exposed than a pure broker, but not unexposed.

What protects the profits

Four things protect the profit stream. The first is breadth: when broking is slow, fund fees and loan interest still arrive. The second is cost flexibility. The group's finance chief said about 70% of costs in wealth management are variable, and that group profit before tax margins have run at 50% to 52% for several years[4]. The third is funding strength. CRISIL raised the long-term rating to AA+ with a stable outlook on 22 July 2026, ICRA rates the group's bonds AA+ with a stable outlook, and India Ratings upgraded it to AA+ on 15 September 2026[13][14][15]. The fourth is the record: operating PAT has compounded at 33% a year over ten years with an average return on equity of 23%, with no equity dilution since listing[3][5].

These protections are real but not complete. Funding strength comes from rating agencies who weigh the investment book as a cushion. That cushion can shrink when markets fall, which is when it is needed. The 33% ten-year growth rate has also slowed: operating PAT grew 31% in FY25 and 16% in FY26[6][5].

Where the evidence is weaker

Three points deserve caution. First, digital brokers are taking new customers. An analyst on the April 2026 call said 60 to 70% of new SIPs are opened on fintech platforms, and management replied that its edge is research and advice for higher-value clients[5]. Second, promoter ownership has slowly fallen from 69.2% in March 2024 to 66.71% in September 2026, with no pledged shares[18]. Part of the fall in July 2026 was a reclassification of 11 promoter group members, holding 24,45,412 shares or 0.42%, as public holders[19]. Third, borrowings were ₹21,794 crore at June 2026 against net worth of ₹14,429 crore[3]. The group says the investments back its borrowings, and the next chapters test that claim.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A financial group should be judged on the numbers that drive its earnings, not on factory measures. MOFSL earns fees from assets under management, interest from loans, brokerage from trades, and gains from its own investments. We therefore chose five measures. Each one answers a different question: how much income repeats, how fast the fee engine grows, whether growth is broad, how stable the investment profit is, and how safely the group is funded.

The scoring rule scores each measure PASS (1.0), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). A company qualifies when the total is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 and no more than one measure fails. MOFSL scores 3.5 out of 5.0 with one fail, so it qualifies as PASS_WITH_WATCH. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

We compared what management said in the April 2025 and July 2025 calls with what the filings show since. The record is good on the rating and on the mix of earnings. It is poor on the capital markets promise. The treasury forecast is too early to judge, although FY26 went the other way. On the April 2025 call the group said a rating upgrade was possible[6]. It got CRISIL AA+, ICRA AA+ on its bonds and India Ratings AA+ within 17 months[13][14][15].

The miss in capital markets is clear. In July 2025 the group managing director, Navin Agarwal, said he had no reason to believe the coming quarters would be weaker than the first quarter of FY26[7]. Segment profit was ₹101 crore in that first quarter. It then ran at ₹90 crore, ₹70 crore, ₹75 crore and ₹76 crore in the four quarters after the promise. In April 2025 the group managing director said treasury profit could outstrip operating profit in the next decade, and that has not yet held up, since the book lost money in FY26[6][3]. 3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter tests the business against the Layer 1 rules in the scoring file. Those rules were written for ordinary companies, so for a group that lends and invests we read each test in its financial form. The measures that matter here are return on equity, the safety of borrowing, the repeatability of profit, and the price paid for it. Return on capital employed (ROCE), which means profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses, is shown for completeness, at 12.7% on the latest Screener reading[2]. Earnings per share (EPS) is the profit divided across all shares. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying assets, and it is not a useful measure for a lender, because loan growth sits inside operating cash.

Three results stand out. Over three years sales grew 31% a year and net profit 26% a year[2]. Return on equity (ROE) in FY26 was 15.5% by Screener's count, against a ten-year average of 22%[2]. And the price-to-earnings ratio, the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit, is 29.5 now, against a three-year median of 17.6[2][21]. The high ratio is a result of the shrunken profit: trailing earnings per share fell from ₹56.72 in October 2024 to ₹32.92 in July 2026[21].

Cash flow needs a separate warning. For a lender, cash from operations includes the growth in loans and the purchase of investments. MOFSL's cash from operations was -₹6,071 crore in FY26 and -₹350 crore in FY24, so the three-year total is -₹5,206 crore against net profit of ₹6,826 crore, a ratio of -0.76[2]. The scoring rule treats a ratio below 0.25 as a red line for ordinary companies. We treat it here as not decisive, because the money went into assets that appear on the balance sheet: loans grew from ₹13,744 crore in March 2026 to ₹16,251 crore in June 2026, and investments from ₹10,299 crore to ₹12,205 crore[3].
