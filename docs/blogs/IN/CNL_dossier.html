Core Investor Question: What does Creative Newtech do, what is the stock worth against its price, and what should you do with new money today?

Creative Newtech Limited (NSE: CNL, BSE: 544631) is a Mumbai technology distributor. It sells other companies' products through a channel of more than 10,000 partners and increasingly sells its own and licensed brands. Revenue grew from ₹371 Cr in FY19 to ₹2,718 Cr in FY26. Attributable earnings per share grew from ₹5.04 to ₹41.20. The company reports two segments. Market Entry Specialist is 86% of FY26 revenue at a 3.8% segment margin, mostly enterprise and channel trade. Brand Business is 14% of revenue at a 33.2% margin, almost entirely the licensed Honeywell consumer and networking range, and it produces roughly half of EBITDA. In H1 FY27 the brand share rose to 17.4% and EBITDA margin rose to 5.28%. Profit after tax rose 35.5% on 16.6% revenue growth. [29][30][31][33]

1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Operating Cycles

Core Investor Question: How did a Mumbai trading concern become a ₹2,700 Cr technology distributor and brand owner, and what do its past cycles say about how reliably it turns growth into cash?

Creative Newtech's history is three stories told at once. One is a founder who kept adding vendors to a Mumbai trading house. Another is a trademark licence that created a second and richer business offshore. The third is a balance sheet that has repeatedly been asked to fund growth faster than operations could pay for it. Between FY19 and FY26 consolidated total income grew from ₹370.70 Cr to ₹2,717.51 Cr, a 33% compound rate [2]. PAT grew from ₹5.85 Cr to ₹70.29 Cr [2]. Over the same eight years the cash-flow statement shows cumulative operating cash flow of about −₹328 Cr against cumulative reported PAT of ₹241 Cr [12]. Those two facts are the spine of this chapter. Growth has been real. Converting growth into cash has been the exception. Management's multi-year promises have been more reliable on revenue than on cash, margin or the size of the licence business.

Origin: a one-man trading concern that became a principal-to-principal distributor

Ketan Patel started a proprietary concern, Creative Computers, in 1992 as a pure trading business with ten employees. A later investor-deck timeline says two, so treat the headcount as folklore and the year as fact [9]. The company was incorporated on 22 September 2004 and took over the proprietorship in 2005. It became a public limited company in March 2017, just before its IPO [9]. The first lines were Microsoft hardware, Epson printers and AOC monitors. The company's own timeline adds Epson dot-matrix printer aggregation and Microsoft sub-distribution in Maharashtra and Gujarat, then branches in Pune, Bangalore and Ahmedabad (Q4 FY23 investor presentation). By FY16 the business booked ₹198.79 Cr of sales through about 20 branches and roughly 6,000 dealers. It bought in bulk from vendors on its own account and sold to resellers, sub-distributors, system integrators and retailers [9]. That principal-to-principal model is the root of every number that follows. The margin is the thin spread between the vendor's price and the reseller's price, so profit depends on how fast inventory leaves and receivables come back.

The step that changed the economics came in 2015–16. Creative signed a trademark licence with Honeywell International dated 4 December 2015, then valid to 31 December 2020, with royalty payable on net sales [9]. The annual report dates the programme from September 2016 [1]. The licence does not make Creative a distributor of Honeywell goods. Creative designs the products, selects contract factories (about 28 facilities across Asia today) and sells Honeywell-branded electronics. It pays Honeywell a minimum guarantee or a percentage of sales, whichever is higher [1] (Q1 FY24 call). Honeywell audits the factories, approves every pack through its design-language system and scores the licensee each quarter on 10 to 12 parameters. Ketan Patel reported a 4.8 out of 5 in the latest quarterly review before the May 2025 call [6]. The licence sits in Secure Connection Limited, a Hong Kong company incorporated on 21 February 2017 [1]. By FY26 the Brand segment generated ₹380.86 Cr of consolidated revenue [1]. How far that business can scale, and on what renewal terms, is the most important structural question in the company's history.

Management says the distribution book still earns its place because it will 'amortize our cost completely', give 'a foot in the door' and supply market intelligence [7]. The consistent feature is that none of these moves shrank the working capital a distributor must carry, which is why the cash story below repeats.

How the Honeywell structure shapes group economics

Housing the licence offshore has three consequences. First, tax: consolidated tax was ₹11.47 Cr in FY26, exactly the parent's own charge, on consolidated pre-tax profit of ₹81.76 Cr against ₹45.82 Cr standalone. The subsidiary's profit therefore bore no tax, and the effective consolidated rate was about 14% [3]. The CFO explained the mechanism in October 2024: Hong Kong levies no tax on export sales (Q2 FY25 call). Second, minority leakage: Creative owns 77.50% of Secure Connection and its chief executive Mohit Anand owns the rest, so ₹8.42 Cr of FY26 consolidated PAT (12.0%) belonged to non-controlling interest [1][8]. Third, related-party flow: the Indian parent bought ₹155.39 Cr of goods from Secure Connection in FY26 (₹100.09 Cr in FY25) [1]. Shareholders were asked to approve up to ₹750 Cr of such transactions for FY27 [1]. The structure is efficient and transparent in the filings. It also means a large part of group profit sits in an entity that is neither fully owned nor taxed.

Subsidiaries, stake changes and exits

Creative's stake in Secure Connection rose from 52.5% to 77.5% through two share swaps. In August 2023 it issued 5,80,000 shares at ₹450 (₹26.10 Cr) to Sapri Trading DWC LLC for 2,267 Secure Connection shares, lifting the stake to 69.5% (release). In March 2024 it issued 57,325 shares at ₹785 (₹4.50 Cr) to V. D. Patel for 1,066 shares (AR FY24). The CFO's rationale was that a minority holder in an unlisted company has no exit while the whole profitable Honeywell business sat in that subsidiary [8]. The CKart B2B platform went the other way: a slump sale to World Goods Marketplace Pvt Ltd for ₹10 Cr in March 2024. It booked a ₹9.9 Cr gain in other income [8] (AR FY24). The buyer appears in the FY26 related-party schedule [1]. The CyberPowerPC joint venture is held through Creative Ecommerce Ventures (Q3 FY25 deck), in which Creative holds 49%. That followed the purchase of 8,51,700 shares for ₹4.26 Cr by two outside investors in FY25, which made it an associate rather than a subsidiary [1]. Rinavaa Technologies, sometimes listed among group entities, is not a subsidiary. It is a related party on whose board the chairman and a whole-time director sit, with a ₹91.5 lakh advance payable [1].

The structure is still growing. Two step-down subsidiaries of Secure Connection (a US LLC and a Shenzhen technology company) were incorporated in H1 FY27 with no significant operations [10]. A Singapore procurement subsidiary with initial capital of up to USD 10,000 was approved on 4 November 2026 (board outcome). Two acquisitions are pending. The first is Infinova (India) Pvt Ltd, with a board budget of up to USD 4.00 Million (release). Management expected it to close by mid-November plus about 15 days for foreign-exchange approvals [4]. The second is Zicom SaaS Pvt Ltd, at an enterprise value of ₹50.50 Cr, of which ₹25.00 Cr is cash and ₹25.50 Cr is shares. It is subject to an extraordinary general meeting on 28 November 2026 (release). Neither contributes to reported revenue yet.

Leadership and governance changes

Two changes matter. Chief financial officer: Abhijit Kanvinde presented the financials on every call in the data set from May 2022 to November 2025. He resigned on 14 April 2026. Ajit Thakur, hired as Vice President–Finance on 4 December 2025, became CFO on 14 May 2026, the day before the FY26 results call [1] (CFO appointment). That reads as a planned handover rather than a walkout, since the successor was in the building for four and a half months. The timing is still awkward, because the resignation came a month before audited results that showed receivables up 138% and operating cash flow of −₹271.88 Cr [1]. The filings do not say why he left. The new CFO's first guidance was candid: he called the 45-day working-capital level reached in September 2026 unsustainable and pointed to 52 to 55 days [4].

Second, the board. Purvi Patel, co-founder and wife of the chairman, resigned as whole-time director on 11 August 2025 'to pursue philanthropic and social work independently'. She was re-engaged on 26 August 2025 as President–Strategy and Sustainability, later styled Chief Strategy Officer. The role carries unchanged pay of ₹81.68 lakh for FY26 and ₹115.10 lakh proposed for FY27, an office of profit that shareholders approved (AR FY25) [1]. The four independent directors (Mihir Shah, Suresh Bhagavatula, Kurian Chandy and Prachi Jain) sat on all committees through FY26. No independent-director resignation appears in the FY23 to FY26 reports, and the statutory auditor, Gupta Raj and Co, is unchanged [1]. The FY26 secretarial audit lists minor lapses: results advertisements published 7.5 hours and more than two days late, and defective charge-modification filings [1]. The related-party schedule deserves attention. Click Retail Pvt Ltd, on whose board whole-time director Vijay Advani sits, is listed in the annual report among entities influenced by key management. It bought ₹26.78 Cr in FY26 and carried a ₹47.74 Cr outstanding balance at year-end, equal to 8.4% of consolidated trade receivables. At 30 September 2026 trade payables stood at ₹467.01 Cr against ₹185.11 Cr at March, and borrowings had fallen to about ₹194 Cr from ₹324 Cr [12]. The turnaround is therefore a liability-side event, financed by suppliers and by advances from counterparties, not a collections event. Management itself says the 45-day working-capital figure will drift back to 52 to 55 days [4].

What the FY25 stall and the FY26 jump actually were

FY25 looked like a stall and was one. Revenue from operations moved from ₹1,713 Cr [8] to ₹1,774 Cr [3] and total income grew 3.5% [2]. Management's explanation on 16 May 2025 was specific: 'the distribution business has been very flat this year'. Electronics prices erode 15 to 20% a year, so volumes must grow 30 to 35% to show 15% revenue growth, and 'we did not add any new major brands' [6]. The enterprise trade lost a sale of more than ₹200 Cr in Q1 and its gross margin slid from 2.61% to 2.09% [6]. The audited notes agree. Enterprise revenue was ₹1,205.95 Cr against ₹1,211.88 Cr, and FMCT fell from ₹243.80 Cr to ₹182.04 Cr. FMSG, the home of Honeywell, rose from ₹256.56 Cr to ₹385.36 Cr (AR FY25). Geography says the same from another side: revenue billed outside India fell 12.6% to ₹1,096.07 Cr while India-billed revenue rose 47.7% to ₹678.41 Cr (AR FY25). Profit still grew 10% because a brand business with a blended gross margin of 35 to 36% (Q2 FY25 call) gained share while a trade earning about 2% shrank.

The FY26 jump is a different animal. Revenue from operations on the deck basis rose ₹925 Cr (52%) to ₹2,699.77 Cr. That basis excludes ₹4.84 Cr of export entitlements, and the audited statement shows ₹2,704.62 Cr with them. Of the rise, ₹815 Cr (88%) came from Market Entry and ₹110 Cr from Brand [3][1]. All of it was billed in India. India-billed revenue went from ₹678.41 Cr to ₹1,797.13 Cr (+165%) while revenue billed outside India fell 17.6% to ₹902.64 Cr [1]. Q4 FY26 made it vivid. Market Entry revenue doubled from ₹321.13 Cr to ₹663.43 Cr, while Brand fell 6.5% from ₹81.86 Cr to ₹76.58 Cr. That happened as the Middle East war lifted container freight from $1,500–1,700 to $7,500–8,000 [3][5]. Management's explanation is thematic rather than numerical. The last two quarters were spent 'focusing on Make in India companies', mainly surveillance and AI, on new categories such as servers, data-centre products and memory, and on extended credit to new customers. It also cites 30 to 40% cost inflation in memory, storage and CPUs that lifts invoice values [5]. It has never published a vendor-level bridge. Our judgement is that it is more likely than not that most of the extra ₹1,119 Cr of India billing came from new Make-in-India, server, memory and government-linked lines plus price inflation, not from organic growth in the older consumer brands. Those are the categories added in the same year and Honeywell was falling in the quarter that doubled.

The cost was cash. Receivables rose from ₹237.77 Cr to ₹565.11 Cr, from 13.4% to about 21% of turnover. Total borrowings rose from ₹71.80 Cr to ₹324.26 Cr, of which ₹171.19 Cr was TReDS supplier finance used to pay small MSME vendors within 45 days [5][3]. Operating cash flow was −₹271.88 Cr in the audited cash-flow statement [1]. The Q2 FY27 deck prints FY26 operating cash flow as ₹271.88 Cr on one page and ₹273.10 Cr on another. The audited statement says ₹271.88 Cr, so that is the figure used throughout [1][2]. Crisil reaffirmed its BBB+/Stable rating on 6 August 2026. It attributes the working-capital stretch to year-end billing and credit extended to a few customers. It also sets a downward trigger if one customer exceeds 40% of revenue, against about 25% now and 50 to 60% historically [13].

Customers, vendors and concentration

The annual report gives no top-customer or top-vendor table, so concentration has to be pieced together. Customers: Crisil puts the largest customer at about 25% of FY26 revenue, down from a historical 50% to 60% [13]. Channel breadth is wide, with 10,000-plus trusted partners, 31-plus branches and 2,305 pin codes served [1]. Related-party customers are small. Click Retail (₹26.78 Cr), World EMS (₹26.70 Cr) and a handful of others total about ₹58.6 Cr, or 2.2% of consolidated revenue, excluding sales to the group's own Hong Kong subsidiary [1]. The one related-party balance that matters is Click Retail's ₹47.74 Cr outstanding balance at March 2026, equal to 8.4% of consolidated trade receivables. The schedule does not break it into trade and advances [1]. Vendors: the annual report lists about 30 vendors by category without volumes [1]. Dependence is highest on Honeywell, which is about 97% of Brand revenue. It is also high on Indian OEMs in surveillance and compute, many of them small suppliers who give little credit. That is why borrowings at March 2026 included ₹171.19 Cr of TReDS supplier finance used to pay MSME vendors within 45 days [3]. Purchases from the group's own Hong Kong subsidiary were ₹155.39 Cr in FY26 [1].

Footprint: people, warehouses, assets

The operating base is thin. The group had 355 employees at 31 March 2026 (the MD&A says 'over 350'), of whom 202 were permanent staff on the parent's own rolls [1]. It runs 18 pan-India warehouses with over 125,000 sq ft and a Bhiwandi mother warehouse authorised as a private bonded warehouse. It also runs 18-plus global warehouses including mainland China and Dubai, with more than 2,000 cubic metres of capacity [1]. FY26 volumes were 533 import containers and 55,453 domestic transactions [1]. Consolidated employee cost was ₹21.30 Cr, 0.8% of revenue from operations, and revenue per group employee was about ₹7.6 Cr [3][1]. Fixed assets were only ₹12 Cr against total assets of ₹937 Cr at March 2026 [12]. Management says logistics costs about 1.1% of sales and that it operates through 14 stocking points and 28 locations (Q2 FY25 call). The asset-light model is why the balance sheet is a working-capital ledger. It is also why employee cost rose 73% in H1 FY27 (₹15.99 Cr against ₹9.26 Cr) as the company hired people who understand servers, surveillance and AI [2][5].

Central and Eastern Europe, CIS, Middle East and Africa; 1990 to 2025

Shared Mechanism: An emerging-market IT distributor uses a thin-margin distribution book (smartphones, components, servers) to fund and feed private-label brands. Far East OEMs make those brands (Canyon, Prestigio Solutions, AENO, Lorgar), and they are sold through the same customers. It is the same distribution-to-owned-brand ladder Creative is climbing, now with a swing into AI servers.

Historical Numbers & Outcome: It started in 1990 distributing Seagate products in the CIS. 2025 revenue was US$3,862,999 thousand, up 28.4% from US$3,008,503 thousand. Gross margin fell to 7.22% from 7.98%. Net profit was US$60.2 million, up 11.2%. Servers and server blocks were US$698.8 million against US$254.7 million. The largest customer was 5.0% of revenue. It has about 20,000 customers in about 60 countries. Research and development came to US$1.1 million behind four own brands.

Direct Implication for CNL: ASBIS scaled revenue through the distribution book and a server boom. Its directors still call own brands a reinforcement 'if they are developed as an addition to the distribution business', and blended gross margin stayed in single digits after three decades. Creative's plan for a 40% Brand share by FY29 is far more aggressive than that outcome. Its FY26 surge was component- and server-led in the same way, so gross margin and cash conversion are the metrics to watch rather than headline growth. [14]

Five real-world walkthroughs

Real-World Walkthrough 1: Market-entry distribution of an experiential brand (Cricut and Razer)

Customer and problem. Cricut makes connected cutting machines that let hobbyists cut, emboss and print on dozens of materials; Razer makes premium gaming peripherals. Neither had an India organisation. Creative signed distribution for Cricut on 10 April 2023 and for all Razer products on 5 May 2023 (Cricut release; Razer release). The buyers are Indian crafters and gamers, reached through Amazon, retailers and influencers.

What is bought and how it is priced. A shopper buys, for example, a Cricut Joy machine. Creative buys the machine in bulk from Cricut on its own account (principal-to-principal). It sells the machine to Amazon or a retailer at a discount to the list price and keeps the spread. The channel business earned a gross margin of 8.5% to 8.7% in 2023 and 6% to 7% in H1 FY26 [7] (Q1 FY24 call). Marketing is largely paid by the brand. The then CFO said brand and promotion spend is 'always passed through' and is included in gross margin [8], so reported gross margin overstates the pure spread. For brands other than Honeywell the company says it pays nothing from its own pocket for marketing [8].

How it is built, shipped, billed and collected. Imports clear through the Bhiwandi bonded warehouse [1]. The first Cricut launch was on Amazon Prime Day 2023 and sold about ₹55 lakh of product in two days. Creative then ran four to five influencer demonstrations a month, 60 to 70 a year, in Mumbai, Delhi and Bangalore (Q1 FY24 call). Channel debtors run about 40 days and logistics costs about 1.1% of sales (Q2 FY25 call).

What Creative earns. On every ₹100 of sales Creative keeps a gross spread of about ₹6 to ₹9. After logistics and selling cost, the parent's EBITDA margin on the whole distribution business was 2.58% in H1 FY26 [7]. Our estimate for a mature brand like this is a 2.5% to 3% EBITDA margin on sales. We also estimate a return on capital employed near the 20% to 22% hurdle the board applies [7]. The economics are modest per rupee of sale; the value to Creative is the foot in the door with retailers that its own brands later use.

Real-World Walkthrough 2: Honeywell licensing and contract manufacturing

Customer and problem. A Delhi household orders a Honeywell air purifier through Blinkit, or a Dubai household buys one on Amazon. The shopper wants a trusted name in a category with few incumbents. Creative's answer is the Honeywell brand on a product Creative designed. Honeywell air purifiers reached number one on Amazon UAE and the top three on Amazon India. The company says it sold one Honeywell product every 60 seconds online in FY26 [1][5].

What is bought and how it is priced. The product is one of four categories. Air purifiers were about ₹79 Cr of ₹255 Cr in FY25, IT accessories ₹84 Cr, audio ₹50 Cr and structured cabling ₹41 Cr [6]. Creative decides the price: 'We can decide the price that we will sell the product' (Q1 FY24 call). Honeywell is paid the greater of a minimum guarantee or a percentage of sales (same call). Gross margin is about 40% to 42% on the latest management figure [4], against 35% to 36% in FY25 (Q2 FY25 call).

How it is built, shipped, billed and collected. The product is built by contract factories in China, Taiwan and India (about 28 facilities). Honeywell audits the factory and approves the design and packaging [1][6]. Certification such as BIS costs about ₹2 lakh a product and takes about seven months in India [7][6]. Secure Connection in Hong Kong buys from the factory and sells to distributors such as Trigon in the UAE (Trigon release). It also sells to the Indian parent, which bought ₹155.39 Cr in FY26 [1]. Honeywell's working capital ran about 60 days against 40 to 45 for other brands in 2023 [8]. Q3 is the peak season for purifiers and audio.

What Creative earns. Per ₹100 of Honeywell sales, gross profit is about ₹40 to ₹42 and marketing about ₹9 (₹32 Cr to ₹33 Cr on ₹369.61 Cr in FY26 [5]). Manpower is about ₹6 to ₹7 [5], leaving an EBITDA of about ₹13 [5]. That leaves roughly ₹12 for royalty, freight, certification and other overheads. The audited notes carry a royalty expense line of only ₹5.96 Cr, 1.6% of Honeywell sales [1]. The note does not name Honeywell as the payee, so we treat it as the likely licence charge rather than a confirmed one. The profit sits in Hong Kong. The parent's own Brand segment earns ₹24.53 Cr on ₹232.24 Cr (10.6%), while the consolidated Brand segment earns ₹126.29 Cr on ₹380.86 Cr (33.2%). The subsidiary therefore earns about ₹101.8 Cr of segment result before overhead, and 22.5% of the subsidiary's profit belongs to its chief executive [1].

Real-World Walkthrough 3: Enterprise and government orders (FCI, BSNL and the other releases)

FCI: what is established. Creative announced on 12 May 2026 a ₹35.89 Cr 'order from the Food Corporation of India' for smart warehousing across 150 depots. The order comes in two phases of 75 sites, with commissioning within 180 days and three years of maintenance. The scope covers sensor modules, bag counting, boom barriers, gateways, software, surveillance and AI monitoring (release). After the exchange sought format details, an addendum on 13 May confirmed the numbers (addendum). On 11 September 2026 the company clarified that FCI awarded the project to its systems-integrator partner NebulArc Technologies. Creative's order for its assigned scope came from NebulArc, which is the direct counterparty [11].

FCI: what is not established. The filings do not say what share of NebulArc's FCI contract Creative holds, the margin, or the payment terms. The original headline implied a direct FCI order. The clarification shows it is a sub-contract; the promoter has no interest in NebulArc, and it is not a related party [11]. Our estimate is a gross margin of 8% to 12%, or ₹3 Cr to ₹4 Cr of gross profit over about 180 days plus the maintenance tail. It rests on management's remark that Indian-made supply earns a higher percentage than the 6% to 7% channel margin [5][7]. This is a small order against ₹2,700 Cr of revenue; its value is the credential.

BSNL: what is established. On 6 June 2026 Creative, 'along with its consortium partner', received an Advance Work Order from BSNL, acting for Digital Bharat Nidhi, for the BharatNet Middle Mile Network in the Odisha circle. The quoted 'total all-inclusive AWO value for the consortium is approximately INR 3,194.83 crore' covers design, supply, construction, installation, upgradation, operation and maintenance over about three years. The order is subject to unconditional acceptance, performance security, an executed agreement and route-wise work orders [11]. Crisil describes the group as project implementation agency for the circle [13].

BSNL: what is not established. Creative's share of the consortium, the partner's name and the performance-security size are undisclosed. On the October 2026 call management split the work into about ₹1,700 Cr of capital spending and ₹1,200 Cr of seven-year operation and maintenance. It guided to billing of ₹150 Cr to ₹200 Cr in Q4 FY27 and ₹500 Cr to ₹600 Cr next year. It also said 'you can expect net profit out of that close to 6% to 7% of the value we bill' [4]. The two parts total ₹2,900 Cr against the ₹3,194.83 Cr headline, a gap the call does not explain. At 6% to 7% the profit would be ₹9 Cr to ₹14 Cr in FY27 and ₹30 Cr to ₹42 Cr in FY28. Because that is two to three times the group's 2.6% PAT margin, we would use a central 4%, or ₹6 Cr to ₹8 Cr in FY27. Two other releases are not orders at all. The 27 October 2026 'award' is an Amazon Ads recognition for WOZOYO (release). The 12 November 2026 AT&T release is a brand licence with no revenue or minimum disclosed (release). The Zicom SaaS release is an acquisition of a subscription-monitoring business with ₹20.09 Cr of FY26 revenue and a net worth of ₹25.62 Cr. The enterprise value is ₹50.50 Cr (board outcome).

Real-World Walkthrough 4: Surveillance and new technology (Sparsh, Matrix, Infinova, drones)

Customer and problem. A state agency or a systems integrator needs CCTV that meets the government's STQC security certification. The certification requires that no component originates in China, that the software and board design files are made in India, and that no director or beneficiary of the maker is Chinese [4]. Matrix and Sparsh are two of the four Indian STQC-certified makers; Creative became their pan-India distributor in November 2025 (Matrix; Sparsh).

What is bought and how it is priced. The integrator buys cameras, recorders and software from Creative at a distributor price. Management says these small Indian makers carry a higher percentage margin than larger vendors but give less credit. Creative therefore pays earlier and carries more receivables. The FY26 deck says 'vendor credit remains limited, resulting in higher advance payments' [5][3]. Revenue from Matrix and Sparsh is not disclosed; the makers' own turnovers were about ₹250 Cr and ₹400 Cr in FY25, according to the chairman [7].

How the build-out works. Creative is buying Infinova (India) Pvt Ltd, a Pune assembler of high-end cameras used in smart cities and airports. The deal comes with a board budget of up to USD 4.00 Million, exclusive India brand rights and its own small factory (release). Infinova's revenue fell from ₹38 Cr to ₹32 Cr last March for lack of STQC. Management's goalpost is ₹75 Cr to ₹80 Cr in the first full year and ₹200 Cr in the next, and 'the crucial number is to do INR75 crores' [4]. On drones, Creative distributes PDRL, Drona Aviation's STEM kits and Jupiter's BIS-certified 24,000 mAh agricultural-drone battery, with no volumes disclosed.

What it means. Surveillance is the best-fitting adjacency, because the same integrators buy Honeywell cabling, and ownership of Infinova would move part of it into the Brand segment. The chairman suggests surveillance 'can easily' reach ₹2,000 Cr to ₹3,000 Cr in two to three years [4]. We regard that as well beyond what the evidence supports, since the only quantified base is Infinova at ₹32 Cr and the target year-one number is ₹75 Cr to ₹80 Cr.

Real-World Walkthrough 5: WOZOYO, the owned brand on Amazon

Customer and problem. A US shopper wants an air purifier or a laptop docking station at an affordable-premium price. Creative cannot sell Honeywell air purifiers in the United States, where the category is about ₹28,000 Cr against about ₹2,000 Cr to ₹3,000 Cr in India. It therefore created WOZOYO under Secure Connection, announced on 15 June 2026 and sold in the United States from the end of July [4] (release).

What is bought and how it is priced. The product is built by the same contract manufacturers that serve Honeywell, sold on Amazon, TikTok and later Walmart and Best Buy. Management's strategy is 'build online trust and credibility' first [4]. In India, quick-commerce platforms usually want sale-or-return from new brands; the chairman says WOZOYO accepts none, even at the cost of sales [4]. The target gross margin is 37% to 40% [4].

How it is built, shipped, billed and collected. Inventory was already in Amazon's US warehouses before the May 2026 launch [5]. The company reports about $2.2 million (roughly ₹20 Cr) sold in the first months and returns of about 3% to 3.5% against 8% to 9% typical. It also reports an Amazon's Choice badge for the docking station and an Amazon Ads 'New Rising Star' award [4]. The launch window is end July to end September, which is two months of selling, although the chairman describes it as three.

What Creative earns. Today, little. Amazon marketing services run at about 50% of sales, falling to 12% to 14% once sales reach about $1 million. The company has spent about ₹12 Cr of a ₹20 Cr to ₹22 Cr brand-building budget [4]. Against a 37% to 40% gross margin, 50% advertising means a negative contribution (about −₹10 to −₹13 per ₹100 of sales before other costs, our arithmetic). At 12% to 14% it turns positive at about ₹23 to ₹28. The FY27 target is ₹45 Cr to ₹50 Cr, the next year ₹100 Cr to ₹150 Cr. Because WOZOYO sits in Secure Connection, 22.5% of its profit or loss belongs to the subsidiary's chief executive. Management calls Secure Connection 'wholly owned' on the Q2 FY27 call, but the annual report shows 77.50%, which is the figure used here [4][1].

Put together, the business is four things under one roof. A low-margin enterprise trade that moves ₹1,000 Cr-plus a year on little capital in good years and a lot of credit in bad ones. A channel distribution book that pays for the national infrastructure and gives access to retailers. A licence business, Honeywell, that earns the profit and sits offshore. And a set of new bets in surveillance, compute, drones and owned brands whose revenue is not yet visible in the segment note. FY26 grew because the first and fourth were enlarged together; H1 FY27 earned more because Market Entry's margin widened and Brand grew 43%. Whether the margin gain and the working-capital release last is the question the next chapters take up.

3. Distribution, Supply Chain & Unit Economics

Core Investor Question: How does a product travel from a vendor's factory to a customer's shelf, how much of each rupee of sales does Creative Newtech keep after funding the credit it extends, and what really explains the cash-flow whiplash between FY26 and H1 FY27?

Creative Newtech does not run one business. It runs three roads from a factory to a customer, and each road has its own margin, its own cash cycle and a different owner of the risk. In FY26 (audited, consolidated) the company booked revenue from operations of ₹2,704.62 Cr. That was made up of ₹2,699.77 Cr of product sales and ₹4.84 Cr of export entitlements. The segment note, the earnings call and the investor decks use the ₹2,699.77 Cr figure. Of that, the Market Entry Specialist segment was ₹2,318.92 Cr (86%) and the Brand Business was ₹380.86 Cr (14%) [15]. Total income, which adds ₹12.89 Cr of other income, was ₹2,717.51 Cr. Yet the brand segment produced ₹126.29 Cr of the ₹213.58 Cr segment result, or 59% [15]. That mismatch, 14% of the sales carrying 59% of the gross earnings, is the single most important fact about how the company is sold and delivered. It means the volume engine and the profit engine are different machines, and the volume engine is the one that eats the cash.

The physical footprint is modest for a business of this size. The annual report counts 31+ branches across India and 18 pan-India warehouses with 125,000+ sq. ft. of space. It adds more than 2,000 CBM of capacity in international warehouses in Mainland China and Dubai. The company also reports a network of 10,000+ channel partners across 38+ countries [15]. The Bhiwandi mother warehouse is authorised as a private bonded warehouse under the Customs Act. The company also holds AEO-T2 status. In plain terms, imported goods can then sit under customs control before duty is paid, and they clear faster [15]. FY26 volumes were 533 containers of imports and 55,453 domestic transactions [15]. The report counts 355 employees across the group, of whom 202 are permanent staff on the parent's own rolls [15]. Consolidated employee cost of ₹21.30 Cr is only 0.79% of revenue, so each group employee carries about ₹7.6 Cr of sales. That is lean, which is why operating leverage is real, but it also means the sales force is small relative to the 10,000 partners it serves.

Vendor terms are the quiet risk in this map. The IPO prospectus warned that distribution agreements may be renewed only at the partner's discretion. Partners can terminate by written notice, some with immediate effect. Some agreements are also non-exclusive, so a vendor may appoint other distributors in the same territory [19]. It also disclosed that the top ten suppliers were 52.79% of purchases and the top five 47.05% in FY2016 [19]. The FY26 annual report gives no current concentration figure. It says only that many partner brands have appointed Creative as exclusive partner and kept the association for over five years [15]. The other end of the chain is thinner still. Creative's own e-commerce arm, Creative eCommerce Ventures, is a 49% associate after two outside investors bought in during FY25. It contributed a ₹0.05 Cr share of loss in FY26 [15]. The marketplace road is therefore run mainly by selling to Amazon, Noon, Blinkit and peers, not through an owned storefront.

On the sourcing side the standalone company recorded imports of ₹455.84 Cr and export earnings of ₹754.03 Cr in FY26 [15]. Imports are paid partly through bank buyer's credit and partly out of cash credit lines. Those lines are payable on demand and carry 9% to 11% interest. They are secured on stock, book debts and property, with personal guarantees from the promoter directors [15]. The annual report says foreign-exchange risk is managed 'to the extent considered necessary' without giving a hedge ratio [15]. For a distributor whose Honeywell flows are largely dollar-priced, that is a disclosure gap rather than a proven problem. The consolidated translation reserve rose ₹8.62 Cr in FY26, a sign that currency moved in the company's favour in the year [15].

Real-World Walkthrough 1: One Honeywell air purifier, from a contract factory to a quick-commerce dark store

Customer and problem. A flat-owner in Delhi searches for an air purifier in November, when pollution peaks and Diwali firecrackers add to it. She wants a known brand, delivery in minutes and a price below the premium imports. Creative's answer is a Honeywell-branded purifier sold on Blinkit, Zepto, Swiggy Instamart or Amazon, where the company now has direct listings [15]. Management says Q3 is always the highest Honeywell quarter for exactly this reason: ₹98.23 Cr in Q3 FY25 against ₹65.40 Cr in Q4 [21].

What is built and by whom. Creative owns no factory for this. Honeywell licenses the trademark. Secure Connection Limited in Hong Kong, 77.5% owned by Creative, designs the product and picks a contract manufacturer from a pool of 28+ Asian facilities. It runs quality control from an office in China [15]. Honeywell first audits the factory, then approves every pack and product against its design language. It scores the licensee each quarter on a 1-to-5 scale. Creative reported its best-ever score of 4.8 on the 16 May 2025 call [21].

How it is priced, shipped, billed and collected. Goods move by sea to India, the Middle East and Southeast Asia. In the Middle East partners hold 45 to 60 days of stock. A Red Sea freight spike from $1,500-$1,700 to $7,500-$8,000 therefore hits the next restock, not the shelf today [16]. In India the company sells to dealers and platforms. For its new WOZOYO brand it refuses consignment or sale-or-return terms even if it loses sales, an unusual stance that Honeywell's pull makes affordable [17]. The royalty expense line in the audited notes is ₹5.96 Cr, 1.6% of Honeywell sales. We take it to be the Honeywell licence charge, although the note does not name the payee. Alongside it runs a stream of marketing spend that management put near ₹32 Cr to ₹33 Cr for the brand [15][16].

What it means for the business. Every purifier carries a gross margin several times that of a monitor or a memory card, yet the licence is borrowed, not owned. The consumer sees Honeywell, not Creative. If the licence moves, the factory network, the 38-country distributor list and the platform listings stay, but the brand premium goes with Honeywell. Chapter 4 prices that dependence in probability terms.

How Make-in-India sourcing and local assembly fit in matters for margin and for cash. In FY26 management began favouring Indian makers of surveillance, AI and components. As Ketan Patel put it on the 15 May 2026 call, these vendors are much smaller, so the distributor earns a higher percentage margin. But the same smallness means the vendors and their smaller customers cannot fund large orders, so Creative carries the credit [16]. The investor presentation concedes the trade-off: vendor credit remains limited, advances are higher and margins temporarily lower [18]. Other current assets rose from ₹113.98 Cr at March 2025 to ₹160.77 Cr at March 2026 and to ₹272.44 Cr by September 2026 [15][20]. Local assembly arrives through partners such as Infinova, whose small factory and assembly line sits inside the pending acquisition. Management says its camera sales fell from ₹38 Cr to ₹32 Cr last March for lack of STQC certification. It also says Creative needs ₹75 Cr in a full year to prove the thesis [17].

Our reading is that the H1 FY27 inflow is real cash but is of lower quality than the headline. Over eighteen months the company generated ₹79.35 Cr of operating cash flow (−₹271.88 Cr plus ₹351.24 Cr) against ₹109.76 Cr of profit after tax. That is 72% conversion, which is acceptable for a business that grew its revenue base by half. We think it is more likely than not, roughly a 70% chance, that a meaningful share of the payable and customer-advance build reverses over the next two to three quarters. The reasons are that vendor terms normalise and the Q3 festive stocking draws cash. The investor should treat a normalised cash conversion of 50% to 70% of profit as the planning range. The nearly nine times profit that the H1 FY27 inflow represents is not a planning basis.

Real-World Walkthrough 2: A government fibre order, from advance work order to cash

Customer and problem. Bharat Sanchar Nigam Limited needs a middle-mile fibre network for the Odisha telecom circle under BharatNet. Creative and a consortium partner received an advance work order with an approximate total value of ₹3,194.83 Cr. The order includes a capex phase and several years of operation and maintenance [17].

How it is priced and built. Management describes about ₹1,700 Cr of capex over roughly 3.5 years and about ₹1,200 Cr of operating revenue over the following seven years. Creative orders pipe, GIS survey work and network equipment. BSNL inspects vendor factories and the bill of materials. An independent engineer certifies progress on a digital tracker [17].

How it is billed and collected. Revenue is recognised only after BSNL quality certification. The CFO expects ₹100 Cr to ₹150 Cr in FY27 and about ₹500 Cr the year after. The chairman guided to ₹150 Cr to ₹200 Cr in Q4 FY27 and ₹500 Cr to ₹600 Cr in FY28, at a net margin of 6% to 7% of billing [17]. Cash goes out to vendors months before BSNL certifies and pays, so this order will test working capital harder than the festive cycle does.

What it means for the business. A ₹500 Cr annual billing line at 6% to 7% net would add ₹30 Cr to ₹35 Cr of profit, roughly half of FY26's total. But it arrives through the road that needs the most advances. The 45-day working-capital figure is the number to watch as this order ramps up.

The ultimate takeaway for Chapter 3 is that the distribution model is sound in its mechanics and fragile in its funding. The mechanics are a lean, bonded, 18-warehouse network and a 10,000-partner channel. They also include direct listings on the fastest-growing retail formats and a brand road that converts a borrowed trademark into a 33% segment margin. The fragility is a low-margin road that tripled its asset base in a year. The cash-flow recovery came from suppliers and advances. Management also still has to prove it can bring the year-end receivable ratio back to the 13% to 14% it targets [15][16][20]. We rate the chance that FY27 closes with operating cash flow above reported profit as roughly even, because the September payable level must hold through the stocking quarter.

4. Industry Structure, Competitive Moat & External Learning Hub

Core Investor Question: Who does Creative Newtech really compete with, what stops its vendors and customers from going around it, and how much of its profit rests on one licence it does not own?

India's technology distribution industry has three layers, and Creative Newtech sits in the middle one while trying to climb into the third. The first layer is broadline distribution, where scale is everything: Redington booked consolidated FY26 revenue of ₹1,19,347 Cr, Savex Technologies about ₹35,301 Cr of gross revenue, and Rashi Peripherals ₹15,827 Cr [22][26][23]. They carry the big principals, HP, Dell, Lenovo, Apple, Samsung and Cisco, and earn operating margins of roughly 2% to 3.5%. The second layer is specialist and market-entry distribution, where a distributor takes on brands that the giants do not want to build from scratch; this is where Creative's ₹2,704.62 Cr of FY26 revenue mostly sits [15]. The third layer is brand ownership and licensing, where the margin pool is different in kind: CP Plus, India's leading surveillance brand, reported a 28.8% gross margin and 13.7% EBITDA margin in FY26, against distributors' low single digits [24]. Creative's strategy is to use the second layer's logistics to feed the third layer's economics. The question for this chapter is how defensible each layer is.

The business is therefore a hybrid whose profit comes from the layer it only partly owns. [15][22][23][24][26]

Three things stand out. First, scale: Creative's revenue is about 2.3% of Redington's and 17% of Rashi's [15][22][23]. It cannot win on purchasing power, and Ketan Patel's own explanation of why Make-in-India vendors pay a higher percentage margin, that these vendors are much smaller, points the same way [16]. Second, cash conversion: even Rashi, the second-largest listed IT distributor, reported operating cash outflows of ₹114.6 Cr, ₹102.0 Cr and ₹299.2 Cr in FY23, FY24 and FY25 while earning profit each year, and only turned positive at ₹113.7 Cr in FY26 [23]. Creative's own FY25 operating cash flow was ₹21.86 Cr positive and FY26 negative ₹271.88 Cr [15]. Negative cash conversion in growth years is therefore the industry norm, not a Creative quirk. Third, the margin gap: CP Plus's 121 debtor days and 138 creditor days show that a brand owner can have the same long receivable cycle and still be funded by suppliers, which is the structure Creative's brand road is trying to copy [24].

Disintermediation: who can go around Creative, and what does it add?

The risk that a vendor skips the distributor takes three forms in this industry, and each has evidence on both sides. The first is direct selling by the vendor or by a marketplace. Channel Dive's June 2026 report notes that the three largest cloud providers have become de facto distributors, that distributors may start billing end customers directly, and that vendors are concentrating around fewer distributors, which Omdia's analyst says will widen the gap between the largest platforms and smaller distributors because platform capability costs tens, if not hundreds, of millions of dollars [27]. Creative is on the wrong side of that scale line. The second form is vendor exit: Savex lost Samsung as a key principal and CRISIL still rated it AA, but only because other principals replaced the volume [26]. The third is regulatory orphaning: Aditya Infotech was national distributor for Dahua for 16 years, and when India refused to certify Chinese-chipset cameras its Dahua business contracted by 80% [25].

What Creative adds is real, but narrow. In the market-entry model it brings a brand that has no Indian presence into 10,000+ partners, 31+ branches and 18 warehouses, handles import and bonded storage, pre-sales demos and after-sales, and in the AR's words trains resellers at the ground level [15]. That value is highest for small and new brands, which cannot justify a direct team, and lowest for brands that reach scale. Creative's own FY26 additions, Kaspersky, PDRL, EIZO, Matrix and Sparsh, are exactly of this kind [15][16]. The risk is graduation: successful brands tend to go direct. The investor should treat the market-entry road as an annuity that must be re-earned every time a brand grows up, and the audited segment margin of 3.8% gives little cushion if one of the bigger lines leaves. We assess the chance that at least one top-five principal reduces or removes Creative's role within three years at about one in three, and the chance that this alone breaks the business at below one in ten, because the brand road and enterprise projects diversify the earnings.

Historical Failure-Mode & Base-Rate Twin: BrightPoint, Inc. (Indianapolis, global wireless-handset distributor) (United States and global markets, wireless-device distribution and logistics, 2011 to 2012)

Shared Vulnerability: A thin-margin distributor dependent on a few vendors and customers lost its largest customer and saw a key handset vendor, Research in Motion, falter within months. It carried working-capital debt and, so far as the sources show, no consumer brand of its own to fall back on, so a vendor shock turned directly into a sale to a larger distributor.

Historical Numbers & Base Rate: 2011 revenue about $5.2 billion and about 4,000 employees worldwide; RIM was about 10% of its distribution business; the lost Cricket account had been 6.8 million devices in 2011; February 2012 earnings guidance cut and March 2012 layoffs; a 2-cent-per-share second-quarter loss flagged and prior guidance withdrawn on 2 July 2012; agreed sale to Ingram Micro for about $650 million in cash plus roughly $190 million of assumed debt.

How CNL Compares Today: Scale and a 112-million-device throughput did not protect BrightPoint once two concentrated relationships failed together. For Creative the analogue is the Honeywell licence and a handful of principals: the brand road is the shock absorber that BrightPoint lacked, but it is also a single, terminable dependency. [28]

The contractual economics are favourable. The royalty expense in the audited notes was ₹5.96 Cr in FY26 against ₹5.88 Cr in FY25, a rise of 1.4% while the brand segment's revenue rose 41% [15]. Ketan Patel's explanation is that the royalty is a fixed-style amount that rises only when a territory is added [21]. The same fact is the risk. A licensor who sees a licensee grow Honeywell sales by about 45% in a year (₹255.17 Cr to ₹369.61 Cr) while paying a flat fee has a clear incentive to reset the terms at renewal. As an illustration, not a forecast, a royalty of 5% of Honeywell sales would cost ₹18.5 Cr, about ₹12.5 Cr more than FY26, equal to 15% of the year's ₹81.76 Cr pre-tax profit [15]. Honeywell also controls design through its design language system and can reallocate categories among licensees, as management says it did with air purifiers [21]. And Creative has no Honeywell rights in the United States, one reason it launched WOZOYO [16][17].

The corporate backdrop adds uncertainty rather than direct risk. Ketan Patel told the May 2026 call that Honeywell was splitting into five companies [16]. Honeywell's own October 2025 announcement describes separating Aerospace and Solstice Advanced Materials from a remaining three-segment automation business, which is a three-way split, not five (see Honeywell's segment-structure release). The difference in count is minor, but the uncertainty it covers is not: which successor entity owns the consumer-products trademark programme, and whether its licensing team inherits Creative's five-year renewal cycle, is not stated in any document we read. Management says it expects renewal and has the best scorecard rating on record, 4.8 out of 5 [21]. Our probability assessment: roughly 65% that the licence renews on broadly current terms, about 25% that it renews with materially higher cost or narrower scope, and about 10% that a major category or territory is lost, within the next 18 months.

Why does this matter so much? Because the brand road is where the profit sits. Audited FY26 segment results put 59% of the ₹213.58 Cr total in the Brand Business on 14% of revenue [15]. The CFO expects brand-business EBITDA of about 13% [16], which on ₹380.86 Cr of brand revenue is roughly ₹49.5 Cr, or 48% of the company's ₹103.95 Cr EBITDA [15]. That is our arithmetic on management's expected margin, not an audited segment EBITDA. Management says the Hong Kong base carries no tax on the brand road [16], but only 77.5% belongs to Creative's shareholders: the non-controlling interest took ₹8.42 Cr, 12% of consolidated profit after tax [15]. Management plans to separate the brand business in two to three years, but not within the next fifteen months [16]. A separated brand company would hold the licence on its own, and the market-entry company would be left with the thin-margin base.

Regulatory moats and shocks

Regulation has created a genuine moat in surveillance, and it is the most interesting new fact in the file. India's electronics ministry introduced essential-requirement norms for CCTV cameras in April 2024 and gave a two-year transition; from April 2026 internet-connected cameras need STQC certification, which in practice excludes Chinese-chipset products [25]. Chinese brands held a third of CCTV sales in 2024; Indian brands held over 80% by February 2026, with CP Plus rising to 45% to 50% from 20% to 25%; and 507 camera models were certified [25]. Management's reading is that surveillance and drones are 'national security' categories that foreign players will never be allowed to dominate [16]. Creative has positioned itself as the pan-India distributor of Matrix and Sparsh, two of the four major Indian STQC-approved players (company decks call the arrangements exclusive; the releases say partnership), and is buying Infinova India, which fell from ₹38 Cr to ₹32 Cr in sales for want of STQC certification [18][17]. The STQC criteria Ketan Patel set out are demanding: no Chinese-origin components, Indian software and board files, and no Chinese owner or director [17].

But a regulatory moat protects the product, not the distributor. Any Indian distributor can sign an STQC-certified brand, and the biggest beneficiary so far is the brand owner CP Plus, not a distributor. The same rule is a shock for Creative's Dahua line, which the annual report lists as video door phones and which sits in a brand family that lost its main market [15][25]. Component costs have risen 15% to 20% for the switch away from Chinese chipsets [25], and management says memory, storage and CPU costs rose 30% to 40% in 2026, with Red Sea freight moving from $1,500-$1,700 to $7,500-$8,000 and about half of the ₹137.60 Cr of Honeywell sales outside India exposed to the Middle East [16]. Import duties, production-linked incentives and export entitlements (₹4.84 Cr, down from ₹8.28 Cr in FY25) are second-order here, and certification and inspection spending of ₹0.87 Cr and ₹0.82 Cr in FY26 is small [15].

6. 10-Year Financial Forensics, Cash Conversion & Balance Sheet

Core Investor Question: Are Creative Newtech's profits turning into cash, and was the swing from −₹272 Cr of operating cash flow in FY26 to +₹351 Cr in H1 FY27 genuine compounding, timing, or financing optics?

The central forensic question is a ₹623 Cr swing in operating cash flow from one period to the next. Real-World Walkthrough 1: ₹100 Cr of market-entry sales, FY26 versus September 2026

The customer and the problem. A Make-in-India camera or components maker is too small to have credit lines from global suppliers. Its buyers (system integrators, corporates, government-linked EPCs) pay in 45 to 65 days. CNL steps between them: it sources the parts and finds the customer.

How the cash moves in FY26. CNL pays the vendor an advance, because the deck says vendor credit is limited, then ships, then waits about 70 days to be paid. Each ₹100 Cr of sales therefore ties up roughly ₹19 Cr of money (₹100 Cr × 70 ÷ 365). At a gross margin of about 3%, that sales line earns ₹3 Cr, and financing the ₹19 Cr at 10% costs ₹1.9 Cr, leaving ₹1.1 Cr before costs.

How it moves in September 2026. The same ₹100 Cr is now billed with customer advances in some cases and vendor invoices paid at about 66 days of purchases. On paper working capital is released and the balance sheet shows cash. Nothing about the underlying contract has changed except who is holding the float.

What it means. The margin on this business is a spread over cost of funds. Once suppliers or customers fund the float, ROCE jumps and operating cash flow swells; once they stop, both reverse. The earnings are real either way; the cash flow is only as stable as the float.

Sustainable or a timing reversal? The CFO answered directly on 29 October: 45 days ‘is not sustainable’ and normal is 52 to 55 days. [32] Our central case assumes 52 to 60 days on FY27 revenue 20% higher than FY26, and after-tax pre-working-capital cash of about ₹126 Cr. On that basis FY27 operating cash flow is ₹150 Cr to ₹220 Cr, with a negative H2 FY27 of ₹130 Cr to ₹205 Cr. If payables fall back to 45 days of purchases and customer advances to a third of September's level, about ₹290 Cr to ₹340 Cr flows back out. Net debt then returns to roughly ₹260 Cr to ₹310 Cr, close to March 2026's ₹302 Cr. So we estimate that 60% to 70% of the ₹479 Cr rise in payables and advances unwinds over three to four quarters. The CFO's own move from 45 to 52 to 55 days already concedes ₹60 Cr to ₹80 Cr of that. What persists is mostly a genuine BharatNet mobilisation advance, if that is what the advances are.

What a clean cash-conversion cycle looks like for this model. Brand sales need 90 to 120 days of capital by the CMD's own account. Market-entry sales need 55 to 65 days of customer credit plus advances to small vendors, and domestic receivables include 18% GST. [32][30] Our estimate of a clean cycle is 50 to 60 days of net working capital, which matches management's 52 to 55. On a ₹3,000 Cr annualised revenue run-rate that is ₹410 Cr to ₹490 Cr of working capital, against ₹337 Cr of trade working capital at September 2026. So ₹75 Cr to ₹150 Cr of the current position is borrowed from vendors and customers. Two definitions are in play. Management's net working capital days (42, 75 and 45 at March 2025, March 2026 and September 2026) match Screener's cash-conversion cycle at the two year-ends. Screener's separate working-capital days line reads 50 and 47 for the same years and is not used here. [29][33]

Historical Failure-Mode & Base-Rate Twin: Noble Group (Singapore-listed commodity trader, 2009 to 2018)

Shared Vulnerability: A thin-margin intermediary reported large, steady profits for five years while cumulative operating cash flow was negative. It bridged the gap with bank and trade-finance lines until short-seller scrutiny, write-downs and a junk rating forced a debt-for-equity restructuring.

Historical Numbers & Base Rate: From 2009 to 2014 reported net profit was S$2.4 billion against cumulative operating cash flow of −S$500 million and cumulative free cash flow of −S$2.4 billion (case-study figures). Over 2010 to 2014 turnover growth was +228% while inventory days fell from 26 to 13. In Feb 2015 it took a $440 million asset write-down, and in Dec 2015 it received a junk rating. It reported an annual loss of $4.9 billion in 2017. In Jan 2018 it agreed a $3.5 billion debt restructuring, with creditors taking 70% of the equity [41].

The number:

A new buyer at ₹1,458.40 needs EPS of about ₹82 to ₹103 three years from now to earn 12% a year. Five years out the need is ₹103 to ₹129, against ₹47.44 today. That is 17% to 29% annual EPS growth, depending on the multiple the stock carries at the end.

How it was calculated:

Required future price = ₹1,458.40 × 1.12 to the power of the number of years: ₹2,049 after three years and ₹2,570 after five. Required EPS = that price ÷ the exit multiple, at 25x (₹82 over three years, ₹103 over five) and 20x (₹102 over three years, ₹129 over five). Annual growth is the rate that takes ₹47.44 to that EPS.

The inputs:
