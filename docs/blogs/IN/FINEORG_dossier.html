Terms used in this report

This chapter explains each technical and financial word once, with an everyday comparison, so the later chapters can use the words without stopping to define them. The words appear in the order the framework needs them.

ROCE stands for return on capital employed. It asks how much operating profit a business makes for each rupee tied up in factories, machines and working stock, much as a shopkeeper asks what a stall earns for the money locked in its shelves. A figure of 21.5 percent means the business earns about 21 paise a year on every rupee it uses.

EPS stands for earnings per share, the company's profit for the year divided by the number of shares. It puts a total profit on a per-share footing, in the way a price per kilogram lets you compare two sacks of different sizes. The target in this report is built from a per-share forecast.

RSI stands for relative strength index, a gauge from 0 to 100 of how quickly a price has recently risen or fallen. Readings above 70 are called overbought and below 30 oversold, and the reading of 60.4 for this stock is in the middle of the range.

ATR stands for average true range, the typical daily price swing over the last 14 sessions. It works as a ruler: a distance measured in ATR units shows how far a price sits from a reference level in terms of ordinary daily movement. The ATR for this stock is ₹164.33.

A base is a sideways stretch, often a few weeks to a few months, in which a stock trades inside a narrow range before its next move. It is like a car idling at a junction while the driver waits for a clear road. The framework measures the lowest price of the last 63 sessions as the base low, which is ₹4,692.00 for this stock.

A breakout is the moment the price closes above the top of a base, ideally on heavier trading than usual. It tells you the waiting period has ended, though some breakouts fail within days. A Core candidate passes the technical test with a fresh breakout from its base, or with a rising trend.

A pivot is the price level a stock has to clear to confirm a breakout, usually the top of its base. The ledger does not record a top-of-base price for this stock, so the momentum gate measures the distance from the base low instead, in ATR units.

Stage 2 is the rising phase of a stock's cycle, when the price holds above averages that are themselves rising. Stage 4 is the falling phase, when the price breaks below averages that are heading down. A stock between them, in the topping or flat phase, is in Stage 3, which the ledger does not treat as a buy state.

An entry zone is the price range in which the rules allow a buy, normally close to a support level. When a stock is outside its entry zone, the rule is to wait rather than chase it. This report sets no entry zone, because the company is rejected at Layer 1.

A stop loss is the price at which a holding is sold to cap the loss. It is set below the level at which the trend would count as failed, so the sale happens before the loss grows. Here the stop is used only to measure the downside in the upside-to-downside ratio, because no position is held.

asymmetry is the ratio of the expected upside to the possible downside: how many rupees of gain the rules expect for each rupee that could be lost. A ratio of 3.0 means three rupees of expected gain for each rupee at risk. The framework calls this the upside-to-downside ratio in its floors.

The price-to-earnings ratio, shortened to P/E, is the share price divided by earnings per share. A P/E of 38 means investors pay 38 rupees for each rupee of a year's profit per share. It is the multiple that the target anchor uses.

The debt-to-equity ratio is the company's borrowings divided by the capital its shareholders own. A value of 0.04 means borrowings are about four percent of owners' capital, and the framework's floor is 0.70.

The cash flow from operations is the cash that running the business brings in, before any money spent on new plant. Comparing it with reported profit shows whether profit is backed by money actually collected. The framework's three-year test divides the sum of this cash by the sum of profit over the same years.

free cash flow is the cash from operations left after capital spending on plant and equipment. It is the money the business can use for dividends, debt repayment or new investment, and it is smaller than operating cash by the amount spent on machines and buildings in the year.

The operating margin is the share of sales left after the running costs of making and selling the product, before interest and tax. It shows how much of each sales rupee the core business keeps. The company's operating margin for the year to March 2026 was 20 percent.

A consensus estimate is the average of forecasts made by several professional analysts for the same figure. It is a market view rather than a company promise, and each analyst's forecast can be wrong. The framework uses the consensus only where a company does not give its own number.

A 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 sessions. A 50-day moving average covers the last 50 sessions, and a 200-day moving average covers the last 200. Traders use these lines as reference points: a price above a rising line is in an uptrend, and a price below a falling line is in a downtrend.

working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less the bills the company itself has not yet paid. When it grows faster than sales, cash is absorbed, which is why it matters for the cash test.

How the framework works for this company

The framework is a set of hurdles that a company must clear in a fixed order. Layer 1 holds the hard floors: returns on capital, the level of borrowing, the quality of profit as cash, the shape of the price chart, the momentum test and the upside-to-downside ratio. A failure at any of these ends the review with a REJECTED verdict, and the later layers are not read. Layer 2 would score three to five business-specific operating measures, and Layer 3 would check where the price sits relative to support and whether a buy is allowed at that price.

Each candidate is tested under one of two strategy buckets. CORE_ALPHA is for businesses expected to compound over three to five years. TACTICAL_MOMENTUM is for moves of three to twelve months, and it needs a Stage 2 trend above rising 50-day and 200-day averages, with an upside floor of 3.5. This company is tested under CORE_ALPHA, because its record looks like a compounder, and the tactical test is run as a check.

The framework never lets a good business excuse a weak chart, and it never lets a strong chart excuse a weak business. Asymmetry and target anchoring

The upside-to-downside ratio compares how far the price could rise with how far it could fall before the stop is hit. The framework requires an anchored target: a forward earnings forecast multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, or a chart-based base target. A target without an anchor is treated as unverified, and an unverified target fails the floor. The rule is strict because a target chosen to look attractive can make almost any stock appear to have room to run.

Fatal-flaw red team

The red team asks what would make the verdict wrong, and the answer should be sharp rather than general. Three fatal flaws are tested below. Each is a reason the rejection could be a mistake, even though the framework's rules are clear on the numbers.

Flaw one: the anchor multiple is too low for this business

The first flaw is that the three-year median of about 30 times may understate what the market should pay for a company with a 21.5 percent return on capital and a low-borrowing balance sheet. The year-end multiple was 21.4 times in the year to March 2023 and 47.3 times in the year to March 2022, so the historical range is wide [12]. If the market has permanently re-rated the company, the anchor would understate the target, and the ratio would be wrong for a reason that the rule cannot see. The defence is that the framework requires the anchor to be a median, not the best year. Under that rule the verdict stands, but the flaw is real and should be watched.

Flaw two: earnings are recovering, and the consensus is stale

The second flaw is that the consensus was last updated on 21 May 2026, before a June quarter in which revenue rose about 18 percent and profit rose about 18 percent on the year before [15][19]. If analysts raise their forecasts after the quarter, the anchor would rise. A profit forecast of about ₹267 a share would be needed to reach the Core floor at the median multiple, which is far above the recovery shown so far. The flaw could matter, but it would need a large revision, not a small one.

Flaw three: cash conversion is lumpy

The third flaw is that the three-year cash test passes only because two years are strong. Operating cash fell to ₹204.0 crore in the year to March 2025, which is about half of that year's profit, and the company's inventory days rose to 104 by March 2026, with a cash conversion cycle of 113 days [11][8]. Stock that sits for longer ties up cash, and a weak year could drag the three-year ratio below the 0.70 floor. The current figure of 1.02 clears the floor, but the cushion is not large in a bad year. The cash flow test is a fair pass, and the flaw is about the next year, not the past one.

What would make the bear case wrong is a clean run of Stage 2 signals: the price holding above the 50-day average on heavy volume, relative strength staying positive, and a consensus upgrade after the June quarter. The price has been rising, and the trend signal is real. The verdict does not rest on the trend alone, because the momentum gate and the upside-to-downside ratio both fail.

An invalidation level is a price or a date that ends a view. For a rejected stock, the relevant levels are the ones that would reopen the case and the ones that would confirm the rejection. An anchored base target of at least ₹8,000.88 would be needed to pass the Core upside-to-downside floor of 3.0. On the current median multiple, that requires earnings of about ₹267 a share, so a forecast revision of that size would reopen the valuation test.
