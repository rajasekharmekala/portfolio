What the company does

CAMS is a registrar and transfer agent, which the industry calls an RTA. An RTA is the back office behind a mutual fund: it keeps the register of who owns each unit, records every purchase, sale and switch, and sends the statements. Think of it as the ledger behind a shop counter. The fund manager decides what to buy, but the RTA keeps the books that say who owns what. Screener describes the company as a mutual funds transfer agency that serves investors, distributors and asset management companies, the firms that run the funds [2]. It was founded in 1988 and is headquartered in Chennai [3].

Most of the money comes from mutual funds. In FY26, 86 percent of revenue came from the mutual fund segment and 14 percent from everything else [2]. The other businesses include the KYC registration agency, which holds identity records for investors, electronic payment collection, insurance and alternative investment funds [3]. Management said the non-mutual-fund side grew faster than the core in the latest quarter, which chapter 2 explains [4].

Scale is the company's largest asset. In the quarter ended March 2026, CAMS serviced about ₹55.1 trillion of average assets under management, which is the average value of the fund assets it keeps records for (its AAuM). That is around 68 percent of the mutual fund assets served across the Indian industry [5] [6]. The company is paid for records and transactions, so its income grows with the number of folios and the volume of trades rather than with the market index. The other large registrar, KFintech, gives a mutual fund AAuM figure of ₹26.4 trillion on its website, which is less than half the CAMS number, though the two figures may not be from the same date [7]. A comparison published by Bull Run puts CAMS at 67.2 percent of serviced assets and KFintech at 32.8 percent of AAuM, which points the same way as the 68 percent figure [8]. The FY26 annual report lists 114.1 million live investor folios and 285 service centres across 25 states and five union territories [9].

Ownership is simple to describe. Screener shows the promoter holding at zero since March 2024 [2], and Trendlyne shows no shares pledged by the promoter at any quarter-end from September 2023 to June 2026 [10]. So there is no founding family or parent group holding a large block that could be pledged as security for a loan. The shares are held by foreign funds, Indian funds, insurers and the public. Chapter 5 returns to the one large holder change that matters for the price, a JPMorgan stake increase disclosed in October.

What this means for an owner: the business works like a toll road on mutual fund activity. It earns money when investors keep investing, switching and paying through funds, not when the market goes up or down on a given day. That is why the company is worth understanding, even though the price test rejects it for now.

Why the business is strong or weak

The main test of a business like this is whether each new rupee of revenue turns into profit. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a common measure of that, and it is the number the company itself highlights. In the June 2026 quarter, operating revenue grew 11.5 percent to ₹395 crore, and EBITDA grew more than 18 percent to ₹183 crore, which management called the highest ever, giving a margin of 46.4 percent [4]. Profit after tax reached ₹127.10 crore, up 17.63 percent from a year earlier [11]. A news summary of the results gives ₹128.02 crore on a consolidated basis [6]. The sources differ and we could not reconcile them. The gap does not change the picture.

The returns on capital are the strongest feature. Return on equity, which is profit divided by shareholders' money, was 36.3 percent [2]. ROCE (return on capital employed), which measures profit against all the money tied up in the business, was 47.0 percent [2]. A business that needs little capital to earn each rupee of profit can return most of that profit to owners, and TradingView lists its last dividend per share at ₹2.50 [12]. The company's own FY26 annual report gives an EBITDA margin of 45.11 percent for the full year and a return on net worth of 39.03 percent on its own measure [9].

The weak spots are in the mix of growth. Management said absolute mutual fund revenue grew under 10 percent in the quarter, while non-mutual-fund businesses grew more than 28 percent [4]. Management also said the equity assets it serviced grew 17.6 percent against an industry figure of about 16 percent, which is good, but that overall asset growth had lagged the company's usual pace in recent years, and that a one-time pricing adjustment made about a year earlier was still part of the story [4]. The company's own commentary therefore points to pricing and mix as the things to watch, rather than volume alone. On Screener's figures, FY26 sales grew 6.6 percent while net profit grew 1.5 percent [2]. Profit is lagging revenue, which is a reason to watch the quality of growth closely, and it is one more reason this dossier stops at the financial tests.

There is also a shareholder story. Foreign portfolio investors held 57.63 percent of the shares in December 2024, and their holding fell to 44.88 percent by June 2026 [2]. The price weakness has not come from the business losing money, since reported profit has kept rising. Competition is real, but the market is narrow. KFintech is the other large registrar in the record, and its own website claims a 52.3 percent share of the corporate registrar market and 31 of the 59 Indian mutual fund houses as clients [7]. Those are the company's own claims, not audited figures.

The rules around the business are set by SEBI, the market regulator. Its master circular for registrars to an issue and share transfer agents, dated 6 February 2026, gathers the rules for this business in one place [13]. A separate SEBI master circular for mutual funds took effect on 1 April 2026 and replaces the June 2024 version [14]. For investors, the practical protection is the arbitration route. AMFI, the industry body, says that if a registrar delays or fails to process a request, the investor can file for arbitration with the stock exchange under a SEBI circular dated 30.05.2022 [15]. That route matters here because service reliability is part of the company's franchise. A rule change that cut fees or added service duties would hit revenue, which is why any proposed fee change is worth watching.

What follows for the verdict: the Layer 1 financial tests support a long-term reading of the business, but quality beyond them was not underwritten. The price is the issue for now. A strong business in a falling trend is exactly the case the technical floor is designed to stop, so chapter 4 takes the price test next.

The numbers, in plain words

The Layer 1 financial floor has three tests, and each one has a fixed threshold. Debt-to-equity is the company's borrowings divided by its shareholders' equity, so a lower number means less borrowed money behind the business. Under the rule, debt-to-equity must be below 0.70. Cash flow from operations is the cash a business brings in from its normal activity, before buying equipment or paying dividends. Over three fiscal years, that cash must be at least 0.70 times the reported net profit. The return on capital employed (ROCE) must be at least 15.0 percent. What the price is doing

The technical floor asks a simple question: is the stock in an uptrend that has not broken? The ledger answers it with moving averages, which smooth out daily noise. A stock above a rising 50-day and 200-day average is in an uptrend. A stock below falling averages is in Stage 4, the phase of decline, and the skill's rule is that a Stage 4 breakdown fails the floor. The second measure is the RSI (relative strength index), a 0 to 100 gauge of recent price momentum, where readings below 30 are called oversold and above 70 overbought. The third is ATR (average true range), the typical size of a daily price move. The table below uses the ledger figures for 9 October 2026, the latest completed session.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in this dossier has a source and a date, but several sources are weaker than we would like. The financial figures come from Screener's consolidated table, which is a third-party presentation of the company's filings [2]. We could not read the primary filings: the Q1 FY27 investor presentation and consolidated financial statements are PDF documents on the NSE website, and the research tools in this run could not extract their text. A mistake in the aggregator would carry into our three financial tests. The tests have comfortable margins, though: ROCE is more than three times its threshold, and the debt ratio is less than a tenth of its limit.

The profit figures vary a little between sources. Screener shows ₹127 crore for the June 2026 quarter, one news summary reports ₹127.10 crore, and another reports ₹128.02 crore on a consolidated basis [11] [6]. We use each figure only for the point it supports, and we say which one. Market share is also a secondary figure. The 68 percent AAuM share comes from results summaries and news and analysis articles, not from a CAMS filing [5] [6]. The KFintech numbers are the company's own statements [7]. Any rough share calculation from them mixes dates, so we do not treat it as a reported share.

The price data needs a caution too. The ledger closes are authoritative for this dossier, but several public pages show later intraday quotes, and the Screener header changed during the day on which we checked it. Those live figures include market capitalization and valuation ratios, which we do not use as gate inputs. The stage classification depends on the ledger's definitions of the averages and the relative strength windows. A different definition could move a borderline case, but CAMS is not borderline: both averages are falling and both momentum tests fail on several conditions at once.

The strongest challenge to the verdict is that a quality business may recover faster than the trend suggests. A stock in a long decline can turn quickly, especially when large buyers return. We cannot rule that out. What the record supports is narrower: on 9 October 2026 the rules said no, and a small set of observable conditions, listed in chapter 7, would say yes.
