Reading the framework

The framework has three layers, and a company must clear each one in order. Layer 1 is a set of hard floors that are either passed or failed. It covers the financial health of the business, the technical condition of the share price, and whether the expected upside is large enough compared with the risk of the stop-loss. A failure at Layer 1 ends the review. The rules then say REJECTED, and the later layers are not scored. Layer 2 scores three to five operating measures that matter for this kind of business, using PASS, WATCH or FAIL. Layer 3 is the timing gate. It asks how far the price sits above the nearest support level, measured in ATR (average true range), and maps that distance to an action. A company can be a sound business and still fail the framework, because the framework judges the entry as well as the company.

The momentum gate is the technical test that matters most here. The code runs it on the frozen price file for the session. It has two routes. Test A asks whether the stock is already in a healthy uptrend that is not stretched. Test B asks whether the stock is entering an uptrend, with a base pivot that is close, and volume that dries up in the base and then expands on the breakout. A share must pass one of the two routes. If it fails both, it receives no allocation, however good the business is. The scoring file marks these thresholds as provisional until they are reviewed against live data, and this dossier reports them as the file sets them.

ATR stands for average true range. It is the average size of a normal daily price move over the last 14 sessions. Here it is ₹45.36. Support is the price level where buyers have stepped in before. This dossier uses the 63-session base low from the ledger, which is ₹1,258.20. The distance from the price to that support, divided by the ATR, tells us how many normal daily moves the price sits above it. The distance here is 3.51 ATR. The rules put that distance into bands. Within 0.5 ATR is the full-size band. Within 1.0 ATR is the standard band. Within 1.2 ATR is a limit-order-only band at reduced size. Beyond 1.2 ATR is the no-buy band, where the rules say to wait for a pullback, a retest, or a new base.

The verdict labels follow from the layers. REJECTED means a Layer 1 floor failed, so the company is not a candidate today. WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT means the business passed and the price is extended, so the rule is to wait for the price to come back to support rather than chase it. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP means the price is near support and the business passed, so the rule allows a standard-size purchase on a small pullback. IMMEDIATE_BUY means the price is at support and the asymmetry is high, which allows full size. Each verdict names the rule and the number that produced it, and each one states the level that would change it. This dossier follows that practice throughout.

Two rules recur in the chapters. The first is the asymmetry floor. The upside-to-downside ratio is the distance from the price to a target, divided by the distance from the price to the stop. For the Core bucket the floor is 3.0. For the Tactical bucket it is 3.5. The target has to be anchored to something that can be checked, such as forward earnings multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, or a measured move from a technical base. A target that cannot be checked counts as a failure, labelled FAIL_ASYMMETRY. The second rule is the citation rule. Every figure carries its source and its as-of date, and any value that could not be verified is written as N/A with a reason, rather than estimated.

How the framework works for this company

The framework is built to stop a good company from being bought at a poor price, and to stop a cheap price from hiding a weak company. It does this by running every candidate through the same ordered steps, and by refusing to score a company that has already failed a floor. For a reader new to the method, the closest everyday comparison is a building inspection. The structural engineer checks the foundation and the frame before anyone looks at the kitchen. If the frame fails, the decision is made, and the finishes are not discussed.

For Kalpataru, the inspection ran in the order the rules require. Layer 1 came first, and it has three parts. The financial floors look at return on capital, debt and cash conversion, using audited annual figures. The technical floor looks at the share price itself, and it runs the momentum gate in code. The upside-to-downside floor checks that a target exists and that the ratio between the distance to the target and the distance to the stop clears the bucket's minimum. Each part can fail on its own. Layer 1 passes only when every part passes.

This company failed the technical part. The financial part passed, and so did the stage test and the death-cross test. The momentum gate is the one that stopped the review. It is a stricter test than the stage test, because it adds the question of how far the price is from support and whether volume confirms the move. The financial floors pass, so the business is sound on paper, but the framework is not a verdict on the business alone. It is a verdict on the business at a particular price, on a particular day, against a particular support level.

The rules are also explicit about what happens after a failure. The later layers are not evaluated. The reason is practical as well as procedural. A high score on Layer 2 would be a comfort that the rules say not to rely on, because the company cannot be bought under the current gate. Scoring it anyway would invite a reader to skip the technical gate in favour of the business story. This dossier therefore reports the Layer 2 and Layer 3 position only as a description of what would apply once Layer 1 passes.

What this means for an investor: the verdict is not a judgement that the company will fall. It is a rule that says the price is too far from support to buy now under the framework, and that the company must be re-tested if the price comes back. The next chapters show each floor, its number and its as-of date, so that a reader can check the arithmetic and see which number would have to move.

Technical floor

The technical floor asks whether the price has the shape of an uptrend, and whether it is free of a breakdown. Stage analysis follows the method of Stan Weinstein, which sorts a stock into four phases. Stage 1 is a base, where the price moves sideways after a fall. Stage 2 is the markup, where the price rises above a rising 50-day average and a rising 200-day average. Stage 3 is the top, and Stage 4 is the decline. The company is in Stage 2 on the ledger. Its close of ₹1,417.50 is above the 50-day average of ₹1,384.40, the 50-day average is 2.49 percent higher than it was 21 sessions ago, and the 200-day average of ₹1,256.75 is higher than its level 63 sessions ago of ₹1,215.25 (ledger). On the stage test alone, the stock passes both the Core and the Tactical versions.

The technical floor does not stop at the stage. The momentum gate adds the questions of extension and entry, which the stage test does not ask. The company passes the stage and the death-cross tests, and it fails the momentum gate, so the technical floor fails as FAIL_TECHNICAL_NO_MOMENTUM. The gate status names the reason so that the failure can be read in the record without re-running the code.

Upside-to-downside floor

The upside-to-downside floor asks whether the reward from a purchase is large compared with the loss if the stop is hit. It is calculated as the distance from the price to a target, divided by the distance from the price to the stop. The Core bucket needs at least 3.0, and the Tactical bucket needs at least 3.5. The result depends on two inputs, a target and a stop. The stop is calculated from the ledger, but a target must be anchored to a checkable source. This company has no checkable target, so the floor is not passed. The full calculation, and the price each bucket would need, are in the asymmetry chapter.

Asymmetry and target anchoring

Asymmetry compares what a buyer can gain with what a buyer can lose. The framework calculates it as the distance from the price to a target, divided by the distance from the price to a stop. The formula is written in the scoring file as (target base minus the close) divided by (the close minus the stop loss). A ratio of 3.0 means the potential gain is three times the potential loss. The Core bucket needs 3.0 and the Tactical bucket needs 3.5. The stop is set by a rule, but the target has to come from outside the rule, and the framework requires that it be anchored to a checkable basis.

There are two accepted anchors. The first is forward earnings per share multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio. The second is a technical measured move, which projects the height of a base onto the breakout point. Neither was verifiable for this company on 13 October 2026. A forward earnings figure needs an analyst consensus that can be opened, read and checked, and the consensus pages that were found returned only snippets with conflicting targets. The three-year median multiple also could not be verified, because the only history found was a five-year average from a third-party page that was not opened. The framework does not permit a broker's headline target to replace either anchor.

The company has not failed on the arithmetic. It has failed on the evidence. The asymmetry floor is therefore recorded as FAIL_ASYMMETRY, with the word unverified, and this is one of the two items in the gate status. The rule is clear that an arbitrary target is forbidden, so no target has been invented for this dossier. The consequence is that even if the price moved into the buyable band, the company would still need a checkable target before it could be bought.

Why the broker targets are not used

Broker targets are the price levels that sell-side analysts publish for a stock, usually twelve months ahead. They are useful for a reader who wants to know what the market's professionals expect. They are not accepted here, for two reasons. First, the targets for this company disagree widely. The snippets listed targets from about ₹1,400 to ₹1,650 for the next twelve months, with other sites giving figures near ₹1,500 and ₹1,600, and none of these pages could be opened to check its method. [25] Second, a target that is not built from earnings and a multiple, or from a measured move, cannot be tested by the reader. The framework needs a target that someone else can rebuild from the same inputs.

A Core buyer would need to believe the stock is worth about ₹2,036 within the framework's horizon, which is roughly 44 percent above today's close. A Tactical buyer would need about ₹1,750. Neither belief is supported by a checkable anchor in this session.

The number

The number is the price target a buyer would need for the upside-to-downside ratio to reach the bucket's floor. At the 13 October 2026 close of ₹1,417.50, that is ₹2,035.83 for the Core bucket (floor 3.0) and ₹1,750.28 for the Tactical bucket (floor 3.5).

How it was calculated

The ratio is the distance from the close to the target, divided by the distance from the close to the stop. Rearranged, the target needed equals the close plus the floor multiplied by the risk, where the risk is the close minus the stop. For the Core bucket that is ₹1,417.50 plus 3.0 times ₹206.11, which gives ₹2,035.83.

The inputs

The inputs come from the ledger and the scoring file. The close is ₹1,417.50. The Core stop is ₹1,211.39, from the 200-day average of ₹1,256.75 less 1.0 ATR of ₹45.36. The Tactical stop is ₹1,322.42, from the swing low of ₹1,345.10 less 0.5 ATR of ₹22.68. No input comes from a broker or a web source, which is why the target itself is the missing part.

The range

The number moves with the entry price. If the entry were the 63-day base low of ₹1,258.20, the Core target needed would be ₹1,398.63, close to today's price. At the current close it is ₹2,035.83. The range is wide, so the answer depends on where the price is when the target is set.

If the number is off

If the target were lower than the number, the ratio would fall below the floor and the bucket would fail. If the Core stop moved by one ATR, the target needed would move by three times ₹45.36, which is ₹136.08. A target that cannot be checked counts as a failure, so the number is not used until a verifiable anchor exists.

Near support the ratio is far more favourable. If the price fell back to the base low of ₹1,258.20, the Core stop would sit ₹46.81 below the price.

A ratio of 3.0 would then need a target of about ₹1,398.63, which is roughly where the stock trades today. The same company, at a lower price, would pass the asymmetry floor more easily if a target were verifiable.

That is the main reason the framework waits for support. The price is doing the work that a target cannot.

Layers 2 and 3

Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five operating measures that describe how this kind of business is performing. Layer 3 is the timing gate, which maps the distance from support onto an action. Both are described here, but neither is scored, because the company failed Layer 1. The rules say that a company which fails a floor is not evaluated further, and this dossier follows that rule so that the verdict does not rest on the operating story alone.

The reason for the rule is that a scorecard can be read as a reason to buy, even when a floor has failed. A company with a strong order book and strong cash conversion would score well on operating measures, and the score would then seem to argue for a purchase that the technical gate has already refused. Keeping Layer 2 out of the verdict prevents that. For the record, the company's order cover at 30 June 2026 would be about 2.45 times FY26 sales, and its three-year cash conversion is 1.56. Those figures are reported in the Layer 1 chapter, and they are not scored here.

What Layer 3 would say

Layer 3 would apply only after Layer 1 passes. Its job is to decide the timing of an entry. The framework maps the distance from support, in ATR, onto a ladder of four bands. Inside 0.5 ATR the action is an immediate buy at full size. Between 0.5 and 1.0 ATR the action is to accumulate on a dip at standard size. Between 1.0 and 1.2 ATR the action is a limit order only, at reduced size. Beyond 1.2 ATR the company is in the no-buy band, and the rule is to wait for support.

On 13 October 2026 the price is 3.51 ATR above support, which is beyond the 1.2 ATR limit. If Layer 1 had passed, the posture would therefore be the no-buy band, and the verdict would be WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The posture is EXTENDED, and the rule is not to chase. The support level is the 63-day base low of ₹1,258.20. The price would return to the buyable bands at ₹1,312.63, which is the limit-order limit, at ₹1,303.56, which is the standard band, and at ₹1,280.88, which is the full-size band. The posture is therefore WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT in the present, and the company is not a candidate for a buy in this run.

Fatal-flaw red team

A red team asks the strongest case against the conclusion. Here the conclusion is REJECTED, so the red team asks what would make a buyer wrong to accept the framework's answer, and what would make the company look better than it is. The three flaws below are the ones the evidence supports most directly. Each one names the evidence, the number, and the level at which it would matter.

Flaw one: the price has already moved past its base

The strongest argument for buying now is that the trend is clean. The stock is above both averages, the 200-day average is rising, the company is beating the index, and the order book is at a record. A buyer who waits for a pullback risks missing a leg of the move. That is the bull case, and it deserves a direct answer. The answer is that the framework was built for this trade-off. A stock 3.51 ATR above its base has already delivered most of the gain that a base breakout would have offered. The rule is to pay for the entry and not for the story. A purchase at ₹1,417.50 would be a purchase after the breakout, with a stop 6.7 percent below on the Tactical rule, and 14.5 percent below on the Core rule. Those are the costs a buyer would carry from the first day.

The level that would weaken this flaw is the price itself. If the price closed back at or below ₹1,312.63, the extension would fall inside 1.2 ATR, and the flaw would no longer apply in its present form. A breakout above ₹1,490.00 would not weaken this flaw, because it would put the price further from its base and the gate would still fail on extension. Until the price closes at or below ₹1,312.63, the flaw stands (ledger levels).

Flaw two: the profit trend is flattered by a one-off quarter

The second flaw sits in the earnings. The three-year profit growth printed by Screener is 36 percent. Arithmetic from the two annual profit figures in the same table gives 33 percent, from ₹435 crore in FY23 to ₹1,031 crore in FY26. [1] That gap is small, but the bigger issue is the FY26 base. The March 2026 quarter alone reported a profit of ₹431 crore, with other income of ₹103 crore and a tax rate of 16 percent, against the June 2026 quarter's profit of ₹312 crore at a 26 percent tax rate. [1] The March quarter is an outlier. A growth figure that starts from a low base and ends with an outlier quarter overstates the run rate. The operating margin is the more reliable test, and it was 9 percent in FY26, the same as FY23, and below the 13 percent of FY18 and FY19. [1]

The level that would weaken this flaw is a clean year. If the FY27 operating margin moved above 10 percent, and the profit grew without an unusual quarter in other income, the margin argument would weaken. The company's own guidance of a PBT margin improvement of more than 75 basis points would need to be met in the full year, not in a single quarter. [4]

Flaw three: the ownership and the penalties

The third flaw is about the shareholder base and the small regulatory items. Promoters held 33.58 percent of the company at 30 June 2026. Trendlyne reports that their pledge was unchanged at 24.55 percent of holdings in the June 2026 quarter. [2] The pledge is a loan secured on shares, and if the share price fell far enough the lender could sell the pledged shares, which would add supply to the market. Trendlyne words the pledge as a percentage of holdings and does not state whether that means promoter holdings or total shares. If the 24.55 percent is a share of promoter holdings, the pledged stake is about 8.2 percent of the company (33.58 percent multiplied by 24.55 percent). That is arithmetic from two published figures, not a published figure. The pledge is high enough to matter in a sharp fall, and it is unchanged, so it is not yet rising.

The regulatory items are small. On 9 October 2026 the company disclosed a GST demand of ₹1.05 crore for an alleged excess input tax credit in FY22, and said it would appeal. Separately, on 13 October 2026 the company disclosed that an appeal on a GST demand was partly allowed, and the penalty was cut to ₹0.14 crore from ₹0.21 crore. [1] The filings do not say whether these are the same matter. Neither matter is large against profit of ₹1,031 crore. They are listed because a reader who relies on the exchange filings should see them, and because they show that the company is still responding to tax matters, which is a small but real cost of its size. The level that would matter here is a pledge rising above the current 24.55 percent, or a penalty that grows materially on appeal.

Invalidation levels and what we would do

An invalidation level is the price at which the reasoning in this dossier stops being true. For a rejected company, the levels describe when the verdict should be re-run, and for a company that later passes, they describe when a position would be cut. The levels below come from the ledger and the rules, not from any forecast of the price. Each one names the rule it feeds, so that a reader can check it against the next ledger without re-reading the report.

The first level is the support itself. A close below ₹1,258.20, the 63-day base low, would mean the price had lost the base that the entry rule is built on. The Core stop would then be the reference, at ₹1,211.39, and the rule would say the setup has broken. The second level is the 200-day average at ₹1,256.75. A close below it would mean the long-term trend is at risk, and the Stage 2 test would be in doubt. Both levels are below the price by about 11 percent, and both are well outside the ATR bands, so they are not likely to be reached by ordinary movement in a single session.

The third level is the swing low at ₹1,345.10 and the Tactical stop at ₹1,322.42. A holder who had bought under the Tactical rule would be out on a close below ₹1,322.42. For a rejected company there is no position, so the level is a marker of how the structure would look to a buyer who entered on a pullback. The fourth level is the upside. A close above the 52-week high of ₹1,490.00, with volume at least as high as the 20-day average, would be a new breakout. It would not change the verdict, because the price would be further from its base and Test A and Test B would fail on extension. It would make the eventual target harder to justify, because a higher entry shrinks the ratio.

A close at or below ₹1,312.63 would move the price into the limit-order band, and the momentum gate would be re-run on that close. A verifiable target that gives a ratio of at least 3.0 on the Core stop is the second trigger for re-testing this verdict.

The action this dossier recommends is simple. No purchase is made. The company is kept on the watchlist with the reason recorded. The next re-test would be triggered by a close at or below ₹1,312.63, which would move the price into the limit-order band, or by the arrival of a verifiable target and a three-year median multiple. On either trigger the momentum gate would be re-run on the new close, the asymmetry would be recalculated against the Core and Tactical stops, and the verdict would be set again. If neither trigger occurs, the verdict stays REJECTED until the next run.

The rule also requires that every figure in this chapter be checked against the next ledger, since the ledger is the only source for prices and technicals. If the price moved, the ATR distances, the stops and the required targets would all change, and the table in the asymmetry chapter would need to be rebuilt on that day's close.

Glossary pointers

ATR (average true range) is the average size of a daily price move over 14 sessions. Here it is ₹45.36. Base low is the lowest close over the last 63 sessions, which is ₹1,258.20. Stage 2 is the markup phase in which the price is above rising averages. ROCE is return on capital employed, and ROE is return on equity. CFO is cash from operations, and PAT is profit after tax. OPM is the operating margin. EPC is engineering, procurement and construction. FAIL_ASYMMETRY means the upside-to-downside floor failed, here because no checkable target exists.
