Before you read

Usha Martin makes steel wire ropes, wires and concrete strands for cranes, lifts, mines, oil rigs and bridges, and 57% of its sales are made abroad.[1] It holds more cash than debt and earned about 20 paise of operating profit on each rupee of sales last quarter.[1] We rate it a good business but not a buy at ₹464.10, because the price offers 1.90 of reward for each 1 of risk, and we need 2.0.[2] We would buy only between ₹449 and ₹458, after the November results, and we would drop the idea below ₹432 or if profit margins fall under 19%.

1. What the company does

Usha Martin makes steel wire ropes. A wire rope is a rope made of twisted steel wires, and it is what holds up a crane hook, a lift cabin, a mine shovel or an oil rig. The company also sells plain steel wire and a strand called LRPC (low-relaxation prestressing concrete strand), which sits inside concrete bridges and buildings to keep them under compression. It was set up in 1960 and calls itself India's number one specialty wire rope maker.[1][3] Its factories are in Ranchi and Hoshiarpur in India, and it also makes rope in Dubai, Bangkok and the United Kingdom.[1][4]

The money comes mostly from rope. In the June 2026 quarter, rope brought in ₹757 crore out of ₹1,033 crore of revenue, which is 73%. Wire brought in ₹124 crore and LRPC ₹89 crore.[1][5] The remaining ₹63 crore, about 6%, is other products. That split hides a useful fact: rope is only half of the tonnes the company ships (50%) but nearly three quarters of its revenue, because a tonne of rope sells for far more than a tonne of wire or LRPC.[1] In the same quarter, management quoted about ₹85,000 a tonne for wire, ₹79,000 for LRPC and ₹1.9 lakh for rope sold in India. International rope fetched ₹3.7 to ₹3.8 lakh a tonne.[6]

Who pays? Industry. By end use, engineering takes 23% of sales, oil and offshore 21%, cranes 17%, construction and infrastructure 9%, lifts 9%, vehicles 7% and mining 5%. By region, India is 43% of sales, Europe 27%, Asia Pacific 12%, the Middle East and Africa 9% and the Americas 9%.[1] So 57% of sales are made outside India.[1][7]

The demand is mostly repeat buying. Management says about 85% of demand is replacement: ropes wear out, and safety rules force owners to swap them on a schedule. A rope on a mine dump truck may last one to two weeks, a port crane rope six to twelve months and a lift rope five to eight years.[6] An analyst who initiated coverage in September 2026 puts replacement demand at about 80% and the company's share of Indian wire rope at about 65%.[8] That repeat demand is why sales stay steady even when new projects pause.

The main inputs are wire rod (steel in coil form) and zinc, which coats the wire. The company buys both in the open market and does not hedge them. The annual report says a 5% move in the price of wire rod would change standalone profit by ₹40.7 crore (4,070 lakh), and a 5% move in zinc by ₹3.3 crore.[9] Usha Martin no longer makes steel. It sold its steel business to Tata Steel Long Products in 2018 and 2019, so it buys rod instead of melting scrap.[8][9] A common question is whether steel scrap prices matter. They do not here: the inputs to watch are wire rod and zinc.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has moved. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical size of one day's price swing, which we use to measure distances in units that adjust to how jumpy the stock is.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is operating profit before those four charges. It is the simplest measure of what the factories earn.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is yearly operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. Higher is better, and 15% is our floor.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow, which is the cash left after paying for plant and equipment. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business earns before that spending.
  • Operating margin is operating profit divided by sales; gross margin is sales minus the direct cost of goods, divided by sales; net margin is final profit divided by sales.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money; net debt is borrowings minus cash, and a negative figure means the company holds net cash.
  • The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) is the share price divided by profit per share; it says how many years of current profit the price pays for.
  • Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid bills, less what the company owes suppliers. Capital expenditure is spending on plant and machines.
  • Guidance is what management says it expects to deliver. A consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow the stock.
  • The 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing prices of the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. Stage 2 is the rising phase of a stock's cycle and Stage 4 is the falling phase.
  • A base is a stretch of sideways trading after a fall. A pivot is the price that must be cleared to end the base. A breakout is a close above that price, and a retest is a later dip back to it.
  • A stop loss is a preset price at which we sell to cap a loss. A limit order is an order to buy only at or below a price we set. The entry zone is the price band in which we would be willing to buy.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain from entry to target divided by the loss from entry to stop. A figure of 2.0 means two rupees of possible gain for each rupee at risk.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A strong wire rope business is hard to copy and easy to describe: customers do not buy on price alone, because a rope that fails can kill someone and stop a crane or a mine. Approval from classification bodies and big buyers takes years. The company's own list of approvals includes the American Petroleum Institute, Lloyd's, DNV, ABS, Class NK and the Indian mines regulator.[1][9] We judge the moat on four tests: share, repeat demand, price power and cost advantage. For context, a market research firm puts growth in the Indian steel wire rope market at about 15.6% a year from 2021 to 2024, but it does not publish its method, so we treat that figure as a rough guide only.[12]

Where the moat is real

Share and repeat demand come first. Management says it holds 65% to 70% of Indian wire rope, 60% to 65% of lift rope, and more than 95% of port rope. In the United States its share is below 5%, and in Europe it is 10% to 12%.[6][7] With 85% of demand coming from replacement, most orders come from customers who already use its ropes.[6] Price power shows up in the mix. Within rope, the value-added share rose from 70% in FY26 to 73% in the June 2026 quarter, and operating profit per tonne rose from ₹34,107 in FY26 to ₹40,581.[6][1] Those numbers are what a company with real pricing power looks like.

The costs are lean. Management says its 'One Usha Martin' programme cut fixed employee costs by 3% and administrative costs by more than 7% in FY26 while sales grew 6%, and that savings added up to ₹65 crore to ₹70 crore over 18 months.[7] An analyst puts the FY26 saving at about ₹70 crore, or about ₹3,380 of profit per tonne.[8] These are management claims, but the margin rise from 17.2% in FY25 to 19.1% in FY26 supports them.[7]

Where the moat is thin

Volume is the weak spot. The company said it expected about 10% growth in rope and wire volumes in August 2024, and it still guides to 10% to 12% for FY27. Yet the June 2026 quarter shipped about 51,000 tonnes, flat on a year earlier, and an analyst's model has production rising only 3.5% in FY27.[13][6][1][8] Middle East volume fell about 28% in the quarter because of the regional conflict, and management says Saudi projects were delayed or stalled.[6] A second transcript records the same 10% to 12% target.[14]

Inputs are a second weak spot. Wire rod was about 7% dearer and zinc about 28% dearer than a year earlier in the June 2026 quarter, and zinc was near a four-year high in mid-2026.[6][15][16] The company passed those costs on, but it holds no hedges, so a quick rise in rod or zinc would hit profit before prices catch up.[9] A third weak spot is money tied up in the business: unsold stock alone equals about 187 days of the business.[17] That ties up cash, and a slowdown could leave unsold stock on the shelves.

What the balance sheet adds

The company ended June 2026 with ₹465 crore more cash than debt, up from ₹332 crore in March, and gross debt of ₹146 crore. Interest cover was 48.2 times.[1] India Ratings lifted its rating from IND A+ (set in August 2024) to IND AA- with a stable outlook in July 2026, and the company says standalone operations are debt free. The rating is reviewed each July or August.[6][18][19][7] Two cautions apply: standalone contingent liabilities of ₹220 crore (22,013 lakh) sit outside the balance sheet, and ₹65 crore is still owed by Tata Steel Long Products from the earlier steel sale.[9] Neither is large next to ₹465 crore of net cash. What this means for you: the balance sheet removes most solvency risk, so the debate is about growth and price, which the next chapter scores in numbers.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 5 gave numbers. This chapter attacks them. For each estimate we name the assumption most likely to be wrong, say what evidence would show it, and say when we will see that evidence. Then we write the best honest case against the idea, as a sceptic who owns none of the stock would write it. Last, we name the one claim on which everything else hangs.

The reason to do this is practical. A verdict that cannot say how it could fail has no stop. In Chapter 7 we turn each of these failure signals into a level or a date, so that the plan tells you when to cancel an order, not only when to place one.

Weakest assumption in our profit estimate

The weakest assumption is that operating profit per tonne stays near ₹39,800 in FY27 and ₹41,000 in FY28. It was ₹26,473 in FY23, ₹32,299 in FY24, ₹30,148 in FY25 and ₹34,107 in FY26, and it reached ₹40,581 only in the June 2026 quarter.[1] One strong quarter and one good one are a thin base for two years. Management helps by saying that 20% is a floor, but it also says margins swing between 20% and 21% by quarter with mix, and a floor stated after one quarter has not yet been tested by a full year of rising rod and zinc prices.[6]

The evidence that we are wrong would be an operating profit per tonne below about ₹37,000 or an operating margin below 19% in the September 2026 quarter. The company has not yet announced the date. Its last three September-quarter results came on 3 to 8 November, and the notice came eight days ahead in 2025, so we expect the board meeting notice between 31 October and 6 November. MarketScreener's calendar estimate is 14 November 2026. The December quarter, expected around 13 February 2027, is the second test.[39][11][31]

Weakest assumption in our target price

The weakest assumption is the 25.0 times P/E. It is 8% below the 3-year median of 27.3, but the stock has traded between 20.4 and 35.6 times in that window, and a market that is in a downtrend can mark any multiple down.[30] Each 1.0 of P/E is about ₹21 on our target. The evidence we are wrong would be a P/E that stays below 22 for several weeks with profit forecasts unchanged. We can see that every day. A fall to 20.4 times our FY28 profit would be ₹428, which is about 1% below our stop and 7% below the ₹458 zone top. That is why we set the stop at ₹432 and not at the weaker level that a 20.4 times P/E would imply. If the stock fell through the stop on a P/E reset alone, we would treat it as a broken thesis and would not average down.

Where our reverse DCF is least reliable

Our reverse DCF rests on a single year of free cash flow, and that figure has ranged from ₹90 crore to ₹489 crore in four years.[17] A discount rate of 12% and a 20 times exit multiple are repository settings, not facts about this company. We would see a better base when the half-year cash flow statement arrives with the September 2026 results. If the half-year shows free cash flow of less than ₹150 crore, the base is weaker than we assumed and the implied growth rises. That would argue for a lower entry price, not a higher one. The reverse is also true: if the half-year shows free cash flow above ₹250 crore, the stock looks a little cheaper than our estimate says, but we would still wait for the zone, because one half-year is a short record and the plant spending planned for FY27 is larger than in FY26.

The red-team bear case: the strongest honest argument against buying

A sceptic would start with the margin. Operating margin was 14% in FY22, 17% in FY25 and 20% now. A record margin is where a commodity-linked business is most exposed, and Usha Martin holds no hedges on wire rod or zinc, whose price swings move profit by ₹40.7 crore per 5% for rod alone.[17][9] The March 2026 quarter's other income also included a ₹19 crore interest refund, so part of the recent profit is not repeatable.[7] Zinc was up about 28% in the June quarter and near a four-year high in August.[6][16]

Next, the volume. Tonnes shipped were flat in the June 2026 quarter, the company missed its volume guide in FY26, a plan of 5,000 to 6,000 tonnes of coated LRPC was cut to 3,500 to 4,000, and the one broker model we read assumes production up only 3.5% in FY27.[1][7][6][8] Without tonnes, profit growth has to come from price and mix, and both have limits. The price already assumes about 10% a year for ten years against sales growth of 4% a year over three years.[17]

Last, the owners. Promoters cut their holding from 51.3% to 40.1% while the share price rose, and we could not find the filings that explain each sale. Stock alone is 187 days of the business, there are ₹220 crore of contingent liabilities, and ₹65 crore is still owed from the steel sale.[17][9] The bear value is ₹339, which is below our stop of ₹432, so a gap down on results could cost more than the plan allows.

Our answer has three parts. We size the position at TIER_3 and start at half. We wait for the September-quarter result, so that margin and volume are tested before any money goes in. And we place the stop one ATR under the 63-day low, so the planned loss is about 5.7% of the entry price if the order fills at ₹458 (6.9% from today's close). None of this removes the risk. It limits what a wrong call costs.
