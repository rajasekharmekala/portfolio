0. Executive Summary

Start here if you have never read a company report. eClerx is a Mumbai-based outsourcing company. It runs data, analytics, finance and operations work for large banks, asset managers, retailers, luxury brands and technology firms, mostly in the United States. About 94 percent of its FY26 revenue came from North America, the UK and Europe, and 93 percent was billed on time-and-material terms, so revenue tracks headcount and billing rates rather than product sales.[3] Think of it as a large team of trained analysts that clients rent by the hour, with the work moving gradually from plain process handling toward data science and automation.

The report judges whether a new position should be opened. It does so in layers. Layer 1 is a pass-or-fail gate sheet with five tests. Return on capital employed (ROCE) asks how many rupees of yearly operating profit the business earns for every hundred rupees of capital tied up in it; 34.4 means Rs 34 earned per Rs 100, a very good rate. Debt to equity compares borrowings with shareholders' money; 0.15 means the company owes about 15 paise for each rupee the owners have put in, and most of that is office-lease accounting. Cash from operations divided by profit after tax (CFO/PAT) checks whether profit arrives as actual cash; 1.17 means every rupee of reported profit produced about Rs 1.17 of cash over three years.

Two further gates are about the price rather than the business. The first is the chart stage, a four-step description of a stock's life cycle: Stage 1 is a quiet base, Stage 2 a rising phase, Stage 3 a top or a stall and Stage 4 a decline. ECLERX is in Stage 3. The second is asymmetry, which divides the likely gain by the likely loss. The likely gain is the distance from the price to a target. The likely loss is the distance from the price to a stop, the level at which you would admit the idea is wrong and sell. For ECLERX that ratio is 0.65, and the house floor is 3.0. A stop is protective, much like a smoke alarm: you hope never to use it, but you set it before you need it.

The company also uses its own vocabulary. ACV, or annual contract value, is the yearly value of newly signed deals and works as an order book. Time-and-material billing means clients pay for the hours worked rather than a fixed project price. ATR, or average true range, is the typical daily price swing; here it is Rs 68.66, and it sizes the stop. A bonus issue of shares in a 1:1 ratio doubles the share count without changing the value of the company, which is why per-share figures in this report are restated. A buyback is the company purchasing its own shares from owners, usually to return surplus cash.

2.2 Unit economics

Unit economics for a labour-based outsourcer come down to three dials: billed headcount, revenue per billed head and the cost of the people and seats behind them. In Q1 FY27 operating revenue was USD 125.9 million, up 15.2 percent year on year and 2.8 percent over the prior quarter, the twelfth consecutive quarter of sequential growth. Utilization was 75.5 percent, attrition 21 percent and days sales outstanding 79. Total headcount fell 0.6 percent quarter on quarter while billed headcount rose, which is the productivity signal management wants to show.[2]

Newly signed annual contract value (ACV) was USD 41 million, up 25 percent year on year. FY26 ACV was about USD 170 million, and management aims to match or exceed that. Typical revenue roll-off (contracts ending or shrinking) is 15 to 20 percent a year, so the company must sign roughly that much new work just to stand still.[2]

Reading across. A 25 percent rise in deal wins supports the growth forecast, but the offsetting roll-off means the quality of the sales engine matters more than any single quarter. Management itself names "sharpening sales focus, improving revenue predictability and reducing client concentration" as opportunities, which concedes that these are not yet solved.[14] Costs are the next piece.
