Bosch Limited is the Indian arm of the German Bosch group, and it makes engine parts, sensors and spare parts for cars, trucks, tractors and two-wheelers.[19][21] The business is excellent, with no debt, a return on capital employed (ROCE) of 21.5 percent and a share price in a confirmed uptrend.[1][32] Our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, because at the 28 October 2026 close of ₹47,625 the target pays only 0.39 times the risk, against a floor of 2.0.[32] We would hold all buy orders until the 6 November 2026 results are out, then release them between ₹42,700 and ₹43,700 only if the numbers still give 2.0 times the risk and a 12 percent return.[4][17] The view is wrong if those results show profit of ₹800 crore or more and a margin of 14.5 percent or better, because the target and the buy zone would then rise.

1. What the company does

Bosch Limited makes the hidden parts of vehicles. When you start a diesel truck, a petrol car or a scooter, parts that Bosch designed decide how much fuel enters the engine, how clean the exhaust is, and when the spark fires. The company is the flagship of the Bosch group in India. The Bosch group has been in India for more than 100 years. Its parent, Robert Bosch, holds 70.54 percent of the shares: 67.76 percent through Robert Bosch Internationale Beteiligungen AG and 2.78 percent through Bosch Global Software Technologies.[13][21] Mobility, meaning parts and systems for vehicles, was 89.4 percent of sales in the year to March 2026. The rest is power tools and a small fire-safety business.[19]

Who pays, and for what? Vehicle makers such as car, truck and tractor companies pay for engine and exhaust parts that are fitted on the assembly line. Garages and parts shops pay for replacement parts, which Bosch sells through more than 50,000 retail outlets in over 650 districts. [19] Builders and workshops pay for power tools. The first group buys in large, planned volumes and negotiates hard. The second group buys in small lots and pays a better margin, but it follows the age and size of the vehicle fleet rather than this year's new-car sales. Revenue from operations was ₹20,035 crore in the year to March 2026, up 10.8 percent.[10][19]

How does Bosch earn its money? It sells a part at a price agreed with the vehicle maker, often for the life of a vehicle model. A bigger share of a fixed-cost factory is used when volumes rise, so profit grows faster than sales in good years and falls faster in bad ones. The company also keeps a large pool of cash and funds, ₹7,981 crore of financial investments at March 2026 in the annual report (the Screener balance sheet in Chapter 3 shows ₹8,020 crore because the two sources group items slightly differently), and earns interest and gains on it. [19]That treasury income was about ₹854 crore in FY26, which is more than a quarter of pre-tax profit before the one-off gain. [33][19]It matters because most of that cash has now gone into an acquisition, described next. Bosch also depends on its parent: about 53 percent of the parts and goods it buys come from group companies, and roughly 75 percent of its exports go to Robert Bosch in Germany.[6][19]

Two things changed in 2026. First, on 1 July 2026 Bosch became the owner of Bosch Chassis Systems India (called RBIC), a sister company that makes anti-lock braking, electronic stability and airbag-sensor systems. [17]The price was up to ₹9,068.68 crore, paid mainly in cash to the two Bosch group sellers. [15]Second, management announced two joint ventures: a 50-50 venture with Tata AutoComp to build electric axles, and a venture with Brakes India and Wheels India for electronic air-brake systems on trucks. [10][14]Because RBIC joins the consolidated accounts from the July-September quarter, every year-on-year comparison from 6 November 2026 will compare a bigger company with a smaller one.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • OEM means original equipment manufacturer, the vehicle maker that fits Bosch parts on the assembly line.
  • Aftermarket means replacement parts sold for repair and servicing after the vehicle leaves the factory.
  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a measure of operating profit before those accounting charges.
  • The operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share, the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow, the cash left after running the business and buying plant and machines.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money.
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and customer bills, less the credit suppliers give.
  • Capital expenditure is cash spent on plants, tools and machines.
  • Guidance is the company's own published forecast for the coming period.
  • A consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for a number.
  • Treasury income means interest and investment gains on the company's cash and funds, which is not part of making and selling parts.
  • Reverse DCF means a calculation that starts from today's share price and works out how fast profit must grow for ten years to earn a 12 percent return.
  • Common-control deal means a purchase between two companies owned by the same parent, here the Bosch group.
  • RSI stands for relative strength index, a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been rising.
  • ATR stands for average true range, the typical daily price swing in rupees.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend: price above a rising 50-day average that is above a rising 200-day average. Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • A base is a sideways stretch of price that a stock builds before a move, and the pivot is the top of that base. A breakout is a move above the pivot on high volume.
  • A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and the entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).
  • Support means a price level where buyers have stepped in before, such as a recent low or a moving average.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Bosch's first strength is technology it does not have to pay to develop alone. The group spent €7.9 billion on research and development worldwide, and India hosts its largest engineering centre outside Germany.[13] Bosch Limited itself spent ₹601 crore on research and ₹410 crore on new plant and machinery in FY26, and in April-June 2026 customers gave it awards for delivery, quality and technology.[12][19][6] For a buyer of the shares the point is simple: vehicle makers rarely switch a supplier of safety-critical or emission-critical parts in mid-model, so Bosch's sales are sticky once a part is designed in.

The second strength is the balance sheet. Borrowings were ₹119 crore against shareholders' funds of ₹14,846 crore at March 2026, a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.01, and finance cost was only ₹26.7 crore.[1][19] The company says it has no debt instrument, so it obtained no credit rating in FY26.[21] The group's own R&D budget and a debt-free local company mean Bosch can fund a new product line from cash. It just did exactly that with a ₹9,069 crore acquisition paid from internal accruals.[19][15]

Third, the evidence on growth is good. In FY26 Bosch's mobility sales grew 16.9 percent while all Indian vehicle production grew 11.8 percent.[11][19] In April-June 2026 revenue grew 22 percent and operating profit before interest, tax and depreciation grew 28 percent. [6][7]Management says it beat market volume growth by a few percentage points. [6]The operating margin has risen for three periods running: 12.8 percent in FY25, 13.2 percent in FY26 and 14.0 percent in April-June 2026.[16][12][7]

Now the weaknesses. The first is the engine itself. Most of Power Solutions is built for internal-combustion engines. Bosch's own forecast, shown to investors in May 2026, still has petrol and diesel at 81 percent of Indian passenger-car production in 2033, but electric cars rise from 233,000 in 2025 to 1.45 million in 2033, and the whole vehicle market grows only 3 to 4 percent a year over that period.[12] A 3 to 4 percent market is a slow base for a share price that already assumes much faster profit growth, a gap we measure in Chapter 5.

The second weakness is dependence on the parent. Bosch buys about 53 percent of its purchased goods from group companies, the highest share in about ten years according to an analyst on the August call, and about 75 percent of its exports go to the parent.[6][19] Management's answer is that volume growth lifted imports and that localisation is on track.[6] The new acquisition is a further related-party deal: the sellers are Bosch group companies and the buyer is a company in which the same group owns 70.54 percent. Independent valuations by PwC and a fairness opinion from ICICI Securities support the price, but the minority shareholder cannot negotiate it.[14][16]

The third weakness is how much of profit comes from outside the factory. Operating profit was ₹2,650 crore in FY26 and treasury income about ₹854 crore, so more than a quarter of profit before tax came from investing cash.[33][19] That cash is now mostly spent. The profit lost has to be replaced by RBIC's profit, and the first quarter that shows the swap is the one reported on 6 November.

Finally, the cycle. Indian vehicle production grew from 23.9 million in FY22 to 36.3 million in FY26, with growth of 13.1, 9.8, 9.1 and 11.8 percent in the last four years.[19] Bosch's own record shows the other side of a cycle. Revenue fell from ₹12,085 crore in FY19 to ₹9,716 crore in FY21, a drop of 20 percent, and operating profit fell from ₹2,154 crore to ₹1,162 crore.[1] We use that history in the bear case: a downturn like FY20-21 would cut profit by about half, and a share price at 59 times profit has little room for that.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter checks two things. The first is the level: is the company safe and profitable today? The second is the direction: are the numbers improving or fading? We call these static levels and trajectory. A company with modest levels but a rising trend can be a better buy than one with high levels that is flattening, and the reverse is also true. The hard tests that remove a company from consideration, such as heavy debt, a pledged promoter stake or a collapsing chart, come first, and Bosch clears all of them.

Five years of results are in the first table. Revenue rose from ₹11,782 crore in FY22 to ₹20,035 crore in FY26, a compound rate of 14.2 percent a year over four years and 10.3 percent over the last three. [1] Operating profit rose from ₹1,457 crore to ₹2,650 crore, which is 16.1 percent a year over four years and 13.6 percent over three. Net profit is harder to read because it contains one-offs: other income was ₹1,566 crore in FY24, after a ₹939 crore quarter in September 2023, and ₹1,410 crore in FY26, which includes the ₹556 crore gain on the May 2025 sale of the video, access and communication business.[1][19] Strip out the sale and FY26 profit is about ₹2,284 crore, which is 11.4 percent of revenue.

5. What could happen next

Every share price carries a story about the future. For Bosch the ledger answer is 21.1 percent a year. It starts from trailing profit of ₹2,363.31 crore and a market value of ₹1,40,475 crore. [32]Growing at 21.1 percent for ten years would take profit to about ₹16,000 crore, nearly seven times today's level. The 21.1 percent is probably too high as a measure of what the price asks, and we should say so. The base profit of ₹2,363 crore is for the twelve months to June 2026, which has no profit from RBIC. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this book rests on assumptions, and each one can be wrong. Estimate 1: our profit per share of ₹961 and ₹1,054

The weakest assumption is how much treasury income remains after the RBIC payment, together with a 13.5 percent profit margin for RBIC. We have ₹436 crore of other income in FY27 and ₹300 crore in FY28. Estimate 2: the growth the price asks for

The weakest assumption is not ours. It is the fixed 12 percent discount rate and 20 times terminal multiple that our scoring file applies to every Indian company. Estimate 3: the base value of ₹50,000 and the probabilities

The weakest assumption is that the three-year median P/E of 49.6 is the right multiple for the next twelve months. The red-team bear case

The strongest honest argument against buying even in our zone has five points. First, the largest use of shareholders' cash in years went to the parent: ₹9,068.68 crore, paid mainly in cash, for a company whose EBITDA margin rose from 8.9 percent in FY16 to 19.3 percent in FY25. PwC valued it with the buyer's management projections, applied a 25 percent discount to peer multiples because of the margin expansion, and its cash-flow value was ₹9,215 crore against a price of ₹9,069 crore. [16]Minority holders cannot test those projections, and the seller and the buyer have the same controlling shareholder. Second, the treasury income that supported profit is largely gone, and the first full set of numbers that shows the swap arrives only on 6 November. [1][33]

Third, the dividend, which is the one cash return a minority holder can bank, fell to 29 percent of profit against a policy floor of 55 percent. [20][19]Fourth, Bosch's own cash-flow model gave ₹30,478 a share in April 2026, 36 percent below today's price, and no one has told us what changed since then except a strong quarter. [16]Fifth, the industry. Bosch's own forecast has the vehicle market growing 3 to 4 percent a year, and Bosch earned its margin in a year when cuts in GST rates lifted vehicle volumes by double digits. A normal year after a boom year is exactly when auto-part companies disappoint. [12][19]If this bear case were right, the bear value of ₹35,300, 25.9 percent below the close, would be the likely outcome and our stop at ₹41,507.96 would fail to protect a gap-down. Two stress tests make the point without any probability attached. A fill at ₹43,700 that gapped to ₹35,300 would lose 19.2 percent of the position, or 0.67 percent of net worth at a 3.5 percent weight, against 0.18 percent for an orderly stop. That is why no order is live across the results.

7. What we would do, and when

Bosch is not in the portfolio today, so this is a decision about a new purchase, a pipeline challenger. The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, which means do not buy at the market and do not leave any order live across the 6 November results; after the results we re-run the numbers and, only if they pass, release limit orders in a lower zone. The chart is 0.65 daily swings above support, which falls in the STANDARD band of our support ladder, so the chart alone would allow a purchase. The rule that stops it is the technical routing rule in our scoring file: a core holding needs a reward to risk of at least 2.0, and the price does not offer that yet. Bosch passes every red line. It qualifies as a compounder, with Layer 2 at 4.0 out of 5.0 and no failed measure. But a core holding needs the base target to be at least 2.0 times as far above the entry as the stop is below it, and at the close of ₹47,625 the ratio is 0.39. [32]That is a price problem, not a quality problem, so the answer is to wait for the price and not to reject the company. REJECTED would apply if the chart turned to Stage 4 with Test C failing, or if a hard red line failed. Neither is the case.

Why this price band? The buy zone is ₹42,700 to ₹43,700.
