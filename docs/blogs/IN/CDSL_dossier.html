1. What the company does

Every share you buy in India is no longer a paper certificate. It is an entry in an electronic ledger, and the company that keeps that ledger is called a depository. Central Depository Services (India) Limited, known as CDSL, is one of two licensed depositories; the other is NSDL. An investor opens a demat account (a "dematerialised" account, the electronic locker for shares) through a depository participant, or DP, which is usually a broker or a bank. CDSL works with more than 588 DPs and, on 30 June 2026, kept 18.59 crore accounts, up from 15.86 crore a year earlier.[4] Management puts its share of all demat accounts at about 80 percent.[6]

CDSL does not charge the investor for holding shares. Three other groups pay it. Issuers are companies whose shares or bonds sit in the system. They pay an annual fee of ₹11.00 per folio, with minimum fees from ₹9,000 to ₹75,000 depending on the size of the issue.[10] A folio is one holder's position in one security. DPs pay a charge when securities leave a client's account, so this line rises and falls with trading activity. Intermediaries pay for KYC records ("know your customer" identity files) through CDSL Ventures, the group's KYC registration agency.[4] SEBI, the market regulator, must approve changes to these tariffs. That is why the fee list moves slowly.[7]

The five revenue lines have very different habits. In FY26 the group's operating revenue was ₹1,144.92 crore.[9] Annual issuer charges were ₹456.38 crore (39.9 percent). Transaction charges were ₹239.54 crore (20.9 percent). IPO and corporate action charges were ₹159.81 crore (14.0 percent). Online data charges from KYC were ₹149.52 crore (13.1 percent). Other operating income was ₹139.69 crore (12.2 percent). The issuer line grows with the number of folios. Folios were 38.73 crore in the June 2026 quarter against 33.2 crore a year earlier, a rise of 17 percent.[6] The transaction and IPO lines follow market mood, which is the cyclical part of the story.

The cost side is the other half of the economics. Almost all of CDSL's costs are people and technology. In FY26 the standalone company spent ₹132.16 crore on employees, up 30 percent. It spent ₹126.01 crore on technology, up 46 percent.[9] The consolidated operating margin fell from 58 percent in FY25 to 51 percent. The operating margin is the share of revenue left after operating costs.[1] Profit also depends on treasury income. The June 2026 quarter included ₹43.8 crore of investment gains and income. That equals 26 percent of the quarter's pre-tax profit of ₹166.42 crore.[6][13] Treat the franchise as steady and the reported profit as somewhat lumpy.

The three subsidiaries and what each adds

CDSL Ventures (CVL) is wholly owned and describes itself as India's first and largest KYC registration agency, holding over 100 million digitised KYC records and, by the annual report's count, more than 70 percent of its market.[4][9] It earns a fee every time a broker fetches or creates a record. SEBI cut those fees from 1 April 2026, from ₹35 to ₹28 for a fetch and from ₹20 to ₹5 for a new record, and about 80 percent of the revenue is from fetches.[7] Centrico Insurance Repository keeps electronic insurance policies (24.42 lakh e-insurance accounts) and Countrywide Commodity Repository issues electronic warehouse receipts.[5] Both are small. In FY26 CVL's profit after tax was ₹55.36 crore against ₹109.95 crore a year earlier, so the subsidiaries matter mainly as a source of risk to profit, not growth.[7]

Why the treasury book is part of the business description

CDSL collects fees in advance and keeps large cash balances, so investment income is a real part of profit. Management says it does not invest directly in equity mutual funds, holds debt schemes, and keeps 5 to 7 percent of its investable money in exchange-traded funds.[6] In the June 2026 quarter, mark-to-market gains and investment income of ₹43.8 crore sat inside a ₹75 crore other-income line.[6] Those gains swing with bond and equity prices, which is why the March 2026 quarter showed only ₹6 crore of other income and the June quarter ₹48 crore.[1]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Depository: a licensed company that holds securities in electronic form on behalf of investors.
  • Demat account (BO account): the electronic locker in which an investor's shares sit; "BO" means beneficial owner.
  • Depository participant (DP): the broker or bank through which an investor reaches the depository.
  • Folio: one holder's position in one security; issuers are billed per folio.
  • ISIN: the 12-character international code that identifies each security admitted to a depository.
  • KRA: a KYC registration agency, which stores verified identity records that every broker can reuse.
  • Market infrastructure institution (MII): an exchange, clearing house or depository that the regulator treats as critical plumbing for the market.
  • Assets under custody (AUC): the market value of all securities held in a depository; CDSL reported ₹88.2 lakh crore at 30 June 2026.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

CDSL's strength is structural. India licenses two depositories, a broker connects to one or both, and moving a large client list between them takes months of work and risk. That keeps CDSL's share of the account count near 80 percent, a level the company held through FY26.[7][9] The group is also a market infrastructure institution, so competitors cannot simply open a rival; entry needs SEBI approval and a trusted balance sheet. Return on capital employed (ROCE, the profit earned for each rupee of capital in the business) was 32.0 percent and the company has essentially no borrowings, with ₹2 crore of debt against ₹1,960 crore of shareholders' funds.[1]

The weakness is that the toll road has a regulated toll. SEBI approves every fee, and the annual issuer rate of ₹11.00 per folio has not been raised for years; management told analysts in February 2026 that an increase would come "at the appropriate time" and said in May that charges had not changed "for many, many years".[10][8][7] The regulator is also watching profitability. The annual report sets out the duties of its public interest directors. They must evaluate the profitability margins of market infrastructure institutions, and the text states that these margins "are among the highest in the country".[9] The KYC fee cut of April 2026 shows the direction a regulator can take when margins look wide.[7]

The competitive picture is stable at the top and softer at the margin. On the August call an analyst said CDSL's share of new accounts had slipped by about 420 basis points since March, to 81.4 percent, and the rival NSDL reported that its own share of net new accounts rose to 17.6 percent in the June quarter from 14 percent in the March quarter.[6][18] NSDL said fintech brokers went from 2 percent to 20 percent of its additions after it fixed onboarding friction.[18] Management told investors in May that a rule letting NSDL alone issue the identity codes for unlisted companies is due to change and "has to yet go live", and in August that it was "not yet gone live".[7][6]

Cyclicality is moderate. Transaction charges fell 9.9 percent in FY26 while trading activity cooled, and group net profit fell 14 percent to ₹455 crore even though revenue rose.[9] The steady issuer income cushioned the revenue line but costs rose faster than revenue, which is the real reason profit slipped.[9] A reader should expect the business to keep growing its customer count through a weak market, while profit growth swings with trading volume, IPO flow and treasury marks.

Competitive position: a duopoly with a closing gap at the margin

The account count tells the long-term story: 1.8 crore accounts in September 2019 and 18.59 crore in June 2026, a tenfold rise, with the incremental share at 85 to 90 percent in management's May description.[7][4] The more recent data tell a narrower story. Quarterly net additions ran at 57, 65, 76, 74 and 58 lakh from June 2025 to June 2026, so the latest quarter was almost flat on a year earlier.[5] Two things could widen the gap in the rival's favour. One is the identity-code issuing right for unlisted companies; the other is broker onboarding. The 31 October 2026 deadline after which clients whose KYC is "on hold" cannot trade adds extra work for every broker and DP.[27] The long-run headroom is large. Management cites a SEBI survey in which only 9 to 10 percent of the population takes part in the market.[7] NSE counted 13.4 crore unique registered PANs on 31 July 2026, against more than 22 crore demat accounts across both depositories, so accounts outnumber investors.[23][7]

Operational and legal risk: the 2022 malware attack and the ₹1 crore penalty

On 20 July 2026 SEBI's adjudicating officer fined CDSL ₹1 crore (₹90 lakh under the SEBI Act and ₹10 lakh under the Depositories Act) for cybersecurity lapses behind the 18 November 2022 malware attack, finding among other things that an internet-facing server was not treated as a critical asset and was left out of penetration testing.[25][26] The company said there was no material impact on its financials apart from the penalty.[26] The amount is small. The finding matters more, because it shows that regulators judge depository resilience closely and that a second incident would carry a heavier cost than ₹1 crore.

Law and regulation: the Securities Markets Code

In December 2025 the government tabled the Securities Markets Code, which would replace the SEBI Act, the Depositories Act and the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act with one law.[24] CDSL says it is studying the Code and engaging with it, and nothing in the sources read says how depository fees or ownership limits would change.[7] We treat the Code as an unquantified regulatory risk, not a negative.

3. The numbers, in plain words

The five-year record shows a business that doubled sales and kept almost every rupee of cash it earned. Its profit has stopped growing. Sales rose from ₹551 crore in FY22 to ₹1,145 crore in FY26. That is 20.1 percent a year over four years, and 27.3 percent a year over the three years from FY23 (₹555 crore).[1] Net profit rose from ₹312 crore to ₹455 crore over the four years, only 9.9 percent a year. From ₹420 crore in FY24 to ₹455 crore in FY26 it grew 4.1 percent a year.[1]

Sales grew 18.7 percent a year over FY24 to FY26, while profit grew 4.1 percent. That gap is the whole analytical story. The operating margin fell from 60 percent to 51 percent. Technology and employee costs rose 46 percent and 30 percent in FY26, and KYC income fell 28 percent.[1][9] The balance sheet is not the problem. Borrowings were ₹2 crore and shareholders' funds ₹1,960 crore at 31 March 2026. The company also holds ₹1,487 crore of investments.[1]

Cash conversion is excellent. Over FY24 to FY26 the company generated ₹1,396 crore of cash from operations against ₹1,401 crore of profit, a ratio of 1.00.[1] Free cash flow is the cash left after spending on equipment and software. It was ₹1,047 crore over the same three years, after ₹349 crore of such spending. That figure is our subtraction of free cash flow from operating cash flow. The caution is that operating cash flow includes interest and dividend income from the investment book. It therefore overstates cash from the core depository business.
