Before you read

Supreme Petrochem makes polystyrene, the plastic inside refrigerators, air conditioners and packaging, and it holds over half of the Indian market[10][1]. It has no debt, but its profit rises and falls with the gap between styrene costs and plastic prices, and a war has widened that gap this year[3]. We reject the stock for now, because the price of ₹713.70 already matches our central value of ₹706[31]. That leaves no gain for the risk taken, and our method asks for at least 2.5 times as much gain as risk[32]. We would be wrong if wide gaps last into 2027 and the new ABS plant runs at full rate, which would lift our value to about ₹840[3][4].

1. What the company does

Supreme Petrochem Limited (SPL) is a plastics maker based in Mumbai, set up in 1989 as a joint venture between The Supreme Industries Limited and the Rajan Raheja group, who each hold 30.78 percent[10]. Its main product is polystyrene, a hard clear or opaque plastic that is moulded into refrigerator trays and cabinets, air-conditioner and cooler bodies, stationery, toys and dairy packaging[1]. It also makes expandable polystyrene, the beads that become the white foam used to pack televisions and fruit and to insulate cold stores[1]. A newer line makes ABS, a tougher engineering plastic used in car interiors, e-scooters, small appliances and helmets[1].

The company buys one main raw material, styrene monomer, a liquid chemical it mostly imports from the Middle East, Singapore and East Asia[10]. It turns the styrene into plastic granules at two plants. The large one is at Amdoshi near Nagothane in Maharashtra, with 300,000 tonnes a year of polystyrene capacity and 115,000 tonnes of expandable polystyrene capacity. The small one is at Manali near Chennai, with 33,000 tonnes of expandable polystyrene[1]. Its money comes from the spread, which is the selling price of plastic minus the cost of styrene. In the year to March 2026 the company sold 363,203 tonnes of manufactured product[1].

Selling prices follow the landed price of imported plastic, so the company cannot set its own price for common grades[4]. It holds 30 to 40 days of styrene in its tanks and another four to six weeks in ships at sea[10]. That means a quick rise in styrene prices can lift profit for a few months, because stock bought cheaply is sold at a new higher price. A quick fall does the reverse. About 17 to 18 percent of sales is styrene the company simply resells without processing, which adds revenue but little profit[3].

The customers are factories, not shoppers. Revenue is split fairly evenly between original equipment makers (OEMs), such as appliance and car-part makers, and smaller processors that buy for stationery, packaging and household goods[10]. Exports were 8.26 percent of turnover in FY26, to customers in about 100 countries[6]. The company calls plastic grades made for a specific customer value-added products and says they are about 40 percent of its production[21]. Because the buyers are many and small, no single customer decides the results. The risk sits on the supply side, in styrene.

The four claims we were asked to check

This name arrived as an unverified idea from the Styrenix report, which listed four claims. Each is checked here against filings. First, a market share above 50 percent in polystyrene and expandable polystyrene is confirmed by the company and by the rating agency, which says the largest rival closed its plant in fiscal 2021[1][10]. Second, an ABS plant of 70,000 tonnes a year as phase 1 of 140,000 is confirmed[10][3].

Third, the claim that operations stopped in December 2025 after an equipment fault is confirmed. The company told the exchanges on 22 December 2025 that a critical machine had failed[12] and corrected press reports the next day to say that the rest of the Amdoshi site was running normally[13]. Fourth, the P/E, which is the share price divided by a year of profit per share, is 25.2 on the page we fetched[2]. That figure is true but misleading, because the last twelve months include a spread windfall, as Chapter 5 shows. Its listed peers trade at about 7 to 12.6 times[24][25][26].

What changed in 2025 and 2026

Four things moved. The ABS plant opened late and then failed. A second polystyrene bead line was finished but waited until April 2026[15]. The company bought Xmold, a compounder, to have a captive buyer for its ABS[10]. And from March 2026 a war in West Asia closed shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, which cut styrene supply and pushed polymer prices sharply higher[6][3]. The next chapters weigh how much of the profit in the latest half year comes from that shock and how much from the business itself.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • capital expenditure is money spent on plants and equipment rather than on running costs.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates from its day-to-day trading, after tax and changes in stock and bills.
  • free cash flow (FCF) means cash from trading minus the cash spent on new plants, so it is what is left for owners.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit from running the business before finance costs, tax and the writing-off of plant cost. Operating EBITDA excludes other income.
  • earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares. Where this report means expandable polystyrene it spells it out.
  • return on capital employed (ROCE) is profit before interest and tax divided by the capital the business uses, shown as a percentage. It tells you how hard each rupee of plant and stock works.
  • operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales, and net margin is the final profit after tax as a share of sales.
  • debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, and net debt is borrowings minus the cash and investments held.
  • working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what is owed to suppliers.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by a year of profit per share. A P/E of 25 means you pay 25 rupees for each rupee of annual profit.
  • consensus estimate is the average of the forecasts that analysts publish for a number such as profit per share.
  • guidance is the company's own forecast or promise about its coming results or projects.
  • relative strength index (RSI) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been moving. Below 30 is called oversold, above 70 overbought.
  • average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing, in rupees. It sets how far a stop must sit from support so that normal noise does not trigger it.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing prices over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. They are slow lines that prices tend to bounce from.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale, Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend, and Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase between them.
  • A base is a sideways stretch of trading that a stock builds before a larger move. The pivot is the top of that sideways stretch. A breakout is a close above the pivot on higher volume, and a retest is a pullback that tests the pivot from above.
  • A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, measured from the planned entry price.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position is strong in scale and weak in pricing power, and the two have to be read together. The company has the largest polystyrene capacity in India, 300,000 tonnes at one site, and the rating agency puts its combined share of polystyrene and expandable polystyrene above 50 percent[10]. The second-largest producer closed its plant in fiscal 2021[10]. Scale helps in three ways: lower cost per tonne, enough volume to keep its own port tank farm busy, and the ability to serve every large appliance maker[1].

The weakness is that plastic prices are set outside the company. For common grades the base price is tied to the landed price of imported material, plus the cost of bringing it in[4]. In FY26 cheaper imports from Thailand, Taiwan, Iran and China cut into local makers' share, and domestic polystyrene sales volume fell 3.4 percent[6]. In Q2 FY27 the government allowed duty-free polymer imports until 15 July 2026, and large volumes of polystyrene and ABS arrived from ASEAN countries and China in the first two months of the quarter[1]. A moat built on scale cannot stop a government decision on import duty.

Where the moat is real

The company can make plastic cheaper than a small rival and has certified grades for appliance makers, which is why it keeps a high share even when imports rise[10]. About 50 percent of its electricity comes from solar, with a 12.5 megawatt plant built with Tata Renewable Energy, and both plants recycle their effluent[1]. Its technology licences are from Huntsman through ABB Lummus for polystyrene and from Versalis of Italy for ABS[1]. These are real advantages. They protect market share. They do not protect the profit per tonne, which depends on the spread.

Where the position is thin

Styrene supply is concentrated. In the June 2026 quarter all three styrene plants in the Gulf, which are among the company's usual suppliers, stopped for safety reasons, and the company turned to China, North-East Asia and South-East Asia[3][21]. Freight for a container to Jebel Ali reached about $4,000 against a normal $250 to $300, and exports fell 65 to 68 percent in the first half of FY27[21]. The Haryana project, meant to serve north India, is on hold until the Indian Oil styrene plant is ready, so the company is spending only on site works there[4].

Return on capital has fallen steadily. The company's own figure for ROCE was 67.46 percent in FY22, 39.10 percent in FY23, 24.16 percent in FY24, 24.86 percent in FY25 and 19.45 percent in FY26[9][8][7][6]. Some of that fall is the end of the 2021-22 spread boom, when operating margin reached 18 to 21 percent[10]. Some is new plant that is not yet earning, because the plant and equipment line rose from ₹691 crore to ₹1,358 crore in FY26 as the ABS plant opened[1].

Cyclicality, customers and what drives volume

Operating margin moved between 3 and 10 percent until fiscal 2020, jumped to 18 to 21 percent in fiscals 2021 and 2022, and settled near 10 percent in FY24 to FY26[10][8][7][6]. Management calls the 19.53 percent margin of Q1 FY27 'like an aberration' caused by unusually wide spreads between styrene and plastic, and expects the gap to normalise over time[3]. The rating agency assumes an operating margin of about 7 percent in steady state[10].

Volume depends on appliance sales. Air conditioners and refrigerators are the largest users, and a mild summer in 2025 held FY26 volume growth to 2 percent[6]. About half the revenue comes from non-OEM processors[10], who bought almost 50 percent less from the company in Q1 FY27 when prices jumped, and who came back in Q2[3][21]. Management now expects total volume growth of no more than 5 percent in FY27, against the 8 to 10 percent it forecast in April[21][4].

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record on promises is mixed. On finances it did what it said: no borrowings, projects paid from the company's own cash, and a dividend that rose from ₹9 to ₹10.50 a share[4][29][22]. On project timing it slipped repeatedly, and on the one project that matters most it missed twice. In January 2025 the CFO said mechanical completion would be 'by end March of 2025, with commissioning scheduled for Q1 FY'26'[16]. The plant started on 25 September 2025, in the second quarter[11].

In July 2025 he told analysts that the plant would take 'maybe three to six months' to stabilise[17]. It stopped 88 days after start-up[11][12]. In April 2026 he said the company should reach up to about 85 percent of rated capacity 'this year'[4]. By October 2026 the company's presentation still describes the plant only as commissioned in September 2025 and does not state its output[1]. The rating agency noted that the plant is insured and under warranty, so the loss is lost sales rather than a write-off[10], which the CFO also said on the January 2026 call[18].

One promise was kept outright (no debt), one was partly met, two were missed and one was cut, and the misses all sit in the ABS and bead projects that the growth story rests on. Timing promises from this team should be discounted by one to two quarters until a project has run at full rate.

Our reading is that the team is financially disciplined and operationally slow to commission new technology. That is a reasonable trade for a debt-free company, but it matters for valuation: the price already assumes that the new capacity earns its way, and the record so far does not yet show it. The next evidence is the transcript of the 27 October 2026 call, which will say whether the plant is running at 65 percent, higher or lower.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate has a weak point. We name them here so you can tell what evidence would change our mind and when it will arrive. The red-team question is the strongest honest case against our own rejection: what if we are too pessimistic about normal profit?

Weakest assumption 1: normal profit per tonne is ₹15,500

This assumes the Q2 FY27 spread is the normal level. If styrene stays near $1,600 a tonne and the Hormuz route stays closed, spreads may stay wide for years, which would be the bull case[21]. Management said the current gap for general-purpose polystyrene is $250 to $275 against a normal $200 and a Q1 peak above $300, and for high-impact polystyrene about $350 against a normal $275 to $300[3]. The evidence will arrive with the Q3 FY27 results, due around the end of January 2027.

Weakest assumption 2: the ABS plant remains at 65 percent

Our base case gives the plant little credit. If the failed machine is repaired and the plant runs at full rate, FY28 operating profit rises. The effective capacity was 45,000 tonnes at 65 percent[4]. A full 70,000 tonnes adds 25,000 tonnes, which at an assumed ₹20,000 a tonne (our assumption, because no ABS margin is published) is ₹50 crore, or about ₹2 per share after tax, so even a full repair adds about 9 percent to our FY28 estimate. On its own that lifts our central value to about ₹771, a reward-to-risk of about 1.8 at ₹707, still short of 2.5. Some of this ramp is already inside our 412,000-tonne volume, so the true gain is smaller. The evidence will be the 27 October transcript and the Q3 FY27 filing.

Weakest assumption 3: the multiple of 33 times

Our multiple is a judgement. The stock has traded between 20 and 50 times trailing profit in three years[27]. Peers trade at 7 to 12.6 times[24]. If the market decides that ₹17 to ₹20 is a trough, it may pay 40 times and the value reaches ₹856 on our own FY28 profit. If it decides the 2021-22 boom is the only time the company earned 18 to 21 percent, it may pay 25 times and the value is ₹535.
