The price used in this report is the 2026-10-26 bar, one session behind the 2026-10-27 as-of date.[1] LTM Limited, called LTIMindtree until March 2026, is an Indian firm that builds and runs software systems for large banks, factories and consumer brands.[21] The verdict is REJECTED for now, which means we do not buy, and the cause is the price chart: the share sits below falling 50-day and 200-day averages.[1] The business passes every money test, with almost no debt, ₹15,300 crore in cash and a three-year cash-to-profit ratio of 1.06, but growth is only about 5 percent in dollars, so the price already asks for more than the facts show.[3][4][2] A close above ₹4,109.46, or growth in the high single digits, would change our view, and a close below ₹3,755.68 would end any plan.

1. What the company does

LTM designs, builds and runs software and technology systems for large companies. A bank may hire it to merge the technology behind wealth and personal banking. A US insurer may hire it to run its global IT services. A carmaker may hire it to predict machine failures before they stop a line.[2][3] Screener describes it as an IT services firm covering application development, maintenance, enterprise solutions, infrastructure management, testing, analytics and artificial intelligence.[4] It was formed when the software firm Mindtree merged into L&T Infotech in November 2022, and Crisil ranks it as the sixth-largest IT services company in India.[24][6] The company changed its legal name from LTIMindtree Limited to LTM Limited on 17 March 2026 after a postal ballot that 99.99 percent of votes approved, and the exchange ticker changed from LTIM to LTM in February 2026.[21][9]

Its customers are large, and they are mostly American. In the quarter to 30 September 2026, North America gave 74.3 percent of revenue, Europe 14.9 percent and the rest of the world 10.8 percent, and 79.3 percent of billing was in US dollars.[2] The firm had 729 active clients and 88,835 employees, of whom 84,132 were software professionals.[2] By industry, financial services gave 34.7 percent, consumer 26.4 percent, production (manufacturing and resources) 19.9 percent and technology services 19.0 percent.[2] LTM does not publish the split between fixed-price and hourly-billed work in the release we read, so that figure is N/A here.[2]

The firm earns money by selling people's time and, increasingly, packaged tools. Its own AI platform, called BlueVerse, now sits inside client projects. AI work was 13.4 percent of revenue in the September quarter. It grew 40 percent in a year.[3] Management says AI deals are shorter and start earning faster than the old multi-year outsourcing contracts. They show up as smaller order numbers.[3] Order inflow is the value of new contracts signed. It was $1.26 billion in the quarter, against $1.59 billion a year earlier.[2] A new five-year plan called Lakshya 2031 aims to roughly double revenue from the FY26 level. It also aims to lift the EBIT margin by about 200 basis points.[15] EBIT is operating profit after depreciation. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point.

Two events are shaping the story now. First, the rupee. The rupee closed the September quarter at 95.83 to the dollar, against 88.79 a year earlier. So revenue in rupees grew 13.6 percent while revenue in dollars grew 4.7 percent.[2] Per-share profit in rupees therefore flatters the real growth. Second, an acquisition. LTM has agreed to buy Randstad's technology and consulting business in Europe and Australia. That business had 2025 revenue of €469 million and an enterprise value of up to €160 million. Completion is expected by the third quarter of FY27.[17][18] The European Commission cleared the deal on 14 August 2026. Management said in October that the remaining approvals are on track.[20][3]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has moved lately; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical size of one day's price swing, used to measure distance from support and to set a stop loss, which is a pre-set price at which a holding is sold to limit a loss.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before those four items. Operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue, gross margin is revenue minus direct costs as a share of revenue, and net margin is final profit as a share of revenue.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares, and the price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by EPS, which shows how many years of current profit the price pays for.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is pre-tax operating profit as a share of the money used in the business, so it shows how well each rupee of capital works.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow, which is cash from operations less spending on plant and equipment. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before financing. Capital expenditure is spending on buildings and equipment, and working capital is the money tied up in unpaid bills and other short-term items.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money, and net debt is borrowings minus cash.
  • Guidance is a company's own forecast for the coming period, and a consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow the stock. Book-to-bill is new orders divided by revenue billed in the same period; above 1 means the order book is growing.
  • A 20-day moving average, a 50-day moving average and a 200-day moving average are the average closing prices of the last 20, 50 and 200 sessions; a rising 200-day line marks a long uptrend.
  • Stage 2 is the rising markup phase where the price is above rising averages, and Stage 4 is the falling markdown phase where it sits below falling averages. A base is a flat resting period after a fall, a pivot is the price level that ends it, a breakout is a close above that level, and a retest is a pullback that tests the level from above.
  • An entry zone is the price range in which we would buy, and a limit order is an order to buy no higher than a stated price. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to our target divided by the loss to our stop: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business rests on four facts. First, scale and parentage: LTM is the sixth-largest IT services firm in India, and it is a subsidiary of Larsen & Toubro, which Crisil calls strategically important to the parent.[6] Second, balance-sheet strength: Crisil reaffirmed its AAA rating with a stable outlook in June 2026, and noted net worth of ₹22,809 crore against lease liabilities of ₹2,310 crore as the only debt.[6] Third, client breadth: 729 active clients, three clients above $100 million a year, and a top-five share of revenue that fell from 25.3 percent a year ago to 22.9 percent.[2] Fourth, record contract wins in FY26: order inflow of $6.6 billion, up 10.3 percent, with six deals above $100 million.[8][5]

The weaknesses are just as concrete. Growth is modest. Revenue in dollars rose 5.3 percent at constant currency in FY26 and 5.4 percent in the first half of FY27, and the second quarter rose only 1.1 percent on the first.[5][3] Rivals are growing faster or earning more per sale. Persistent grew revenue 20.0 percent in dollars in the same quarter and Coforge 40.1 percent including an acquisition, while HCLTech earned an EBIT margin of 18.2 percent and Infosys 21.0 percent against LTM's 15.6 percent.[35][34][37][38][2] North America gave over 70 percent of revenue every year, which Crisil flags as a concentration risk.[6] Order inflow of $1.26 billion in the second quarter was 21 percent below a year earlier, and our own sum gives a book-to-bill of about 1.02 times ($1.26 billion of orders divided by $1.235 billion of revenue).[2][24]

Two outside shocks hit the latest quarter. A war in the Middle East cut Saudi revenue sharply, which management says cost about 0.6 to 0.7 points of sequential growth, and a seasonal renewal at the top technology client held back that segment, which fell 4.1 percent in dollars.[3][2] The wider industry is also under pricing pressure. Infosys cut its FY27 revenue-growth outlook to 1-2 percent from 1.5-3 percent, citing softer volumes, pricing pressure and clients' higher productivity demands, and HCLTech narrowed its range to 2.5-4 percent.[38][37] For LTM, the practical meaning is that its 5 percent growth is above Infosys and HCLTech but far below the mid-sized rivals, and its margin is below all of the large peers.

The cash side is solid over several years but was weak in one quarter. Cash from operations was ₹5,422 million in the September quarter against profit of ₹13,799 million, a 39 percent ratio, because of gratuity funding after the labour-code charge, advance tax and tax on the final dividend.[2][3] The CFO said most of the gratuity funding is done and the ratio should return towards normal in Q3, though not fully.[3] We treat this as timing, not as a loss of quality, but it is a number to watch at the next result.

Is there a moat?

A moat is a lasting edge that lets a company keep its profit against rivals. LTM's edge is real but narrow. It comes from the L&T name, which opens doors with industrial and financial clients, from domain depth in financial services and manufacturing, and from scale that lets it bid for deals of $100 million or more.[6][2] It does not come from lock-in on a product or a network. Clients can move work to another vendor, and the AI shift lowers the hours needed for the same task, so price pressure applies to LTM as it does to every rival.[38][8] The CEO himself says short-tenure AI deals are replacing some long outsourcing contracts, which makes revenue visibility lower than before.[3] We therefore rate the moat as narrow-to-moderate, and we price it that way in the targets of Chapter 5.

What the Randstad deal adds and costs

The Randstad business is large in headcount and small in price. It has about 2,900 billable staff and 2025 revenue of €469 million, and LTM is paying up to €160 million in cash, which is about 0.34 times that revenue.[19][18] The cheap price reflects a shrinking business: its own filing shows revenue of €609 million in 2023, €541 million in 2024 and €469 million in 2025, a fall of 23 percent in two years.[17] LTM expects onsite gross margin of 19-20 percent in the acquired business, against its own consolidated 28.0 percent, and says there will be no material margin effect in FY27 and improvement from year two.[15][2] The deal also brings a five-year IT services contract with Randstad's India centre and a talent-supply partnership.[17] Our reading is that it adds scale and European access at a low price, and it carries integration risk that will not show up until FY28.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management credibility matters because the verdict depends on its outlook for the second half of FY27. We checked seven promises. The record is mixed. Near-term promises, such as cost savings and the Randstad timetable, have been kept so far. Long-range targets, such as a 17 percent margin and $10 billion of revenue, have been pushed out more than once.[29][30][31][15]

The margin target is the clearest case. In January 2024 the then-CEO said the plan for a 17 percent EBIT margin by the March 2024 quarter was being deferred, and in November 2024 the company again named 17-18 percent EBIT and $10 billion of revenue as its aim.[29][31] Actual EBIT margin was 14.5 percent in FY25 and 15.4 percent in FY26.[33][5] The revenue goal moved too: in June 2024 the CEO said $10 billion would come before 2030, an analyst note from November 2024 recorded it as organic by FY32, and the 2026 plan says roughly double the FY26 level by FY31, which is about $9.5 billion from $4.76 billion.[30][32][15][8]

Weakest assumption in the profit estimate: that growth speeds up in H2

Management says H2 FY27 will be better than H1 and that Q3 and Q4 will each beat Q1.[3] The evidence so far is thin. Weakest assumption in the multiple: that AI is margin-neutral and revenue-neutral

AI work earns a higher gross margin than traditional services, on management's account, and AI rate cards are priced above traditional ones, but AI deals also run only six to twelve months.[3][25] An analyst on the call quoted a top banking client as saying its technology-vendor spending is down 30 percent a year because of AI and in-house work.
