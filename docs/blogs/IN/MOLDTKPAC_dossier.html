Before you read

Mold-Tek Packaging makes plastic tubs, pails and bottles for paint, lubricant, food and drug companies in India. Our verdict is REJECTED for now, because the return on capital that made it look like a quality compounder is 13 percent, not the 22 percent in the screening seed, and only one of the four improvement signals the rules ask for is clearly positive[15][16][35]. The business itself is sound: debt is low, cash comes in, and profit per kilogram is rising[11][3]. The price of ₹292.30 is 25.6 times profit, cheaper than the stock's own history[33][15]. Results arrive on 2 November 2026, and two improvement signals in them would reopen the case, while a close above ₹318.51 would clear the chart test but not change the verdict on its own[13].

1. What the company does

Mold-Tek Packaging turns plastic granules into containers. Melted polymer is pushed into a steel mould, cools, and comes out as a pail, tub, bottle or cap. The pails hold paint and engine oil, the tubs hold ice cream, edible oil and spreads, and a small but fast-growing line of bottles and caps holds tablets and capsules[1]. The company was founded in 1985, listed in 1993, and runs ten plants across India with installed capacity of 66,900 tonnes a year in the July 2026 deck (the annual report says 63,000 tonnes a year at March 2026)[11][3][1]. Its best-known skill is in-mould labelling (IML), a process in which a printed film label is placed in the mould so that it fuses with the container wall. There is no sticker to peel and no screen printing, and the picture is sharper. IML made 75.8 percent of the tonnage and 77.8 percent of the sales value in the June 2026 quarter[4].

The customers are paint, lubricant, food and drug companies. The July 2026 deck names Asian Paints, Castrol, Shell, Mondelez and Hindustan Unilever, and the rating agency adds Grasim Industries, the paints arm of the Aditya Birla group, Gulf Oil Lubricants and several drug makers[3][11]. Paints are the biggest block. ICRA puts paints at about 44 percent of FY26 revenue and 46 percent in the June 2026 quarter, and the ten largest customers together bought about 62 percent of FY26 revenue[11]. A reader should hold on to that last number. When a company sells most of its output to ten buyers, one lost contract matters, and Mold-Tek lost one in the lubricant line in 2025 (BPCL, about ₹14 to ₹15 crore of yearly sales)[5].

How the money is made, and why revenue and volume can part ways

The company buys polymer from refiners, chiefly Reliance Industries, moulds it, labels it and sells it by the kilogram. The polymer price moves with crude oil, and the company passes the move on to customers with a lag of a month to a quarter[11]. That is why revenue and tonnage can part ways. In the June 2026 quarter revenue rose 24.9 percent to ₹300.45 crore, but tonnage rose only 6.25 percent, to 12,089 tonnes[3]. The gap is mostly resin: management says the polymer price jumped from about ₹105 to ₹110 a kilogram to almost ₹155 after the West Asia conflict began[4].

The correct profit gauge is therefore EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, which means operating profit before financing and accounting charges) per kilogram, not the percentage margin. EBITDA rose 19.1 percent to ₹56.43 crore, and EBITDA per kilogram rose to ₹46.68 from ₹41.64, while the percentage margin slipped to 18.78 percent from 19.70 percent because the same rupee profit sat on a bigger, resin-inflated revenue[3]. A falling percentage with a rising per-kilogram figure is a pass-through effect, not a squeeze. It becomes a squeeze only if customers refuse a future price rise, and nothing in the quarter says they did.

Three changes in two years that explain today's numbers

First, the company closed three Hyderabad plants (Units 2, 4 and 6) and a Hosur warehouse and moved their output to Units 1 and 10, taking the Hyderabad count from five to two. Management and the rating agency credit this with the jump in profit per kilogram, and the annual report says the full benefit starts in FY27[1][11]. Second, it entered pharma packaging in June 2024. Pharma sales were ₹35 crore in FY26 against ₹11 crore a year earlier, and management targets ₹50 to ₹55 crore in FY27[11][1][4]. Third, it spent heavily on new plants, mainly for paints and food. The annual report says about ₹500 crore went into expansion over four years, which is more than the company earned in profit over that time[1][2].

That heavy spending explains the one number in the seed that was most wrong. A plant takes two to three years to fill, so for a while the capital is on the books and the profit is not yet there. Return on capital fell from the mid-20s a decade ago to the low teens, and Chapter 3 shows the path in full[15][1].

Who owns the company, and the October 2026 bonus issue

The founding families hold 33.17 percent, and only 1.76 percent of that holding is pledged to lenders[14]. Mutual funds hold 18.89 percent, and foreign funds hold 7.94 percent, down from 16.03 percent in December 2023[14][15]. Foreign selling has been steady for three years, and that is a real part of why the share price has weakened. On 12 October 2026 the company issued one free share for every share held (a 1:1 bonus), allotting 3,32,28,914 shares and taking the total to 6,64,57,828[21]. A bonus issue changes the share count but not the value of the company. Every per-share figure in this report is stated after the bonus, so last year's reported profit of ₹21.93 a share appears as ₹10.96, and the price of ₹292.30 compares with that adjusted history[1][15].

Governance has two soft spots, neither disqualifying. The company is run by members of the promoter families, and at the 21 September 2026 annual meeting about 16 percent of the votes opposed the chairman's pay and about 20.6 percent opposed an independent director's appointment, both resolutions passed, but a sizeable minority of shareholders objected[20]. The votes are read from a scanned scrutinizer's report, so the exact digits carry some risk of misreading, and Gate exceptions says so.

Why the screening seed numbers do not hold

The watchlist seed said return on capital of 22.2 percent, a price-to-profit multiple of 30.5 and an operating-profit margin of 19 percent. The first two came from the consolidated tab of a screening site, and that tab holds no data after March 2022[16]. Its return-on-capital row ends at 22 percent for FY22, so the 22.2 percent is a four-year-old figure. The same site's standalone tab, which runs through June 2026, shows return on capital of 13.3 percent, a multiple of 25.6 and an operating-profit margin of 19 percent[15]. Only the margin survived. The rating agency also works from the standalone statements, so the standalone view is the one to trust[11]. This correction changes the whole underwriting, and the verdict follows from it.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is operating profit after depreciation (earnings before interest and tax) divided by the money the business uses, meaning shareholders' funds plus borrowings. A mill that turns ₹100 into ₹13 a year has a 13 percent ROCE.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares. After the 1:1 bonus, FY26 EPS is ₹10.96.
  • Capital expenditure is spending on plants, machines and moulds that last for years. Mold-Tek bought about ₹135.6 crore of plant and equipment in FY26 (ICRA counts ₹133.1 crore)[1][11].
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company itself owes suppliers. It rises when a business grows or when raw material gets dearer.
  • Guidance is the company's own published forecast, such as 10 to 12 percent volume growth for FY27. Chapter 2B checks past guidance against what happened.
  • Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share, which means the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit. At 25.6, you pay ₹25.60 for ₹1 of profit.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' funds. Mold-Tek's is 0.32, so for every ₹1 of owners' money the company owes lenders 32 paise.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) means the cash left after paying for the machines that keep the business running and growing. It is negative here, because spending on plants has exceeded the cash from operations.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself brought in during the year before financing and plant spending.
  • Operating margin is operating profit divided by revenue. gross margin is revenue less the cost of materials, divided by revenue. net margin is final profit divided by revenue.
  • Net debt is borrowings less cash. The company holds almost no cash, so its net debt is nearly equal to its borrowings.
  • Consensus estimate is the average of forecasts published by the analysts who follow the stock.
  • Dilution is the fall in each share's claim when more shares are issued for cash. A bonus issue does not do this, because it hands out shares for free and everyone gets the same increase. buyback is the company buying its own shares back, which shrinks the count.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days. The 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average use 50 and 200 days. They are slow lines that price tends to bounce off.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has moved. ATR (average true range) is the usual daily price swing in rupees. Under 30 on the RSI is called oversold.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. A base is a sideways stretch of trading that the price builds before its next move, and a pivot is the top of that stretch.
  • Breakout is the move through the top of a base on strong trading volume. A retest is the pullback that tests that level from above.
  • Stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry (reward-to-risk) is the ratio of possible gain to possible loss: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). A measured move is the height of a base projected above it as a target.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A packaging maker is strong when customers cannot easily switch and when it can keep its profit per kilogram while resin prices swing. Mold-Tek shows both traits in part. The strongest evidence is the June 2026 quarter, when resin jumped by about half and management still raised profit per kilogram by 12 percent, which means the cost was handed to customers faster than it was incurred[4][3]. The rating agency rates the company [ICRA]A+ with a Positive outlook on ₹240 crore of bank lines, and reaffirmed that on 23 September 2026[11][12].

The weakness is capital. The business is a heavy user of money: each new tonne of capacity needs steel moulds, injection machines, labelling lines and buildings, and the return on that money has been falling for a decade. A strong moat would earn more on new capacity than the company's cost of money, and the current 13 percent is only just above it. That is the central tension of this report.

Evidence that the position is real

The company holds four patents and has five more in the pipeline, including a patented design and locking system behind its Q Pack square tubs, and the annual report says copied moulds were seized by the authorities[1]. It makes its own moulds, labels and IML robots, which cuts cost and shortens the time to launch a new shape[11][1]. Customers that tried cheaper alternatives came back: the chairman said clients that had left were returning because Mold-Tek could hold material supply during the war disruption[4]. That claim comes from management and cannot be checked from outside, but the tonnage figures agree with it. Paint tonnage rose 10.8 percent in the June 2026 quarter and food and FMCG tonnage rose 26 percent[3].

Pricing power shows up in the profit per kilogram series. It was ₹37.60 in FY25, ₹40.74 in FY26 and ₹46.68 in the June 2026 quarter[6][3]. The management view is that about ₹3 of the Q1 gain came from the richer mix of pharma and food, and about ₹3 from the Hyderabad consolidation and efficiencies[4]. Both are structural in nature, but the Hyderabad saving is a one-time step. It does not repeat each year, so it should not be extrapolated.

Evidence that the position is thin

The customer list is short and the supplier list shorter. Ten customers bought about 62 percent of FY26 revenue, paints alone were 44 percent, and most polymer comes from one supplier, Reliance Industries[11]. The annual report itself names industry capacity additions as a risk to margins[1]. Losing BPCL in lubricants is the proof of how this works: a state buyer buys on lowest price, Mold-Tek would not match it, and about ₹14 to ₹15 crore of sales went[5]. Lubricant tonnage fell 12.99 percent in FY26 and 17 percent in the June 2026 quarter, partly because two private lubricant makers could not get oil feedstock after the war began[1][4].

Customers also have a way to say no. In Q Pack, the square tubs sold to edible oil and chemical makers, tonnage grew only 2 percent in the June 2026 quarter because customers moved back to tin or other packaging when polymer jumped from about ₹100 to ₹150 a kilogram[4]. A packaging maker with true pricing power would not see that. It is a reminder that the pass-through works best with the largest, stickiest accounts, and less well with smaller buyers who can switch.

Capacity use: the quiet drag on returns

FY26 sales were 42,629 tonnes against installed capacity of 63,000 tonnes (annual report) or 66,900 tonnes (July 2026 deck). That is 64 to 68 percent used, depending on which capacity figure is right[1][3]. Utilisation moves by season: a broker note shows 74 percent in the June 2025 quarter and 63 percent in September 2025[19]. The newest plants run at about 65 percent after sitting in the 50s a year earlier, and management wants 78 to 80 percent overall[5][27].

The consequence is simple. The company already has room to grow tonnage by 10 percent a year without a big new plant, but it plans to add another 10 to 12 percent of capacity every year anyway[4]. If volume growth lags capacity growth, utilisation stays flat and return on capital stays stuck near today's level. This is the main reason the improvement in return on capital is slower than the profit growth.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management talks openly and at length on every call, and it gives numbers: volume targets, profit per kilogram, capital spending and pharma sales. That makes it possible to score what it said against what happened. The record is mixed. Operating promises on mix and cost have been met or beaten. Promises on volume and on spending have not been met, and the spending miss is large[7][5][4][11].

The cleanest miss is capital spending. On 19 May 2025 the chairman said ₹70 crore to ₹80 crore for FY26. By October 2025 a broker note carried about ₹100 crore, and the rating agency counted ₹133.1 crore for the year, which is 66 percent above the top of the original range[7][22][11]. A year earlier the same overshoot occurred: an analyst on that call put FY25 spending at almost ₹140 crore against an earlier ₹70 to ₹80 crore guide[7]. The cash-flow statement shows ₹124.06 crore of plant purchases in FY25 and ₹135.71 crore of total investing outflow once work in progress and advances are added, so the analyst's figure is close to the total and the two numbers are different definitions, not a conflict[1]. Two years of overshoot is a pattern, not an accident.

The best hits are on the growth lines. Pharma sales of ₹34.4 crore beat the 'Rs. 30 plus crores' marker, food and FMCG tonnage grew 22.19 percent against a 15 to 20 percent target, and profit growth of about 20 percent was delivered for FY26[7][5][1]. The profit per kilogram targets show a pattern of cutting, then beating. A FY26 target of ₹41 to ₹42 was lowered to 40-plus in October 2025, then FY26 ended at ₹40.74[7][23][6]. In 2026 the target of ₹42 to ₹43 for FY27 was raised to ₹44 to ₹45 after a ₹46.68 quarter[5][4][27].

Capital allocation tells the same story from another side. FY26 free cash flow (operating cash less plant purchases) was about minus ₹10.3 crore, borrowings rose from ₹181.00 crore to ₹219.44 crore, and the company still paid ₹13.29 crore in dividends and proposed a bonus issue[1]. An investor asked on the February 2026 call whether the dividend should be cut to repay debt; the chairman answered that the payout had already come down and the trend would continue[9]. The bonus issue costs no cash and is neutral for value, but it does not fix the underlying rule: the business keeps spending a little more than it earns while it grows.

Management also says it may sell two of the closed Hyderabad plots 'when a good price comes', which would return some capital[4]. That is an option, not a plan, and we assign it no value. The walk-the-talk score is what it is: strong on cost and mix, weak on capital. We carry that weakness into Layer 1 and Chapter 6.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate above rests on a few assumptions that could fail. This chapter names the weakest ones, says what evidence would show they were wrong, and gives the date by which we would know. It then gives the best honest case against our own verdict, because a rejection of a stock that may rise needs as much scrutiny as an approval.

Weakest assumption 1: tonnage grows about 10 percent a year

Our central case needs 10 percent tonnage growth in both FY27 and FY28. The record is mixed: FY26 grew 11.41 percent against a stated 15 percent, and the June 2026 quarter grew 6.25 percent with lubricants down 17 percent and Q Pack up only 2 percent[1][7][3][4]. Management says lubricants will recover from the July–September quarter and Q Pack will return to double-digit growth, and the new Panipat and Cheyyar capacity is meant to feed both[4]. If lubricants do not return, paints and food must grow faster to compensate, and the company has not yet shown that it can do so in a quarter without festive support. We will know on 2 November 2026 from the September-quarter tonnage, and again with the March 2027 results.

Weakest assumption 2: profit per kilogram holds at ₹44 after the one-time Hyderabad step

About ₹3 of the ₹46.68 came from consolidation and efficiencies, and that is a one-time step; another ₹3 came from the mix of pharma and food[4]. The mix gain lasts only if pharma reaches ₹50 to ₹55 crore of sales and food keeps growing. Pharma earned ₹10.46 crore in the June 2026 quarter, so it needs ₹11 to ₹15 crore a quarter from here[3][4]. A weak September quarter would show it. Profit per kilogram below ₹43 on 2 November, or a pharma figure below ₹10 crore, would count against us. Resin falling sharply is a mild risk too: the pass-through lag means the company can gain on the way up and lose a little on the way down.

Weakest assumption 3: the multiple of 28 times is fair for a 13 percent return on capital

The median multiple since May 2024 is 32.04, but it was earned when return on capital was 19 percent and then 14 percent, and it is now 13 percent[15][34]. If the market settles on 22 to 25 times because returns have fallen, the central value drops to ₹311 to ₹354 and the case loses most of its margin of safety. Two plastics peers earn more on capital and trade at 16 to 17 times[28][29]. This is the assumption we cannot test with one quarter. The only evidence that would settle it is a trailing return on capital that crosses 15 percent, which our model does not reach before FY28.

The next table is Layer 3, which turns a verdict into an entry, a stop and a size. For this name the size is zero, and the levels are for reference if the verdict changes. Four terms in it need plain words. A risk budget is the share of the whole portfolio you accept to lose if the stop is hit, counted in basis points (bps): 1 bp is 0.01 percent, so 50 bps is 0.5 percent. A tier is a conviction class that sets a cap on how large one position may be, and TIER_0 means no capital at all. Portfolio heat is the sum of those risks across every open position, which the rules keep under 12 percent. The long rule cells in the table mean this: the stop goes just below the nearest recent support, not far down at the 200-day line, and after an entry the stop trails under the 21-day exponential average (21-EMA, a faster-reacting average of the last 21 closes) or the 50-day simple average (50-SMA).

What this means for you: do not buy on the strength of the seed's 22 percent return on capital, because it was wrong. If you already own the shares, nothing here forces a sale; the stop of ₹273.85 is the line at which the technical case is void. If you are watching, wait for the results. The company is not a failure and the stock is not expensive. It is a decent business that has not yet shown that the new plants earn their keep.
