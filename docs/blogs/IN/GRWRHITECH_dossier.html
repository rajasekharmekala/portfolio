1. What the company does

Garware Hi-Tech Films Limited, listed on the NSE as GRWRHITECH and on the BSE as 500655, turns petrochemical raw materials into thin plastic films with coatings that block heat, ultraviolet light and scratches. The company was incorporated in 1957 and has about seven decades of experience in polyester film.[17] It is a chips-to-film maker: it buys purified terephthalic acid and mono ethylene glycol, converts them into polyester chips in its own plant, and then draws the chips into film.[20] It runs two manufacturing sites near Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar (Aurangabad), with 66,000 tonnes a year of chip capacity and 42,000 tonnes a year of industrial film capacity.[2]

The money comes from two divisions. The Consumer Product Division made 73% of revenue in the first half of FY27 and sells sun control film (SCF, a tinted film stuck to car and building glass), paint protection film (PPF, a clear film stuck on car paint), safety film and a home-solutions range.[2] The Industrial Product Division made the other 27% and sells shrink film, electrical insulation film, release liners and plain film to packaging and electronics buyers.[2] In the September 2026 quarter, SCF was 51% of sales, PPF was 24% and the industrial division was about 25%.[3] Management quotes profit as EBITDA, which stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is the profit from running the plants before those four items, and the company counts other income inside it.[1][2]

The customers are mostly outside India. Management said 80% of Q2 FY27 sales were exports and 20% were domestic, with the United States at 51%, Europe at 15% and the Middle East and nearby countries at 12%.[3] For the full year FY26, the audited note shows ₹935.82 crore of sales to the United States, ₹525.37 crore to India and ₹658.92 crore to the rest of the world, on total sales of ₹2,120.11 crore.[11] One customer group alone bought about 31% of the group's product sales in FY26, down from about 34% a year earlier.[11] That single fact explains much of the risk story in later chapters.

Here is how a sale actually happens. An applicator (a trained installer) or a distributor buys film rolls, cuts them to a car or a window, and sells the finished job to a car owner or a building owner. Garware has built its own channel on top of this: more than 250 Garware Application Studios in India and 14 studios in the United States and the Middle East for paint protection films, plus 14 Garware Home Solutions studios in India that sell and fit window films directly to home owners.[1][2] The idea is to move from selling rolls to businesses toward selling a branded service to the end customer, which usually earns a higher margin.

Capacity is the other half of the model. Installed capacity is 4,200 lakh square feet (LSF) a year of sun control film and 600 LSF a year of paint protection film.[2] Management said sun control ran at 75% and paint protection at 73% of capacity in Q2 FY27.[3] Two projects add capacity: a ₹118 crore thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) film line of 360 LSF, due in Q3 FY27, and a ₹191 crore sun control line of about 1,200 LSF, due in the first half of FY28.[1][2] TPU is the clear plastic layer inside paint protection film, so making it in-house replaces a part that is bought from outside today.[19]

How the plastic becomes a film, in plain terms

A polyester film starts as a clear plastic sheet. Garware's edge comes from what is added to it afterwards. For sun control film the sheet is dyed, coated with heat-blocking nano-particles (tiny particles that reflect infrared heat) and given an adhesive that management says lasts at least 15 years.[3] For paint protection film the sheet is replaced by a TPU layer. Each layer raises the price per square foot, which is why value-added products were about 86% of H1 FY27 revenue against 87% in FY26.[2]

The raw materials follow crude oil. Management said raw material costs rose about 4% in Q2 FY27 and logistics costs rose ₹9 crore, a combined headwind of about ₹37 crore in the quarter, while crude traded between 100 and 110 dollars a barrel.[3] Management also said the link to crude is only 10% to 12% for sun control and 5% to 7% for paint protection, because most of the value is in coatings, dyes and adhesives, while the industrial division passes costs on to buyers.[3] This is a claim from management and we treat it as such.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A competitive position is the reason customers keep buying from one maker at a good price. For Garware it rests on three things: owning the whole chain from chips to coated film, holding patented coating technology, and having a trained installer network. Management said the company holds 60% to 65% of the Indian sun control film market and about 60% of the Indian paint protection film market, with imports supplying the rest.[3] The company also describes itself as India's only maker of professional-grade paint protection film.[2] These are company statements, but the rating agencies agree with the direction: ICRA credits an IP-backed product range that gives premium pricing and a technology edge over peers.[17]

Pricing shows the strength best. Management said that when it started selling architectural film its prices were only 30% to 40% of the leading global brand, then it added products, built a dedicated marketing team and raised prices to 80% to 85% of the leader, and to 85% to 120% on some products.[3] A maker that can nearly double prices without losing volume has something buyers value. The credit ratings moved the same way over four years: CARE rated the company A+ in July 2023, AA- in July 2024 (reaffirmed in July 2025), and AA in July 2026, while ICRA assigned AA with a stable outlook in July 2026.[16][15][14][13][17]

The weak points are concentration and trade policy. One customer group bought about 31% of product sales in FY26, and the United States took ₹935.82 crore of ₹2,120.11 crore, which is 44% of the year's sales.[11] ICRA notes that the United States and India together made about 70% of FY26 revenue and names higher US tariffs and permeability-related rules for sun control films as risks.[17] The tariff damage was real: the company said a sudden 50% tariff lasted until about 2026-02-20, and FY26 revenue grew only 0.5% after growing 25.8% in FY25.[6][11][10] The US Supreme Court held on 2026-02-20 that the emergency-powers law behind such tariffs does not let the President impose them.[24]

Competition is real but unevenly spread. The economy end of the market is held by Chinese and Korean makers, while US and European brands compete at the top.[20] Garware says it targets the premium end where it ranks in the top three brands in the United States and Europe; that ranking is the company's own claim and we could not verify it independently.[2] The size of the paint protection market is itself uncertain: Grand View Research puts it at US$527.5 million in 2025 growing 4.3% a year to 2033, while Market.us puts it at US$639.7 million growing 6.4% a year.[26][27] Garware's growth therefore has to come from taking share, from India where only about 3% of cars get paint protection film against close to 20% in the United States and China, and from new products.[3]

Where the advantage is real

The first strength is cost and quality control from owning the chain. Garware makes its own chips and many coating inputs, which lets it control defects and delivery. A second strength is balance-sheet freedom: the company has repaid its bank loans, funded about ₹500 crore of capacity in six years from its own cash, and held ₹933 crore of cash and liquid investments at 2026-09-30.[3][1] A third strength is tight working capital, which means the cash tied up in stock and customer bills minus supplier credit. Management put it at 29 days at 2026-09-30.[1] A fourth strength is the retail channel. More than 250 application studios in India and 14 abroad mean the brand reaches the car owner, and the studios are where warranties are honoured.[2]

A fifth strength is the Indian regulatory tailwind. The DGTR recommended an anti-dumping duty on Chinese TPU paint protection film on 2026-06-12, after an application filed on behalf of the domestic industry, and Garware told the exchanges it expects to benefit.[21][22] The recommendation is not yet in force because the Central Government must notify it, and management said the Finance Ministry has not approved it so far.[22][3] If it is notified, cheap Chinese film becomes dearer in India and Garware's domestic paint protection share could rise; if it is not, nothing changes for the numbers in this report.

Where the advantage is thin

The advantage is thinner in four places. First, the industrial division sells commodity film that earns only 10% to 12% at the EBITDA level and rises and falls with Indian supply and demand.[3] Second, paint protection film capacity is only 73% used, so the second line is still filling.[3] Third, the earnings reported in one quarter can include one-off help: management said ₹11 crore of tariff refunds had reached the books by October and another ₹16 crore was expected in Q3.[3] Fourth, a US anti-dumping order on plain PET film from India dates from 2002; requests for a review of Garware were withdrawn on 2025-11-20, so Garware is not under review today, but the order shows how exposed Indian film makers are to US trade actions.[23]

What this means for an owner: the business has a genuine technology and channel edge that shows up in margins, in prices at 80% or more of global leaders and in AA credit ratings. It is not a pure monopoly, it depends heavily on one US customer group, and the refund and tariff story can move reported profit by tens of crores. The next chapter tests whether the specific operating numbers back the edge up, and whether management has done what it said it would do.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means checking what management said one to two years ago against what happened. The record is mixed but mostly honest. In May 2025, management said it was confident of reaching ₹2,500 crore of revenue in FY26 and guided an EBITDA margin of 25% plus or minus 3%.[10] By August 2025, after the 50% US tariff, it declined to give any revenue guidance.[9] In November 2025 it narrowed the FY26 margin range to 22% to 25%.[8] FY26 revenue then came in at ₹2,120 crore.[11] The target was missed by about ₹380 crore, or 15%, for a reason outside management's control, but the same ₹2,500 crore figure has now been moved to FY27.[6][1]

Delivery on plants and capital has been better. The second paint protection line, promised for September 2025, was commissioned in Q2 FY26, the TPU line promised for October 2026 is now described as on track for Q3 FY27, and the company stayed free of bank debt while funding capacity itself.[10][11][6][1] The weaker item is the tariff refund: in August 2026 management expected about ₹50 crore or more to stay with the company, and in October it reported ₹31 crore received, ₹20 crore passed to customers, ₹11 crore kept, and ₹16 crore more expected to be kept in Q3, a total near ₹27 crore.[4][3]

On capital allocation, management has not borrowed and holds ₹933 crore of cash and liquid investments, yet it paid only ₹12 a share of dividend for FY26, about 8% of profit.[1][11][28] A shareholder asked on the October call for a higher dividend, and the answer was that it was noted and that large capital spending is coming.[3] In August, management also said it was evaluating inorganic growth, which means an acquisition; no target has been named.[4] A cash-rich company with an acquisition in mind is a risk that a Layer 2 reading has to flag, and we do.

Red-team bear case: the strongest argument against buying

A skeptic would say the following. First, reported margins flatter the business: the company counts ₹39.25 crore of H1 other income, earned on its treasury, inside EBITDA, and ₹11 crore of tariff refunds are in the books. On a plain operating basis H1 margin is 25.7%, and Screener's trailing margin is 23%.[2][3][28] Second, one customer group is 31% of sales and the United States is 44%. The company has already lived through a year, FY26, in which a single policy change erased growth.[11][6] The Supreme Court ruling helped, but the Court of International Trade refund class excludes importers hit by India tariffs, so refund timing and size are not guaranteed.[25]

Third, the capital cycle is turning. The company is spending ₹118 crore on TPU and ₹191 crore on a sun control line while it says an acquisition is being evaluated.[1][4] A cash-rich company with a promoter family that holds 60.72% and just lost a Joint Managing Director is a case where governance and succession matter more than usual.[18][4] Other small flags: a ₹25 lakh penalty from the Maharashtra pollution board for restarting a boiler during trials, and an income-tax demand of ₹18.37 crore for assessment year 2020-21 that the company says it is seeking to rectify and has not treated as a contingent liability.[3][11] Fourth, valuation: the stock trades at 38.9 times trailing earnings, and its price is up from about ₹2,691 at the 52-week low, so much of the gain is a higher multiple.[28][32]

The bear case does not say the business is poor. It says the price leaves no room for a miss, which is why our expected value is below the price. Our answer is to agree on the facts and wait for a lower price rather than argue the quality.
