Before you read

Sai Life Sciences is a Hyderabad company that does research, development and manufacturing work for drug firms, and it earned ₹2,192 crore of sales and ₹349 crore of profit in FY26.[6][9] It passes every hard safety test we run, with almost no debt, no promoter pledge and strong cash conversion.[1][9][16] Our verdict is WAIT FOR SUPPORT, with no buy order standing, because the ₹1,487.20 price assumes 26 percent profit growth a year for ten years and our central target is ₹1,450.[34] The ₹1,290 to ₹1,305 shelf clears our 2.0 gain-to-loss test only at 70 times earnings and fails at 60 times, so it is a place to check the case again after the 5 November results.[34] We are wrong if profit runs at the street's pace or if the stock closes below ₹1,240.90[19][34].

1. What the company does

Sai Life Sciences does the laboratory and factory work that drug companies hand to outside specialists. Think of a restaurant brand that designs the recipe but pays a contract kitchen to test, refine and cook it at scale. Sai Life is the contract kitchen and the test lab for medicines. Its trade term is contract research, development and manufacturing organisation, shortened to CRDMO, and its work covers small-molecule drugs, the pills and capsules whose active chemical can be written as a precise formula.[2] The company was founded in 1999, has labs in Hyderabad, Boston and Manchester, and runs manufacturing at Bidar in Karnataka.[2]

The business has two arms that feed each other. The first is contract research, which Sai Life calls discovery or CRO. Chemists and biologists design and test molecules for a customer who has a disease target but not enough staff. This arm earned ₹775 crore in FY26, up 24 percent.[7] The second arm is contract development and manufacturing, called CDMO or CMC. It works out how to make a promising molecule reliably, then makes it in plants from lab flasks to reactors of several thousand litres. It earned ₹1,417 crore in FY26, up 33 percent, and is about 65 percent of sales.[7] Discovery customers who succeed become development customers, and development molecules that reach the market become commercial supply. That hand-off is the whole strategy.

Who pays? Mostly large drug companies and, in discovery, young biotech firms. Sai Life says it serves more than 300 clients, including 19 of the top 25 global pharma companies.[2] Large pharma gave 49 percent of FY26 revenue, up from 28 percent in FY22.[8] In FY26 the top customer was 12 percent of revenue, the top five 37 percent and the top ten 54 percent.[8] Customers who came back made up over 90 percent of revenue in both FY25 and FY26.[5] Almost everything is exported. ICRA, the rating agency, puts exports at about 99 percent of revenue, mostly to the United States and Europe, so the rupee price of a dollar matters to profit.[15]

How does it earn money? Discovery work is mostly sold by the scientist-year, so a customer pays a fixed rate for a team of chemists or biologists. Development and manufacturing are sold by project, by batch or by the kilogram. The valuable end is commercial supply, where a drug that has won approval needs the same intermediate every year for a decade. The company reports 33 active commercial molecules and 14 more in late-phase trials, which means a final test with thousands of patients that decides whether a drug is approved.[5] It tracks about 155 earlier programmes, but it counts them as projects rather than molecules because most early drug candidates fail.[5]

Unit economics tell the next part of the story. In FY26 the company kept 73.9 percent of each sales rupee after paying for raw materials, which it calls material margin, up from 72.5 percent a year earlier.[7] After staff, power and other running costs, EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), which is a rough measure of operating profit, was 30.1 percent of sales, up from 25.1 percent.[7] Staff is the biggest cost, at about ₹683 crore of ₹2,192 crore of revenue, so each scientist who is billed well makes the margin better and each idle scientist makes it worse.[9] Plants work the same way: Bidar had about 700 kilolitres of reactor volume, with management aiming for 1,150 kilolitres by the end of FY27, and the first quarter of FY27 ran at 65 percent utilisation.[2][12] That is the cyclical risk in the plain: capacity arrives in steps, while orders arrive in lumps.

How a molecule moves through the company

A typical customer begins in discovery, paying for chemistry, biology and drug-handling studies, and then moves to process research, where chemists find a route to the molecule that is cheap, safe and repeatable. The next step is clinical supply, then late-phase supply and validation batches, then commercial manufacture. Each step needs more regulatory paperwork, so the customer's cost of moving to another supplier rises at every stage. We return to this switching cost in the next chapter.[2]

In the first quarter of FY27 the company said production had begun on three of the four molecules it recently added to its commercial pipeline, and the chief financial officer said the company is probably the main supplier on two of those three. It also said that about 65 percent of its discovery customers now buy integrated services, meaning more than one of chemistry, biology and drug-handling studies.[2][5]

Where the plants and labs sit

The research campus is in Genome Valley, Hyderabad, where a new 100,000 square foot facility opened in the June 2026 quarter and, according to the chief financial officer, is already sold out. Manufacturing is at Bidar. A greenfield site near Hyderabad is planned for peptide manufacturing, expected to run in 2028, at a spend the chief financial officer put below ₹300 crore.[3][5] Boston gives early contact with US biotech customers, and Manchester gives process-development skills that large pharma wants nearby. Management describes both as satellite centres that bring bigger work back to India.[5]

What changed recently

FY26 was a strong year: revenue up 29 percent, EBITDA up 56 percent and profit up 105 percent, from ₹170 crore to ₹349 crore. The company's own headline of 109 percent uses profit before a one-time ₹8 crore charge.[6][9] The first quarter of FY27 was slower. Sales rose 12 percent to ₹554 crore from ₹496 crore a year earlier, profit rose 22 percent to ₹73 crore, but CDMO sales grew only 6 percent[33][35], and compared with the March 2026 quarter sales were down 8 percent and profit down 30 percent.[2][4][5][32] Management says the second half of FY27 will be stronger than the first because new capacity comes online then. That claim is the first thing the 5 November results will test.[5][19][35]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position rests on one idea: a drug company that has trusted Sai Life with a molecule finds it costly to leave. The reasons are practical. A manufacturing route is written into the regulatory file for an approved medicine, so moving it to a new plant means new validation batches, new paperwork and a risk of delay. Customers also build working habits with a team of scientists over years. Sai Life says the average large pharma relationship has lasted more than 11 years, and returning customers made up over 90 percent of revenue in FY25 and FY26.[2][5] Those are the numbers of a supplier that is hard to replace, not of one that wins every contract on price.

Quality evidence supports this. The company reports a 100 percent record in regulatory inspections by the United States Food and Drug Administration (USFDA) and Japan's regulator, PMDA, across its research and manufacturing sites.[2] In the 12 months to September 2025 it passed 35 customer audits and 3 regulatory audits with zero data-integrity deviations and zero critical observations.[13] ICRA, which gave the company an AA (Stable) rating on 26 February 2026, notes that no regulator has taken a material adverse action against it, though future inspections remain a risk.[15] The summary of the September 2026 shareholder meeting also records no qualifications, observations or adverse comments in the auditor's report.[18]

Why customers stay, and why more are arriving

The strongest sign of a widening moat is the move from one-off projects to teams that work only for one customer. Management calls this the dedicated FTE model. The chief financial officer said that over 15 months the company added six late-phase molecules, five of them through large pharma customers with which it already ran FTE contracts, and that one top pharma relationship has grown into an end-to-end deal from discovery to commercial supply.[5] This matters because a molecule won through a team that helped develop it is more likely to stay with Sai Life when it goes commercial. The chief executive said the stated intent of every pharma customer is to leave the programme with Sai Life through the product's life, except when capacity does not match.[5]

A second tailwind is policy. The BIOSECURE Act became United States law on 18 December 2025 as part of the FY2026 defence authorisation act, and it pushes American drug firms to diversify away from certain Chinese suppliers.[22] The company's annual report says the act has reinforced customers' focus on supply-chain diversification and multi-source strategies, and the chief executive said on the August call that the trend has continued and strengthened.[10][5] India's gain is a trend to be watched in orders, not a contract the company has booked, and management has not put a revenue number on it.

Where the economics are strong

Return on capital has risen sharply. The company's own measure, operating profit divided by equity plus borrowings plus lease liabilities, went from 3.2 percent in FY22 to 12.3 percent in FY25 and 18.2 percent in FY26.[7][10] Debt has almost vanished: the IPO in December 2024 raised ₹950 crore of fresh money, ₹910 crore after ₹40 crore of issue expenses, of which ₹720 crore repaid borrowings, and net debt to equity fell to 0.01 by March 2026.[9][7] Interest cover, EBITDA divided by finance cost excluding leases, was 41 times in FY26.[10] A balance sheet this light gives the company room to fund a large capex cycle without strain.

Where the economics are weak

Four weaknesses stand out. First, customers are concentrated: the top ten were 54 percent of FY26 revenue and the largest alone was 12 percent, so one delayed shipment from one customer moves a whole quarter.[8] Second, almost all revenue comes from the United States and Europe, so a slowdown in biotech funding or a policy change in Washington reaches the company quickly. ICRA noted that United States biotech funding fell 53 percent in the first seven months of calendar 2025.[15] Funding has since recovered in 2026, with $23.9 billion across 385 venture rounds so far, but quarterly funding eased to $6.7 billion in the September quarter from $9.9 billion in June.[21]

Third, tariffs. On 2 April 2026 the United States used Section 232 to tax imports of patented medicines and the active ingredients that go into them. The second wave of rates started on 29 September 2026, and the rate depends on the importing customer: 0 percent for firms with an onshoring plan and a pricing agreement, 20 percent with a plan alone, and 100 percent for everyone else.[24][23] Management said in May 2026 that it saw no impact so far and that big pharma were structuring deals to reduce their own tariff cost.[7][8] That comment pre-dates the September step-up, so the November results are the first test.

Fourth, the business needs a lot of capital and is not yet producing free cash flow. FY26 cash from operations was ₹509 crore, but cash spent on plants and equipment was ₹593 crore in the cash-flow statement (the company's own capex figure, which includes some accruals, is ₹633 crore), leaving free cash flow of minus ₹83 crore.[1][9][6] For FY27 the company plans ₹1,100 to ₹1,300 crore of capex, to be funded from internal cash and debt, so free cash flow will be clearly negative for at least a year.[8][5] The chief financial officer said the company's internal hurdle rate for new projects is a little above its target return on capital and return on equity, and that it has slowed spending before when demand slowed.[5]

How peers show the sector is uneven

Sector membership does not guarantee growth. Over the trailing twelve months, Neuland Laboratories' sales rose 78 percent, but Syngene's fell 6 percent and its profit fell 43 percent, and Anthem Biosciences' sales fell 2 percent.[25][27][28] Sai Life's trailing sales grew 18 percent.[1] The lesson is that growth depends on each company's customers and product mix, which is why the next chapter scores the operating measures that matter for this company in particular.

What this means for an owner

An owner gets a business with a real but not absolute moat, strong recent returns on capital, a clean balance sheet and a management that guides conservatively. The owner also accepts customer concentration, lumpy quarters and a capex plan that will absorb all of the cash the business makes for a while. These are manageable if the price is right, which is the subject of the later chapters.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record is judged by lining up what it said a year or two ago against what was reported. Five of the eight items in the table below have a clear result today: three were delivered and two are ahead of plan on the headline number. Two are not yet testable because the date has not arrived, and one is mixed. The first item, capex, shows a team that spends a little less than it plans: it said about ₹700 crore for FY26 in May 2025 and spent ₹633 crore.[14][6][8]

The margin promise is the most important one. In May 2025 the company, which had just reported a 25 percent EBITDA margin for FY25, said it expected to reach a 28 to 30 percent margin in about two years. FY26 came in at 30.1 percent, one year early.[14][36][7] The June 2026 quarter then printed 26.7 percent, below the band, and the chief financial officer said in May that higher input and logistics costs from the Middle East conflict would pressure cost, with customer price revisions that may lag.[32][8] So the promise was kept on the year, and the test now is whether it holds on a rolling basis.

The use of IPO money was also kept. The December 2024 offer carried a fresh issue of ₹950 crore, and the March 2026 results show net proceeds of ₹910 crore after ₹40 crore of issue expenses. Of that, ₹720 crore went to repay borrowings and ₹190 crore to general purposes, and the filing reports no unutilised balance.[9] Management's tone on guidance is conservative: after a 29 percent year it kept the 15 to 20 percent three-to-five-year revenue guide, and the chief financial officer said on the August call that the company does not want to put every last dollar behind the top end.[5][7] That habit makes the guidance easier to trust, and it also tells us the street's higher numbers are the street's own.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter starts with the basis. We use consolidated figures, which add the US and German subsidiaries and a small Indian subsidiary to the parent. Standalone numbers are close: FY26 standalone sales were ₹2,153 crore and profit ₹341 crore, against ₹2,192 crore and ₹349 crore consolidated.[9][1] Our watchlist screen said return on capital employed (ROCE) of 19.6 percent and three-year sales growth of 22 percent a year. Both match Screener's consolidated page, so the screen is verified on that basis.[1] The company's own measure, which divides operating profit by equity plus borrowings plus lease liabilities, gives 18.2 percent for FY26.[10] Our own check, using the balance-sheet figures shown later in this chapter, gives 18.3 percent on year-end capital and 19.3 percent on average capital, so the honest range is 18 to 20 percent.

Look at the five years as a story in three steps. From FY22 to FY23 the company was a ₹1,000 crore business earning almost nothing: profit was ₹6 crore and ₹10 crore, because interest of ₹54 to ₹81 crore and depreciation of about ₹90 to ₹99 crore ate nearly all of operating profit.[1] In FY24 and FY25 sales grew 20 percent and 16 percent and profit rose to ₹83 crore and ₹170 crore. In FY26 sales rose 29 percent to ₹2,192 crore and profit doubled to ₹349 crore, helped by interest costs falling from ₹78 crore to ₹41 crore after the IPO repaid debt.[1][9] So the high growth rate of profit is partly a recovery from a low base and partly a one-time fall in interest. The more durable part is the operating margin, which climbed from 14 percent in FY23 to 29 percent in FY26 on the screen. The company's own measure rose from 25.1 to 30.1 percent in FY26 alone.[1][7]

Cash is where the picture needs care. Cash from operations beat profit in every year, which is a good sign of honest accounting. But the company spends heavily on plants. In FY26 it generated ₹509 crore from operations and spent about ₹593 crore on plants, so free cash flow was minus ₹83 crore.[1][9] Capex was ₹408 crore in FY25, ₹633 crore in FY26 and is planned at ₹1,100 to ₹1,300 crore for FY27.[36][6][8] Working capital, the money tied up in unsold stock and unpaid bills, takes about 105 days of sales on the company's measure, down from 219 days in FY22.[7] Screener's different method gives 70 days for FY26, up from 66 days, which is why we show both rather than choose the one that looks better.[1]

The decision is WAIT FOR SUPPORT, and no buy order stands today. This stock is a candidate, not a holding, so no position exists. It passes every Layer 1 red line and qualifies as a COMPOUNDER. The probability-weighted 12-month price is 5.6 percent below the market. The ₹1,290 to ₹1,305 shelf is a reference zone to test again, not an order to place.[34]

Any purchase needs two fresh underwritings. The first comes after the 5 and 6 November 2026 results. We re-estimate FY27 and FY28 EPS, re-price the bull, base and bear cases and their probabilities, recompute the gain-to-loss ratio, and re-run the momentum gate on the new price snapshot. The second comes at fill time, when the price first trades into the zone. We re-run the gate on that day's data, check that the stock still holds the swing-low and base-low shelf, and look for new company or tariff news. We leave no resting order in the market. A purchase is allowed only when three tests pass together: a ratio of at least 2.0 at 70 times earnings, a ratio of at least 1.0 at 60 times, and a gate pass (Test C or better) on the fill-day data.[19][20][34]

Today's numbers pass the first test and fail the second. At 70 times our FY28 EPS of ₹20.72 the target is ₹1,450 and the ratio at ₹1,305 is 2.26. At 60 times the target is ₹1,243.20 and the ratio is minus 0.96. A ratio of 2.0 at ₹1,305 needs a target of ₹1,433.20, which is 69.2 times ₹20.72, or EPS of ₹23.89 at 60 times. A ratio of 1.0 needs ₹1,369.10, which is 66.1 times ₹20.72, or EPS of ₹22.82 at 60 times. Moving the zone lower does not fix this. At 60 times the target is only ₹2.30 above the stop. No entry within the ₹1,290 to ₹1,305 reference zone passes the 60 times stress. Mathematically only an entry within about ₹1 of the stop would, so no usable entry exists. Only better earnings evidence can pass both tests, and if the November results do not supply it, the verdict moves to REJECTED.

The portfolio uses a support ladder, which grades how close the price is to support in units of average daily swing. Under 0.6 swings is the full-size band, meaning a full-sized position is allowed if the other tests pass. The price is 0.34 swings above the 20-day average of ₹1,469.98, so the chart alone reads full size. But the ladder only says whether the price is near support. It does not say whether the reward is worth the risk. The 20-day average is a soft support that a normal pullback loses first. The structural support is the 63-day base low of ₹1,291.50, which is why the reference zone sits there.[34]

The stop comes from the ledger. The base-low formula is the 63-day low of ₹1,291.50 minus one average swing of ₹50.60. That gives ₹1,240.90. The other formula uses the 20-day swing low of ₹1,360.60 minus ₹50.60, which gives ₹1,310.00. We do not use it. An entry below ₹1,360.60 means the swing low has already broken, which is one more reason the fill-day gate check is required. The distance from ₹1,305 to the stop is ₹64.10, or 4.9 percent of the entry price.[34]

Asymmetry is the potential gain divided by the potential loss. At ₹1,487.20 it is (₹1,450 minus ₹1,487.20) divided by (₹1,487.20 minus ₹1,240.90). That is minus 0.15. At the top of the zone, ₹1,305, it is (₹1,450 minus ₹1,305) divided by (₹1,305 minus ₹1,240.90). That is 145 over 64.10, or 2.26. At the bottom, ₹1,290, it is 3.26. All three use our base target of ₹1,450, which is ₹20.72 times 70. The bull target is ₹1,871 and the bear target is ₹842.

If an authorized order fills, size comes from the risk budget. The ledger's default for this setup is Tier 3 at 3.5 percent of net worth. We override it and cut the starting tier to Tier 4, the lowest-conviction tier, because the setup fails the 60 times test today. Tier 4 allows 35 basis points of net worth to be lost if the stop is hit. A basis point is one hundredth of one percent, so that is 0.35 percent. Dividing 0.35 percent by the 4.9 percent stop distance suggests 7.1 percent of net worth. That is above the Tier 4 cap of 2 percent, so the cap decides and the position starts at 2 percent. If the shares then rise, the let-winners-run rule applies. We do not trim just because the price is overbought or the weight passes 15 percent. We let the position grow up to the 22 percent runaway-winner cap and trail the stop to the rising 21-day average or 50-day average.
