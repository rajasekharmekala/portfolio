1. What the company does

Balu Forge Industries makes parts that other companies build into their machines. Its first and still largest product is the crankshaft, the heavy steel shaft inside an engine that turns the up-and-down push of the pistons into rotation. The company first hammers or presses hot steel into a rough shape, which is called forging. It then cuts the part to exact size on computer-controlled machines, which is called machining. Parts weigh from 1 kg to 1,000 kg and run up to 3 metres long.[2] The firm was founded in 1989 and listed in 2020.[9]

The buyers are equipment makers, known as original equipment manufacturers (OEMs), and spare-parts channels in more than 80 countries. ICRA, the rating agency, reports that more than 90% of revenue comes from outside India, and that the five largest customers gave about 37% of standalone revenue in FY25 and 35% in the first half of FY26.[9] Tractors and other farm machinery are the largest end market at 36% of FY26 revenue. Commercial vehicles are 20%, heavy engineering and industrial machinery 16%, power generation 10% and oil and gas 5%. Defence, aerospace and railways together are 13%, up from 5% in FY24.[5][3]

The unit economics start with steel. Raw material was ₹1,915 million of the ₹3,007 million revenue in Q1 FY27, about 64 paise of each rupee of sales.[3] The company says it passes steel price rises on to customers, and ICRA notes that price pass-on arrangements exist with key customers.[5][9] What is left after running costs is EBITDA, which stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a measure of operating profit before accounting charges. EBITDA was 27.0% of sales in FY26.[4] Depreciation has been small, only ₹10 crore in FY26, because much of the machinery was bought second-hand at auctions and liquidations, including plants from Ursus in Poland, Thyssenkrupp in France and a Mercedes-Benz truck machining unit in Germany.[1][5] That is changing. In FY26 about ₹346 crore of standalone assets moved out of construction into use, and Q1 FY27 depreciation was 96.5% higher than a year earlier.[12][3]

The cash side works differently from the profit side. Customers pay late. Trade receivables, the money customers owe, stood at ₹4,251 million (₹425 crore) at 31 March 2026, which is 140 days of FY26 revenue by our calculation from the audited figures.[5] ICRA records that a few legacy customers get more than 180 days of credit, and that working capital, the cash tied up in day-to-day operations, absorbed 41.0% of sales in FY25 and 44.3% in the first half of FY26.[9] Put simply, each extra rupee of sales locks up about 40 paise of cash before it comes back. That is the central fact about this business, and chapters 3 and 7 return to it.

Sales are cyclical because tractors, trucks and oil-field equipment follow farm incomes, freight demand and energy prices. Q4 FY26 sales fell 2.3% from a year earlier after the Middle East conflict disrupted shipping and hurt the India-UAE operations.[4] ICRA also flags a long-run risk: crankshafts go into combustion engines, and a shift to electric vehicles could make them obsolete, although the shift has been slower in tractors and commercial vehicles.[9] Management's answer is to diversify into artillery shell bodies, heavy gear and wheel forgings, aerospace parts, railway wheels and turbine blades.[5] Those lines need new plant. The 46-acre campus at Hattargi near Belagavi has forging capacity of 100,000+ tonnes a year and machining capacity of 52,000+ tonnes, with targets of 150,000 and 80,000.[3]

  • debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share. A low ratio means each rupee of profit costs less.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Balu Forge's edge is that it owns most of the chain from hot steel to finished part. Forging and machining sit on one campus, which cuts transport and handling and lets the company control quality.[5] A new part takes 2-3 months of research and 3-5 months of product development before an OEM approves it, and the company says the customer relationship then lasts 15 to 20 years.[5] Once a crankshaft or a shell body is approved, a buyer rarely switches for a small price saving. The company says it holds approvals for 180+ products and, in Q3 FY26, was inducted into the NATO supply chain.[5][8] These are the company's own statements, and ICRA's view of the long track record is consistent with them.[9]

The evidence of pricing power is in the margins. Operating margin rose from 9% in FY21 to 27% in FY26 while sales grew from ₹142 crore to ₹1,107 crore.[1] A business with no edge would not lift margins eighteen points while growing almost eight times. Return on capital employed (ROCE) is the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses, and it was 21.7% in FY26.[5] But the margin is no longer rising. It was 27% in both FY25 and FY26, fell to 22.7% in Q4 FY26, and was 28.2% in Q1 FY27 against 31.0% a year earlier.[1][4][2] Interest and depreciation are climbing as new plant starts, so Q1 FY27 profit grew 15.9% while sales grew 29.0%.[2]

Three things weaken the case. First, growth consumes cash. In FY24 to FY26 the company spent ₹904 crore on capital equipment and generated only ₹148 crore from operations, and it raised ₹805 crore from issuing shares to cover the gap.[16] Second, the main product is exposed to the electric-vehicle shift, a point ICRA lists among its rating constraints.[9] Third, FY26 profit was helped by a lower tax rate. Tax was ₹472 million on ₹3,060 million of pre-tax profit (15.4%) in FY26, against ₹501 million on ₹2,539 million (19.7%) in FY25, so profit grew 27.0% while pre-tax profit grew 20.5%.[5]

The quarter just before FY26 ended also needs a careful read. Q4 FY26 profit of ₹657 million was flat on the previous quarter only because other income jumped to ₹235 million from ₹45 million, while operating profit fell from ₹845 million to ₹599 million.[5] Other income is not what the plant produces, and the company does not break out what it was in the sources we read. Strip it out and Q4 operating performance was the weakest in the five quarters shown.

Governance and disclosure flags

The auditor, M. B. Agrawal & Co., gave an unmodified opinion on the FY26 accounts, which means it did not object to how the numbers were put together. It still added two emphasis of matter paragraphs, which are notes that point the reader to a risk without changing the opinion. The first draws attention to ₹339.08 crore of trade receivables, export and domestic, outstanding at year end. It records management's statement that collection of certain export receivables was interrupted by geopolitical and market conditions, and that management considers the amounts recoverable. The second notes that the bank reconciliation includes ₹24.41 crore of customer cheques recorded in the books but not deposited as of the report date.[12] A further note says ₹5.24 crore owed by the wholly owned subsidiary has been outstanding beyond the time the Reserve Bank of India allows for export proceeds.[12]

The receivable figure is large against the size of the parent company. Standalone revenue from goods and services was ₹330.60 crore of exports plus ₹289.87 crore of domestic sales, ₹620.47 crore in all.[12] The parent company earned ₹153.21 crore of the group's ₹258.89 crore profit.[13] ₹339.08 crore of dues on ₹620.47 crore of sales is about 200 days of revenue. The same report gives a reassuring detail: the stock and receivable figures the company gave its banks reconcile with the books for each quarter.[12] That points to slow collection rather than invented sales, but it does not prove the money is collectable. The cash flow data also show a ₹18.1 crore bad-debt provision in FY26 against ₹0.14 crore the year before, so the company is setting aside some money for losses.[16]

Three other facts sit alongside the accounts. First, the Income Tax Department searched offices and plants from 7 to 13 January 2026. The company said no incriminating documents were found or seized and operations were not affected.[11] By 30 May 2026 it had still received no written outcome.[5] ICRA noted the search and said it would keep monitoring.[9] Second, the Enforcement Directorate, Raipur, a government agency, is listed as a holder of 3.86% of the shares at June 2026, down from 4.06% in September 2025.[18][19] The company says the shares reached the agency through a market transaction between shareholders and that it has no direct or indirect link to the parties.[3] We have no information on the underlying case, and none of the sources we read ties the company to an allegation. We treat it as an ownership overhang, not as proof of wrongdoing.[20]

Third, promoters have pledged some shares for the first time in the period we could see. The pledge was zero in every quarter shown from April 2025 to December 2025, then 1.40% of the promoters' holding in the March 2026 quarter and 1.37% (875,000 shares) in June 2026.[17] That is far below our 20% red line. The direction matters more than the size, because the pledge appeared in the period after the stock fell by almost half, from ₹667 on 16 December 2025 to ₹341.05 on 29 January 2026.[23] Promoters' share of the company also fell from 54.39% to 53.51% in the June 2026 quarter, and the founder's own stake fell from 52.58% to 50.52% while his son Jaikaran appears with 2.10%, which looks like a transfer inside the family.[1][18]

Defence: what is contracted and what is hoped for

Defence is the growth story, so it deserves separate scrutiny. Three items are documented. First, an order for 30,000 shells of 152 mm calibre from an Indian energetics company, with supplies from June 2026, and the company says serial production and dispatches have started.[3] Second, a five-year memorandum of understanding (MoU) with a NATO-affiliated buyer, reported as 40,000 shells a month (30,000 of 155 mm and 10,000 of 152 mm) at US$315 each, about US$151.2 million a year, with deliveries from April 2026 and a 20% advance with the rest by irrevocable letter of credit within 30 days of invoice.[30][31] Third, a ₹100 crore work order dated 17 August 2026 for 155 mm extended-range shells, starting with a 10,000-shell pilot batch.[32]

Set those against the plant and the reported numbers. The empty-shell line is rated at 360,000 shells a year, or 30,000 a month, which is below the 40,000 a month the MoU describes, and the company itself says it plans to expand beyond 360,000 from internal accruals.[3][31] Defence, aerospace and railways made up 14% of Q1 FY27 revenue, about ₹42 crore on ₹300.7 crore, and the share has barely moved from 13% in FY26.[3][2] If the MoU were running at its stated rate, one quarter would bring in about US$37.8 million, several times that ₹42 crore. So the MoU is not yet visible in the reported numbers. The payment terms are good news for cash if it does ramp: a 20% advance and a letter of credit are far better than the 140 days the legacy book takes.[31][5]

Three smaller items round out the picture. The company won its first aerospace order, from Alpha Aircraft Systems Inc of the United States.[36] It announced a ring-rolling line that can forge seamless rings up to 6.7 metres across and 18,000 kg each.[35] And its subsidiary Naya Energy Works was renamed Quantum Energetics, with permission to make explosives such as TNT, subject to approvals and licences.[37] None of these shows in revenue yet, and the explosives plan adds a new regulated business with its own capital needs.

Management's record matters here because the investment case depends on promises about future cash and capacity. We compared statements made between May 2025 and May 2026 with what the later filings show. Some promises were kept. The shell line was built and the first defence orders are shipping. The large promises about growth and cash were not kept.

The evidence that would show this wrong is a Q2 profit growth rate below 10%, or an operating margin below 25%. We will know at the Q2 FY27 results in mid-November 2026.

Bull case: receivables collect and the NATO contract ramps

The bull case leans most on the stated shell volumes turning into revenue within twelve months. The reported MoU volume of 40,000 shells a month exceeds the 30,000 a month the line can make, and defence, aerospace and railways were 14% of Q1 FY27 revenue, about ₹42 crore.[3][31] Evidence it is wrong: Q2 revenue in those segments that stays near ₹40-50 crore, or a ₹ order book that does not appear. Evidence it is right: a share of revenue above 20% and a half-year operating cash flow above ₹60 crore. We will know in mid-November 2026 and again in February 2027.
