1. What the company does

Balu Forge Industries turns steel into precision parts. It heats steel billets, shapes them under hydraulic hammers and presses (forging, the shaping of hot metal under pressure), and then cuts them to exact size on computer-controlled machines (machining). The parts range from 1 kilogram to 1,000 kilograms and up to 3 metres in length[5]. The best-known product is the crankshaft, the spinning shaft that turns an engine's up-and-down piston motion into rotation. Other products include brake parts, axle parts, lifting hooks, railway wheels and, more recently, empty artillery shell bodies[3]. The founder, Prehlad Singh Chandock, started a small machining plant in Belgaum in 1989 as a job-work vendor for crankshafts[3]. The business became a listed public company in 2020[14].

The people who pay are vehicle, machinery and defence makers, and most of them are abroad. The company says it supplies 25 global original equipment makers across 80 countries[3]. ICRA, a credit rating agency, wrote in February 2026 that more than 90 percent of revenue comes from exports and that the five largest customers took about 37 percent of standalone revenue in FY25[14]. The end-market split in the June 2026 quarter was agriculture 34 percent, commercial vehicles 20 percent, heavy engineering 17 percent, power 10 percent, defence, aerospace and railways 14 percent, and oil and gas 5 percent[6]. Farm and truck engines are still the largest customers, which matters because those engines are the part of the business most exposed to the shift to electric vehicles[14].

The money is made in the plant. Raw steel is the main cost, and the company has kept an operating margin, the share of sales left after running costs, of about 27 percent: FY26 operating profit before depreciation and interest was ₹2,995 million on sales of ₹11,074 million, a margin of 27.0 percent[3]. Operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax is called EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization). The company bought used forging and machining plants from Mercedes-Benz Truck in Germany, Thyssenkrupp in France and Ursus in Poland, and says its asset turnover is high because second-hand equipment costs less to buy[3][11]. That keeps the capital cost low, but it also means the plant mix is older and more varied than a single new line.

The reported numbers combine the Indian company with subsidiaries. Consolidated FY26 sales were ₹1,107 crore, against ₹647 crore for the Indian company alone[1][2]. The ₹460 crore difference comes from subsidiaries. The filings we read name one operating subsidiary in the same line of business, Safa Otomotiv FZ LLC[16], and the company lists a one-acre unit in Ras Al Khaimah, UAE[3], but they do not split the ₹460 crore by entity. We therefore treat the consolidated statements as the deciding basis for every test in this report, and use the standalone statements only as a cross-check (chapter 3).

The new bets are in defence and aerospace. A line making 360,000 empty large-calibre shells a year was commissioned at the Belgaum campus[6]. In February 2026 the company signed a five-year memorandum of understanding with a NATO-affiliated buyer, covering up to 30,000 shells of 155 mm (M107 type) and 10,000 shells of 152 mm a month, priced against the London Metal Exchange, with an advance and a letter of credit for the balance[23]. A first 30,000-shell order for 152 mm came in June 2026[24], a 10,000-shell pilot for 155 mm ERFB shells in August 2026[25], and a first aerospace order from Alpha Aircraft Systems of the United States, with an advance tooling payment on 25 May 2026[26]. A subsidiary was renamed Quantum Energetics and given permission in its charter to make explosives such as TNT, RDX and HMX, subject to licences[18][3].

Two money ideas run through the rest of this report. The first is working capital, the cash tied up in stock and in bills that customers have not yet paid, less what suppliers are still owed. The company's own chart shows working capital of 140 days in FY26, up from 104 days in FY25[3]. The second is dilution, the fall in each shareholder's slice when new shares can be created: a convertible bond is a loan that can later turn into shares, and the company has 12,13,91,900 shares today[9].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong where it is hard to copy quickly. Balu Forge owns forging and machining in one place, so it can take a steel billet and ship a finished part without handing the work to a second supplier. It bought three used plants in Europe and installed the equipment, which gave it capacity at a lower price than new machines[3][11]. Its customers are spread across many countries, and ICRA found the top five customers took only about 37 percent of standalone revenue in FY25, which is low for a parts maker[14]. The promoters have more than three decades in machined forgings, which both rating agencies note as a strength[14][16].

The business is weak where money moves slowly. Customers pay late: ICRA reports receivables of 158 days at March 2025 and 141 days at September 2025, because a few older customers get credit of more than 180 days and export sales carry longer terms[14]. CRISIL, a second agency, found that about 39 percent of the money owed by customers was more than six months old at March 2025, and that the company's packing credit lines were about 99 percent used over the six months to September 2025[16]. That is why CRISIL moved its outlook on the BBB+ rating to Negative in November 2025, and set a downgrade trigger at debtor days staying above 130[16]. ICRA took a different view and assigned A- with a Stable outlook in February 2026[14][15]. The two agencies disagree, and the CRISIL update of January 2026 said it was still awaiting information from the company[16].

A second weakness is dependence on engine parts. ICRA says a considerable portion of revenue comes from engine components, mainly crankshafts for tractors and commercial vehicles, and that a shift to electric vehicles is a risk[14]. Defence, aerospace and railway work is the planned answer, but it is 14 percent of sales so far[6].

A third weakness is governance flags that are small in rupees but real. The Income Tax Department searched offices and plants from 7 to 13 January 2026, and the company said no incriminating documents were found[17]. The auditor's limited-review report on the June 2026 quarter nevertheless says the department has started block assessment proceedings and that the outcome and any liability cannot yet be determined[7]. The same report draws attention to a ₹22.50 crore purchase of property from the Managing Director, with ₹9.58 crore already paid as an advance, and to ₹5.24 crore of export receivables from a wholly owned subsidiary that have stayed unpaid past the Reserve Bank of India's time limit[7]. None of these items changes the audit opinion, but each is a reason to keep position size small.

Competitors in artillery shells, and what they were paid

The shell business is crowded. A trade article reports that Sunita Tools targets about 3.6 lakh shells of capacity a year by March 2028, Goodluck India already runs about 1.5 lakh, and Balu Forge is scaling toward about 3.5 lakh[28]. Sunita Tools announced a ₹576 crore order for 2,40,000 empty 155 mm M107 shells in February 2026[27]. Dividing the one by the other gives about ₹24,000 a shell; this is our own division, it is another company's contract, and Balu Forge's prices are not disclosed. If Balu Forge earned a similar price, its stated 40,000 shells a month would be about ₹96 crore a month, and the whole 360,000-shell line about ₹864 crore a year. The June 2026 quarter shows nothing like that: defence, aerospace and railways together were 14 percent of ₹301 crore, or about ₹42 crore, which would equal about 17,500 shells at the same price[5][6]. The gap says the MoU volumes were still ramping in the quarter, or that Balu Forge's prices are lower, or both; the filings do not tell us which.

Bharat Forge, the large listed forger, is the other natural comparison. It is bigger, slower growing and, on Screener's figures, earns a lower return on capital (12.6 percent against 22.7 percent for Balu Forge)[30][1]. Chapter 3 sets the two beside four other listed forging and auto-parts names.

What this means for an owner

For an owner, the strengths are real but earned over years: an integrated plant, global customers and strong margins. The weaknesses are about the quality of the profit, not its size. A business that reports ₹259 crore of profit and collects ₹32 crore of operating cash is lending its customers money, and the lenders who fund that gap have started to say so. The shell and aerospace orders could change the mix, but they are early and unpriced in the filings. Those weaknesses also mean the shares are priced as an unproven story. That is acceptable for a small position, but only while the filings keep improving. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in the last chapter has a weak point. This chapter names the weakest assumption in each, shows the effect of changing it alone, and says when we will know. We also set out the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, because the verdict to sell is itself a call that could be wrong.

The weakest assumption in the earnings estimate: a 26 percent margin on 22 percent growth

The earnings build assumes the company holds a 26 percent operating margin while growing sales 22 percent and starting new plants. The margin has ranged from 22.7 to 31.0 percent in the last five quarters[1][5], and the June 2026 quarter was 2.8 points lower than a year before. New capacity usually carries start-up costs, and the company itself warns that input prices are volatile[3]. If the margin were 24 percent instead of 26, FY27 earnings per share would be about ₹1.8 lower, and if the lower margin persisted into FY28 the central value would fall by about ₹39 at the same multiple. We will know from the September-quarter margin, and a figure under 25 percent would tell us the earnings build is too high. The margin matters more than sales growth here because each point of margin is worth about ₹0.9 of earnings per share.

The weakest assumption in the target: a multiple of 21.4 on earnings the market doubts

The 21.4 multiple is the one-year median. It was earned when the story was growth, before the cash question was widely asked. The stock fell from ₹667 in December 2025 to ₹341.05 in January 2026 and in the last month has traded between ₹417.20 and ₹529.00[36][22]. If the multiple stays at today's 18.6, the central value is ₹445, only 5.5 percent above the close, and at the 2-year low of 16.3 it is about ₹390, 7.6 percent below it, even if the earnings forecast is fully met. The ₹512 target is therefore a conditional valuation and not an expected return. The multiple will rerate only when cash conversion changes, which is why chapter 7 uses half-year cash conversion as its upgrade test. A multiple is a statement about trust, and the market's trust has to be rebuilt by filings, not by announcements. Until then we would not pay more than the current 18.6 times, and we would not count on getting 21.4. This is also why the central value, though above the close, is not enough on its own to justify adding.

The weakest assumption in the reverse DCF: that profit is cash

The low implied growth of 6 percent assumes the reported profit is real money. Three things could make it less so. First, other income of ₹23.5 crore in one quarter, which is not repeatable. Second, receivables: customers owed ₹4,251 million at March 2026 against ₹3,273 million a year earlier, up 30 percent on sales up 20 percent[3]. Third, the auditor's note on ₹5.24 crore of export receivables from a subsidiary that are past the Reserve Bank of India's time limit[7]. We do not suggest the profit is false. We say the filed cash flow does not yet confirm it. The next evidence is the half-year cash flow statement, which companies publish with their September-quarter results. If it shows cash from operations well above the first-half FY26 level of ₹404 million, the worry eases quickly[11].

The red-team bear case, and the case for keeping the shares

The strongest bear case is a sequence. Customers stretch payment further, the packing credit lines, already about 99 percent used in 2025, run out of room[16], and the company turns to the convertible bond that shareholders approved on 4 September with 99.82 percent of votes in favour[9]. The terms (conversion price, coupon, size actually raised) are not disclosed, so dilution cannot be sized[7][8][29]. The same meeting approved a ₹1,000 crore borrowing limit and the right to create charges on the company's assets[10]. Meanwhile the tax block assessment could produce a liability[7], the ICRA and CRISIL views stay split, and a falling market takes the stock toward the January low of ₹341.05 or below. That path produces the ₹247 bear value.
