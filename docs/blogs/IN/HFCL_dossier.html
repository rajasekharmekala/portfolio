1. What the company does

HFCL Limited is an Indian technology manufacturer based in Gurugram. It started as a telecom equipment maker and still bills itself as the number one optical fibre cable supplier in India.[1][14] Optical fibre is a strand of glass thinner than a hair that carries data as pulses of light. A cable bundles many strands inside a protective jacket. A modern AI data centre needs far more strands than an older one, because thousands of computers must talk to each other at once. That is the reason HFCL's sales have jumped, and the reason this report spends so much time on it.[10]

The company earns money in four ways. It makes optical fibre and cable and sells it by the fibre-kilometre. It makes connectivity products such as patch panels and cabinets that sit inside data centres. It makes telecom and networking equipment, for example Wi-Fi access points, radio links and routers. And it makes defence electronics such as thermal weapon sights, electronic fuzes and radars.[1] A fifth, older business builds networks for others on turnkey contracts: the customer pays as each milestone is verified. That business includes the government's BharatNet rural fibre programme and an operations-and-maintenance contract for the army.[3]

The people who pay are of three kinds. The first is hyperscalers, the very large cloud and AI companies. Management says about 70% of its export fibre orders come from them and that their names are under non-disclosure agreements.[3] The second is telephone operators in India and abroad. The third is governments and defence forces. In the quarter to September 2026 exports were 63% of revenue, private customers 92%, and products 91%.[1] Nuvama says the company has pivoted from turnkey contracts to telecom and networking products: 83% of Q1 FY27 revenue against 27% in FY21.[33] Turnkey work ties up cash for months before it is billed, so the shift to products matters because products are billed faster.

The cycle matters more than the story. In FY25 revenue fell 9% to ₹4,065 crore, the fourth quarter showed an operating profit margin of minus 2.79% and a net profit margin of minus 10.40%.[7][14] By FY26 revenue was ₹4,949.27 crore, up 21.77%, and in Q2 FY27 it was ₹2,541.79 crore.[8][1] CARE says fibre makers faced oversupply and weak prices through much of 2024 and 2025, and demand recovered in FY26.[10] A company that swings from a loss to a ₹394 crore quarter in about 18 months is a company whose earnings can swing back. 2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position of HFCL rests on three things. It makes its own optical fibre. It runs its fibre lines flat out. And it has qualified with customers who are slow to switch supplier. Fibre capacity doubled from 14 million fibre-kilometres in FY25 to 28 million in FY26, which CARE says now lets HFCL meet all of its own fibre needs.[10] Management says fibre runs at 100% of capacity in value terms and that the data-centre cable lines run around the clock.[3] The plan is to reach 43.1 million fibre-kilometres of fibre and 62 million of cable by July 2028.[4] The order book has grown from ₹21,206 crore at March 2026 to ₹26,665 crore in June and ₹28,911 crore in September.[1][32] These are real strengths. They are strengths of execution and scale, not of a patent that others cannot copy.

The weak side of the position is that fibre is close to a commodity, so price decides profit. Management said blended prices rose about 20% in the second quarter and that standard telecom fibre sells for roughly US$15 while the thinner 180-micron data-centre fibre sells for US$25 to US$27 (the transcript does not state the unit).[3] A rise of that size in one quarter is a market squeeze, not a steady improvement in the product. The same call says margin “depends upon kind of customers, kind of cable, kind of solutions”, so the product mix and the buyer decide it.[3]

Pricing power and the raw material problem

Preform is the glass rod from which fibre is drawn, and it is now the scarce item. Management said open-market preform prices have gone up by 600% to about US$300 a kilogram and called that level insane.[3] HFCL has not paid that price, because it buys under fixed supply contracts of one year, eighteen months or two years. When those contracts end, costs reset. Customer contracts carry price-reset clauses each year, while contracts of four to six months do not.[3] The fix is the company's own preform plant. It is planned at 600 tonnes a year, to be built by October 2028, and management says it will cover only about half of HFCL's need.[4][3]

What this means for an owner is that today's margin is partly borrowed from a shortage. If the shortage eases, fibre prices fall and the margin follows. HFCL gets cost relief on preform from late 2028, or from early 2027 if the interim solution that management mentions but will not describe works.[3] The rating agency makes the same point: it names raw-material price swings and intense competition as weaknesses, and says it will watch whether the pricing environment lasts.[10] The next subsection looks at who competes with HFCL for these orders.

Competition, including Chinese fibre

CARE says HFCL faces established global competitors, which limits its freedom to set prices in export markets.[10] On the Q2 call an analyst pointed out that global makers had announced capacity increases of 40% to 50%. Management replied that preform prices will have to fall once capacity grows, but that HFCL would not be hurt because its contracts are already fixed.[3] That answer is about cost, not about selling price, and more fibre supply usually pushes selling prices down as well.

On China specifically, the evidence we could fetch is narrow. China's Ministry of Commerce renewed anti-dumping duties of 7.4% to 30.6% on single-mode fibre from India for five more years from 14 August 2026.[29] The named Indian producers are Sterlite Technologies (7.4%), Birla Furukawa (11.4%), Corning India (24.5%), Aksh Optifibre and Finolex Cables (30.6%), and all others pay 24.5%. HFCL is not named.[30] That tells us China is a hard place for Indian fibre makers to sell. It does not tell us how Chinese suppliers compete with HFCL in the United States and Europe, where HFCL's hyperscaler orders sit. No fetched source says so, so we record Chinese competition as a risk we cannot size, not one we have measured.

Demand: AI data centres, 5G and BharatNet

CARE says AI-focused data centres need substantially more fibre per building than older ones and lists AI data centres, cloud, home fibre and 5G as the drivers of the recovery.[10] Management estimates data centres at 65% to 75% of its telecom-products revenue and telecom operators at 25% to 35%.[3] It puts India's data-centre capacity at about 1.6 gigawatts today and expects 6 gigawatts or more by 2030, against about 130 gigawatts already in the United States, heading to 300.[3] So the demand that fills the order book is American, and Indian data centres are a later opportunity.

Telecom demand is the quieter story. Management said operators stopped buying fibre about two years ago because prices rose so much, and that some are now returning with concrete enquiries.[3] BharatNet, the government's rural fibre programme, is a cost today: the turnkey business booked expenses for BharatNet work in Q1 but could not bill them until milestones were verified, and management expects the turnkey loss to shrink to a combined ₹75 to ₹100 crore across the next two quarters.[3] The telecom and BharatNet orders therefore matter for volume, while the data-centre orders matter for profit.

How wide is the moat?

We judge the moat narrow. Return on capital employed was 10.8% on the latest trailing numbers and only 8% in FY25, which is low for a business with a deep moat.[14] The company lost money in the fourth quarter of FY25 when demand for fibre cable fell.[7] What HFCL has that others lack is capacity that is already built, long qualification cycles with large customers, and a preform plant that is approved, with part of its cost covered by promoter warrant money.[4][10] Taken together the position is strong now and cyclical underneath. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of numbers tell a plain story. Revenue was ₹4,727 crore in FY22 and ₹4,949 crore in FY26, which is almost no growth in four years. In the twelve months to September 2026 it was ₹7,492 crore.[14] Profit moved from ₹326 crore to ₹329 crore, then to ₹927 crore on a trailing basis. The five-year growth rate that Screener prints is 2% for sales and the trailing rate is 101%.[14] Neither says much. The price is based on the last two quarters, and the older years show what the business looked like before. Every figure in this chapter is stored in the run's research record.[36]

The cash line is the weak spot. Cash from operations was positive in FY22, FY23 and FY25 and negative in FY24 and FY26. Added up over FY24 to FY26 it was minus ₹27 crore against ₹840 crore of profit.[14][9] The reason is stock and unpaid bills. Inventory days went from 98 in FY22 to 191 in FY26, and the cash conversion cycle, the days between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, stretched to 218 days.[14] The first half of FY27 is the first sign of relief, with cash from operations of ₹455.27 crore.[2]

Debt is moderate. Borrowings were ₹1,869 crore against equity of about ₹5,649 crore at September 2026, a ratio of 0.33 on Screener's figures, and 0.32 on the company's own balance sheet.[14][1] CARE upgraded the rating to A with a positive outlook in July 2026 and says gearing was 0.41x at March 2026.[10] Interest cost rose 72% from ₹147 crore in FY24 to ₹253 crore trailing, which is why interest cover is worth watching even though it is comfortable today.

Ownership has changed. Promoters held 37.69% in March 2024, 34.37% in March 2025 and 28.29% since December 2025, while foreign institutions rose from 7.08% to 17.24% in the two quarters to September 2026.[14] Part of the fall is dilution: the share count rose from about 144 crore to 153 crore with the ₹550 crore QIP, which on its own lowers 34.37% to about 32.4%.[14][2] The rest suggests the promoters also sold. The June 2026 filing shows 433,044,594 promoter shares, while 34.37% of about 144 crore shares is about 49.6 crore, a difference of roughly 6.3 crore shares (our calculation).[11][14] The filings we fetched do not say why. Separately, 7.5 crore warrants were allotted to the promoter group at ₹74 each, with 25% paid up front.[9][2]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, the evidence that would show it wrong, and when that evidence arrives. We also give the red-team case, which is the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion.

The red-team case in one paragraph: HFCL's profit rose 5.5 times in a year because fibre prices rose and its hyperscaler contracts started to deliver. Both are cyclical. The stock has gained about 221% in a year, promoters hold only 28% of the company, the operating cash flow of the last three full years was negative, and management has told investors it is conservative at the very moment the stock is priced for the optimistic case. If prices turn, earnings could stall while the multiple falls to 16 times, which gives a value near ₹96, and the 200-day average of ₹156.18 would be the first level to test.[14][11][34] The same case also attacks the buy zone. Weakest assumption of the base case: the margin holds

The base case needs EBITDA margins in the low 20s. The margin was 16.70% in FY26 and 25.89% in the latest quarter.[8][1] Management says the rise came from volume and price together and that there are no diamonds falling from the sky, meaning no one-off high-margin orders.[3] The risk is that the price part reverses. Management expects preform prices to fall once capacity grows, and analysts noted that global makers plan capacity increases of 40% to 50%.[3] CARE's negative rating trigger is an operating margin below 12%.[10]

We would see this assumption fail first in the price of the fibre sold, then in the margin. The evidence is visible each quarter: blended realisation, the share of data-centre cable, and the EBITDA margin. The next dated check is the Q3 FY27 result, expected on 2 February 2027 per a third-party calendar.[28]

Customer concentration and the hyperscaler contract

One supply contract of about US$1.1 billion, or ₹10,159 crore, over five years is 35.1% of the order book. Minimum quantities run from 2026 to 2028 and extend to 2030.[22][1] A second contract of US$244 million (₹2,329 crore) runs from 2027 to 2029.[23] The buyers are not named. Management says about 70% of export orders come from hyperscalers and that the names are under NDA.[3] CARE expects the large contract to fill over 50% of fibre-cable capacity.[10]

If one hyperscaler slows its building, HFCL has little room to place the freed capacity elsewhere in the short run. On the Q2 call an analyst asked about rising US interest rates slowing data-centre building. Management replied that data centres are a necessity and that Europe, Asia and telecom buyers would offset any slowdown.[3] That is an assertion; no signed order from those buyers has been shown yet. The cost of being wrong is a fall in the most profitable part of the order book.

Cash, working capital and funding

The company has not converted profit into cash over three years, and the exemption in our rules for fast-growing manufacturers is why that does not reject the idea. The test is whether H1 FY27's ₹455.27 crore of operating cash flow repeats in H2.[2] If receivables and inventory grow again with sales, cash will not arrive and borrowings will have to rise to pay for the ₹1,800 crore plan.[4] CARE's positive trigger is a collection period under 150 days; its negative trigger includes slower-than-expected collection of debtors.[10]

The estimate in Chapter 3 shows the plan is fundable from identified sources if the H1 pace holds, with a gap of about ₹127 crore. The gap is about ₹1,040 crore if cash from operations halves, and ₹667 to ₹847 crore on CARE's earlier forecast of accruals.[2][9][10] The annual report also names receivables as a key audit matter because of concentrated credit exposure to major customers, predominantly government and large organisations.[9]

Promoters, warrants and the non-disposal undertaking

Promoter pledge is only 2.08% of promoter shares and passes our hard line.[12][13] The June 2026 filing shows a further 55.78% of promoter shares under a non-disposal undertaking, so 57.86% of promoter shares are encumbered in total.[11] The hard line counts pledges, where a lender can sell the shares. An undertaking only restricts the promoter, and the filing does not say what it secures, so we treat it as a watch item and not as a pledge.

Two more facts need weighing. First, 7.5 crore warrants were allotted to the promoter group at ₹74 each, a price that is 70% below the ₹250.11 close, and the balance of ₹55.50 a warrant (₹416.25 crore) is due within 18 months of May 2026.[9][2] Giving promoters shares at ₹74 when the market price is ₹250 is legal if the pricing formula was followed, but it is a value transfer to the promoters of about ₹1,320 crore at today's price (7.5 crore × (₹250.11 − ₹74)), and it dilutes other holders by about 4.7%. Second, the promoter holding fell from 37.69% to 28.29% in two and a half years.[14] An aggregator shows a pledge of 49.72% of promoter shares in March 2025 and 1.10% by June 2025; the filings we fetched do not explain the change.[13] None of this breaks a red line. It is a governance watch item, and a reason to size small.

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT for a TACTICAL_MOMENTUM candidate, and it is stricter than a standing buy order: there is no order today and none stands in the market. At the stress value the top of the zone gives only 0.42:1. So ₹202 to ₹218 is a band to watch, not a band we can call safe, and an entry above ₹202.64 depends on the 20 times multiple being right.

The chart does not authorise a fill at these prices either. The ledger anchors are frozen at 27 October. At ₹218 the price is below the 50-day average of ₹237.21, so Test A fails. It is 2.70 average-true-range units above the 63-day base low of ₹181.05, against a limit of 1.5, so Test B fails; at ₹208 the distance is 1.97. Test C is the only gate a pullback entry can use. It asks for a floor that holds, a price back at or above its 20-day average (₹249.75 today) with positive short-term strength, and a price within 2.0 units of support. At ₹218 the floor and the support distance pass but the 20-day average condition fails.[34] A buy in this zone therefore needs the stock to stop falling, let the 20-day average sink towards the zone and turn up through it. That is a base-and-turn pattern that would take weeks, and it is the only way an entry and the 2.5:1 floor can both hold.

For that reason the zone is a conditional alert, not a standing authorisation. A close at or below ₹218 triggers a fresh check, not an order. On that session's data we re-run the momentum gate, the average true range, the 20-day swing low, the stop, the target from the latest estimates and the reward-to-risk. An order is placed only if at least one of Tests A, B or C passes, the stock is not in Stage 4, and the reward-to-risk is at least 2.5:1 at the order price on the new stop and target. If any of these fails, no order is placed, and an existing order is cancelled.

Size follows the risk. We use conviction tier TIER_4, the smallest live tier. A basis point is one hundredth of one percent, so the tier's budget of 35 basis points is 0.35% of net worth lost if the stop is hit. At ₹218 the stop is 11.85% away, so 35 ÷ 11.85 = 2.95% of net worth, and the tier caps that at 2.0%. If the full 2.0% were filled at ₹218 and stopped out, the loss would be about 24 basis points, inside the budget. If an order is placed the plan is 2.0% in total, split into 1.0% at ₹218 and 1.0% at ₹208. If only the ₹218 half fills the risk is about 12 basis points. If only the ₹208 half fills it is about 8, because that stop is 7.6% away. If both fill it is about 20. We stay at this tier because the cash record is weak, the customers are unnamed, the margin is cyclical and governance has watch items. A let-winners-run rule applies after a fill: no automatic trim at an RSI above 70, the weight may grow to 22% of the portfolio only through price gains, and the stop trails the rising 50-day average.
