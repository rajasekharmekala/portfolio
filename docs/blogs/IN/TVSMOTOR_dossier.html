Before you read

TVS Motor is India's third-largest two-wheeler maker, and it owns a finance arm, TVS Credit.[7] Our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: the business is sound without the finance arm, but at ₹4,280 it offers only 0.41 of reward for each 1 of risk, against a floor of 2.0.[40] We would buy only with an order at ₹3,900 or lower, where the ratio is 2.33, once refreshed numbers support it, with the stop at ₹3,620.44.[40] The hinge is whether profit per share grows about 21 percent by March 2028 with a margin near 13 percent, and whether the market keeps paying about 40 times that profit.[40] At the brokers' 35 times, no price in the zone clears the floor of 2.0, and the call is wrong if electric price wars cut the margin, if the market stops paying 40 times, or if the family dispute reaches the shares.

1. What the company does

TVS Motor Company builds motorcycles, scooters, mopeds and three-wheelers. It is the only Indian maker with a product in all three two-wheeler categories, including the low-priced moped.[7] It sold 5.89 million two- and three-wheelers in the year to March 2026, a record.[24] Its plants at Hosur, Mysuru and Nalagarh in India, Karawang in Indonesia and Solihull in the United Kingdom could build 74 lakh two-wheelers and 2.8 lakh three-wheelers a year. A lakh is one hundred thousand.[7] Names a reader may know are Jupiter and NTORQ (scooters), Apache, Raider and Ronin (motorcycles), iQube and Orbiter (electric scooters), alongside a range of three-wheelers.[24]

The people who pay are retail buyers, who pay a dealer in the showroom, and overseas distributors. The company reports sales to dealers, so a unit count measures shipments into the dealer network. India has 6,094 dealers for its two-wheelers.[7] Management says dealer stock stays between 21 and 30 days. That suggests the factory is not stuffing the channel to flatter a quarter.[4] Exports are about 23 percent of standalone revenue.[7] In the September 2026 quarter, export revenue was about ₹4,378 crore and spare parts revenue was about ₹1,314 crore.[4] Spare parts are a steady stream, because every vehicle sold needs service parts for years.

Profit per vehicle comes from price, mix and cost. In the June 2026 quarter the average price per vehicle was ₹85,200, up 8 percent on the year.[13] Raw materials took about 72.8 percent of sales.[13] The gross margin fell 160 basis points to 37.2 percent. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent. Steel, aluminium and other inputs had cost more.[13] Even so, operating margin reached 12.8 percent, because of a better product mix, currency gains and tight cost control.[13] EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is the profit from running the vehicle business before interest, tax and depreciation. In the September quarter the company raised prices by about 1.6 percent, against a material cost rise of about 1.9 percent.[4] It recovered most of the increase, but not all of it. A government production-linked incentive (PLI) pays cash for building electric vehicles locally. It is worth about 1.3 to 1.4 percent of turnover.[4] That is a large slice of a 13 percent margin.

The finance arm changes how the numbers are read. TVS Credit lends to buyers of two-wheelers, tractors, used cars and consumer goods.[9] Its loan book reached ₹33,818 crore in September 2026.[4] TVS Motor owned 80.76 percent of it on 30 June 2026.[9] The group balance sheet includes TVS Credit's borrowings. So the group shows debt of ₹39,453 crore against ₹11,348 crore of shareholders' money.[1][40] That is not a fair picture of the vehicle business.

On 28 October 2026 the board approved a split. The investment in TVS Credit would move into a new holding company, Harita ARC.[6][5] TVS Motor shareholders would receive its shares one for one. The appointed date is 20 July 2027, and approvals will take 8 to 10 months.[6][5][4] This report therefore reads every ratio twice. Once for the group, and once for the manufacturing business.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ICE: internal combustion engine, the ordinary petrol vehicle, as against an electric vehicle (EV).
  • EV penetration: the share of all new two-wheelers sold that are electric. It was 11.6 percent in September 2026.[4]
  • NBFC: non-banking financial company, a lender that is not a bank. TVS Credit is one.
  • PLI: production-linked incentive, a government cash payment for building eligible vehicles in India.
  • NCRPS: non-convertible redeemable preference shares, a debt-like instrument issued in 2025 and repaid in September 2026.[24][8]
  • Gearing: debt divided by shareholders' money. A gearing of 5 means ₹5 of borrowing for each ₹1 of owners' funds. That is normal for a lender and dangerous for a factory.
  • Demerger: splitting a company in two and giving shareholders shares in the new company in proportion to what they hold.
  • Registration (Vahan): the government vehicle-registration count, used as a retail sales measure. It can differ from company wholesale numbers.
  • Realisation: average revenue per vehicle sold.
  • Reward-to-risk (also called asymmetry): the gain you expect if the value is reached, divided by the loss you take if the stop is hit.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong because it wins share where winners show up in monthly data. In the year to March 2026 its domestic petrol two-wheeler sales rose 19 percent. The industry rose 9 percent.[24] Its two-wheeler exports rose 31 percent against 12 percent for the industry. Its three-wheelers rose 63 percent against 28 percent.[24] The pattern held in the September 2026 quarter. It grew 16 percent against 10 percent at home, 33 percent against 27 percent abroad, and 47 percent against 26 percent in three-wheelers.[4] It also beat the industry in the December 2025 quarter (21 percent against 16) and the June 2026 quarter (21 percent against 13).[30][13] That is how a share gainer looks.

Profit has followed volume because mix and scale help the margin. Standalone EBITDA margin rose from 7.1 percent in FY17 to 12.9 percent in FY26. It was up 60 basis points in the last year alone.[23] Return on capital employed (ROCE) was 34.96 percent on the company's own standalone figure for FY26. ROCE is the profit earned on the money the business uses.[23] CARE Ratings rates the company AAA. It credits premium products, cost reduction and a strong supply chain.[7][8] It also notes that TVS is the only maker with a presence in every two-wheeler category.

The money cycle is unusually good for a manufacturer. On the consolidated page the cash conversion cycle was minus 60 days in FY26, and working capital days were minus 37.[1] On the standalone page they were minus 56 and minus 55.[2] Negative days mean suppliers are paid after the customer has paid. So growth releases cash instead of using it. That is why standalone free cash flow (FCF) reached ₹3,805 crore in FY26, on ₹6,079 crore of operating profit.[2] Free cash flow is the cash left after running the business and buying plant.

Scale, breadth and the dealer network

Scale lowers the cost per vehicle. It also spreads the cost of design and technology. CARE ranks the company third in two-wheelers and second in three-wheelers in India.[7] Its dealers grew from 5,563 to 6,094 in a year.[7] That reach is hard for start-ups to copy quickly. Dealer stock of 21 to 30 days means retail demand, not factory push, is behind the growth.[4]

Electric leadership, and why it is not a moat yet

TVS sold 3.71 lakh electric two-wheelers in FY26. Its share rose from 11.7 percent in FY23 to a market-leading 24.4 percent.[7] In September 2026 registrations it led with 54,105 units, a 26.1 percent share.[26] Bajaj Auto was second at 23.4 percent, Ather third at 14.7 percent, Hero's Vida fourth at 11.7 percent and Ola Electric fifth at 6.5 percent.[26] The gap to Bajaj is 5,623 registrations. TVS, Bajaj and Vida together hold about 61 percent.[26][40] Incumbents are winning, but the market is a three-way fight. No one holds a durable lead.

Profit from electric vehicles is not disclosed. Management says it improves each quarter and gives no figure.[4] The PLI payment helps. The company has passed 10,000 cumulative units, which qualifies it for a further 2 percent slab.[4] A reader should treat electric volume as proven and electric profit as unproven.

International diversification

Exports were about 23 percent of standalone revenue in FY26, and they grew about 30 percent.[7] Top markets include Guinea, Nigeria, Congo, Colombia, Brazil and Mexico.[7] The September 2026 quarter set a record of about 0.5 million units, up 35 percent.[3] Management says demand exceeds the capacity now installed.[13] The risk is currency and import-duty shocks in emerging markets. Management flagged new Mexican duties, though its exposure is low.[30]

Where the competitive position is thin

Three things limit the moat. First, commodity inflation is only partly recovered through price. The September quarter saw about 1.6 percent of price against 1.9 percent of material cost.[4] Second, management says the entry-level motorcycle segment is not growing as fast as the other categories.[30] Third, Norton and the loss-making e-bike businesses have absorbed large sums. Management put cumulative e-bike losses at about ₹1,300 crore over four to five years.[4] CARE says the company sold its loss-making European e-bike retail business in May 2026. That removes one loss source.[7]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks two questions. Do the static levels, where the business stands today, pass the solvency and quality floors? And does the trajectory, the direction and speed of change, support the case? A Weinstein Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend. A Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend, and it is the technical red line. A base is a sideways stretch of trading that forms a floor for a later move. The pivot is the top of that base. A breakout is a rise through the pivot on strong volume. The entry zone is the band of prices where we would buy. The central value is ₹4,550, which is 31 percent higher. The DCF asks what the business is worth to an owner who earns 12 percent a year. The central value asks what the market will pay for the shares in a year, and our scoring rules (the target-anchoring rule in .agents/config/scoring.json) define the reward-to-risk test on that kind of price: forward EPS times a multiple. We therefore use the multiple for the 2.0 test, and treat the DCF as a warning. The warning is that even ₹3,900 is about 13 percent above our DCF value, so the central value assumes the market keeps paying a premium. That is also why we do not lean on the 50 percent weight. If we put 25 percent on bull, 40 percent on base and 35 percent on bear, the weighted value is ₹4,295, which is 10 percent above ₹3,900 and only 0.4 percent above the close. The entry therefore rests on a thin margin of safety. We handle that with a small size (TIER_3) and an order that stays inactive until refreshed numbers support it.[40]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A good-looking estimate is easy to write. This chapter does the opposite. It names the weakest assumption behind each estimate. It gives three ways we could be wrong. It says what would tell us.

Fatal flaw 1: electric profit is not there, and incentives fade

Our central case assumes the margin holds near 13 percent. That margin includes about 1.3 to 1.4 percent of turnover from PLI. Management will not state electric profit.[4] If electric competition forces price cuts, the lead is thin. The gap to Bajaj is only 5,623 registrations a month. A rival can take first place in one good month.[26] A fall of 100 basis points in margin on standalone TTM sales of ₹56,283 crore is about ₹563 crore of operating profit. After tax at 26 percent that is roughly ₹415 crore. It is about 9 percent of TTM standalone net profit of ₹4,487 crore.[2][40] We use that stress to set two tripwires. A margin below 12.5 percent, half of the stress, is a warning that keeps any order inactive and forces a re-underwrite. A margin below 12.0 percent, the full stress, rejects the idea, because FY28 EPS would then be near the bear ₹100 and the floor would fail. We would see it first in the margin in the December 2026 results.

Fatal flaw 2: the cycle turns while the multiple is at 53 times

Management expects the industry to slow to single digits in the December quarter. The base from the 2025 festive season is high. Material costs are still rising, though more slowly.[5][4] A stock at 53 times earnings leaves little room. Suppose growth slows from about 45 percent to 15 percent. A multiple of 33 times on EPS of ₹100 then gives ₹3,300. That is below our stop.[40] The multiple alone is enough. At the brokers' 35 times on our ₹111, the value is ₹3,885 and no price in the zone clears the 2.0 floor. Earnings delivery cannot rescue the entry if the market stops paying a premium.[13][40] Evidence would come from the monthly company sales release on the first of each month. It would also come from Vahan registrations in November, the festive month.

Fatal flaw 3: the holding structure, the demerger and the family

Three structure risks overlap. The demerger needs shareholder, regulator, court and creditor approvals over 8 to 10 months. A separate NBFC merger under a regulator's direction is due by March or April 2027.[4][5] TVS Motor may also put another ₹1,200 crore into TVS Credit before the split.[5] The split also changes what the share is. Our ₹4,550 is a value before the split. After it, the TVS Motor price falls by roughly the value of the Harita ARC share that holders receive. Judging the stop on the TVS Motor price alone could mistake that fall for a failed thesis. The family dispute has reached the courts through eight caveats. Mallika Srinivasan holds 0.01 percent of TVS Motor directly. She says no economic settlement has been agreed.[20][21][18] The risks to a minority shareholder are delay, a court order on the holding company's shares, or a distracted management. A forced sale looks unlikely given CARE's 49 times cover.[12]
