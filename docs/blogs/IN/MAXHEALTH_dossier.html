1. What the company does

Max Healthcare owns and runs private hospitals. A patient pays the hospital for a bed, a surgeon, a scan, a drug or a night in intensive care, and the hospital keeps what is left after it pays doctors, nurses, drugs and rent. At 17 August 2026 the group had 21 facilities with more than 6,100 beds, and about 73% of those beds were in large cities[16]. Delhi and its suburbs bring in 53% to 55% of revenue, and the rest comes from Mumbai, Lucknow, Nagpur, Mohali, Bathinda, Dehradun and, since May 2026, Bhubaneswar[10][8].

The unit that matters is the ARPOB, the average revenue per occupied bed per day. A hospital makes money when its beds are full and each full bed earns a lot. In the June 2026 quarter, occupancy was above 75% and ARPOB was ₹81,900, up 5% on a year earlier and 5% on the March quarter[12]. Network gross revenue for that quarter was ₹2,982 crore, up 16%[12]. Professional and consultancy fees, which include what doctors are paid, cost ₹1,794 crore in FY26, or 21.4% of consolidated revenue. Drugs, consumables and implants cost another ₹1,799 crore, or 21.5%, and staff cost ₹1,357 crore, or 16.2%[5]. What is left after these running costs is the operating profit. As a share of sales it is the operating margin, and it is the first number a hospital investor checks.

The payers matter as much as the beds. Patients who pay cash or through private insurers and corporate plans are the profitable ones. Government and public-sector patients are a low-margin group: the Central Government Health Scheme (CGHS), ex-servicemen's cover and state-owned employers paid for 21.9% of FY26 revenue, up from 19.4% a year earlier. The company calls this business a contribution to fixed costs and says it is not profitable on its own[10][12]. International patients were 9.1% of revenue in FY26, against 8.8% a year earlier[10]. About 32% of revenue now starts with an online lead or booking[12].

Growth comes in three ways. The first is filling the beds it already has. The second is adding floors to existing sites, called brownfield expansion, such as the 400-bed tower at Max Smart in Delhi and a 268-bed tower at Nanavati in Mumbai. The third is buying weak hospitals and fixing them, as it did at Lucknow, Nagpur and Noida, or building a new site, called greenfield, as with the 500-bed hospital in Gurgaon Sector 56[13][12][14][16]. Management plans to roughly double bed capacity in four to five years, and the credit rating agency counts about 10,000 beds by FY30[16][10].

A reader should keep two sets of numbers apart. The audited consolidated accounts cover Max Healthcare Institute Ltd and its subsidiaries: revenue of ₹8,373 crore in FY26. The company also publishes a "network" view that adds the partner hospitals run for trusts under service contracts, which lifts FY26 net revenue to ₹10,028 crore and profit after tax to ₹1,631 crore[15]. Share prices follow the first set, but analyst estimates often follow the second, and that gap matters in Chapter 5.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Occupied bed day (OBD): one bed used by one patient for one day. Revenue divided by OBDs gives ARPOB.
  • Occupancy: occupied bed days as a share of the beds that are open for use. Max Healthcare reported 75.44% in the June 2026 quarter[10].
  • Brownfield and greenfield: adding beds at an existing hospital site is brownfield; a new hospital on a new site is greenfield. Brownfield is cheaper and ramps faster.
  • Network: the listed company plus its subsidiaries plus the partner hospitals it runs for trusts. The network numbers are not audited as one set of accounts.
  • Partner Healthcare Facilities (PHFs): hospitals owned by charitable trusts that Max Healthcare manages under long service contracts and lends to; the group had ₹1,179 crore of loans and other balances with them at 31 March 2026[8].
  • ALOS: average length of stay in days. A longer stay with the same bill lowers ARPOB.
  • PBILDT: profit before interest, lease rentals, depreciation and tax, the credit rating agency's version of operating profit.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The first strength is price per bed. Max Healthcare's company-compiled comparison shows ARPOB of ₹82,000 in the June 2026 quarter against ₹70,000 at Medanta, ₹67,000 at Apollo and ₹74,000 at Fortis, with occupancy of 75% against 63%, 70% and 69%[16]. The group earns more per bed and fills more of them, which is the combination that produces its operating profit of ₹71 lakh per bed per year against ₹55 lakh to ₹62 lakh for the other three[16]. That comparison comes from the company's own presentation, so we treat it as a claim from Max Healthcare, not an audited fact.

The second strength is a repeatable way to fix bought hospitals. CARE Ratings reports that Lucknow grew from about ₹268 crore of revenue in FY25 to about ₹528 crore in FY26, with ₹184 crore of operating profit. Nagpur went from about ₹130 crore to ₹227 crore, with ₹39 crore of profit. Noida earned ₹106 crore on ₹537 crore, and Dwarka ₹79 crore on ₹447 crore[10]. Management now says its own bar for an acquisition is a 20% ROCE within four years[16]. The first test of that claim at Bhubaneswar is weak: occupancy 50%, ARPOB ₹35,000 and an operating profit of ₹2 crore on ₹19 crore of revenue in its first partial quarter[12][16].

The third strength is clinical depth and a good lender view. In FY26 the group performed about 52,900 cardiac, 15,000 neurosurgical and 14,300 oncology surgeries, and CARE affirmed CARE AA+; Stable on ₹1,818.88 crore of bank lines in October 2026 after raising the amount from ₹1,296.02 crore[10]. The agency lists a sustained operating margin below 20%, or adjusted net debt (which is debt minus cash) above 1.8 times operating profit, as reasons to cut the rating. That debt measure was 1.46 times at 31 March 2026[10]. Neither trigger is close.

The weaknesses are concentration, mix and rules. More than half of revenue comes from Delhi and its suburbs, so one regulator or one competitor in that market matters a great deal[10]. A fifth of revenue comes from the low-margin government and public-sector channel, and the Central Government Health Scheme capped payment for certain cancer drugs at about 70% of the maximum retail price, which led the company to stop supplying some high-value drugs to those patients[10]. The share of oncology in inpatient revenue fell from 26% a year earlier to 22% in the June 2026 quarter, and management expects it to stay near 21% to 22% instead of returning to 25% or 26%[12][13].

The newest weakness is outside the company. On 30 September 2026 hospital shares fell about 5% after the Supreme Court asked the central government to look at hospital drug mark-ups. The Bloomberg report quotes the court asking whether a uniform 16% margin on medicines should apply, and Max Healthcare fell 5.3% that day[28]. The Yahoo price history shows a close of ₹982.10 on 29 September 2026, the day before the remarks (the high that day was ₹1,000.10), so the stock has fallen about 15.0% since then[27]. No rule has been made, and an analyst quoted by Bloomberg called it a sentiment effect, but the company's own annual report lists expanding price controls on consumables and revisions to government schemes as a threat to realisations and margins[28][9].

The competitive picture is not one-sided. The company's own annual report says capacity expansion by leading hospital chains is raising competition in key micro-markets including Delhi[9]. Rivals have their own plans. The debt-to-equity ratio, which is borrowings divided by shareholders' equity, is about 0.90 at Apollo on our calculation. It is about 1.29 at Narayana and about 1.89 at KIMS. So some peers are borrowing more than Max Healthcare to grow[30][33][34]. Max Healthcare's lower debt gives it room to keep building if the market stays weak, and that is a real edge, but it does not make the shares cheap.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

We compared what management said in May 2025 with what the company reported by August 2026. The pattern is steady growth in revenue and good control of debt, with new capacity arriving later than promised. That matters because the profit forecasts in Chapter 5 rest on beds that are not yet open.

On capital allocation, the group is spending ahead of its cash. In FY26 it bought plant and equipment for ₹1,485 crore and lent ₹420 crore to other healthcare providers. It ended the year with ₹165 crore of free cash flow, which is the cash left after running costs, interest, tax and building spending, on Screener's measure[6][3]. In 2026 it added ₹298 crore for Kalinga Hospital, a ₹1,400 crore plan for 700 beds at Shaheed Path in Lucknow, a ₹425 crore tower at Vaishali and an in-principle plan to open medical colleges at about ₹300 crore for each 150 seats[8][13][12]. CARE expects capital spending of ₹1,500 crore to ₹1,800 crore a year for two years[10].

Management's stated return bar is a 20% ROCE within four years of an acquisition and 20% to 25% on money borrowed at 8% to 8.25%[16][14]. The company's own audited ratio fell from 21.81% to 20.71% in FY26, and its network pre-tax figure was 24% in the March 2025 quarter and 21% in the March 2026 quarter[7][15]. Returns are above the bar but moving the wrong way, which is what you would expect while capital is tied up in unfinished buildings.

Two governance points are worth a reader's attention. Shareholders approved a performance share plan for up to 73.5 lakh shares, about 0.76% of the 97.3 crore shares in issue on our calculation, and a special resolution on the chairman's pay on 19 October 2026[3][4]. Separately, the group had ₹1,179 crore of loans and other balances with partner hospitals at 31 March 2026, up from ₹825 crore, and management judges them fully recoverable[8]. Neither is a red flag today. Both should be watched in the next annual report.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Over five years Max Healthcare has grown from ₹3,937 crore of sales in FY22 to ₹8,373 crore in FY26, a 2.1-fold rise. Over the last twelve months it reached ₹8,712 crore[3]. Much of that came from buying hospitals. It bought the Jaypee hospital in Noida for ₹1,716 crore net of cash, the Sahara hospital in Lucknow and the Alexis hospital in Nagpur. In May 2026 it bought Kalinga Hospital in Bhubaneswar for ₹297.97 crore[14][16][8]. Sales rose faster than profit because interest and depreciation grew with the buildings.

Debt has gone up too. Borrowings including leases were ₹913 crore in FY22 and ₹3,478 crore in FY26. Equity grew from ₹6,283 crore to ₹10,747 crore. So the debt-to-equity ratio is 0.32, the same as a year earlier[3][7]. The audited accounts show net debt of ₹2,813 crore at 31 March 2026, or 1.25 times EBITDA. The company's own internal limits are 2 times equity and 4 times EBITDA[7]. Network net debt rose ₹476 crore to ₹2,384 crore in the June 2026 quarter[12].

Cash tells a kinder story than profit. Cash from operations was ₹1,633 crore in FY26, against profit of ₹1,442 crore. It has beaten profit in each of the last five years[3]. The cost of growth shows in the next line. The company spent ₹1,485 crore on plant and equipment in FY26. It also lent ₹420 crore to other healthcare providers. The loan balance with partner hospitals was ₹746 crore at year end. So free cash flow (FCF), the cash left after building spending, was only ₹165 crore on Screener's measure[6][3]. The rating agency expects spending of ₹1,500 crore to ₹1,800 crore a year for two more years[10].

Earnings per share (EPS) rose from ₹6.24 in FY22 to ₹14.82 in FY26, and ₹14.98 over the last twelve months[3]. The jump in FY26 is partly a tax effect. A tax credit in one quarter added about ₹1.60 a share, on our calculation. Underlying EPS was nearer ₹13.20.

The latest quarter is the one to read slowly. Sales were up 16.7% and operating profit was up 14.3%. But profit after tax was up only 4.9%. Interest and depreciation each grew about 27% to 29%[3]. Management says the new towers should add profit in the second and third quarters after opening. The September 2026 results on 30 October are the first look at that claim[12][19].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report rests on assumptions, and the weakest one decides how much to trust it. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, says what evidence would show it wrong, and gives the date on which that evidence arrives. It then makes the strongest case against our own conclusion and names the single claim on which everything else turns.

Weakest assumption in the earnings estimate: the margin recovers to 27% by FY28

Our FY28 earnings of ₹17.20 need the operating margin to climb from 25% in the June 2026 quarter to 27%. Sales must also grow 17% to 18% a year. The evidence for it is management's remark that new towers add profit in the second and third quarters after they open. The rating agency also expects the margin to stay above 25%[12][10]. The evidence against it is plain. The network margin fell from 26.8% to 24.8% in one quarter. Clinician costs were up about 230 basis points on the year in March. Management itself said a new hospital must be staffed before it fills[12][15].

We will know first on 30 October 2026, when the September 2026 quarter is reported. The call follows on 3 November at 12:00 noon IST[19][20]. A consolidated operating margin under 25% would say our central case is too generous. A margin of 27% or more would say it is too cautious.

Weakest assumption in the multiple: 55 times earnings holds

Our central value uses the multiple the stock has now. That multiple has already fallen by more than a quarter from its five-year average of 76.7. It can fall further if rules change[23]. The Supreme Court asked the government to look at hospital drug mark-ups and floated a uniform 16% margin on medicines. Drugs, consumables and implants cost 21.5% of consolidated revenue, so a cap on mark-ups would reach a large line[28][5]. A Parliamentary committee's 176th report also spoke about affordability and caps on foreign investment. Management said those must be weighed against the need for new beds and viability[12].

We found no hearing date for the Supreme Court matter in any source we could read. Watch for a government notification, a court order on margins or a change in Central Government Health Scheme rates. Foreign holders have already cut their stake from 60.69% in December 2023 to 40.52% in September 2026. Domestic institutions raised theirs from 11.55% to 31.01%[3].

The Kalinga hearing on 29 October 2026 is small, and we say why

The note that put this name in our queue pointed to a National Company Law Tribunal hearing on 29 October 2026. The filings confirm it. The petitions were filed by the legal representatives of BRS Capital Two Pte. Limited. They name Kalinga Hospital, the other shareholder from whom Max Healthcare bought its stake, and Max Healthcare. On 8 September 2026 the tribunal told the respondents to file replies within a week. On 29 September it moved the 28 September hearing to 29 October for lack of time[17][18]. The company has disclosed no settlement, claim amount or penalty[17][18].

Asked about the dispute on the August call, the chief financial officer described minority shareholders of 39%. He said they sit outside India and face liquidation proceedings in the UAE. He said they want Max Healthcare to buy their shares. The company declines without a court order, because it says the shares cannot be transferred. The call does not name BRS Capital, so linking the two is our reading[12][17]. We think the size is small. Max Healthcare paid ₹297.97 crore for 58.28% of Kalinga. That is 0.37% of the ₹81,257 crore market value. CARE expects ₹100 crore to ₹120 crore of revenue from the hospital in FY27, about 1% of our FY27 sales[8][10]. One illustrative adverse outcome is a forced purchase of the 39%. It is not a proven limit on the risk, and no filing gives an amount, so we cannot size it.

We treat the hearing as a headline risk, not a value driver, but we cannot rule it out as one cause of the slide. The petitions were public from 19 August and were heard on 8 September. The shares then fell about 6% in three sessions, from ₹1,046.00 on 24 September to ₹982.10 on 29 September, and the 29 September order moved the hearing to 29 October. No source we read gives a cause for that move[27][17][18]. The 5.3% fall on 30 September is tied by the Bloomberg report to the Supreme Court's drug mark-up remarks[28].
