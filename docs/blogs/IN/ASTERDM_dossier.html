Before you read

Aster DM Quality Care runs 39 hospitals in 28 Indian cities after merging with Blackstone-backed Quality Care India on 1 July 2026.[2][5] In the year to March 2026 the combined group earned ₹9,273 Cr of revenue and ₹2,013 Cr of EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, or profit before financing and accounting charges).[3] We reject the stock for now because 66.99% of the promoters' shares are pledged to lenders, up from 18.17% in July, against our limit of 20%.[11] Even setting that aside, the price of ₹662.80 offers about 1.1 units of reward per unit of risk, against a floor of 2.5. We would re-underwrite only if a filing shows the pledge below 20%, and then only at a better price.

1. What the company does

A hospital chain sells one thing: a night in a staffed, equipped bed, plus the surgery, tests and drugs that come with it. The company now owns or runs 39 hospitals across 28 cities and 9 Indian states, with 10,898 beds on paper and 10,559 in daily use.[3] Its four brands are Aster, CARE Hospitals, KIMSHEALTH and Evercare, and management says each will keep its own name.[8] About 35% of the capacity sits in big metros and 65% in smaller cities.[3]

The company you can buy today is not the one the older data describes. Aster DM Healthcare bought a 5% stake in Quality Care India Limited (QCIL) in April 2025 by issuing its own shares, then absorbed all of QCIL on 1 July 2026. QCIL holders received 977 Aster shares for every 1,000 QCIL shares.[5] The share count rose from 51.81 Cr to 87.17 Cr, an increase of 68%.[15] The company was renamed on 3 July 2026.[1]

Money comes in three ways. Walk-in patients paying cash were 53% of hospital revenue in the June 2026 quarter, corporate and insurance cover was 34%, government health schemes were 9%, and international patients travelling for care were 4%.[3] The biggest specialties are cardiac care at 15%, neurosciences at 11%, and oncology, gastro care and orthopaedics at about 10% each.[3] Hospitals and clinics supply 95% of revenue on the legacy Aster side, with labs at 3% and pharmacies at 2%.[3]

The core earnings equation is simple. Revenue is beds, times the share of beds filled, times the money earned per filled bed each day. That last figure is called ARPOB, short for average revenue per occupied bed. For the merged group it was about ₹49,500 in the year to March 2026, with occupancy near 62%.[7][8] A bed costs nearly the same to run whether it is full or empty, so a rise in occupancy flows quickly into profit.

How the merger changes the numbers you will see

Almost every public figure for this ticker needs a label. The Screener page and the quarter to June 2026 show only legacy Aster, because the merger took effect one day after that quarter ended.[1][22] The first quarter that includes QCIL covers July to September 2026, and results are due by 14 November 2026.[25] Until then the combined figures are company-made pro forma numbers (a pro forma figure adds the two firms together as if they had always been one), and the company says they are unaudited and may change.[3]

Three accounting facts matter for returns. First, the merger used the pooling-of-interest method, so assets moved over at book value and no new goodwill appeared.[7] Second, QCIL already carries intangible assets of about ₹2,717 Cr from its earlier purchases of KIMSHEALTH and STS Holdings.[7] Third, lease contracts are booked as debt under the Indian accounting rule called Ind AS 116: ICRA puts lease liabilities at about ₹1,780 Cr within total debt of more than ₹5,600 Cr at March 2026.[8]

Headline margins also sit on a favourable definition. The company's operating EBITDA is the accounting figure adjusted for one-off costs, share-option cost and the variable fee paid to hospitals it manages for others.[3] For Aster alone in the June 2026 quarter, that adjusted figure was ₹277 Cr (21.1% of revenue), the accounting figure was ₹264 Cr (20.2%) and the figure after deducting rent was ₹232 Cr (17.7%).[3] All three are legitimate, but they are not the same number, and the gap is about 3.4 points of margin.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ROCE stands for return on capital employed: the operating profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share: the company's profit divided across all its shares.
  • Free cash flow is the cash left after running the business and paying for new hospitals and equipment; its short form is FCF.
  • RSI stands for relative strength index: a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been moving, where below 30 is called oversold.
  • ATR stands for average true range: the typical daily price swing in rupees, used to size stops and measure distance.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the four-stage trend scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend; Stage 1 is the sideways floor between them.
  • A base is a sideways stretch of trading that forms a floor, and the pivot is the top of that floor, the price a rising stock must clear.
  • A breakout is a move above the pivot on heavy trading, and a retest is the dip back to that level that either holds or fails.
  • A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold, and a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better.
  • The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry is the same idea as reward-to-risk: the possible gain divided by the possible loss from the entry price.
  • Operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue, and gross margin is revenue minus direct material cost, as a share of revenue.
  • Debt-to-equity is total borrowings divided by shareholders' money, and net debt is borrowings minus cash.
  • Price-to-earnings is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit.
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in day-to-day operations, such as unpaid bills from patients and insurers.
  • Capital expenditure is money spent on new hospitals, beds and equipment, and cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates before financing.
  • Guidance is the company's own published forecast, and a consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts.
  • Dilution is the fall in each shareholder's slice when new shares are issued.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The operating business is stronger than the stock chart suggests. Revenue per patient admitted rose 10% year on year to ₹1,36,802 in the June 2026 quarter, and patient volumes rose 13%.[3] Blended occupancy reached 64%, up 510 basis points (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point), with the legacy Aster hospitals at 62% and the QCIL hospitals at 65%.[3] Growth came mostly from more patients, not just higher prices, which is the healthier way to grow.[4]

The company grades its hospitals by age and margin. Mature hospitals, those over three years old with unit margins of 25% or more, supplied 73% of revenue, grew 19% and earned a 29.7% unit margin.[3][9] Focus hospitals, the older ones earning 15% to 25%, earned 16.8%. New hospitals earned 12.6% and the weakest older units only 5.0%.[9] This grading is useful because it shows where margin can still rise: lifting the 1,478 focus beds and the 1,161 under-performing beds toward the mature level is the heart of the margin plan.[9]

Scale gives real advantages. A chain with 7,400 doctors can share a rare programme, such as deep brain stimulation or liver transplants, across several hospitals instead of building one team per site.[3][4] It can also buy drugs and consumables in bulk. Management targets extra operating profit of 10% to 15% of the FY24 pro forma figure, or ₹150 Cr to ₹200 Cr a year, but says none of it showed up in the June quarter.[3][4]

Lenders agree that the credit is sound. CRISIL assigned AA+ with a stable outlook and ICRA upgraded the company to AA with a stable outlook, both in July 2026.[7][8] CRISIL counted cash surpluses above ₹3,000 Cr at March 2026 and a pro forma gearing of about 0.6 times.[7] The merger is also cash-neutral, which means no new borrowing was needed to complete it.[6]

Where the business is thin

The first weakness is how little each rupee of capital earns on the standard measure. Screener shows a return on capital of 11.6% and a three-year average return on equity of 6%.[1] The company's own pro forma return of 21.1% leaves out unfinished projects, intangibles and revalued land, so it cannot be compared with Screener's figure.[3][5] Both are true; they answer different questions, and the gap is large enough that a buyer should plan on the lower one until audited merged accounts arrive.

The second weakness is competition for doctors. In FY26 the Karnataka hospitals lost doctors and slipped to single-digit growth, and management says it has since hired more than 18 doctors in Bengaluru.[4] Management also admits Maharashtra is lagging because of a dropped insurance scheme.[4][9] The third is delay: construction of an Indore hospital, once due to start in early FY27, is now expected in the fourth quarter, and opening is set for the second quarter of FY29.[7]

The fourth is policy. In late September 2026 the Supreme Court questioned why hospitals sell medicines at ten times cost, and it floated a single 16% margin cap on drugs.[23] The government has already capped trade margins on non-scheduled cancer drugs at 30%.[23] ICRA names price controls as a standing risk for this company.[8] Hospital shares, including this one, fell on both news days.[23][24] A further hearing was listed for 27 October, the day of our price date.

Who owns the company, and why the pledge matters

After the merger, two groups share control, and promoters together hold 53.79%.[21] The Moopen family holds about 24.58% and Blackstone's vehicle, BCP Asia II Topco IV, holds about 29.20%.[11][13] TPG, the other former QCIL investor, sold 6.66% of the company in August for ₹4,451 Cr through the market, which is about ₹767 a share on our arithmetic.[13] The Moopen family then bought 0.57% back from TPG at ₹760.[13] Insiders buying at ₹760 is a positive signal; the stock now trades about 13% below that price.

The problem is what each group did with its shares. On 19 August 2026, BCP pledged 24,89,52,574 shares, or 28.56% of the company, to secure a term loan of up to US$750 million (about ₹7,181 Cr).[12] The loan is for paying dividends and distributions to the borrower's own shareholders and for refinancing older debt.[12] That reads as a financing at the holding level. The listed company did not borrow, and this does not add to its debt.

It still matters to a minority shareholder, for three reasons. First, the pledged shares are worth about ₹16,500 Cr at today's price, so the loan is about 44% of their value, up from 36% on the pledge date; the lenders' trigger level is not published.[12] Second, Trendlyne puts the total pledge at 31.4 Cr shares, or 66.99% of all promoter holdings, and about 36% of the whole company.[11] Third, that is roughly 286 days of normal trading volume, using the ₹728 mn average daily value reported by Prabhudas Lilladher.[9] A forced sale would swamp the market. The Moopen family also pledged 65,116,298 shares, or 34.85% of its Union Investments holding.[11] The promoters cut the pledge from 99% to 41% in March 2025.[14] It was 18.17% in July 2026 and is 66.99% now.[11]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A management team earns trust by doing what it said it would do. We checked what this team promised in 2024 to 2026 against what the filings show. Operating promises mostly held. A pledge cut the promoters reported in 2025 has reversed.

The merger closed about 19 months after the board approved it in November 2024. That is slow, but 96.68% of votes backed it.[5] In March 2025 the promoters reported cutting the pledge from 99% to 41% after refinancing with JP Morgan, HSBC and Barclays.[14] We found no written promise to hold it there. By September 2026 the pledge was back at 66.99%.[11] That reversal is the clearest negative change in the record. It is also the one that matters most here.

The number

We estimate the merged company's operating EBITDA for the year to March 2028 (FY28) at about ₹2,870 Cr, a 23.2% margin on revenue of about ₹12,370 Cr.

How it was calculated

We grew FY26 pro forma revenue of ₹9,273 Cr by 16% in FY27 and 15% in FY28, then applied a 23.2% margin, a little over a point above the June 2026 quarter's 22.2%.[2][3] That gives revenue of 9,273 × 1.16 × 1.15 = ₹12,370 Cr, and EBITDA of ₹2,870 Cr.

The inputs

The June 2026 quarter grew 20% and management called lower-to-mid-teen growth sustainable, from 5% to 6% more patients and 7% to 8% higher revenue per patient.[2][4] The margin target is 24% to 25% by FY28 to FY29.[4] The street's FY28 EBITDA is ₹2,872 Cr at ICICI Securities and about ₹3,070 Cr at Motilal Oswal.[10][19]

The range

In the bull case, revenue grows 18% then 17% to ₹12,800 Cr at a 24.5% margin, giving about ₹3,140 Cr. In the bear case, growth of 12% then 11% gives ₹11,530 Cr at 22.0%, or about ₹2,540 Cr.

If the number is off

Each point of FY28 margin moves EBITDA by about ₹124 Cr. After minority holders, that is about ₹108 Cr, which at our multiple is roughly ₹2,800 Cr of value, or about ₹32 a share.

The number

We value one share at about ₹714 in twelve months in the base case, which is 7.7% above the ₹662.80 close.

How it was calculated

We took the share of EBITDA that belongs to our shareholders, 87.3% of ₹2,870 Cr or ₹2,505 Cr, and multiplied by 26 to get an enterprise value of ₹65,141 Cr. We then subtracted net debt of ₹1,162 Cr and lease liabilities of ₹1,780 Cr, leaving ₹62,199 Cr for shareholders, and divided by 87.17 Cr shares to reach ₹713.6.[3][8][9]

The inputs

The 87.3% comes from Prabhudas Lilladher's pro forma EBITDA after minority holders, ₹24,834 mn ÷ ₹28,455 mn for FY28.[9] Shares outstanding are 871.67 mn.[9] The merger priced Aster at 36.6 times and QCIL at 25.2 times FY24 EBITDA, and brokers use 27 to 32 times.[6][9][10][18] At today's price the stock trades at about 24 times our FY28 EBITDA attributable to shareholders, so 26 times assumes a modest re-rating once savings appear.

The range

At 30 times on bull-case EBITDA the value is about ₹909. At 21 times on bear-case EBITDA it is about ₹500, near the 52-week low of ₹519.

If the number is off

Each turn of multiple is worth about ₹29 a share. A one-turn miss is smaller than the distance between our base value and the ₹918 street average, which would need about 33 times on our EBITDA.

5. What could happen next

A price is a bet on future growth. We can solve for the bet by asking what yearly growth in cash flow, for ten years, would justify today's market value of ₹57,668 Cr.[1] This is called a reverse DCF (discounted cash flow). We use a 12% discount rate and a 20 times exit multiple on the tenth year's cash flow, which are our fixed rules for India.

The merged company has not published a combined cash flow statement, so there is no clean starting cash flow. We built a proxy from CRISIL's expected annual cash generation of ₹1,400 Cr to ₹1,600 Cr and expected capex of ₹1,000 Cr to ₹1,300 Cr, which leaves ₹350 Cr at the mid-points.[7] On that base the market is pricing growth of about 35% a year for ten years. If every rupee of expansion capex is treated as growth spending and the base is the full ₹1,500 Cr, the figure drops to about 15% a year.

The number

How it was calculated

We found the growth rate g that makes the sum of ten years of cash flow, each discounted at 12%, plus 20 times year-ten cash flow, discounted the same way, equal to ₹57,668 Cr. The formula is: value = sum of 350 × (1+g)^t ÷ 1.12^t for t = 1 to 10, plus 20 × 350 × (1+g)^10 ÷ 1.12^10. We solved it by repeated trial.

The inputs

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above can be wrong in a specific way. This chapter names the weakest assumption in each, builds the strongest argument against our own conclusion, and lists the dates that will tell us who is right.

Weakest assumption in the EBITDA estimate

The weakest assumption is the 23.2% margin for FY28. It needs savings that management says have not started, and it needs Kerala and Karnataka to keep growing in a competitive market.[4] The evidence that would show us wrong is the September 2026 quarter, the first with QCIL consolidated: a combined margin below 21.5% would mean our path is too high. Weakest assumption in the value per share

The weakest assumption is the 26 times multiple. Hospital multiples depend on investors believing that prices and margins are safe, and the Supreme Court's remarks and the 30% cap on cancer-drug margins have damaged that belief for now.[23] If the 21 times bear multiple were applied to our base EBITDA and nothing else changed, the value would be about ₹570, or 14% below the close. Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF

The ₹350 Cr cash flow base is our own proxy from two rating-agency mid-points. The true merged figure could be anywhere in a wide band. Until audited combined accounts show a cash flow statement, the 35% figure is an upper bound, and the 15% figure is the lower bound.

The red-team case against rejecting the stock

The strongest case for buying is this. The stock is down 25.6% from its high while combined operating EBITDA has grown about 20% a year since FY24, and CRISIL and ICRA have just rated the credit AA+ and AA.[7][8][32] The pledge is a loan to Blackstone's holding vehicle at roughly 44% of share value, not a loan to the hospital company, and the Moopen family is buying, not selling, at ₹760.[12][13]

The next table shows the sizing. It matters because it turns the stop distance into a position size. We rank positions in tiers. Tier 1 is the highest conviction, with 7% to 10% of the portfolio and a risk budget of 100 basis points (1% of the portfolio lost if the stop is hit).
