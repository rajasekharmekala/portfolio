## PROLOGUE: The Business, The Price & Our Independent Verdict

> *"A backlog is a promise to build; cash is proof the promise travelled."*

Sit with me at the desk and begin not with the chart, but with the machine. TD Power Systems designs and builds custom alternating-current generators and electric motors for steam, gas, hydro, diesel and other prime movers. Its generator turns the shaft power of a turbine or engine into electricity; its motor does the reverse, turning electricity into controlled mechanical work. The product is not a generic box pulled from a catalogue. A buyer specifies power, speed, voltage, cooling, site conditions and the interface to the prime mover, then qualifies the design before volume orders can follow. That engineering intimacy is the source of the moat—and of the company’s dependence on a narrow set of large OEM customers.

The business has reached a new scale quickly. Consolidated sales were ₹1,856.2 crore in FY26, up from ₹1,278.8 crore in FY25, and net profit was ₹238.8 crore. ## CHAPTER I: How the Business & Stock Got Here — Past Cycles & Pivots

> *"Every mature machine begins as a set of inherited tolerances."*
> — **Research Desk (original epigraph)**

You and I can understand TD Power Systems best by starting where its story starts: not with a data-centre boom, but with a transfer of engineering capability. The company says it was founded in April 1999 after acquiring generator technology from Toyo Denki of Japan, then established its Bengaluru manufacturing facility in 2001. Its early proposition was practical rather than grand: make a rotating electrical machine to a customer’s mechanical and electrical specification, test it, and deliver it into a prime mover package that could not tolerate an unreliable generator. That technical starting point explains why customer qualification and applications engineering remain more important than consumer branding.

The company’s evolution is a sequence of widening interfaces. It developed vertical hydro generators with Voith in 2009, then signed a Siemens licence in 2012 for two-pole generators up to 250 MVA. It began generator manufacture in Istanbul in 2017, while the core Indian facilities remained centred near Bengaluru. In 2019, it supplied a large four-pole machine for the Maxau project in Germany. Each milestone added a new combination of speed, rotor design, cooling, test requirements or geography. You should read these milestones as evidence of accumulated engineering and qualification, not as proof that every adjacent market will become profitable.

The operating record shows why a custom-machine company can be both cyclical and scalable. Screener’s consolidated series places revenue at ₹380 crore in FY17 after a downturn, with operating margin below 3% and a net loss; by FY19 the company had returned to profit, and by FY21 the operating margin was around 12%. From FY22 onward the scale recovery accelerated: revenue moved from ₹797 crore to ₹872 crore, ₹1,001 crore, ₹1,279 crore and ₹1,856 crore in FY26. Operating margin rose from approximately 12% to approximately 18%. The decade is not a smooth compounder’s line; it contains a demand trough, recovery, and a recent export-led order surge.

That history has two lessons for your underwriting. First, fixed manufacturing capability creates operating leverage: when demand and factory loading fell in the earlier cycle, margins nearly disappeared; as utilisation and mix recovered, profit grew faster than sales. Second, the recovery is not purely a software-like recurring-revenue story. Customers buy a large capital good when their own power, infrastructure or industrial projects proceed. When those projects slip, orders and dispatches can bunch up. The annual reports describe sustained expansion, but the 2015–18 trough remains a useful stress case: technology did not prevent cyclicality, and a good product did not guarantee the factory stayed fully loaded.

The stock’s public-market story has become much more dramatic than the factory story. The current Screener page reports approximately 42% annualised price return over ten years, 78% over five years, 65% over three years and 56% over one year; those look-back windows overlap and should not be added together. More recently, the 2026 quote reached ₹856.10 before the 8 November snapshot at ₹611.90, a pullback of about 29% from that high. The same share can therefore represent two very different experiences: a long-term holder who rode a multi-year rerating and a new buyer arriving after the momentum premium began to compress. We do not infer exact historic peak-to-trough drawdowns from CAGR data.

The corporate perimeter also evolved. TDPS now reports an Indian parent with subsidiaries in the United States, Europe, Turkey and Japan, and the group’s FY26 consolidated statements include overseas entities. The Istanbul facility provides manufacturing and service reach, but Q1 FY27 management described Turkey as a small business with roughly €3.5 million of execution for the year and no large next-year pipeline, retaining it partly as a service-insurance facility. That is a useful counterweight to a simple “global footprint equals global moat” claim: some international assets deepen customer support, while others may carry costs without a visible new growth engine.

The product mix has also shifted toward more demanding machines. The FY26 report says gas-engine generators became a larger contributor to turnover than before, followed by steam-turbine generators; gas-turbine and gas-engine demand was particularly strong, driven partly by U.S. data-centre applications. Meanwhile, the company is developing larger 40–200 MW capability through a UK R&D branch. The orderbook story now reaches beyond the legacy 1–50 MW range, but larger machines take longer to engineer and build. During the Q1 FY27 call, management said the existing large-generator opportunities were over and above the current sub-100 MW business, while remaining cautious about the timing and exact capacity plan.

You should also keep capital-market changes in this chronology. The QIP completed on 12 October 2026 placed 7,741,935 new shares at ₹775 and raised almost ₹600 crore; the paid-up share count rose from 31.37 crore to 32.14 crore shares. That is meaningful capital for a business whose growth absorbs inventory and receivables, but it is not free: each share now represents a smaller slice of future earnings. The post-issue price later fell below the QIP price. Institutional participation is evidence of demand for the issue, not a floor under the stock and not proof that returns on the capital will exceed the cost of dilution.

The cross-border parallels make the cycle easier to see. In the United States, Cummins has repeatedly shown how engines, power generation and service businesses can benefit from infrastructure investment while still moving with industrial demand. In India, Triveni Turbine’s history links steam equipment to captive power, biomass and waste-heat projects—markets that can grow steadily without becoming immune to capex pauses. In Europe, Siemens Energy’s technology and scale demonstrate how long qualification cycles can protect a supplier, while its own project exposure shows that complex energy equipment carries delivery and margin risk. TDPS is smaller than all three; the lesson is about the shape of the economics, not equivalence of scale.

So when you ask “what did the company become?”, the answer is not simply a data-centre proxy. It became a specialised generator and motor maker with a global OEM interface, broader products and a better balance sheet than at the old trough. But every cycle still asks the same questions: can it win an order, manufacture to specification, deliver on time, collect cash, and reinvest without diluting away too much of the per-share value? That is the bridge to Chapter 2, where we separate the machine families from the accounting segments—and refuse to invent revenue splits management has not published.

There is a second way to read the history: by the factory’s learning curve. Each additional prime-mover family expands the engineering library, but it also adds its own validation burden. A hydro rotor turning slowly under hydraulic torque is not interchangeable with a high-speed gas-turbine rotor; a locomotive motor must survive repeated acceleration and vibration; a large two-pole machine asks for different machining and balancing capability. The company’s investments in R&D, in-house manufacturing and testing therefore build a portfolio of options rather than one universal machine. This can create a durable niche if the same designs are reused across OEM programmes, but it can also strand equipment if demand shifts to ratings or technologies that TDPS has not qualified.

The 2026 equity issue belongs in the company’s evolution because it changes the resource base. Before the QIP, TDPS funded growth largely through retained earnings and modest borrowing; at the end of FY26 consolidated cash plus other bank balances substantially exceeded short-term borrowings. Yet the next growth leg required more working capital and capacity. The October placement raised almost ₹600 crore and increased the share count to 32.14 crore. This capital structure is healthier for absorbing a large production ramp, but investors should ask what proportion will become productive plant, what proportion will support receivables and inventory, and when those investments earn returns. Until later filings answer that, we count the cash as resilience and the new shares as dilution, not as automatic value creation.

## CHAPTER II: Inside Every Segment — Products, Customers & Real-World Examples

> *"A shaft turns only when every interface agrees."*
> — **Research Desk (original epigraph)**

You and I need to distinguish the product catalogue from the segment note. TD Power Systems makes custom AC generators and electric motors; its FY26 audited financial statements say the chief operating decision maker monitors the business as one operating segment under Ind AS 108. The annual report then discusses product families—gas-engine, gas-turbine, steam, hydro, motors, rail traction, diesel, wind, spares and Turkey activity—but it does not disclose a revenue or operating-profit table for each family. We can give you order-book mix and describe which products are growing; we cannot honestly label a product’s share of revenue or profit when the company has not published it.

At the June 2026 quarter, management reported a ₹2,208-crore manufacturing order book. Generator and motor manufacturing together accounted for ₹1,929 crore, railway for ₹211 crore, spares and aftermarket for ₹22 crore, and Turkey for ₹46 crore. Those values imply approximately 87.4%, 9.6%, 1.0% and 2.1% of the stated book respectively; rounding means the shares may not sum to precisely 100%. This is a snapshot of contracted backlog—not a segment revenue mix, not an annual sales forecast and not a profit contribution. The FY26 annual report separately says gas-engine generators had become the larger contributor to turnover, followed by steam-turbine generators, but gives no percentages.

Start with the main product: the generator. A prime mover supplies rotation; the generator’s rotor turns inside a stator and electromagnetic induction creates alternating current. TDPS says its range spans roughly 1 to 250 MVA and is designed for steam, gas, hydro, wind, gas-engine and diesel-engine applications. For a disclosed end-to-end example, the FY26 annual report reports significant volume orders from a major U.S.-based gas-turbine OEM for generators intended for AI and data-centre applications. The buyer is the prime-mover OEM, not necessarily the data-centre operator. The OEM needs a matched alternator that can operate with the turbine’s speed, voltage, cooling and control architecture; TDPS designs and manufactures the stator and rotor, tests the machine to specification, and ships it for integration into the OEM’s power package. The customer’s name, unit price, and order-by-order margin are not disclosed.

Now picture the site after delivery. A U.S. data-centre developer may need power before a grid connection or utility upgrade is ready. Its engine or turbine supplier engineers an on-site generation train; TDPS supplies the electrical machine that converts shaft power into electricity. The project is assembled from several suppliers, so the generator is only one component of the total investment, but it is a critical component: a late or underperforming machine can delay commissioning of the entire train. Management says customers are taking shipped products even where downstream site execution may lag. That is positive for order conversion, but it shifts our diligence toward dispatch acceptance, warranty, customer concentration and the timing of cash collection.

Steam-turbine generators are the more established bridge between industrial heat and power. A sugar, paper, steel or chemical plant may burn biomass or recover waste heat to raise steam; the steam turbine turns the shaft, and a TDPS generator feeds the plant bus or exports surplus power. The annual report describes steady demand from captive power, biomass and waste-heat recovery, and mentions larger 30–60 MW opportunities. For a concrete disclosed application, the company reported healthy steam-turbine generator orders across domestic and export markets, but did not name every plant owner or publish contract prices. Treat the following as an engineering archetype, not a claimed TDPS customer: an Indian paper mill buys a generator matched to its steam turbine, operating speed, grid frequency and site conditions; TDPS quotes the engineered machine and agreed test scope, while the mill or EPC contractor coordinates the boiler, turbine, electrical protection and installation.

Hydro is a different engineering problem. Water head and flow turn a Pelton, Francis or Kaplan turbine; the generator must be designed around the turbine’s speed, grid connection and powerhouse limits. TDPS’s FY26 report cites Vietnam projects for two 7.7 MW, 6.3 kV, 600-rpm horizontal hydro generators and package orders across India and Nepal. That gives us a real product specification and geography, even though the developer’s identity and price are confidential. At the factory the engineering team must match the generator to the hydraulic machine, assemble the rotor and stator, verify electrical insulation and mechanical balance, and run acceptance tests before dispatch. The owner buys an engineered generator package, not a commodity kilowatt-hour.

The motor business starts at the other end of the energy conversion. TDPS lists induction motors up to 20 MW and synchronous motors up to 50 MW, with traction motors up to 1,250 kW. A motor buyer cares about torque, starting current, load profile, hazardous-area requirements, mounting orientation, cooling and service access. Consider a clearly illustrative use case: a European process plant replacing a large pump drive specifies a synchronous motor to its voltage, shaft, bearing and site-temperature requirements. TDPS would need drawings, load data and qualification testing before production; a steel mill, compressor station or irrigation plant could present a different duty cycle and package. The public materials show product capability and customer qualifications, not a named 50 MW sale or a published price list.

Rail is technically adjacent but commercially distinct. A traction motor must tolerate repeated acceleration, braking, vibration, thermal cycling and tight space constraints inside a locomotive or electric multiple unit. TDPS reports existing railway business and international qualification activity; by Q1 FY27 the order book included ₹211 crore of railway work, while management said it was not taking fresh railway orders and would review the segment’s sustainability after the Indian Railways contract. A representative end-to-end program begins with a locomotive OEM’s traction duty and envelope, proceeds through prototype and vehicle testing, then qualification and serial supply. The customer and unit-level pricing are not public, so we do not turn qualification into a booked future revenue claim.

Spares, refurbishment and service connect the installed base to the factory. A hydro operator may need a rotor refurbishment or replacement parts during a planned outage; the service partner identifies the machine number and rating, scopes the repair, sources parts and schedules technicians around the outage window. TDPS lists a 57-centre service network across continents. Management has described service as a small share of current sales—about 5–6%—because a well-manufactured generator may need little service during its first years. This limits the recurring-revenue story: the installed base is a future opportunity, not a software-like annuity already visible in the accounts. Public filings do not disclose a representative service ticket or price, which depends on the machine, parts and outage scope.

Turkey is a manufacturing and service node rather than a large standalone growth engine today. At the Q1 FY27 call, management described around €3.5 million of Turkey work for the year, limited next-year pipeline and a continuing role as an insurance facility for service in Europe. This is useful when a regional repair or customer support requirement would otherwise require a long shipment from India, but idle capacity still carries cost. The Q1 book’s ₹46 crore Turkey bucket should therefore be watched separately from the ₹1,929-crore combined generator-and-motor book.

Across these families, revenue follows a direct, relationship-heavy go-to-market model. TDPS qualifies products with turbine, engine, locomotive and industrial OEMs; a qualification can lead to repeat programs, but the end-user, OEM, EPC contractor, importer and operator may be different legal entities. In FY26, export and deemed-export orders were 79% of inflow, while exports and deemed exports were 66% of standalone sales. Q1 FY27 order inflow was 93% export and deemed export, but the quarter is too small a sample to call that a permanent sales mix. The export machine is built in India, tested, shipped under the contract’s delivery terms, then commissioned by the customer or integrator. TDPS says its U.S. shipments are ex-works India, so it is not the importer of record.

To connect business mix with cash, keep one last distinction in view. A generator order enters the book when confirmed; manufacturing draws copper, electrical steel, forgings, insulation, labour and factory time; dispatch can occur before the customer has paid the full invoice; receivables and work-in-progress therefore rise ahead of cash. A separate aftermarket job may have a faster service cycle but a smaller ticket. One “segment” in the accounts can therefore contain very different order duration, material intensity and working-capital profiles. Because management does not publish profit by product family, our moat and valuation work will judge the consolidated economics and the disclosed order mix, not pretend we can see hidden segment margins.

The international parallels help you see the product boundaries. In the United States, Baker Hughes sells turbine and power systems into large energy projects, while Cummins serves engine-driven power applications; both show why the prime mover’s demand cycle influences the generator supplier. In India, Triveni Turbine sells steam turbines into captive-power and industrial heat recovery, while CG Power’s motors and electrical products serve adjacent industrial loads. In Europe, Siemens Energy and Voith operate in turbine, hydro and power-generation equipment where technical qualification, delivery discipline and customer integration matter. TDPS’s role is narrower: it supplies a critical rotating electrical machine into those systems rather than owning the whole power plant.

That is the product map you should carry forward. The generator-and-motor order book dominates, hydro and gas demand offer growth, rail is under review, spares are small but strategic, and Turkey is service insurance more than a fresh growth pillar. The next chapter follows how the company reaches OEMs, moves material through a custom factory, prices contractual risk, and converts ₹100 of revenue into free cash flow.

The segment disclosure itself is an analytical constraint, not a clerical detail. If generator and motor work are combined in one order-book bucket, we cannot infer a motor margin from the movement in that bucket. If the annual report says gas-engine generators were the largest turnover family but gives no revenue percentage, we can rank the products qualitatively but cannot put a made-up split into the model. Likewise, customer concentration is disclosed at the consolidated level: the ten largest customers together accounted for 84% of revenue in FY26. That is enough to conclude that several counterparties matter; it is not enough to name the top customer or to assign its share.

For a hypothetical buyer, the quote would be an engineered contract price rather than a fixed published tariff. A 7.7 MW hydro generator and a high-speed gas turbine machine consume different materials, labour, engineering hours, tests and freight scope; a motor’s hazardous-area rating or mounting design changes the specification again. Customers may separately contract installation, EPC integration and commissioning. TDPS does not disclose the unit prices or standard payment schedule for the cited contracts, so any precise rupee-per-MW estimate would be false precision. The real commercial evidence is the repeat order, price-variation clause, acceptance and cash collection.

## CHAPTER III: How It Is Sold & Delivered — Distribution & Unit Economics

> *"Revenue is a route; cash is the destination."*
> — **Research Desk (original epigraph)**

You and I should follow a TDPS order from the first engineering drawing to the last rupee of cash, because the company’s distribution model is not a retail channel. It sells custom rotating machines to a short list of turbine, engine, locomotive and industrial OEMs, and those OEMs place the generator inside a larger package. The end user may be a data-centre developer, a hydro utility, a sugar mill or a steel plant, but the technical gatekeeper is often the prime-mover maker or EPC partner. That relationship can bring repeat orders across projects; it also creates concentration and bargaining power on the buyer’s side.

Qualification precedes volume. A prime-mover OEM supplies speed, shaft interface, voltage, thermal and environmental limits, fault-duty requirements and test protocols. TDPS’s engineering team selects a rotor and stator design, resolves interfaces, and builds prototypes or first-of-kind units when the application is new. The customer’s test and field teams then approve the design before a programme can move to repeat production. The company’s FY26 report describes volume orders from a U.S.-based gas-turbine OEM as well as prototype orders from a global gas-engine OEM; the names are withheld. This is why a new customer can take time to appear in revenue even after technical work begins, and why a lost qualification would matter beyond a single purchase order.

The route to market is mainly direct OEM engagement and project coordination rather than a broad distributor network. TDPS reports selling into more than 100 countries and advertises 57 service centres, but those service partners do not imply that custom generators are stocked on dealer shelves. The company’s own statement that U.S. goods ship ex-works India is especially important: the customer or its logistics chain takes title and arranges import, while TDPS must still meet technical delivery and documentation conditions. Tariffs, ocean freight, customs delays and FX may affect the customer’s economics or the company’s ability to deliver, but they do not all sit in the same contract account.

Manufacturing is weighted toward custom in-house work. The company describes its Bengaluru facilities and Istanbul plant, in-house stator and rotor manufacture, high-capacity machine tools and testing beds. A heavy rotor is not just a bill of materials: balancing, insulation, winding, impregnation, tolerances and full-load or other acceptance tests determine whether the assembly can operate safely at its designed speed. In FY26 the company invested in plant and equipment, and management discussed debottlenecking to raise the FY28 capacity envelope toward ₹3,200 crore. Capacity is a ceiling subject to the product mix, labour, machine loading and test-bay schedule; it is not dispatched revenue already won.

Raw materials create a second commercial interface. Copper, electrical steel, forgings, mild steel and insulation have different price cycles and supplier lead times. TDPS has said that it uses hedges and customer price-variation clauses, but sudden commodity spikes can outrun the repricing lag. In the Q4 FY26 call, management described gross contribution margins around 33–34% on a consolidated basis and linked protection to pricing, cost reduction, mix and currency. The company does not publish a product-by-product price card or average selling price. We can describe a custom quote as a function of rating, design, material, test scope and delivery terms; we cannot invent a generator price per megawatt.

The payment cycle is the hard part of the model. A long-lead order can require materials and work in progress before dispatch; the buyer’s acceptance, shipping documents and payment terms determine when receivables clear. When an analyst asked about collection terms, managing director Nikhil Kumar replied: “We have payment terms fixed with our customers, and we're not going to be able to alter them dramatically.” That is a direct admission of the trade-off: preserve the customer relationship and grow the programme, or push for terms that could make the customer choose another supplier. The company is growing into the cash it needs to fund the growth.

Now let us run the audited FY26 consolidated numbers through a ₹100 revenue waterfall. For each ₹100 of revenue from operations, other income added about ₹1.15; reported expenses absorbed about ₹83.58; profit before tax was about ₹17.57; tax reduced that to approximately ₹12.86 of net profit. The ₹12.86 is an accounting result, not the cash left for shareholders. Before working-capital changes, operating cash generation was about ₹18.72 per ₹100; receivables, inventory and other working-capital movements pulled cash generated from operations to about ₹10.58, and cash taxes left operating cash flow at about ₹6.95.

Capital expenditure then takes another approximately ₹5.96 per ₹100 of sales, leaving only about ₹0.99 of free cash flow on this FY26 definition. In rupees, audited CFO was ₹129.1 crore and additions to property, work in progress, intangibles and leasehold land were about ₹110.7 crore, so free cash flow was around ₹18.4 crore. The waterline between operating profit before working capital and cash generated from operations was a ₹151.1-crore absorption, or about ₹8.14 per ₹100 revenue. The arithmetic uses the reported statements; it is not a forecast that every future rupee of sales converts at the same rate.

FY26’s working-capital indicators underline the same tension. The Screener consolidated series shows debtor days rising to 146 and inventory days at 148, with the cash-conversion cycle still around 173 days. The cycle improved from 189 days in FY25, but it remains long for a business whose orders are now accelerating. The company’s Q4 call explained that short-term order batches require inventory and that retained earnings were going into working capital; the subsequent QIP raised ₹600 crore. Funding access reduces the probability that growth stops for lack of cash, but it does not make the cash cycle disappear or prove that incremental return on capital will be attractive.

For a U.S. gas-turbine order, the chain may begin with an OEM forecast and a capacity slot, move through a confirmed purchase order, engineering release, long-lead material procurement, in-process inspection, final test, ex-works shipment and customer acceptance. For a hydro package in India or Nepal, the interfaces are more project-specific: the generator must fit the turbine and powerhouse; an EPC contractor may coordinate civil, electrical and control systems. For European service, a local partner may inspect the installed machine and coordinate spares. Payment milestones differ by contract, and public filings do not provide a standard advance percentage, invoice schedule or DSO by customer. We therefore describe the mechanism but keep contract-level terms as unknown.

The global comparison is useful. In the United States, Cummins combines an engine platform with a service network; in India, Triveni Turbine’s industrial projects illustrate how an OEM sale is embedded in a plant’s capex plan; in Europe, Siemens Energy and Voith show the value and execution burden of engineering-to-order equipment. TDPS sits between the prime mover and the electrical grid, not at the final power consumer. You should expect repeat OEM awards when qualification holds, but you should also expect long receivable cycles and uneven delivery schedules when projects, testing or logistics slip.

Our unit-economics verdict is therefore two-sided. The direct OEM model can sustain repeat programmes and an international reach without funding a consumer channel. In-house manufacturing and qualification make the company harder to replace after design approval. Yet FY26 cash flow demonstrates that growth is not self-funding at the same pace as earnings: working capital and capacity investment consume much of the operating surplus. Track order inflow, dispatches, customer acceptance, receivable days, inventory days, and CFO against PAT together. A backlog expansion without those cash measures is a promise to manufacture, not yet a compounding return.

The October QIP changes one part of this chain but not all of it. It raised ₹600 crore after the June quarter and expanded the share count to 32.14 crore. That should make it easier to hold safety stock, support receivables and fund the planned debottlenecking without relying solely on short-term borrowing. Yet a funded order still has to earn a return. If the proceeds sit as cash, the business earns a lower yield; if they fund work in progress, the cycle remains exposed to delays; if they buy capacity, the added machines must be loaded. We therefore separate “liquidity strengthened” from “cash conversion solved.” The first is true at the issue date; the second remains to be measured in post-QIP quarterly statements.

## CHAPTER IV: The Battlefield — Rivals, Switching Costs & Moat Reality

> *"A moat is a customer’s reason not to requalify."*
> — **Research Desk (original epigraph)**

You and I should define the moat narrowly. TD Power Systems is not the owner of the gas turbine, the data centre, the hydro project or the grid. It is a specialist supplier of a rotating electrical machine whose design has to match a prime mover, a customer’s test protocol and the site where the equipment will run. Once a generator design is qualified for an OEM platform, engineering familiarity, test history, delivery performance and failure risk can raise the switching cost. Those are real advantages. They do not create a legal monopoly, and they do not prevent customers from qualifying another supplier when capacity, price or delivery disappoints.

The first moat layer is application engineering. A high-speed gas turbine generator sees different centrifugal forces from a hydro machine turning at 600 rpm. A traction motor lives with vibration, repeated load changes and tight packaging. TDPS’s product pages describe different rotor, stator, mounting and test requirements; the FY26 report points to more than two decades of manufacturing and a UK R&D branch developing larger generators. Competitors must reproduce not just a drawing but the experience that lets a machine pass a customer’s qualification and run reliably in a real plant. A design asset matters only when it translates into accepted orders, repeat business and warranty performance.

The second layer is qualification and customer access. The company states that it works with major global turbine and engine OEMs, and the annual report records volume gas-turbine orders and new prototype work. Q1 FY27 management said the biggest opportunity is deeper engagement with current customers rather than adding a large number of new ones. That can be a moat if an OEM expands its production line and allocates TDPS more share; it is also concentration risk if one platform is delayed, insourced or awarded to another supplier. FY26’s top ten customers represented 84% of consolidated revenue. A high switching cost and high customer concentration can be true at the same time.

The third layer is cost and export competitiveness. Bengaluru production combines skilled labour and local supply chains with machines large enough to process heavy rotors and stators. Exports and deemed exports were 79% of FY26 order inflow, while exports made up 66% of standalone FY26 sales. That gives the company access to demand pools beyond Indian industrial capex, but it also links results to freight, tariffs, FX and customers’ overseas project schedules. The Q1 call’s 93% export share of that quarter’s order inflow is strong evidence of global demand; it is also evidence that TDPS is exposed to a small number of global project cycles.

Pricing power is less complete than the bull story sometimes implies. Management has described customer price-variation clauses, hedges and price increases, yet also said abrupt material-cost surges cannot be fully passed through at once. The Q4 FY26 gross-contribution margin range of roughly 33–34% is a useful historical reference, not a floor. In that same year, a delayed Turkey delivery led to an unusually high liquidated-damages penalty on one contract and reduced consolidated margins. The event was described as one-off, but it illustrates the mechanism: a missed delivery date can transfer value to the customer even when the underlying machine remains technically sound.

Competition is layered. The global generator field includes large diversified manufacturers and specialist electrical-machine makers; the company itself has cited Siemens, Baker Hughes, Mitsubishi, Fuji Electric and Toshiba as significant players in large generators. In India, Triveni Turbine is adjacent through steam systems, while Bharat Bijlee, CG Power, BHEL, Cummins India and ABB compete across motors, power equipment and industrial electrical systems. Not every name competes for every TDPS order. The relevant rival depends on rating, prime mover, voltage, location, service coverage, delivery slot and customer qualification. That is why a peer P/E table is context, not a pure-play valuation formula.

The bull steelman says the equipment shortage is structural enough to extend the cycle. AI data centres need power faster than new grid connections can arrive; gas engines and turbines can provide dispatchable generation; grid stabilisation and industrial electrification widen the use cases; and renewable build-out creates hydro, geothermal, biomass and waste-heat niches. TDPS already has the OEM interfaces, reports strong order inflows and has a ₹600-crore QIP to fund expansion. If the large OEMs increase output and TDPS delivers on time, greater factory loading can support operating leverage without a proportionate rise in overhead.

The bear steelman says the current demand narrative can outrun its proof. Some data-centre orders depend on project financing, grid availability, gas supply and the return on AI infrastructure. A generator shipment is not the same as a commissioned data centre, and one delayed customer schedule can shift revenue. Top-ten customers already provide 84% of group revenue; export orders dominate; gross material exposure is high; and FY26 FCF was only about ₹18 crore after working capital and capex. The QIP alleviates near-term funding risk but dilutes existing shareholders and cannot guarantee attractive returns on the proceeds. If order conversion normalises, today’s earnings multiple can contract even if the company remains profitable.

There are instructive parallels on three continents. In the U.S., Baker Hughes has publicly described a sharp rise in data-centre-related power orders, showing that the theme is real while reminding us that even a large prime-mover supplier must execute a complex backlog. In India, Triveni Turbine has built a durable business around industrial steam and aftermarket opportunities; its narrower technology focus shows how specialisation can compound, but its economics do not map one-for-one onto TDPS. In Europe, Siemens Energy and Voith show that platform qualification and engineering reputation are valuable, while project penalties and capacity bottlenecks can still dominate a year’s margin. These are analogies, not forecasts for TDPS.

For a learning hub, start with TDPS’s own factory and product pages, then compare its FY26 report with the Q1 FY27 presentation and the verbatim Q1 call. For the global demand context, read Baker Hughes’s 2025 report and Siemens Energy’s 2025 annual report; for Indian industrial turbine economics, use Triveni Turbine’s FY26 annual report. These sources let you distinguish company claims from order data, and component demand from a broad addressable-market number. The useful question after every presentation is: which of these forecasts has become a signed order, a dispatched machine, a receivable, then cash?

Our moat conclusion is qualified. TDPS has meaningful engineering, qualification and customer-relationship advantages in a market where a failure can be expensive and a new supplier must prove itself. Its moat is not customer ownership, pricing freedom or recurring service revenue. The strongest proof will be repeat OEM awards, on-time dispatch, stable gross contribution and cash conversion that improves alongside growth. The moat weakens if customers diversify supply, if larger machines require a capital burden beyond the QIP, or if working-capital needs rise faster than retained earnings. That sets up Chapter 5: the leading KPIs are not just order book and capacity; they are the conversion checkpoints between them.

A moat can erode without a dramatic product failure. An OEM may multi-source to protect its own delivery schedule; a competitor may add balancing, machining or test capacity; a new prime mover can change the generator interface; or a customer can redesign its package to favour a larger incumbent. Qualification lowers the probability of a sudden switch, but it does not freeze market share. At Q1 FY27, management said it saw little scope for new major customer additions and expected more of the opportunity to come from deeper engagement with existing customers and a growing market. That is a strength when those programmes expand, but it also means the company’s incremental growth is tightly linked to the plans of its current buyers.

The learning hub should therefore be read with a sceptic’s pencil. Start with the company’s generator catalogue, then compare the FY26 annual report’s order and capacity discussion with the Q1 FY27 presentation. Read the Q1 transcript around the customer, rail and working-capital questions, not only management’s opening remarks. Compare Triveni Turbine’s report to understand captive steam and waste-heat applications; read Siemens Energy and Baker Hughes to see what global prime-mover makers say about power-generation demand. A product brochure explains capability; an audited report explains cash; a call transcript reveals where management declines to give numbers. Each source answers a different question.

> ### NARRATOR'S REFLECTION
> Qualification is valuable precisely because repeating it costs time and creates risk. But you should not turn that friction into a claim of permanent lock-in: buyers can multi-source, renegotiate and redesign when capacity or delivery fails.

> ### GLOBAL PARALLEL (US / INDIA / EUROPE)
> In the U.S., Baker Hughes competes on power systems and turbine technology; in India, CG Power and Triveni Turbine show adjacent motor and turbine capabilities; in Europe, Siemens Energy and Voith bring deep platform histories. These examples show a large competitive field around TDPS, not a set of exact head-to-head matches.

> ### MENTAL MODEL
> Moat = engineering difficulty × qualification persistence × customer repeat demand. Reduce the score when pricing is weak, a customer can dual-source, or the supplier must fund long receivable cycles.

## CHAPTER V: Management on Trial — Leading KPIs & Walking the Talk

> *"A forecast is a hypothesis that invoices must test."*
> — **Research Desk (original epigraph)**

You and I need a short dashboard that predicts the income statement before it arrives. For TD Power Systems, the first leading indicator is order inflow by product and geography, not the headline backlog alone. FY26 order inflow was ₹2,238.5 crore; exports and deemed exports were 79%. Q1 FY27 brought ₹734 crore of inflow, 93% export and deemed export, while the reported manufacturing book reached ₹2,208 crore. The second KPI is book-to-bill and backlog age. TDPS ended FY26 with ₹1,972.9 crore of pending orders, then had a larger book by June. Management has said the FY26 book was expected to execute within FY27, and later raised the FY27 revenue guide. A healthy conversion rate should show up in quarterly revenue and dispatch volume without an unusual rise in overdue receivables, inventory or contract assets. Ask management not only “how large is the order book?” but “how much is scheduled for this financial year, what slipped, what has shipped, and how much remains subject to acceptance?” The public disclosures do not give a full project-by-project age schedule.

The balance sheet at March 2026 carried ₹742 crore of trade receivables and ₹503 crore of inventory. The sixth KPI is capacity and capex. The ₹600-crore QIP completed in October provides a larger funding pool after the June balance sheet, but until the company reports deployment, capex, post-issue cash and returns, we cannot attribute a specific capacity uplift to the proceeds.

Now let us audit guidance without giving management credit twice. FY26 audited sales were ₹1,856.2 crore and profit after tax ₹238.8 crore. In the May 2026 Q4 call, management set an initial FY27 revenue expectation around ₹2,400 crore and discussed the strong order pipeline. In the August Q1 FY27 call, after a strong first quarter and higher order inflow, it raised the guide to ₹2,600 crore. That is a positive revision supported by bookings; it is not yet a delivered result. The annual report’s FY26 guidance of ₹2,400-plus for FY27 and the Q1 revision show adaptive forecasting, while the investor must still wait for the audited FY27 execution record.

On cash, management’s commentary has been candid but not yet conclusive. The CFO said inventory build would convert to cash in the following quarter; the managing director said customer payment terms could not be changed dramatically without risking the business. In Q4, he put the point plainly: “all our retained earnings are basically going into working capital.” The later ₹600-crore QIP is consistent with a business planning for working-capital and growth needs, but the October allotment occurred after Q1 results. We do not yet have a post-QIP quarterly cash-flow statement at the 8 November cutoff, so we cannot tell how much was committed, spent or retained.

The global comparison helps you pick leading indicators correctly. In the United States, Cummins and Baker Hughes each discuss order and backlog visibility in businesses where the equipment-maker’s schedule depends on customer project execution. In India, Triveni Turbine’s order inflow and execution data remind us that power-equipment books convert over quarters, not days. In Europe, Siemens Energy’s backlog and capacity plans show that a large order pipeline can coexist with component bottlenecks and project risk. For TDPS, watch three transitions: prototype to serial award, order book to dispatch, and PAT to CFO.

The guidance passage does not specify standalone versus consolidated basis, so we record delivery above the initial number but do not claim a precise like-for-like beat percentage. The walk-the-talk verdict is “credible demand, unproven cash outcome.” The rise from ₹2,400 crore to ₹2,600 crore in FY27 guidance is backed by order momentum, and the capex plan is relatively modest against reported scale. Yet the cash cycle is the gating variable: new orders must not simply create another round of inventory and receivables. If quarterly sales approach guidance while CFO/PAT remains weak and debtor days continue rising, the quality of growth is lower than the headline suggests. If CFO catches up, customer concentration moderates, and the QIP funds capacity with attractive incremental returns, the bull case gains evidence. That leads to Chapter 6, where we follow every rupee across ten years rather than relying on a single quarter’s guidance.

We can make the guide audit more explicit. FY26 delivered ₹1,856 crore of consolidated sales and ₹239 crore PAT; the Q4 call’s first FY27 revenue guide was around ₹2,400 crore, then Q1 raised it to ₹2,600 crore. At the same call the company said the then-existing capacity could support about ₹3,200 crore in FY28 after roughly ₹50 crore of debottlenecking. These are different metrics—annual revenue guide, quarterly order intake and nameplate capacity—and must not be substituted for one another.

Second, working capital must not grow faster than revenue; the Q1 discussion of fixed payment terms means collection gains may be incremental rather than a management lever that can be pulled overnight. Third, new designs must convert from prototype to serial orders while existing plants deliver on schedule. Management has supplied direction on each, but Q2 and Q3 results after the report cutoff are needed to test them. We do not score a future goal as achieved simply because the capacity plan is plausible.

The KPI dashboard separates demand from delivery and delivery from cash. The order book leads revenue, but receivables and inventory decide whether growth is self-funded; the next scorecard checks whether management’s guide has crossed from forecast into audited results.

## CHAPTER VI: Following the Cash — 10-Year Financials & Earnings Quality

> *"Profit is a statement; cash is a sequence."*
> — **Research Desk (original epigraph)**

You and I now follow the cash through the full cycle, because the income statement is only half of TD Power Systems’ story. Over FY17–FY26, the consolidated business moved from a demand trough to record scale: sales rose from ₹380 crore to ₹1,856 crore; operating margin moved from roughly 2.8% to roughly 18%; and PAT improved from a ₹4-crore loss to ₹239 crore. The path had pauses and large swings in cash, not a straight line. Read this ten-year record as proof of recovery and operating leverage, then ask whether the recent growth can fund itself.

The P&L expansion is clear. Sales were ₹872 crore in FY23, ₹1,001 crore in FY24, ₹1,279 crore in FY25 and ₹1,856 crore in FY26. Operating profit increased from ₹134 crore to ₹171 crore, ₹235 crore and ₹335 crore over those years. Net profit reached ₹239 crore in FY26, while the latest TTM snapshot through June 2026 showed approximately ₹2,124 crore of sales and ₹275 crore of PAT. The higher scale has supported high-teen operating margins and a reported FY26 ROCE of 34%. This is a substantial improvement from the low-utilisation period, but the FY26 record includes both a strong market and a favourable export/product mix; it should not be projected mechanically.

The annual table in the dossier gives ten years of sales, operating margin, PAT, cash from operations and free cash flow. Two features deserve more attention than a single CAGR. First, margins were only about 2–3% in FY17–FY18 and recovered as volumes returned; this tells you that fixed-cost absorption matters. Second, reported CFO and free cash flow have been volatile: FY25 produced about ₹40 crore of CFO and negative ₹16 crore of FCF despite ₹175 crore of PAT; FY26 improved to ₹129 crore CFO and roughly ₹18 crore FCF against ₹239 crore of PAT. The cash result is better, but the gap remains material.

The five-year cumulative comparison is less flattering than the latest quarter. FY22–FY26 PAT sums to about ₹699 crore, while CFO sums to about ₹353 crore, so only around half of accounting profit has appeared as operating cash across that span. The FY26 audited consolidated cash-flow statement records ₹347.5 crore of operating profit before working-capital changes. Receivables, inventory and other working-capital movements reduced cash generated from operations to ₹196.3 crore; cash tax then left CFO at ₹129.1 crore. Capital investment of approximately ₹110.7 crore reduced free cash flow to about ₹18.4 crore. This is the core earnings-quality issue: customers’ growth consumes cash before those invoices settle.

At March 2026, consolidated trade receivables were ₹742 crore and inventories ₹503 crore, up from ₹437 crore and ₹377 crore respectively a year earlier. Cash and cash equivalents were ₹78 crore and other bank balances ₹120 crore; short-term borrowings were about ₹18 crore. The balance sheet therefore entered the FY27 ramp with low financial debt and a reasonable liquidity base, but the cash figure alone understates the amount tied up in the operating cycle. In June, management cited roughly ₹240 crore of cash; that quarter-end figure preceded the October QIP. We do not roll the June balance forward or assume the QIP proceeds remain untouched.

The days metrics explain where cash sits. Debtor days were 112 in FY24, 125 in FY25 and 146 in FY26; inventory days were 139, 165 and 148; payable days were 78, 101 and 121. The net cash-conversion cycle therefore peaked around 189 days in FY25 and improved to 173 in FY26, yet the receivables component worsened. A longer payable period helped offset inventory and debtor investment, but suppliers cannot fund an unlimited ramp without consequences for terms or resilience. Management has said contract payment terms are substantially fixed. The investor’s question is whether customers’ advances and payment schedules rise with order size, and how quickly work in process becomes accepted, invoiced and collected.

Consolidated and standalone results should not be mixed. FY26 standalone sales were ₹1,716.7 crore and PAT ₹216.4 crore; consolidated sales were ₹1,856.2 crore and PAT ₹238.8 crore. Subsidiaries in the U.S., Europe and Turkey affect consolidation, and the Turkey operation can add both customer support and execution cost. The company reported one operating segment for accounting purposes, so there is no auditable public segment margin bridge for gas generators, steam generators, hydro, motors or rail. Product-wise narrative and order-book components are useful for demand analysis, not substitutes for segment P&Ls.

Capital spending is moderate in relation to the growth ambitions but not trivial. FY26 payments for property, plant and equipment and work in progress were around ₹104 crore, with further payments for intangible assets and leasehold land. The FY26 report describes a third manufacturing facility and investment in capacity; management later discussed a further ₹50-crore debottlenecking effort for an output capacity near ₹3,200 crore. A larger-generator programme may need specialised machinery and longer lead times, which the company has said could take roughly 18–20 months to manufacture large machines. The QIP proceeds of nearly ₹600 crore give management funding flexibility; until the company discloses deployment and return, we should treat this as capital available, not capital already earning a return.

Share dilution is the other balance-sheet bridge. At 31 March 2026, paid-up capital was ₹31.37 crore shares of ₹1 face value after employee share allotments. The October QIP added 77.4 lakh shares and raised the total to 32.14 crore shares. Existing holders now own a smaller proportion of the company than before the issue. If the new cash finances capacity or working capital that earns an attractive return, dilution can be accretive in value; if the funds merely finance slow collections or underused assets, per-share growth will lag headline sales growth. At the issue price of ₹775, QIP investors accepted a modest discount to the regulatory floor; the post-issue market later traded lower, so the issue price is not a guaranteed support level.

The global parallels sharpen the test. U.S. industrial suppliers such as Cummins can show strong margins while cycles in engines and power generation dictate working capital. Indian capital-goods firms such as Triveni Turbine show that order growth can lead revenue by several quarters. European engineering groups such as Siemens Energy show that backlog can be enormous and still require disciplined cash management, supplier capacity and milestone execution. For TDPS, the best evidence of compounding will be a multi-year rise in CFO that tracks PAT, stable receivable days, restrained inventory days and incremental ROCE above the cost of equity—not just another record book.

Our forensic verdict is “improving, not yet proven.” The balance sheet was strong enough to support the business before the QIP, and the QIP materially increases funding capacity. The most concerning signal is not debt; it is the amount of working capital and capex needed to translate 18% operating margins into free cash. If two or three subsequent quarters show that receivables unwind and cash catches up, the bull case earns credibility. If sales and order book rise while the cash gap widens, growth will need more capital and may dilute per-share returns. Chapter 7 turns that test into valuation: the stock price already assigns a high value to future earnings, so cash conversion is not a footnote—it is part of the price.
