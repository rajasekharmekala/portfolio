Before you read

Bhansali Engineering Polymers makes ABS, a tough plastic used in car interiors, refrigerators, mixers and electronics, at two plants in Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh.[1] The company has no debt, has paid out about 55 percent of its profit as dividends, and earned ₹62.5 crore in the September 2026 quarter, up 54 percent.[1][7] We rate it WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT because the share fell 13.8 percent in two weeks and the company moved its expansion from September 2026 to March 2027.[26][2] We would buy only on a dip into ₹105.0 to ₹108.4, and a bounce above ₹120.85 would make us re-run the numbers, not buy at once. The view is wrong if ABS prices fall back faster than we assume, if the expansion slips again, or if the price closes below ₹102.25.

1. What the company does

Bhansali Engineering Polymers (BEPL) turns three chemicals into one plastic. The plastic is called ABS, short for acrylonitrile butadiene styrene. It is hard, takes a knock without cracking, and takes paint and colour well. That is why car makers use it for dashboards and trims, appliance makers use it for refrigerator and washing machine parts, and electronics firms use it for casings.[1] The company sells more than 80 variants and offers over 1,500 colours under the ABStron brand.[1] Its customers are the moulding firms and manufacturers who buy the plastic by the tonne, so the company sells a material, not a finished product.

Two plants do the work. The Satnoor plant in Madhya Pradesh makes a rubber powder called HRG. The Abu Road plant in Rajasthan makes SAN, a clear plastic, and then blends SAN and HRG into ABS. Installed ABS capacity is 75,000 tonnes a year, and the SAN capacity of 52,000 tonnes a year is used inside the company, not sold.[1] Making these two ingredients in-house removes a middleman's margin. The company also owns a joint venture with Nippon A&L of Japan, which supplies technology and colour know-how.[1] The joint venture is tiny in the accounts: it was carried at ₹2.4 crore in March 2026.[5]

The economics start with raw materials. Styrene and acrylonitrile are imported, and together they are more than 85 percent of what goes into the plastic. Only butadiene, about 15 percent by weight, is bought in India.[5] In FY26 the cost of materials was ₹848.1 crore on sales of ₹1,276.0 crore, which leaves a gross profit of ₹428.0 crore.[1] Divided by the 71,501 tonnes sold, that is about ₹59,900 of gross profit per tonne.[5][1] The company keeps low stocks of imported chemicals on purpose, because a sudden price fall would leave it holding expensive material.[5]

Capacity is the limit on growth. In FY26 the plants made 71,601.5 tonnes against 75,000 tonnes of capacity, which is a use rate of about 95 percent.[5] Sales volume was 71,501 tonnes, down from 74,381 tonnes the year before and 75,143 tonnes two years before.[5][6] So revenue moves with the ABS price, not with volume, until the expansion to 100,000 tonnes starts. The price in turn follows Asian import prices, because India still buys a large share of its ABS from abroad: 148,465 tonnes came in during 2024, 89,386 tonnes of them from South Korea.[17]

The company also earns money that has nothing to do with plastic. Interest on bank deposits and on loans to outside companies came to ₹36.9 crore in FY26, and total other income was ₹39.0 crore, about 16 percent of pre-tax profit.[5][7] The company's cash flow statement puts this interest in investing activities, so the operating cash flow we use later excludes it.[5] The business is also cyclical. Operating profit was ₹463 crore in FY22, when ABS prices were high, and ₹177 crore the next year.[7] Which customers matter most is not published: neither the annual report nor the presentations give a customer concentration figure.[5][1]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • relative strength index (RSI) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been moving. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing, in rupees. We use it to measure how far the price is from support.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit before financing costs, tax and the accounting charge for wear on equipment.
  • return on capital employed (ROCE) is the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses.
  • earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and paying for new equipment.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before any financing.
  • debt-to-equity is total borrowing divided by shareholders' money.
  • net debt is borrowing minus the cash held. A negative figure means the company holds more cash than it owes.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by the profit per share; it is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit.
  • gross margin, operating margin and net margin are profit as a share of sales after, respectively, the cost of materials, all running costs, and everything including tax.
  • working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, less what the company owes its suppliers.
  • capital expenditure is spending on plants and equipment.
  • guidance is the company's own published forecast or target date.
  • consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for a number.
  • The 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing prices over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days; the price tends to bounce off these slow lines.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase between them.
  • base is a sideways stretch the price builds before a move; pivot is the top of that sideways stretch.
  • breakout is a move above the pivot on strong volume, and a retest is a pullback that checks the same level from above.
  • measured move is the height of a base projected upward from the pivot as a price target.
  • entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy.
  • limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, so it never chases the market.
  • stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, both measured from the entry price.
  • Layer 1, Layer 2 and Layer 3 are the three parts of our underwriting. Layer 1 tests solvency, returns and the price trend. Layer 2 scores the five numbers that run this business. Layer 3 decides when to buy and how much, from the distance to support.
  • A basis point is one hundredth of a percent. A risk budget of 35 basis points means a stopped-out position should cost about ₹0.35 for every ₹100 of the portfolio.
  • A tier is our conviction rank, from TIER_1 (strongest) to TIER_4. Each tier has a risk budget and a cap on the share of the portfolio: TIER_1 is 100 basis points and 7 to 10 percent, TIER_2 is 75 and 4 to 6.5, TIER_3 is 50 and 2 to 3.5, and TIER_4 is 35 and 1 to 2.
  • The FULL_SIZE band is a price up to 0.6 average true ranges above support, where a full-size position is allowed. The STANDARD band runs up to 1.2, a reduced starter band runs to 1.6, and beyond that is NO_BUY.
  • risk-unit sizing, also called portfolio heat, is a way to size a position so a stop-out costs a fixed share of the portfolio: the weight is the risk budget divided by the percentage distance from the entry to the stop, capped by the tier.
  • TACTICAL_MOMENTUM is our bucket for trades of three weeks to six months that rely on trend and a catalyst, and CORE_ALPHA is our bucket for multi-year compounders with high returns on capital.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

BEPL is a good low-cost producer in a market that still imports a lot. The company says 30 to 40 percent of Indian demand is met by imports, and a trade-research summary puts imports at about 200,000 tonnes against demand of 350,000 to 370,000 tonnes.[1][23] Every tonne that moves from an import to a local plant is room to grow. That is the central reason to own an ABS maker.

The company's edge is cost and know-how, not pricing power. It makes its own SAN and HRG, it has worked with Nippon A&L of Japan since 2013, and its plants run near full.[1][5] FY26 gross profit per tonne was about ₹59,900, up from about ₹55,100 in FY25 and ₹51,900 in FY24 (our arithmetic from gross profit and tonnes sold).[1][5][6] Rising profit per tonne while volume fell means the company kept its spread, which is the gap between what it charges and what the chemicals cost.

The weakness is that the price is set by others. Korean producers shipped 89,386 tonnes to India in 2024, and Chinese ABS is cheaper still.[17][18] India withdrew its mandatory BIS quality orders on 14 petrochemical products, including ABS, on 12 November 2025, so that shield is gone.[21][20] A trade-sourcing site also reports no anti-dumping duty in force on Chinese ABS as of April 2026.[20] The company's own slide still lists potential anti-dumping duties as a possible help, which is a hope, not a protection.[1]

Prices also swing hard. In the first half of 2026, after war broke out in the Middle East, East China ABS prices rose 37 to 45 percent, then fell 25 to 30 percent from the March high by June.[19] BEPL's sales jumped 53 percent in the June 2026 quarter and 24 percent in the September quarter.[3][16][1] We read that as mostly price, because volume is capped at 75,000 tonnes. The company does not publish quarterly volumes, so we cannot separate the two.

Competitive position, in plain terms

The main domestic rival is Styrenix Performance Materials, which is adding 50,000 tonnes of ABS capacity in FY27.[24][23] In its June 2026 quarter, Styrenix sold 26 percent less volume than a year earlier, 38.9 thousand tonnes, while its margin jumped, and its management said the high margin would not last.[23] That is a warning for BEPL, because both firms sold into the same price spike. On 28 October 2026 Styrenix reported September-quarter standalone sales of ₹779.7 crore, down 2.4 percent, with profit up 14.5 percent.[25] We could not verify Supreme Petrochem's ABS plans from its own filings, so we do not count them.

We rate the moat as narrow. The cost position and the Japanese licence are real, and the debt-free balance sheet is a clear strength.[1][7] But the product is close to a commodity, 85 percent of its inputs are imported, and a competitor is adding capacity at the same time as BEPL.[5][24] The honest summary is a well-run low-cost plant whose profit depends on the ABS spread.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 asks which five numbers decide whether this business is healthy, and whether management does what it says. We chose five for an ABS maker. Gross profit per tonne shows the spread. Operating margin before other income shows how well that spread survives costs. Use rate and volume show whether the plants can grow. Cash conversion shows whether profit becomes cash. Expansion execution shows whether the one growth project is on time.

A score of PASS is 1.0 point, WATCH is 0.5 and FAIL is 0. The rule is a total of at least 3.0 out of 5.0 with no more than one FAIL.[27] BEPL scores 3.5 out of 5.0, with two PASS, three WATCH and no FAIL, so it qualifies as PASS_WITH_WATCH.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

We checked what management promised in the FY25 annual report, the April and July 2026 presentations and the FY26 annual report against what it delivered. The record on money is good and the record on timing is weaker. The company says it keeps a zero debt policy, and it does: borrowings are nil at September 2026.[6][7]

The clearest test is the expansion. The FY25 report says a study by Toyo Engineering priced a 200,000-tonne plan at about ₹1,700 crore, and the board set it aside to stay debt-free.[6] It chose a ₹200 crore plan to reach 100,000 tonnes by September 2026, paid from own funds.[6] The FY26 report, the April deck and the July deck all kept the September date.[5][4][3] On 19 October 2026 the board moved it to March 2027, citing container shortages, foreign engineers' schedules and a late reactor.[2]

One claim does not match the numbers. The Q2 FY27 deck lists consistent positive operating cash flows as a moat, while the same deck reports minus ₹22.8 crore for H1 FY27.[1] Operating cash flow was positive in each of FY24, FY25 and FY26, so the claim is true for years and false for this half. The line about reaching full use of the new capacity by the end of FY28 appeared in the April and July decks but is not repeated in the October deck.[4][3][1]

One more item belongs here because it is about capital allocation. The company lends spare cash to other companies. 3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter gives five years of numbers and then applies Layer 1 of the underwriting rules. Layer 1 compares static levels, such as debt and returns, with the direction of travel. A business can pass on levels and still be getting worse, or fail a level and be recovering.

BEPL's levels are strong. It has no debt, a 23.8 percent return on capital on Screener's basis, and a three-year cash conversion of 94 percent.[7][1] Its direction is mixed. Profit is rising fast, but cash flow turned negative in the latest half, and sales are flat over five years.[7][1]

Two features of the accounts need care. First, other income of about ₹40 crore a year is about a fifth of operating profit before other income and is partly interest on loans to outside companies.[7][5] Second, the company's own cash flow statement places interest received in investing activities, so operating cash flow is a clean measure of the plant.[5]

The number

Over the three fiscal years FY24 to FY26, operating cash flow was 0.94 of net profit. In plain words, each ₹1 of profit turned into about 94 paise of cash.

How it was calculated

We added audited operating cash flow for FY24, FY25 and FY26 (₹229.9, ₹108.7 and ₹168.1 crore, total ₹506.7 crore) and divided by net profit for the same years (₹179.4, ₹180.0 and ₹180.2 crore, total ₹539.6 crore).

The inputs

Operating cash flow by year is in the company's cash flow statement.[1] Net profit by year is in the income statement.[1] Screener shows ₹231, ₹109 and ₹168 crore and agrees within rounding.[7]

The range

If FY25's weak ₹108.7 crore were repeated in all three years, the ratio would fall to about 0.60, still above the 0.25 red line. If the H1 FY27 figure of minus ₹22.8 crore is added to the window in place of FY24, the ratio over FY25, FY26 and half of FY27 would be about 0.52.

If the number is off

A ratio below 0.70 would stop the name qualifying as a COMPOUNDER and would move it to a different archetype test. The recent half is the risk to watch.

The number

We estimate earnings per share for the year to March 2027 (FY27E) at about ₹9.4, of which ₹5.15 is already reported.

How it was calculated

We took the reported EPS for the first half (₹2.64 plus ₹2.51) and added an estimate for the second half. For the second half we assumed sales of ₹765 crore and an operating margin before other income of 17 percent, which is ₹130 crore. We added other income of ₹19 crore, subtracted depreciation of ₹4.2 crore, added ₹0.8 crore from the joint venture, and taxed the result at 27.5 percent. That gives profit of ₹105.6 crore and EPS of ₹4.24 on 24.886 crore shares.

The inputs

First-half EPS is in the quarterly table.[7] Sales of ₹643 crore in the second half of FY26 (₹301 crore and ₹342 crore) are the starting point.[7] The 17 percent margin is the FY26 level and the middle of the 13-quarter range.[7] Other income of ₹9.7 crore a quarter and a tax rate near 28 percent are from H1 FY27.[1] The share count is 248,858,460.[5] This is our own estimate; no analyst forecast exists.[26]

The range

Bull: if the margin stays at September's 19.6 percent, FY27E EPS is about ₹10.0. Bear: if sales are ₹700 crore and the margin falls to 14 percent, the low end of the range, FY27E EPS is about ₹8.5.

If the number is off

Every point of margin on ₹765 crore of second-half sales moves FY27 EPS by about ₹0.22. At the 15.3 median P/E that is about ₹3.4 on the share price. The December quarter result, due around mid-January 2027, is the first test.

Our variant perception

The market treats BEPL as a cyclical whose profit is at a peak. That view has support: the margin is at the top of a 13-quarter range, ABS prices spiked in 2026, and the Indian peer's management says its own high margin will not last.[7][19][23] The price, at 12.3 times earnings and below 15.3 times median, already reflects some of this.

Our variant view has two parts. First, the price pays little for cash and for the added capacity: about 20 percent of the market value is cash and loans, and the price needs cash flow growth of about minus 1 to plus 6 percent a year (2.0 in the ledger, 4.2 on our rework). A one-time 33 percent lift in volume, spread over ten years, is about 2.9 percent a year if the price of ABS holds. So the price gives roughly fair credit for the expansion and none for anything beyond it, such as a better margin or the cash.[1][5] Second, we do not think profit is much higher than normal. At a 17 percent margin, the FY26 level, profit would still be about ₹210 crore a year at today's sales.

Where we part from a bullish reading is the quality of the profit. Profit in the last half was helped by the price spike, volumes have not grown, and cash flow turned negative. So we use a central case in which earnings per share ease from FY27 to FY28. The expansion adds volume, and the margin and price give some back. We call this a modest variant: the downside is cushioned by cash and the upside needs the price to hold.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above rests on assumptions that could be wrong. This chapter takes the weakest one in each and states the evidence that would show it, and when that evidence arrives. We also give the strongest case against our own view.

The red-team case against buying is simple. The profit is at a peak that the ABS price spike produced. Sales have not grown in five years. Operating cash flow has turned negative while the company lends more cash to outside firms. The expansion has already slipped, and a larger competitor is adding capacity. On that reading, 12.3 times earnings is a fair price for peak earnings, not a bargain.[7][1][24]

Flaw 1: the margin may be above normal

Our central case holds the margin at 16.5 percent. The September 2026 margin is 20 percent, and the quarters in the same year were helped by the ABS price spike.[7][19] The Indian peer's management says its elevated margin is not sustainable and its volume fell 26 percent.[23] A margin fall alone is milder than our bear case. If the margin returned to 14 percent with price, volume and the multiple unchanged, FY28 EPS would be about ₹7.41 and the target ₹113 at 15.3 times, which is only 0.81 to 1 from ₹108.40. Our bear case of ₹82 combines that 14 percent margin with a lower price of ₹180,000 a tonne, 75,000 tonnes, depreciation of ₹10 crore and a 13 times multiple. The entry zone clears the 2.5 floor only if the margin holds above about 15.4 percent. The evidence would be the December and March quarter margins. The December quarter is due around mid-January 2027, and the March quarter in late April 2027, because the board met on 24 April 2026 last year.[5]

Flaw 2: cash flow and the loan book

Half-year operating cash flow was minus ₹22.8 crore, driven by a ₹156.6 crore working capital outflow.[1] Part of that is trade payables falling from ₹151.7 crore to ₹65.6 crore which likely reflects payment for the stock that was at the port in March 2026.[1][5] That would reverse. But loans to other companies rose to ₹306.6 crore in six months, and the borrowers are not named in the sources we could retrieve.[1][5] The yield on this book looks high: interest income other than bank deposits was ₹27.4 crore on balances that averaged about ₹158 crore in FY26, roughly 17 percent, which is our arithmetic and may include other items.[5] A yield like that can mean higher-risk borrowers. The evidence would be a change in the loan balance at March 2027, and any write-off. If the loan book passed ₹400 crore, or any loan were written off, we would treat it as a governance failure.

Flaw 3: the expansion, and what it adds

Only about ₹55 crore of the ₹200 crore budget appears to have been spent by September 2026. That is our estimate: ₹35.2 crore of equipment spending in FY26 plus about ₹20 crore in H1 FY27, from the rise in plant and work in progress of ₹16.1 crore plus ₹4.1 crore of depreciation.[5][1] The FY26 accounts also show only ₹5.2 crore of unbooked capital commitments.[5] The company has not published spending to date, so we cannot say. The evidence would be the reactor arriving in November 2026 and a March 2027 start. A second delay would turn KPI 5 into a FAIL. Reactor delivery is a milestone, not proof of commissioning, saleable output or demand.

The addition is also modest: 25,000 tonnes, a third more than today. Styrenix is adding 50,000 tonnes in the same period.[24] Extra supply in a market where imports set the price can push prices down, which can offset the gain from extra volume.

Flaw 4: what we could not check

We could not retrieve conference call transcripts, so quotes from management come from filings and presentations, not from live questions.[28] No customer concentration or analyst forecast is published. Our three secondary sources on the Indian ABS market (trade sites and a vendor summary) are less reliable than filings, and we used them only for market-level facts.[20][22][23]

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule is the technical routing rule: a name that passes the red lines, qualifies on archetype and KPIs, and clears the asymmetry floor, but sits in a Stage 3 pullback, is not rejected. It is held for a limit order in a support zone.[27] BEPL passes every red line and scores 3.5 out of 5.0 on Layer 2. Its central-case reward-to-risk of 2.81 to 1 at the current price clears the 2.5 floor, but only just, and it depends on a central target that a margin-only stress cuts to about ₹113. The price is 0.9 average true ranges above its support of ₹105.04, which falls in the STANDARD band (up to 1.2), but all three momentum tests fail, so we do not buy at market.[26]

The entry zone is ₹105.0 to ₹108.40. The low end is the 20-day swing low at ₹105.04. The high end is 0.6 average true ranges above it, the edge of the FULL_SIZE band, which is the zone closest to support where a full-size position is allowed. The price is ₹1.65 above the top of the zone, so a further 1.5 percent fall would reach it.

The stop is ₹102.25. For the tactical bucket the rule is the lower of the swing low and the 20-day or 50-day average, minus half an average true range: 105.04 minus 0.5 times 5.58. That is just below the 200-day average of 102.69, so one level protects the structure twice. A close below it is the exit, and the name is rejected on structure.

The targets are ₹132 (central), ₹172 (bull) and ₹82 (bear). Reward-to-risk to the central target is 2.81 to 1 from ₹110.05 and 3.84 to 1 from ₹108.40, above the 2.5 floor in both cases. The order is valid only while the central target stays above about ₹124, the level that gives 2.5 to 1 at the top of the zone. If the target falls below that, withdraw the order.

The next heading, Layer 3, turns the decision into order levels and a position size, and the table under it lists each level with its rule. Position size follows the risk-unit rule. We assign TIER_4, the lowest tier, with a risk budget of 35 basis points, which is ₹0.35 of loss per ₹100 of the portfolio if the stop is hit. We chose the lowest tier because the Layer 2 score is only 3.5 of 5.0, the loan book and the expansion are open questions, and a margin-only stress already fails the reward-to-risk floor. Dividing 0.35 percent by the 5.67 percent distance from ₹108.40 to the stop gives 6.2 percent, which is above the TIER_4 ceiling of 2 percent, so the ceiling applies and the initial weight is 2.0 percent. If the stop is hit, the loss is about 0.11 percent of the portfolio.

The let-winners-run rule applies if the entry fills and the stock rises. We do not trim solely because RSI passes 70 or because the weight crosses 15 percent. A winner may grow to 22 percent of the portfolio while the stop trails the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average. We would trim only if the trend breaks or the loan book or the margin test in the triggers below fails.

The strategy bucket is TACTICAL_MOMENTUM, our bucket for trades that rely on trend and a catalyst. We did not move it to CORE_ALPHA, our bucket for multi-year compounders, because five-year sales are flat, the margin is cyclical and the growth project is one small step. A core holding needs a longer record of steady growth.
