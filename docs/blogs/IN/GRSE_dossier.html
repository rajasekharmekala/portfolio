Before you read

Garden Reach Shipbuilders & Engineers (GRSE) is a government-owned shipyard in Kolkata that builds warships for the Indian Navy and the Coast Guard. Its yearly profit has grown almost fourfold in four years, yet its stock of signed work has fallen by about 40 percent in 15 months and the share price is in a confirmed downtrend. This report concludes REJECTED for now. Three years of cash from operations are negative, the chart fails the turnaround test, and our central estimate of value sits below today's price. The conclusion would change if the roughly Rs 33,000 crore Next Generation Corvette contract is signed and the price stops falling[9][24][37].

1. What the company does

GRSE builds ships. It was founded in 1934 as Garden Reach Workshops, was nationalised in 1960, and today is a central government company under the Ministry of Defence. The government holds 74.5 percent of the shares, and the rest trades on the stock exchanges.[25][13] It runs three shipbuilding yards in Kolkata, has delivered 118 warships and more than 800 platforms, and is the only Indian yard building specialised research vessels.[25][16] Think of it as a very large, very specialised contractor whose main client is the same one that owns it.

The customer decides almost everything. About 76 percent of the signed work comes from the Indian Navy and the Coast Guard, and about 21 percent from commercial and export buyers such as a German shipping group and the West Bengal government.[25] In the year to March 2026 the company earned Rs 7,002 crore, of which about 93 percent came from shipbuilding, 4 percent from repair, and 3 percent from engineering products such as portable steel bridges.[25][2] Export revenue was Rs 270.70 crore, up from Rs 73.63 crore a year earlier, so exports are real but still only about 4 percent of sales.[10]

Money arrives in stages. The navy pays advances and milestone payments while the ship is built, so the shipyard holds other people's cash for years. On 31 March 2026 those customer advances, which accountants call contract liabilities, stood at Rs 5,751 crore, down from Rs 6,900 crore a year earlier.[8] The company recognises revenue as work is done, not when cash arrives. The chairman describes a slow start (design work is 5 to 10 percent of a project), a middle phase (hull building is 20 to 25 percent), and a steep final phase when engines, weapons and sensors are fitted.[17] This pattern explains why one quarter of profit tells you little, and why cash flow from one year can look odd.

A second source of income is interest. GRSE holds about Rs 3,500 crore in bank deposits and earned Rs 246 crore of interest in FY26, which is about a quarter of its pre-tax profit.[25][7] That is a real cash flow, but it is not shipbuilding, and it shrinks if the advances shrink. Keep this in mind when you read the profit numbers in chapter 3.

What a ship contract looks like

A warship contract is fixed in price, long in duration, and large. The P-17A frigate project, the biggest piece of the current order book, has Rs 4,519 crore left to bill, and the Next Generation Offshore Patrol Vessel project has Rs 3,075 crore left.[19] Because the price is fixed, a rise in steel, equipment or wage cost comes out of the shipyard's margin. The rating agency names this as the main weakness of the credit.[25] About 65 percent of a ship's cost is bought-in equipment such as engines, radars and weapons, and the customer often names the supplier, so GRSE acts as a platform integrator rather than a maker of every part.[20][19] This is why the company reverses cost provisions when a project nears completion: an earlier guess about cost turns out to be too high, and the extra is booked as profit, as with the Rs 433 crore release on the P-17A project in FY25.[24] We return to this in chapter 2B because it flattered FY25 and FY26 margins.

The orders still to come

The share price is built on one pending contract. GRSE is the lowest bidder (called L1) for five Next Generation Corvettes worth about Rs 33,000 crore, and price talks are finished, but the contract has not been signed.[9][17] Management also points to about Rs 80,000 crore of live navy and Coast Guard tenders, led by a Rs 70,000 crore P-17 Bravo frigate project, and to another Rs 70,000 crore of tenders expected in calendar 2026.[16] None of that is signed work. The company has missed its own signing dates before, which chapter 2B documents, so this report treats the pipeline as an option and not as an asset. For an owner, the point is that the share price already reflects hope for these tenders, while the filings show only the contracts already in hand.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Order book means contracts signed but not yet turned into sales; it is the shipyard's stock of future revenue.
  • Contract liabilities are customer advances that the company has received but not yet earned; they fund the work in progress.
  • L1 means the lowest bidder in a government tender; it normally wins the contract after negotiation.
  • Platform is the navy word for one vessel or craft; a four-ship project has four platforms.
  • Navratna is a status the government gives to successful public companies, allowing them to invest and form ventures with less approval.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

GRSE's competitive position is strong where it already has contracts and unproven where it does not. The company is the only Indian shipyard building specialised research vessels, it holds a nominated frigate contract that came without a tender, and it has delivered ships ahead of schedule.[16][25][10] In FY26 it delivered eight warships, up from four the year before, including three on a single day, and five of them were early.[10][11] Its revenue has grown for 17 quarters in a row, and the government rated its performance "Excellent" with a score of 94.35 out of 100 for FY25.[17][11] These facts are the evidence for the claim that the yard can build what it sells.

The balance sheet is the second strength. Borrowings were Rs 37 crore at March 2026, almost all lease liabilities, against shareholders' funds of Rs 2,626 crore.[4][11] CARE Ratings rates the bank facilities AAA with a stable outlook, counts cash and bank balances of Rs 3,511 crore, and puts interest cover at 35.75 times.[25] Return on capital employed (ROCE, the profit earned on each rupee of capital the business uses) reached 43 percent in FY26 after 27 percent in FY24.[4] A buyer of this stock does not carry a solvency risk. That is the reason the question in this report is about the cash cycle and the price, not about survival.

The weaknesses are about what comes next. The order book has fallen from Rs 22,680 crore in March 2025 to Rs 13,596 crore in June 2026, the first time in about ten years it has been below Rs 15,000 crore.[24][16] The yard can build about 28 vessels at once, with a limit of roughly 10,000 tonnes and 200 metres, so it cannot bid for the large merchant ships that India is now tendering without partners or a new yard.[18] The propulsion engines for warships are still imported, and the company says full indigenisation will take five to ten years.[17] Competitors Mazagon Dock and Cochin Shipyard earned higher margins in recent years on nominated and repair work, while GRSE's new orders will mostly be won by competitive bidding, which narrows margins.[24]

The market has noticed. On 22 October 2026 Goldman Sachs started coverage with a Sell rating and a Rs 1,515 target, arguing that record revenue now reflects peak billing on a shrinking backlog and that Indian shipbuilding is lumpy and slow to pay back.[30][31] DAM Capital took the other side on 7 October, saying shipyard margins and growth could peak in FY27 but that the correction in the shares had improved the risk and reward.[30] We weigh the Goldman point on the order book because it can be checked against the company's own filings, and we weigh the DAM point on valuation because that is what the price table in chapter 4 shows.

Governance and the government as owner

GRSE is a government company, so the owner also appoints the directors, sets pay under public-sector rules, and decides on share splits and bonus issues. The chairman told investors on 6 August that corporate actions such as a stock split wait for instructions from the Ministry and the disinvestment department.[19] The annual report records that the Audit Committee was not reconstituted during the year because there were not enough independent directors, and two independent directors were named on 6 October 2026, one of them effective 16 October.[13][2] This is a recurring weakness of public-sector boards and it is not an accounting concern by itself. It does mean minority holders have little influence. The promoter pledge reading is clean at 0.0 percent, because the promoter is the Government of India.[6] For a reader, the practical point is that decisions about capital, such as the Rs 4,400 crore expansion, are taken by the owner and not by the market.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 shows what the filings say about profit, cash and debt over five years, and then tests the numbers against the hard rules in the underwriting framework. Revenue grew from Rs 1,754 crore in FY22 to Rs 7,002 crore in FY26, a compound rate of about 40 percent over the last three years, and profit after tax grew from Rs 190 crore to Rs 748 crore.[2][11] On those two lines GRSE is one of the fastest-growing names in the defence sector.

Profit is not cash, however. Cash from operations (CFO, the cash the business generates from its own activity before financing and investing) was minus Rs 707 crore in FY24, plus Rs 16 crore in FY25 and minus Rs 290 crore in FY26.[14][15][7] Over those three years GRSE earned Rs 1,633 crore of profit and generated minus Rs 981 crore of operating cash, a ratio of minus 0.60. The framework requires at least 0.25 and prefers 0.50 to 0.70. The reason lies in how the yard is funded: customers' advances were large in FY23 (cash from operations was plus Rs 1,450 crore) and are now being used up as ships are delivered.[15][8]

Interest income hides part of the problem. Other income was Rs 274 crore in FY26, and profit before tax was Rs 1,005 crore, so about 27 percent of pre-tax profit comes from interest on deposits and similar items.[2] The operating margin (operating profit as a share of sales) was 11 percent in FY26, up from 7 to 8 percent in FY24 and FY25, but the rating agency says the improvement came partly from better fixed-cost absorption and partly from the reversal of excess cost provisions.[25] Quarterly profit shows the effect: Rs 154 crore, Rs 171 crore and Rs 303 crore in the last three quarters of FY26, then Rs 173 crore in Q1 FY27.[3]

The valuation sits in the middle. The price-to-earnings multiple is the price paid for each rupee of last-year profit. GRSE trades at 27.6 times, below its three-year median of 43.9 times, which is a lower multiple than any peer in the table at the end of this chapter.[1][5] But the median covers three years in which the order book stood between Rs 18,000 crore and Rs 23,000 crore, so a discount to that median is not a bargain by itself.[21][24] We read 27.6 times as the price of peak earnings on a falling order book, and we set our target using the current multiple, as the framework requires when it is the lower of the two.[38]
