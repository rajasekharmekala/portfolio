Before you read

Firstsource runs customer calls, claims, collections and back-office work for banks, health insurers, hospitals and phone companies, mostly in the US and UK. On 29 October 2026 it reported a quarter with sales up 22.2 percent and a higher floor for its yearly sales forecast[3]. The share price closed at 306.45 rupees that day, before the market could react, and it sits about 10.6 percent above its 10-week trend line[1]. Our call is to wait for a pullback into 272 to 282 rupees, where the reward is about three times the risk, instead of paying 306 rupees today. The call would be wrong if the margin gains stall, if healthcare keeps shrinking quarter on quarter, or if the price closes below 258 rupees.

1. What the company does & day-to-day vertical operations

Firstsource Solutions is a business process management company, often shortened to BPM. In plain words, large companies hand it a piece of their daily operations, and it runs that piece for a fee. The work includes answering customer calls, chasing unpaid bills, processing insurance claims and checking loan files.[3][17] The company says it serves clients across banking and financial services, healthcare, communications, media and technology, plus utilities and retail.[2]

It is part of the RP-Sanjiv Goenka group, an Indian conglomerate. The group holding vehicle, RPSG Ventures, owns 53.66 percent of the shares, and none of those shares are pledged as loan security.[13] The company says it has 39,937 employees and more than twenty-five years of experience.[3][17] Its 2026 reports call the new strategy “Intelligence That Operates”. That means pairing human agents with software agents that do part of the work.[18][19]

The business matters to an investor for one reason: its revenue is recurring and spread across thousands of small transactions. Calls, claims and collections do not stop in a slow quarter. Management said on the 29 October call that it does not expect the year-end furloughs that hit IT services firms, because its clients run mission-critical operations all year.[5] The flip side is that the work is people-heavy, and people cost money. We return to that in chapters 2B and 3.

Banking and financial services: collections and compliance at scale

This vertical is now the largest at 35.5 percent of sales.[4] The company says it serves 14 of the top 20 US mortgage lenders and servicers, 7 of the top 10 US credit card issuers and 4 of the top 6 UK retail banks.[4] The main jobs are collecting overdue payments, checking customers for fraud and money-laundering risk, and servicing mortgages from application to payment.

A day in the job looks like this. A bank sends a file of overdue accounts. Firstsource agents, many of them offshore or nearshore, call or message the customers, follow strict legal scripts and record every step for the regulator. Pricing is shifting from a fee per agent hour to a fee per interaction or per outcome, and management says more than half of revenue is now “non-linear”, meaning it does not rise one-for-one with headcount.[5] Cash comes in after the client pays the invoice, which management puts at about 67 to 69 days of sales outstanding on a normalised basis.[5]

The risk here is regulatory timing. A large UK collections deal slipped by a quarter because the regulator took longer than expected to approve it, and that cost the company growth in the March 2026 quarter.[8] The reward is size: banking grew 9 percent on the quarter in Q2, and management added headcount ahead of revenue for a major banking client.[5]

Healthcare: health plans on one side, hospitals on the other

Healthcare is 31.6 percent of sales and the weakest vertical this quarter.[4][5] The company serves 11 of the top 15 US health plans and more than 200 US health systems, according to its own presentation.[4] For health plans (payers), it processes claims, answers member calls and checks eligibility. For hospitals (providers), it runs the billing cycle: registering patients, coding the visit, sending the bill, fighting denied claims and collecting from patients.[20]

Some of the work is sold as “business process as a service”, a long contract where Firstsource runs the whole process and takes a share of the savings or recovery. These contracts ramp slowly and can unwind fast. In Q1 FY27 one such payer contract was wound down after a leadership change at the client, and the company took an exceptional charge.[7][6] Healthcare revenue fell 2 percent on the quarter at constant currency in both the June and September quarters. Its yearly growth slowed from 16 percent in March to 11 percent in June and 5 percent in September.[8][7][5] The next dated catalyst is a US rule change from January 2027, when Medicaid renewals for some adults move from yearly to every six months, which means more eligibility checks that Firstsource already runs at scale.[5]

Communications, media and technology: steady but lumpy

This vertical, at 20.6 percent of sales, handles customer care, billing queries and tech support for phone, broadband, TV and consumer-technology brands.[4] It serves, by the company's count, four of the top five US consumer-tech companies and one of the top two UK broadcasters.[4] Work comes in packets. A client launches a new phone or changes a price plan, call volumes jump, and revenue jumps with them.

That is why this line was flat on the quarter after a 4 percent fall in the March 2026 quarter and a 9 percent rise in the June quarter.[8][7][5] Management calls the swings normal. We treat them as noise unless two quarters in a row fall.

Diverse industries: utilities and retail, with seasonal peaks

Diverse industries, 12.3 percent of sales, covers UK energy and water utilities and retailers.[4] Retail volumes peak around the year-end shopping season, so the company hires extra people in the autumn. Management said it expects retail to help the December quarter and part of the March quarter.[5] Revenue here grew 25 percent year on year.[5] Management also counts two small purchases, Pastdue Credit Solutions in the UK utilities space and TeleMedik in Puerto Rico, as part of its growth bridge, adding about 1.5 points to FY26 growth.[8][9][23]

Plain-English glossary for this monograph

  • BPM is business process management: a company runs part of another firm's daily operations for a fee.
  • Constant currency growth strips out exchange-rate moves, so it shows how fast sales grew in the clients' own currencies. Firstsource grew 22.2 percent in rupees but 12.3 percent at constant currency in Q2 FY27, because the rupee weakened.
  • EBIT margin is operating profit before interest and tax, divided by revenue. Firstsource reports it at 12.5 percent for Q2 FY27.
  • Large deal is Firstsource's label for a contract with at least US$5 million of annual value.
  • Non-linear revenue is revenue priced on outcomes, platforms or transactions, not hours worked. Management says it is more than half of sales.
  • Attrition is the share of staff who leave in a year. Firstsource reports 28.4 percent for staff with more than 180 days of tenure.[3]
  • DSO (days sales outstanding) is how many days of sales sit unpaid in receivables.
  • Exceptional item is a one-off cost or gain that a company shows separately from regular profit.
  • Guidance is management's own forecast for the year ahead. Firstsource guides to sales growth and EBIT margin.
  • Operating margin is operating profit as a share of sales. Screener's version is slightly higher than the company's EBIT margin.
  • EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is a rough measure of cash profit from operations.
  • EPS is earnings per share, which means profit divided by the number of shares.
  • Price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share. It tells you how many years of today's profit the price equals.
  • ROCE is return on capital employed, which means operating profit as a share of the money invested in the business.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running costs and spending on equipment. A firm can pay dividends or cut debt from it.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates from its day-to-day work. Interest and lease payments are booked elsewhere in Firstsource's statements.
  • Net debt is borrowings minus cash. A low figure means the company could repay its loans quickly.
  • RSI is the relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has moved. Readings above 70 often mean the move is stretched.
  • ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price move. For FSL it is 11.50 rupees.
  • Stage 2 is Stan Weinstein's name for an advancing phase, with the price above a rising 30-week average. Stage 4 is the falling phase, with the price below falling 30-week and 50-day averages.
  • Base is a sideways stretch in the price before a move up. A pivot is a price level, often a recent high, that the stock must clear. A breakout is a close above that level. A retest is a later pullback to the same level, to see if it now holds as support.
  • Limit order is an order to buy only at a set price or lower. Reward-to-risk is the gain to the target price divided by the loss to the stop price.

2. Competitive moat, industry structure & external learning hub

A moat is whatever keeps customers from leaving and rivals from copying you. For a business-services firm, the moat is usually long client ties, rule-heavy know-how and a cost advantage from where the work is done. It is rarely a patent. This chapter weighs each of those for Firstsource, then lists where you can read and watch more yourself.

The evidence for a moat is strongest on client ties. The top five clients have been with the company for 22 years on average, and the company says they keep growing at the industry rate even as work moves offshore.[5] The share of sales from the top five clients fell from 32.5 percent in Q2 FY25 to 27.0 percent in Q2 FY27.[4] The count of clients paying over US$5 million a year rose from 26 to 51 in the same period.[4] More, bigger clients make the revenue base harder to knock over.

What protects the business

First, domain depth in regulated work. Banks and health plans need agents who follow legal scripts and keep audit trails. The UK collections deal needed regulator approval before it could start, which shows how much rule-following sits inside the job.[8] That is hard for a new entrant to copy quickly.

Second, a delivery network spread over India, the Philippines, South Africa, Mexico, Romania and other places, which lets the company move work to where it costs less.[3] About 80 percent of gross hiring is offshore or nearshore, and India and the Philippines made up more than 85 percent of the Q2 headcount increase.[5] Third, the Kairos software layer. Management says its intelligent-context part is attached to almost half of its top 50 clients and that a client went from demo to live in eight weeks.[5] We treat that as promising but unproven, because the company has not published what Kairos earns or what it cost to build.[5]

What does not protect it

The work is still mostly people, and people are easy to hire for any competitor with the same pay. Attrition is high: 28.4 percent for staff with more than 180 days of service in the press release, and 34.0 percent in the presentation table, which uses a different base that the company does not explain.[3][4] Each leaver must be replaced and trained, and that cost sits inside the margin.

Artificial intelligence cuts both ways. Management says clients are asking for outcome-based pricing, and that on some renewals the company kept revenue flat by taking on extra work to offset price cuts.[5] An analyst on the call asked whether AI is shrinking deal sizes at peers. The CEO did not rule it out. He said the answer is “yes and no”.[5] That is an honest answer, and it is also the central unresolved question for this sector.

Industry structure and who the rivals are

Firstsource cites a US$220 billion global BPO market growing at 4 to 5 percent a year, and says AI widens the target to a far larger services-as-software pool.[4] Growth of 4 to 5 percent is the industry rate. Firstsource's 12.3 percent constant currency growth is about two to three times that rate, which is why the company calls its guidance top-decile.[5] Larger global rivals include Genpact, EXL, Concentrix and Teleperformance; we name them from general industry knowledge and did not pull their numbers for this report.

Listed Indian peers are smaller or weaker on returns. eClerx earns a 34.4 percent ROCE (return on capital employed) and trades at 21.5 times earnings. Sagility, a healthcare specialist, earns 13.4 percent and trades at 19.7 times.[25][27] Hinduja Global Solutions earns 1.46 percent and is not a useful comparison today.[26] The next table lists places where you can read the sources, watch management speak and follow the filings.[36][37]

Is the company reaching its potential & are the CEOs walking the talk?

Walk-the-talk means checking what management promised one to three years ago against what happened. The CEO, Ritesh Idnani, joined in the middle of FY24. In his words, the plan was a US$1 billion revenue run rate by Q4 of FY26, flat margins in FY25, then 50 to 75 basis points of margin gain each year for three years, heading for a 14 to 15 percent EBIT band.[8] A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point.

On the first two promises the record is good. Firstsource crossed the US$1 billion run rate in Q4 of FY25, a year early.[8][11] FY25 margin was flat at 11.0 percent.[11] FY26 margin was 11.7 percent, up about 74 basis points.[8] H1 FY27 margin is 12.4 percent.[4] The 14 to 15 percent band is the open promise, and it is a multi-year one.

On the yearly guidance the record is mixed. In April 2025 management guided FY26 growth of 12 to 15 percent at constant currency and a margin of 11.25 to 12 percent.[11] In February 2026 it raised the growth range to 14.5 to 15.5 percent.[9] The company then delivered 13.6 percent, below the raised range, and blamed a UK regulatory delay and Medicare Advantage program pauses.[8] The margin of 11.7 percent stayed inside the band.[8] The lesson is that a raised forecast is not the same as a met forecast.

Is Firstsource reaching its potential? On growth and margin, yes, and ahead of schedule on the first goal. It is a US$1.1 billion business growing at a pace its peers do not match, and H1 EBIT grew 33.4 percent against sales growth of 22.5 percent.[3][4] On cash, the answer is not yet. The first half showed a Rs 3,390 million working-capital drain, and the receivable balance grew 12.9 percent in six months.[4] On capital allocation, management has made small acquisitions and paid out 57 percent of FY26 profit as dividends, and the goodwill from older purchases is 44.2 percent of total assets.[2][4] Governance looks clean on the surface: the promoter holding has stayed at 53.66 percent for years, with no pledge.[13] Independent summaries of the release and presentation agree on the headline figures.[34][35] Layer 2 score: 4.0 out of 5. What this means for an owner: the business is executing, and the main thing to monitor is cash. The next chapter puts numbers on that.

3. Financial trajectory & valuation sanity check

This chapter lays out five years of sales, profit, cash and debt, then compares the static level of each number with its direction. Static means where the number stands today. Trajectory means how fast and in which direction it is moving. A cheap company with a rising trajectory is more interesting than a good company with a falling one.[2]

One data note first. TradingView shows a price-to-earnings ratio of 32.19, and Screener shows 25.4.[1][2] The gap comes from one-off charges. Reported profit for the last twelve months includes the Q3 FY26 labour-code charge and the Q1 FY27 charges, so it is lower than the underlying profit.[4] We use 25.0 times, based on four quarters of adjusted earnings of Rs 12.25 per share (Q3 FY26 Rs 2.87, Q4 FY26 Rs 2.91, Q1 FY27 Rs 3.16 and Q2 FY27 Rs 3.31, as computed in our script).[9][8][6][3]

5. Multi-horizon investment view: short-term momentum, medium-term catalysts & scenarios

A good business can still be a poor purchase at the wrong price. This chapter asks three things. What growth is today's price already assuming? The first answer comes from a reverse DCF. DCF stands for discounted cash flow. A normal DCF predicts cash and finds a price. 6. Key risks, thesis breakers & bear case

Every estimate stands on one weak assumption, and the useful question is which evidence would show it to be wrong and when we will see that evidence. We take the estimates from chapters 3 and 5 in turn. Then we state the single claim the thesis hinges on, and the strongest case against us.

Weakest assumption in the EPS estimate: margin keeps rising while headcount runs ahead of revenue

Our FY27 and FY28 EPS rest on margins reaching about 12.5 percent this year and 13.1 percent next year. In Q2 headcount grew 8.3 percent on the quarter, while constant-currency revenue grew 2.0 percent.[4][5] Management says the extra people are for a major banking client whose revenue comes later, and that senior staff pay rises land in Q3.[5] If revenue does not follow in the next two quarters, the margin gain stalls.

Evidence it is wrong: Q3 EBIT margin below 12.25 percent, the guided floor, or constant-currency growth under 2 percent for two quarters. A third fall in healthcare revenue would make it worse. We will know in early February 2027, when Q3 FY27 results are due. Last year the Q3 release came on 3 February, and no Q3 FY27 date was confirmed in our sources.

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF: that free cash flow is the right base

The headline free cash flow ignores about Rs 470 crore of interest and lease payments each year. We showed both bases. Our verdict does not rest on the cheaper one.[4] The working-capital drain in H1 was Rs 3,390 million, and receivables grew 12.9 percent in six months while Q2 revenue was only 9.4 percent above the March quarter.[4] Evidence it is wrong: receivables rising again in Q3 while cash flow from operations stays below profit. We will know in February.

Weakest assumption in the target multiple: that 22 times is available for a people-heavy services firm

We use 22 times FY28 EPS, which sits above two listed peers.[25][27] If investors decide AI will cut deal sizes across the sector, services multiples can compress even while earnings rise. An analyst on the 29 October call raised exactly that point about peers.[5] Evidence it is wrong: a peer cutting guidance for AI-linked price cuts, or Firstsource reporting a falling revenue per employee. There is no fixed date. The earnings season in late January and early February is the next place to see it.
