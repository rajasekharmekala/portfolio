1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Stock Cycles

Core Investor Question: Which parts of today’s growth are the product of a durable franchise, and which are the result of a new portfolio mix?

Let us walk backward before we walk forward. Firstsource was incorporated in 2001 and became a listed company in 2007. Its earliest economic identity was a classic business-process outsourcer: take work that a bank, insurer, telecom operator, or media company performs internally, standardize it, and deliver it through a managed workforce. The client received lower cost and predictable service levels; Firstsource received recurring contract revenue and the chance to expand its scope. That formula was not new, but it was difficult to execute across languages, regulations, and time zones. The company’s evolution is best understood as a steady move from transaction processing toward domain-led operations, with acquisitions used to add geographies and workflow expertise. That is a strategic direction, not proof of a software-like moat. [5]

In the early 2010s, the company used acquisitions to deepen US healthcare and financial-services operations. MedAssist brought medical claims and revenue-cycle capabilities; later purchases added mortgage servicing and broader customer-management work. You should notice the trade-off. A purchased workflow can bring customer contracts, trained staff, and local credibility faster than building from scratch, but the buyer also inherits integration work, intangible assets, debt or cash outlay, and a need to retain the acquired managers. The acquisition thesis works only if new capabilities cross-sell into the group and make the customer relationship broader. If the units remain separate or the expected contracts shrink, goodwill records yesterday’s purchase price while tomorrow’s cash flow bears the shortfall. [8][9]

The ISGN transaction is a useful case study in portfolio ambition. Firstsource acquired a mortgage-services platform in the mid-2010s, enlarging its exposure to US loan origination and servicing. That area can be attractive when refinancing and home sales are active: lenders process more applications, require verification and document handling, and may outsource the administrative burden. But mortgage volumes are cyclical and interest-rate sensitive. When rates rise, refinancing demand can fall abruptly, leaving providers to absorb fixed costs or seek replacement work. The later push to diversify beyond mortgage dependence is therefore not merely a marketing pivot; it is an attempt to reduce the amplitude of one customer-budget cycle. You should judge the pivot by subsequent revenue mix and client wins, not by the announcement date. [9]

The acquisition ledger

For each acquisition, write down the capability bought, the customers retained, the cross-sell achieved, the cash paid, and the carrying value left on the balance sheet. If the public record does not disclose a purchase-level return, say so and use group cash flows as the test. A deal rationale is a hypothesis; integration and return on invested capital are its scorecard.

The ten-year financial arc from FY17 to FY20 was one of measured scale-up rather than an uninterrupted boom. Consolidated sales moved from ₹3,556 crore in FY17 to ₹4,099 crore in FY20, while reported net profit moved from ₹279 crore to ₹340 crore. The business faced normal telecom and banking budget cycles, wage inflation, currency movement, and the challenge of balancing onshore client proximity against offshore labour economics. The market generally gives an outsourcer a higher multiple when its growth appears predictable and a lower one when customers delay discretionary projects. We do not have a verified daily share-price series in this evidence pack for every historic turning point, so I will not invent exact peak-to-trough returns. The financial cadence gives us the more reliable cycle markers. [10][11]

FY21 was the sharpest visible scale change in the supplied decade table: revenue rose to ₹5,078 crore, up 24% over FY20, while operating profit advanced to ₹839 crore. Healthcare became strategically more important as payers and providers needed remote claims, patient support, and revenue-cycle work during a public-health shock. Some demand was structural; some was pulled forward by extraordinary operating conditions. Here is the first discipline I want you to borrow from credit analysis: one exceptional year does not establish a new normal. Watch what happened after the emergency, whether new client work survived the reopening, and whether the margin remained. In FY23 operating profit fell from the prior year even though sales grew modestly, demonstrating that a larger revenue base can still produce uneven operating leverage. [11]

FY22 revenue grew to ₹5,921 crore, then FY23 was nearly flat at ₹6,022 crore. FY24 reached ₹6,336 crore, a restrained increase, before FY25 accelerated to ₹7,980 crore and FY26 to ₹9,556 crore. That more recent step-up reflects both organic demand and acquisitions; the distinction matters because acquired revenue arrives with a purchase price and may carry different margins. The three clocks of M&A

Acquisitions run on three clocks: revenue recognition begins first; operational integration takes longer; cash return on the full price takes longest. A rising revenue chart can look successful while the return clock is still behind. When you compare FY growth, separate acquired from constant-currency organic growth and ask whether balance-sheet goodwill is earning its keep.

Across the cycles, the same pattern repeats. Firstsource has repeatedly used specialist knowledge and acquisition to widen its addressable work, then relied on execution to integrate the pieces. When volumes expand, fixed delivery capacity can support margin. When programs transition, customer budgets slow, or a purchased operation underdelivers, that leverage works in reverse. This is why we will track receivables, cash flow, and goodwill alongside revenue. A new chapter in the corporate story is meaningful only when it changes those old economic constraints. You and I are not buying the origin story; we are buying the next decade of cash flows. [9][10]

3. Distribution, Supply Chain & Unit Economics

Core Investor Question: How does Firstsource find work, transition it, price it, deliver it, and turn invoices into owner cash?

A manufacturer ships a product through wholesalers; Firstsource sells a transition and an operating promise. There is no dealer network in the physical-goods sense. The route to market is enterprise selling: industry specialists identify a large process problem, account teams map the client’s operating chain, technology and risk teams design a solution, procurement negotiates the contract, and delivery leaders plan the transition. The sales cycle can be long because a bank or health plan must authorize data access, security, compliance, workforce locations, and continuity. The company defines a large deal as one with annual contract value above US$5 million. That threshold gives us a scale marker, but a deal’s ACV is not the same as recognized revenue, booked cash, or profit. Ramps, transition periods, and client volumes determine when it becomes economic. [2]

The most important “distribution channel” is the existing customer relationship. A provider already running collections can propose fraud operations, customer service, analytics, or a second geography to the same client. Expansion can reduce customer-acquisition cost because the vendor already knows the client’s systems and risk controls. The danger is concentration: one renewal or scope reduction can remove a large block of revenue, and a broad relationship can still be repriced aggressively at renewal. New-logo wins diversify the funnel but initially consume leadership, hiring, security, and transition effort. In Q2, 17 new logos were reported, including strategic accounts; strategic status means management sees potential for at least US$5 million of annual revenue, not that the target is already achieved. Track the conversion from first project to scaled relationship, not the logo count alone. [3][4]

A typical program moves through discovery, process documentation, technology and access setup, knowledge transfer, parallel running, acceptance, and steady-state operations. On the customer side, domain owners define rules and exceptions. Firstsource’s transition manager creates training plans, staffing schedules, quality measures, escalation rights, and a cutover calendar. The first months can be expensive: both old and new teams may run in parallel, managers must repair process gaps, and early productivity can lag. If the contract charges full rates only after acceptance, the supplier can carry start-up cost before revenue reaches mature levels. If a large migration slips, it can defer growth while leaving fixed people and facilities underutilized. The Q2 headcount increase partly reflected associates moving from a major banking client; that may secure continuity, but the source of headcount growth matters when interpreting utilization. [4]

Delivery is geographically distributed, but the map is not a simple wage-arbitrage grid. Onshore teams can handle sensitive interactions, specialized decisions, client workshops, or language needs. Nearshore centers can offer time-zone overlap and cultural familiarity; offshore teams can deliver large volumes at lower cost. Firstsource lists operations across the US, UK, India, Philippines, Mexico, Romania, Trinidad and Tobago, South Africa, and Australia. Each additional geography brings hiring pools and resilience, but also local labour laws, data rules, currency exposure, and manager complexity. The company’s investor presentation shows roughly 42.3% offshore and nearshore revenue in Q2 FY27, so a majority remained onshore. A move offshore may raise gross margin only if service quality holds and the customer does not capture all of the cost saving in a lower price. [5][6]

Pricing is contract-specific and rarely public. In a traditional arrangement, the buyer may pay per full-time equivalent, per hour, per transaction, or for a defined service bundle with minimum volume. A more automated engagement may shift to per claim, per resolved case, per collected balance, or a hybrid containing a fixed platform fee and outcome component. Firstsource says it wants to underwrite outcomes rather than effort, and management has discussed better margins for outcome-linked constructs. But public materials do not disclose the proportion of revenue under each model, the actual risk-sharing formula, or realized unit economics. So we distinguish a strategic objective from proven pricing power. The strongest evidence would be renewal growth, stable price per unit, quality-adjusted savings, and cash receipts despite lower labour content. [6]

Distribution models around the world

US-based Concentrix combines large direct enterprise accounts with multi-country delivery, showing the value and complexity of a global footprint. India’s WNS was acquired by France’s Capgemini in 2025, demonstrating how a specialist BPS channel can become a strategic route for a larger technology integrator. Europe’s Capita has repeatedly reshaped its portfolio as public and private buyers reconsider what to outsource. These cases underline that enterprise distribution is built on trust, transition discipline, and renewal economics—not on a dealer count.

A good distribution engine makes the buyer comfortable transferring sensitive work and makes the vendor hard to remove after acceptance. Here is the unit-economics question you and I will carry into the next chapters: when Firstsource automates a task, who captures the saving? A reduction in headcount is not automatically productivity, and a larger ACV is not automatically value. Only durable cash per client relationship closes the loop. [5][6]

4. Industry Structure, Competitive Moat & External Learning Hub

Core Investor Question: What makes the customer stay with Firstsource after the first contract, and what could make it leave?

Let us define the battlefield without euphemism. Firstsource competes with specialized business-process companies, technology-led operations firms, large consulting and IT-services integrators, local contact-centre operators, and the client’s own shared-service center. The rival list changes by vertical. In healthcare, the relevant competitor may know payer claims and payment integrity. In banking, a global systems integrator may bundle software and operations. In customer experience, a low-cost multilingual center may quote a cheaper per-contact rate. The customer may also choose to automate the task internally. This fragmented structure is a positive for an agile specialist: no single rival owns every process. It is a negative because buyers can benchmark vendors, split work across suppliers, or bring repeatable tasks in-house. [5]

Firstsource’s strongest possible moat is accumulated process knowledge coupled with implementation risk. A bank does not replace a team that understands exceptions, scripts, permissions, controls, and escalation paths without a transition plan. A health plan cannot move claims handling overnight if inaccurate decisions create appeals, provider abrasion, or regulatory exposure. The switching cost is real when the process is well integrated and the supplier’s knowledge is embedded in workflows. But it is not necessarily permanent. Contracts expire, client systems change, managers rotate, and competing platforms can make migration easier. The moat is therefore conditional and operational: service levels, clean audit records, staff stability, client satisfaction, and successful change requests have to renew it each year. [16][17]

Switching cost is a customer cost

When management says client relationships are long-standing, ask what practical cost a client would incur to switch: revalidation, retraining, data migration, service disruption, audit sign-off, or missed revenue. Then ask whether that cost is large relative to the annual savings offered by a competitor. The moat is the gap between those two figures, not the relationship’s age alone.

Scale creates a second advantage. A multi-vertical workforce can spread training systems, analytics, cyber controls, and management overhead across programs. A global footprint can provide language coverage and resilience if one location is disrupted. Yet scale can also be a burden. Onshore delivery carries higher labour and facility costs; offshore operations require recruiting and retention; each regulated geography adds security and employment obligations. If clients capture every wage or automation saving, the supplier’s scale may produce more volume but not more economic rent. The most useful question is not whether Firstsource has many centers, but whether utilization, quality, and realized revenue per employee improve without causing client churn or control failures. [17]

The industry is undergoing a bargaining-power shift. Generative AI can summarize interactions, suggest next steps, classify documents, and draft responses; agentic systems may execute steps across enterprise software. These tools may reduce unit labour, but they also create new work in data governance, process redesign, testing, exception handling, and model oversight. FSL’s Kairos proposition attempts to package workflow knowledge, orchestration, and people into an operating system for client processes. That can raise relevance if clients want one accountable operator. It can also invite substitution if the client buys a software platform directly and treats the vendor as interchangeable implementation labour. The outcome depends on who owns the client data, workflow logic, and measurable result—not on the presence of an AI label. [24][25]

A thoughtful competitor can attack Firstsource on price, specialization, or integration. Concentrix brings global customer-experience scale. Genpact combines process operations with analytics and technology capabilities. EXL is strong in data-led operations and insurance/healthcare domains. Sagility is a focused healthcare administration peer. eClerx sells specialist analytics and operations. Teleperformance operates globally in customer experience with multilingual delivery. Large IT integrators can package business-process services beside cloud migration and systems work; captive centers can insource core processes. These are not interchangeable peers, so we should compare economics within each vertical rather than average their growth rates. The number of named logos is not evidence that Firstsource has won market share unless the client or analyst data says so. [25]

Pricing power is similarly uneven. A highly regulated, exception-heavy workflow can support higher switching cost, especially when the provider has accumulated historical data and demonstrated accuracy. A high-volume voice queue with standardized scripts is easier to benchmark and automate. Client procurement typically retains leverage because contracts can be rebid and large enterprises have multiple vendors. The company’s move toward outcome-based models might improve the economic share captured by Firstsource, but only if the metric is within its control and the definition of success is clearly specified. Outcome contracts can also transfer downside risk: a collection rate, claim outcome, or customer satisfaction measure can move with external factors. The most defensible evidence would be disclosed client-level renewals, stable pricing, and margin gains net of transition investment. [26][27]

Three competitive archetypes

US-based EXL illustrates a data-and-domain specialist model; India-based eClerx illustrates analytics-rich operations; France-based Teleperformance illustrates global CX scale. Capgemini’s completed purchase of WNS adds a European technology integrator to the list of competitors that can bundle process, data, and systems. For Firstsource, the lesson is to defend the workflow and outcome rather than compete only on hourly labour cost.

The portfolio has some diversification but remains exposed to concentrated markets and clients. In Q2 FY27, North America represented 65.6% of revenue and Europe, Middle East, and Africa 32.7%; the rest was small. The company’s FY26 presentation also disclosed a meaningful top-client revenue share, though that metric should be read with its exact period and definition. High concentration makes a few relationships economically important. Long tenure can reduce the chance of immediate loss, but it can also make the buyer more sophisticated at extracting price concessions. A customer relationship is an asset only if the renewal terms preserve a fair return. If a large program transitions out, Firstsource may face idle capacity before it finds replacement work. [27]

Regulation cuts both ways. In US healthcare and financial services, privacy, auditability, consumer-protection rules, and permissible data access make process changes more deliberate. This can deter a casual entrant that lacks controls. It also raises compliance costs and the potential severity of an operational incident. In Europe, data-protection and outsourcing rules add contractual oversight. In India, labour and tax rules affect domestic delivery. No single regulation gives Firstsource a permanent barrier; the advantage is a record of control execution across jurisdictions. A buyer will still compare providers on security certifications, disaster recovery, data location, subcontractor oversight, and remediation history. We should track disclosed incidents, audit opinions, regulatory notices, and contract penalties rather than assume the absence of headlines means risk is absent. [28][29]

I would call Firstsource’s moat moderate, not wide. There are meaningful relationship and process-integration costs, especially in complex healthcare and financial operations. The company has acquired relevant capabilities and broadened its geography, but its revenue still depends on human delivery, client renewals, and the economics of each transition. AI could strengthen the company if Firstsource owns the redesign and monetizes outcomes; AI could weaken it if clients use automation to lower the vendor’s billable volumes. Our conclusion will change only when we see retention, cross-sell, stable pricing, attrition control, and cash conversion together. You do not need certainty about who wins every industry contest. You need to know whether the company’s current defenses are strong enough to justify its price. [30][36]

Moat scorecard

Score the moat through five observable items: renewal and expansion, pricing per unit, client concentration, transition success, and cash conversion. A company can look differentiated in a market presentation yet fail this scorecard if renewals require discounts or DSO keeps widening. One good quarter is evidence; repeated behaviour is a moat.

5. Leading KPIs, Structural Potential & CEO Walk-the-Talk Audit

Core Investor Question: Which operating signals should improve before reported earnings, and how has management performed against its own promises?

We now put management on a scorecard. A growth business should have leading indicators that tell you whether revenue and profit are likely to follow. Firstsource offers several: large deals, new logos, strategic accounts, pipeline, headcount, vertical growth, attrition, DSO, and margin. The danger is selecting only the flattering ones. Deal count says the sales engine is active; it says nothing about ramp speed or price. Headcount says delivery is being staffed; it can be transferred employees or new hires. A margin rise can reflect utilization, currency, mix, or a one-time comparison. Our job is to connect each leading KPI to the financial line it should eventually move and identify the delay between cause and result. [1]

First is constant-currency revenue growth. Reported rupee growth in Q2 FY27 was 22.2%, but management reported 12.3% in constant currency and 11.7% in US-dollar terms. The rupee number contains translation and mix effects, so it is not the cleanest measure of demand. H1 FY27 constant-currency growth was also 12.3%. FY27 guidance is 10.5–13%, with the lower bound raised after Q2. Management expects H2 to be stronger than H1, supported by deal ramps, transitions, seasonal retail demand, and new logos. That outlook is falsifiable: upcoming quarters should show sequential growth in the underlying currency measure, not merely a larger rupee translation benefit. [2][3]

A rising employee base can be a sign of growth, but if revenue per employee stalls or attrition remains high, management spends time and money replacing trained staff. Automation can reduce repeatable workload, yet it also changes skill needs and requires reskilling. A better workforce dashboard would show attrition by geography and vertical, training time, absenteeism, revenue per employee, and the share of work handled with automation. Public information is incomplete, so we avoid claiming that headcount scale itself is a productivity moat. [6]

How peers signal execution

US-based Genpact now reports an advanced-technology solutions business beside core services, so investors can watch mix as well as revenue. India’s eClerx reports stronger specialist margins than a broad contact-centre operator, making productivity and expertise visible in results. France’s Capgemini communicates synergy goals after buying WNS; the test is whether cross-sell and operating leverage appear in segment numbers, not just acquisition rationale.

Fifth is cash conversion. CFO Dinesh Jain said on the Q2 call, “Normalized DSO continued to be in the range of 67-69 days.” The half-year cash-flow statement showed CFO of ₹4,969 million, below ₹5,927 million in the prior-year half, with a larger working-capital outflow. These figures are not necessarily contradictory: the normalized DSO definition can exclude items reflected in the consolidated working-capital cash-flow line. But the gap demands reconciliation. The balance sheet’s trade receivables increased to ₹23,471 million by September. If revenue growth pulls more cash into unpaid invoices, then a reported margin gain may not yet represent owner cash. The cash KPI must improve with the sales KPI, not trail it indefinitely. [16][18]

Sixth is vertical balance and customer breadth. BFS and Healthcare together represented 67.1% of Q2 revenue, while North America was 65.6% of geography mix. CMT and Diverse Industries can lower concentration if they grow organically and renew at similar economics. New logos in UK banking, US healthcare, student lending, and digital banking indicate a varied pipeline, but most client identities and deal prices are not disclosed. We can therefore track mix, client revenue buckets, and concentration disclosures without pretending to know an individual account’s profitability. A growing logo count is most valuable when existing clients expand while no single account becomes a larger share of the whole. [18]

Now audit management’s record. The FY26 investor presentation described revenue guidance in the mid-teens, including acquisition contribution, while the eventual constant-currency growth figure was 13.6%. On that stated basis, the lower end of the earlier 14.5–15.5% band was not reached. The result still represented healthy growth, and acquisition timing or foreign exchange can explain part of the difference, but investors should record the miss rather than retroactively reinterpret the target. FY26’s 11.7% EBIT margin landed within management’s 11.5–12.0% band. In FY27, the current H1 growth and margin readings are broadly on track with the revised guidance, but the company explicitly depends on stronger H2. This is a mixed but not alarming walk-the-talk record: credible on margin, less precise on top-line pacing. [22][23]

A fair walk-the-talk test

Freeze the guidance as it was stated at that time, preserve its currency and acquisition assumptions, and compare it with the period-end result. Do not quietly replace an old target with a newer one. A miss can be reasonable if management explains the driver and resets early; repeated misses without reconciliation are a credibility cost.

Management’s longer-term language also deserves a proper clock. The move from 11.7% FY26 EBIT margin to 14–15% over the next two to three years would require continued efficiency, higher-value work, and careful integration. If the path is roughly 50–75 basis points of annual improvement, then quarterly noise should be assessed over several periods. The company should not be judged against the aspiration as if it were a contractual guarantee; we should judge it against the observable steps: margin, cash conversion, proportion of technology-led work, renewal economics, and delivery mix. A transformation story becomes credible when its operating markers move before management repeats the slogan. [23]

Our management verdict is cautiously constructive but evidence-led. The sales engine has genuine momentum; the current H1 margin is encouraging; and leadership has raised the revenue floor after a solid Q2. At the same time, FY26 growth guidance was missed at the low end, normalized DSO and reported debtor days are not reconciled in public materials, attrition remains high, and the medium-term margin aspiration has yet to be fully delivered. As you listen to the next call, write down the promises and the dates. Then compare them with the audited or reported figures. This scorecard provides a direct basis for evaluating management credibility against reported outcomes. [1][2]

Leading KPI to financial proof

Deal wins should lead to ramped revenue; ramped revenue should lead to margin; margin should lead to CFO after working capital; and CFO should fund capex, debt reduction, or distributions. If any arrow repeatedly breaks, find the reason before moving your earnings forecast higher.

The current ratio can therefore look weak without implying near-term insolvency, but liquidity remains tied to cash generation and access to working-capital facilities. When we compare peers, we should not mix enterprise values that include lease liabilities with those that exclude them without adjustment. [11]

Capital returns have been respectable but require context. Screener reports return on capital employed around 15% in FY25 and 17% in FY26, while the company’s presentation reports FY26 ROCE near 17.7% and H1 FY27 annualized around 20.2%; definitions and annualization differ. As an investor, you need a hurdle rate. We use 10–12% as an analyst WACC range for sensitivity, not as a company-disclosed cost of capital and not as a precisely estimated beta-based number. Reported returns appear above that illustrative hurdle, but acquisitions inflate goodwill and operating leases complicate invested capital. The economic question is whether incremental returns on new acquisitions and AI investments stay above the hurdle, not whether one headline ROCE exceeds it. [12][13]

Share count is comparatively stable in the long table: equity capital remains around ₹697 crore from FY22 to FY26, with modest employee-option issuance rather than a large equity-funded deal. Promoter ownership stayed near 53.66% through September 2026; domestic institutional ownership increased while foreign institutional ownership declined from its earlier level. This is not a guarantee of governance quality, but it suggests recent growth was not funded by a major new share issuance. We still track ESOP dilution, especially because a technology-oriented transition depends on retaining skilled employees. The appropriate measure is fully diluted share count and stock-based compensation relative to operating profit; headline equity capital is only a rough proxy. [13]

Our financial-quality verdict is balanced. Firstsource has delivered a decade of sales growth, positive profit, and meaningful free cash flow; the business is not an accounting illusion. Yet cash generation is lumpy, receivable days have risen, H1 FY27 working capital weakened, and acquired goodwill is now a large balance-sheet asset. FY26’s FCF recovery is encouraging, but the half-year comparison reminds us that cash conversion can reverse. We would call earnings quality sound but working-capital-sensitive. If cash from operations remains above net profit over a full cycle and DSO stabilizes, confidence rises. If revenue keeps outpacing collections and acquisitions add goodwill without strong returns, the multiple should compress even if reported growth remains attractive. [4][5]

Four checks before trusting EPS

Check earnings against four companions: cash from operations, free cash flow after capex, receivable days, and goodwill/lease-adjusted invested capital. EPS that grows with worsening receivables or falling incremental returns deserves a lower confidence multiple. EPS that converts to cash while reinvestment earns above the cost of capital is what compounds.

One final forensic adjustment is currency and tax. Firstsource invoices many customers in dollars and pounds while paying a large share of delivery costs in rupees and other local currencies. Hedges reduce some near-term translation volatility, but they do not remove the long-term mismatch between contract currency and delivery cost. A weakening rupee can lift reported revenue and margin even when local-currency productivity is unchanged; a strengthening rupee can do the opposite. The effective tax rate also changes with geography and exceptional items. When you build a normalized earnings view, hold exchange rates and tax assumptions separately from volume and margin. Otherwise you may credit the operating team for a currency tailwind or blame it for a translation headwind. The long-run test remains real cash earned across currencies after local reinvestment. A reverse DCF lets us ask what the current enterprise value needs rather than pretending to know the correct future. We start with FY26 free cash flow of approximately ₹1,082 crore, market capitalization near ₹20,223 crore, and company-reported net debt of ₹1,532 crore at September 2026, giving a simplified enterprise value around ₹21,756 crore before lease adjustments. We assume a 4.5% terminal growth rate and hold free-cash-flow growth constant for ten years, then calculate the growth that equates discounted future cash flows to the current enterprise value. This deliberately simple model is not a forecast. It isolates the expectations embedded in today’s price and makes the discount-rate sensitivity visible. [27]

Using an illustrative 11% WACC, the model implies roughly 7.5% annual free-cash-flow growth for ten years, followed by 4.5% terminal growth. At 10% WACC the implied rate is about 5.2%; at 12% it is about 9.6%. These calculations use FY26 company-presented CFO less capex as the cash-flow starting point, and exclude lease obligations from net debt, so the output is a sensitivity rather than a precise intrinsic value. The interpretation is more useful than a single number: the market does not require extraordinary FCF growth if the discount rate is low, but it requires almost 10% if capital costs are higher. If cash conversion remains weak or lease-adjusted leverage is materially higher, the true hurdle rises. [28][29]
