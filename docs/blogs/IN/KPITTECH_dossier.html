1. What the company does

KPIT Technologies is a global engineering and software company that serves one industry: mobility. In plain terms, a car used to be mostly steel and an engine. Today it is also a rolling computer, with chips, sensors, screens and code that must work for fifteen years. Vehicle makers (the industry calls them OEMs, short for original equipment manufacturers) need armies of software engineers to build, test and update that code. KPIT supplies those engineers and some ready-made software tools. The company describes itself as a partner for software-defined vehicles, with development centres in Europe, the USA, Japan, China, Thailand and India[2]. It says it has worked on more than 2,000 vehicle production programs and that its software powers more than 20 million vehicles[3].

The customers are the car and truck makers and the large parts suppliers that sell to them, called Tier 1 suppliers. One third-party analysis puts roughly four-fifths of revenue in passenger cars, with Europe the largest region[34]. The company's own Q2 FY27 numbers show the split moving. Passenger-car revenue fell 7.8% from a year earlier, while commercial vehicles, which means trucks plus construction, mining and farm machines, grew 27.3%[16]. Management groups trucks and off-highway machines together because both are starting the same shift that cars made about four years ago: from hardware-first to software-defined designs[7].

Here is how the money works. KPIT mostly sells engineering time and finished projects. Fixed-price programs, where the client pays a set sum for a result, have risen to about 70% of the work[6][7], and management likes them because AI tools can then cut KPIT's own cost without cutting its price[7]. The company also sells products. These include an AI platform for vehicle makers called Beacon, an in-car gaming product, after-sales and diagnostics software, validation tools called Technica and a cybersecurity business, Cymotive, that it is buying in stages[8][19]. In Q2 FY27 products grew 25% from a year earlier while services were flat, but the company does not yet publish product revenue, so we cannot say how large products are[7]. It has promised more detail in 2027.

Money also moves with currencies. In the June 2026 quarter, about 31.5% of revenue was billed in US dollars, 37.9% in euros, 9.5% in pounds, 14.4% in yen and 3.4% in rupees[36]. The company reports in rupees. That is why Q2 FY27 revenue rose 6.5% in rupees but only 0.2% when currency swings are removed, a figure called constant currency[3]. It is also why the quarter contained a ₹17.1 crore foreign-exchange loss, which a reader of the profit line cannot see without the company's own notes[14].

One more piece sits outside the main accounts. Qorix is a middleware joint venture, meaning a company that builds the plumbing software between a car's chips and its apps. KPIT does not control it, so it adds only a share of Qorix's loss to its own result. That share was ₹15.6 crore in Q2 FY27 and ₹14.0 crore in Q1[15][36]. Management said on 28 October that the car programs Qorix expects to sell into have slipped from 2028 to 2029 or 2030, so the loss will not end soon[7].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization: profit before the accounting and finance charges, and the company's preferred measure of operating profit.
  • EPS is earnings per share: profit divided by the number of shares, and the figure our valuation multiplies.
  • ROCE is return on capital employed: operating profit before tax divided by the capital the business uses, a test of how well each rupee is put to work.
  • RSI (relative strength index) scores recent price momentum from 0 to 100, and ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in rupees.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment.
  • Operating margin is operating profit divided by sales, and guidance is the forecast a company gives about its own future results.
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in customer bills and supplier credit; it is negative when customers pay before the company's own costs fall due.
  • Stage 4 is the markdown phase of a long price downtrend, and Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend.
  • A base is a stretch of sideways trading that a stock builds before a big move; the pivot is the top of that base, and a breakout is a move above it on strong volume.
  • A limit order is an instruction to buy only at or below a price you set, and the entry zone is the price range where we would place that order.
  • Reward-to-risk is the gain we expect divided by the loss we take if the stop is hit; the portfolio rulebook calls the same idea asymmetry.
  • Constant currency is growth after removing exchange-rate moves, and an OEM is an original equipment manufacturer, here a vehicle maker; a Tier 1 is a supplier that sells directly to an OEM.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A business is strong when customers keep paying more for something rivals cannot easily copy. KPIT has real evidence on the strong side. Its return on capital employed was 26.3% in the latest Screener reading, and its three-year average return on equity was 27%[2]. Over FY24 to FY26 it turned ₹2,076 crore of profit into ₹3,587 crore of operating cash, a ratio of 1.73[2]. Its working-capital days were minus 74 at March 2026, meaning customers paid before the company's own costs fell due[2]. The promoters have pledged none of their shares[24], and ICRA reaffirmed its AA (Stable) rating on 18 March 2026[17]. These are the marks of a well-run supplier, not a distressed one.

The weak side is the revenue line. Between FY26 and the first half of FY27 the story changed from a growth company to one that is working to hold its revenue steady. Constant-currency revenue grew 0.1% in Q1 FY27 and 0.2% in Q2, and dollar revenue fell 2.2% from a year earlier[3][4]. Management says two of its largest clients cut deeply, one in Japan whose vehicle program was cancelled late, and one in Europe[7][8]. The press release says revenue from those top two accounts fell about 13% over the year, while on the call management described the decline as 13% of total revenue. We cannot reconcile the two wordings, so we use neither as a precise size and flag the gap[3][7]. What both agree on is the direction and the timing: management expects the decline to bottom out over the next couple of quarters[3].

Is the moat real? The best test is whether clients keep awarding KPIT new work when budgets shrink. They do. New engagements closed were $202 million in Q3 FY26, $349 million in Q4, $257 million in Q1 FY27 and $213 million in Q2[6][5][4][3]. Together that is $1,021 million over four quarters against about $720 million of revenue in the same period, a ratio near 1.4 to 1[3][4][5][6]. The weakness is conversion. Wins are signed but programs start late, and Kishor Patil said on 28 October that the pipeline is active but 'it is taking some time for realization'[7]. A moat that wins contracts but cannot turn them into revenue within a year protects the long term more than it protects the next four quarters.

The third force is AI, and it cuts both ways. Management says about 9% of revenue now comes from AI-first projects, that the internal AI platform has more than 9,000 users and that 12,000 engineering agents have been built[7]. If AI lets one engineer do the work of two, KPIT's cost falls, and on fixed-price programs the saving stays with KPIT. But the same tools let a client write more code in-house. One broker note on the Q4 FY26 print named 'near-term AI-led cannibalization in certain services' as a cause of weak FY26 growth[23]. Nobody can yet measure which effect wins, and that uncertainty is part of why the market pays 26 times earnings, not 60.

Customers, concentration and the car cycle

Concentration is the central risk. KPIT depends on a small group of global vehicle makers, and when two of them cancel programs, the effect shows up within one quarter. We could not find a published top-five or top-ten client share in the sources we reached, so we record that metric as not published rather than estimate it[2]. Cars are also cyclical. When European makers issue profit warnings, they stop new programs first. KPIT's own warning on 30 June 2026 named 'sudden actions by some European OEMs triggered by their recent profit warnings'[11]. The shares fell 17% in one session on 1 July[12].

The cycle has not turned. On 27 October 2026, the last session of our data, Mercedes-Benz cut its 2026 return-on-sales guidance to 2% to 3% from 3% to 5%[26]. In June, Volkswagen was reported to be preparing to cut as many as 100,000 jobs and close four German plants[27]. European makers like these are KPIT's client base[11]. Sachin Tikekar said on 28 October that all European passenger-car OEMs are looking to cut costs dramatically, and Anup Sable called the European recovery 'very long drawn'[7]. Cost cutting is also the reason clients outsource more, which is the bet KPIT is making.

Capital allocation: buying capability while revenue is flat

KPIT bought the carved-out engineering business of Caresoft, which is strong in trucks and off-highway work. The board first announced a price of up to US$191 million and, on 8 August 2025, settled at up to US$157 million, including US$15 million paid only if revenue and synergy targets are met, over four years[18]. That price cut is a sign of discipline. The balance-sheet effect is large: fixed assets, which include acquired intangibles, rose from ₹1,972 crore at March 2025 to ₹3,985 crore at March 2026[2]. Trucks and off-highway are now the growth engine, so the purchase is working, but it raises what must be earned to keep returns high. ROCE fell from 40% to 26% in the same year[2].

On 6 May 2026 KPIT agreed to buy a majority of Cymotive, an Israeli vehicle-cybersecurity firm, for between $60 million and $120 million depending on revenue and profit milestones, with $10 million paid up front and full ownership expected by mid-2029[19]. The structure limits downside, and KPIT paid about ₹95 crore of the initial amount in Q2[16]. It also paid a ₹143 crore dividend in the same quarter, and net cash fell from ₹902 crore to ₹620 crore[13][16]. A company that pays dividends and buys stakes while revenue is flat is confident. It is also spending its cushion.

What the competitive position adds up to

Put together, KPIT has the skills, the clients and the cash of a leader, and the revenue of a company in a slump. Our reading is that the moat is intact but narrower than the 2023 to 2025 share price assumed. At that time the stock traded near 60 times earnings, its five-year average[21]. Today the market pays about 26 times trailing earnings, near the low of its recent range[22]. That de-rating is the market saying growth will be slower and less certain. Our job is to test whether the new price already reflects that. The KPI scorecard in the next chapter is how we test it.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management credibility matters more for a turnaround than for a steady compounder, because the whole case rests on promises about the next four quarters. We checked what management said one to two years ago against what was delivered, using call transcripts and results.

The record is mixed. In April 2025 KPIT had raised its FY25 margin guide from 20.5% to 21% and said it had met it[10]. For FY26 it held margins at 20.8%, close to FY25[5]. That is a good record on margins in a hard year. The break came in FY27. On 6 May 2026 management guided FY27 EBITDA to 20.5% to 21.2% and said the two big programs ending would be 'largely compensated' by new accounts[9]. Less than two months later it warned the first half would be 'unsatisfactory' and that margins would fall faster than revenue[11]. Q1 came in at 17.2% and Q2 at 17.8%[4][3]. The guide was missed by about three percentage points.

Not everything slipped. Management promised on 29 July that margins would 'improve successively every quarter' and that commercial vehicles would grow the next quarter. Q2 delivered 60 basis points of margin gain and 17.6% quarter-on-quarter growth in off-highway and commercial[4][7][8]. The next promises are untested: 'H2 is going to be better than H1', growth in Q4 FY27, and the 22% to 24% EBITDA margin aspiration for FY29, which was neither restated nor withdrawn on the Q2 call[7][8][34]. Qorix is the clearest miss on capital allocation: management said in July that the loss would continue for 'at least next quarter or two', and in October the loss widened to ₹15.6 crore and the programs slipped by about two years[7][8][15][36].

5. What could happen next

Chapter 5 asks what the share price already assumes, how professional forecasters are changing their numbers, and where we differ. The reverse DCF works backwards from the price. A discounted cash flow, or DCF, values a company by adding up the cash it will earn in future years, shrunk back to today's rupees. A reverse DCF starts from today's price and asks what growth rate would justify it. Our rulebook uses a 12% discount rate and a 20 times terminal multiple for India[37].

At ₹531.90 the price implies about 9.6% a year of profit growth for ten years, measured from trailing net profit of about ₹602 crore derived from the ledger[1]. Our own FY27 profit estimate is about ₹533 crore (EPS ₹19.4 on about 27.5 crore shares), and Screener's trailing twelve months also show ₹533 crore[2]. On the lower base the growth the price needs is higher than 9.6%, so we treat 9.6% as a floor on what the market assumes, not a precise reading. Is 9.6% realistic? It depends on the starting year. Our path puts EPS at ₹19.4 in FY27, ₹26.5 in FY28 and about ₹29.7 in FY29 (we assume 12% growth a year after FY28), which is 8.6% a year from the FY26 level of ₹23.25 and about 19.7% a year from the FY27 trough to FY30. From the trough the price looks cheap; from the peak it looks fair. That is why the reverse DCF alone does not settle the case.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 attacks our own work. For each estimate we state the weakest assumption, we build the strongest honest case against our verdict, and we name the single claim on which the whole view depends and the date we will learn whether it holds.

Weakest assumption in each estimate

For the FY27 EPS of ₹19.4, the weakest assumption is that margin rises 60 basis points a quarter. Management said only that it would rise 'to some extent every quarter' and that the full recovery comes 'early next year'. Q3 carries wage rises and an attrition rate in the mid-teens, which raises cost on a flat revenue base[7]. The evidence that it is wrong would be a Q3 company EBITDA margin below 18.2%. We will know when Q3 FY27 results are published, which for KPIT has been in the last week of January[6].

For the FY28 EPS of ₹26.5, the weakest assumption is revenue growth of 9% when the two big clients may still be shrinking in the first half of FY28. Management says they bottom 'over the next couple of quarters' but the timing has slipped before[3][34]. The evidence that it is wrong would be a Q4 FY27 print with no sequential growth. We will know in late April or early May 2027, when Q4 FY27 and the full year are reported (the last two Q4 releases came on 28 April 2025 and 6 May 2026)[10][5]. For the ₹689 value, the weakest assumption is the 26 times multiple. It is a judgement between the two-year median of 40.7 and the lower multiples of large IT firms, and no filing can prove it.

The red-team case: the strongest honest argument that WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT is wrong

The strongest case against our verdict is that it should be REJECTED. The share price is falling because earnings are falling, and a falling earnings figure makes a low P/E look cheaper than it is. On Screener's twelve-month profit of ₹533 crore the P/E at ₹531.90 is 28.2, not 25.0, and our own FY27 EPS of ₹19.4 puts it at 27.4 times[2]. Analysts' targets are being cut, the Stage 4 trend is intact, the two positive trajectory signals are thin, and the Layer 2 score is exactly on the 3.0 line.

We take this seriously because the ratio only passes in the entry zone if the central value is at least ₹650, which needs about ₹25 of FY28 EPS at 26 times or a 24.5 times multiple on ₹26.5. A wrong guess of one turn of multiple or ₹1 of EPS removes the zone. Our answer is that the verdict is a conditional order and not a purchase: no money moves unless the price falls into the zone and the valuation still holds. The cost of being wrong is limited to a starter position of 1.0% of the portfolio with a stop 10.5% below the entry.

Fatal flaw 1: the two big clients do not bottom, and others follow

Management says the two largest clients will stabilise in a couple of quarters. If a third European maker cancels or delays programs, which is plausible after Mercedes cut its 2026 guidance on 27 October and Volkswagen planned deep job cuts, revenue stays negative and the 2.7-point margin gap to guidance never closes[26][27][7]. The base case needs growth in Q4 FY27. A miss there makes FY28 EPS about ₹20.7 and the price about ₹414, below our stop.

Fatal flaw 2: AI lowers the price of the work faster than it raises KPIT's productivity

Fixed-price programs are now about 70% of work, and management cites AI as the way to protect margin[7]. If clients use their own AI tools to write and test code, they will ask KPIT to cut prices by the amount KPIT saves. Profit then does not rise even if revenue holds. One broker named AI-led cannibalisation as a cause of the FY26 slowdown[23]. We cannot see this in the data until at least two more quarters of margin figures.

Fatal flaw 3: acquisitions and currency turn a recovery into a write-down

Fixed assets doubled in FY26 with the Caresoft purchase, and the Cymotive purchase adds up to US$120 million over time[2][19]. If truck and off-highway growth slows, ROCE could slide below 20% and impairment charges could follow. Currency adds a second risk: about 62% of billing is in euros, pounds and yen[36], and the rupee strengthening against all three at once would lower reported earnings. The forex loss in Q2 was ₹17.1 crore on a quarterly profit of ₹121 crore[14][2].
