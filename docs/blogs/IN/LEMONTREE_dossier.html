1. What the company does

Think of Lemon Tree as a hotel chain with two jobs under one roof. In the first job it owns or leases hotels and keeps the room and restaurant income, and it pays the staff, the power bill, the rent and the interest. In the second job it lends its brand, booking system and loyalty club to hotels that belong to someone else, and it collects a fee. On 30 June 2026 the group had 34 owned hotels with 5,186 rooms, 7 leased hotels with 573 rooms and 94 managed or franchised hotels with 6,187 rooms, spread across 80-plus cities and sold under seven brands from the upscale Aurika to the budget-friendly Keys Lite[1].

The first job makes most of the money today. In the June 2026 quarter the group booked ₹344.6 crore of revenue and ₹151.9 crore of EBITDA, which is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a profit figure that ignores financing and accounting charges; the company reports a net EBITDA margin of 43.8 percent[1]. Guests spent ₹576 crore across all hotels in the chain that quarter, a number the company calls network revenue. Owned hotels produced ₹320 crore of it and managed hotels ₹256 crore, but Lemon Tree keeps only a fee on the managed share: ₹22.8 crore from outside owners and ₹22.6 crore from its own Fleur Hotels unit, ₹45.4 crore in all[1].

A fee works like a landlord's commission turned upside down. The brand owner takes a basic fee and a sales and marketing fee and, on top, an incentive that moves from 1 percent to 5 percent of revenue depending on how much profit the hotel makes[2][4]. The fee company carries almost no rent, debt or building risk, which is why management talks about margins of 70 percent or more once the business is large[1]. Fees from outside owners were ₹73.9 crore in fiscal 2026, up 23 percent, and fees from Fleur were ₹95.8 crore, up 8 percent[4].

Owned hotels have a strong seasonal beat. Revenue in the four quarters to March 2026 was ₹316 crore, ₹306 crore, ₹406 crore and ₹416 crore, with EBITDA margins of 44, 43, 50 and 52 percent[8]. The June and September quarters are the weakest, with September the lowest, because corporate travel and weddings concentrate in the winter months[2][12]. This matters for reading any single quarter, and it is why the 43.8 percent margin in June 2026 is not a sign of a collapse.

What the 2027 split changes for a shareholder

In January 2026 the board approved a plan to pull the two jobs apart. Lemon Tree will become a pure fee and brand company with no meaningful debt. Fleur Hotels, now a 58.9 percent subsidiary, will own the buildings and be listed on its own, and Lemon Tree shareholders receive Fleur shares in exchange for the hotels moved across[21]. Warburg Pincus is buying the 41.09 percent stake held by the Dutch pension fund APG and will put up to ₹960 crore of new money into Fleur[21]. Management says the swap lifts the effective share of Fleur that public shareholders own from 45.8 percent to 57.5 percent[1].

On the company's own pro forma numbers for fiscal 2026, the new Lemon Tree would have had ₹247 crore of revenue and ₹149 crore of EBITDA, a margin of 60.5 percent, while Fleur would have had ₹1,333 crore of revenue and ₹554 crore of EBITDA across 39 hotels and 5,556 rooms[1]. ICRA, a rating agency, notes that Fleur supplies about 70 percent of today's consolidated revenue, so reported sales will shrink sharply once Fleur is no longer fully counted[6]. For a buyer today, the practical point is that you own a bundle of both pieces, and the bundle will be unbundled during 2027. Management's best case is the first half of 2027, and its cautious case is the late second half[2]. In January the rating agencies reacted in different ways: ICRA left its [ICRA]A+ (Positive) rating in place, while CRISIL put its rating on watch until the scheme is approved[22][7].

Why the leadership changes matter

Patanjali Keswani, the founder, steps back from his executive role at Lemon Tree in March 2027 and will chair Fleur, while Neelendra Singh (managing director since October 2025) runs the brand company and Saurabh Shatdal (chief executive since August 2025) runs Fleur[1]. Both newcomers are a few quarters into the job. Leadership changes can shake a company even when the plan is sound, because the people who made the promises are not always the ones who must keep them. Here the founder keeps a seat at both tables, which helps continuity. The cost is that two new executives must prove themselves while a complex split runs in the background. That is a fair reason to give the fee-growth story a little less benefit of the doubt than a long-serving team would earn, and chapter 2B tests whether the promises made in 2025 were kept.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for this business rests on three things that can be checked: it fills rooms better than the market, it keeps costs low, and its fee business is growing quickly. The case against it rests on a pricing problem, a tax change and a very large renovation bill. Both lists are real, and the verdict depends on which list gets bigger over the next year.

Where the strength is real

The company calls itself the largest mid-priced and third largest overall hotel chain in India[8]. Its same-store occupancy ran at 72 percent in fiscal 2025 against 68 percent for India as a whole, based on figures the company draws from the Hotelivate industry survey[1]. In the June 2026 quarter occupancy rose by 314 basis points to 75.7 percent, a basis point being one hundredth of a percentage point[1]. A chain that sells more of its rooms than the market gets a better return on every fixed cost, which is the main reason its EBITDA margin of 48.1 percent in fiscal 2026 is higher than the 33 to 45 percent at the listed peers in chapter 3[1][24][25][26].

The second strength is the fee engine. About 51 percent of the 11,946 operating rooms run under management or franchise contracts, up from 35 percent in March 2020, and management fee income more than quadrupled from ₹17.6 crore in fiscal 2022 to ₹73.9 crore in fiscal 2026[6]. The company signed 55 hotels with 4,912 rooms in fiscal 2026, against 20 hotels with 1,523 rooms opened[4]. Because a signed hotel takes about 30 months to open, signings today set openings in 2029, and management expects to open about 5,000 rooms in that year[2]. On 16 October 2026 it added a memorandum of understanding with the developer Omaxe to operate about 4,000 keys, lifting the pipeline to nearly 16,000 keys[19][33]. It also signed a Keys Lite hotel in Bhilwara on 23 October[31].

Third, supply is tight where it counts. The company's industry slide shows branded room supply growing 5.3 percent a year in the top six cities from fiscal 2025 to 2030 against 9.6 percent for India, with Delhi at 2.5 percent and Pune at 3.2 percent[1]. Demand elsewhere is healthy: the Indian Hotels Company reported second-quarter revenue up 22 percent in the week to 22 October 2026 and raised its full-year growth forecast to 15 to 17 percent[18]. The industry is not in a slump; Lemon Tree's own pricing is the question.

Where the strength is thin

Pricing is the first soft spot. The average room rate grew only 2 percent in the June 2026 quarter while occupancy jumped, because corporate travel dropped after the West Asia conflict and the company filled rooms through lower-priced retail channels[2]. Management called this temporary and says it returned to a balanced approach in July and August, but the proof will only be in the September-quarter numbers due on 6 November 2026[2][17]. In Mumbai about 2,000 new rooms opened near the airport over two years, which is why occupancy across the group's Mumbai hotels fell 322 basis points[1][2].

A tax rule is the second. Since a goods and services tax change that took effect during fiscal 2026, hotels cannot claim back the tax paid on inputs for rooms priced below ₹7,500 a night[4]. About 52 percent of Lemon Tree's rooms sit below that line, and the loss cost 2.3 percent of revenue in the June 2026 quarter[2][1]. Management plans to lift prices so more rooms cross the line and says every new Aurika will, but the drag stays at about 2 percent of revenue this year and 1.7 percent next year in its own plan[1].

The third is the catch-up renovation. Renovation, technology and the tax change together cost 5.8 percent of revenue in fiscal 2026 against 3.2 percent in fiscal 2025. The company plans 4.8 percent in fiscal 2027 and 3.7 percent in fiscal 2028[1][3]. The money is going into hotels whose earlier renovations paid back well: the Keys brand posted a 19 percent jump in revenue per available room in the June 2026 quarter and management targets ₹60 crore of EBITDA from it[1][2]. It is a bet that has started to pay, yet the margin gain keeps being pushed back.

What this means for an owner

For someone who owns the stock, the moat is mostly scale, occupancy and a cost structure that the company works hard on. It is not pricing power, since the average room rate barely moved in the latest quarter. The fee business is the part with the longest runway, and it is also the part least visible in the price today. The next chapter checks whether the company actually did what it promised, because a long runway is only worth something when management delivers on dates. In practice, expect the share price to react more to quarterly room-rate news than to the fee story for now. A fee business compounds quietly and is easy to miss in consolidated numbers. That gap between what is growing and what is reported is the main reason the split could matter. Until then, the owned hotels set the tone, and they are still working through renovation and tax costs.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter asks whether the company is financially sound, honest with cash and fairly priced, and whether it fits one of our three types of qualifying company. We start with five years of accounts, then the fixed red lines, then the three types. Four terms matter. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the profit before interest and tax earned on every rupee of capital the business uses. Debt-to-equity is total debt divided by shareholders' money. Net debt is interest-bearing debt minus the cash held. The price-to-earnings ratio is the price paid for each rupee of yearly profit.

Debt needs one clarification because two correct numbers circulate. Under Indian accounting rules, rent on long leases is booked as debt. Lemon Tree's balance sheet at 31 March 2026 held ₹1,500.25 crore of bank and bond debt plus ₹503.40 crore of lease liabilities, ₹2,003.65 crore in total, which is the ₹2,004 crore that Screener reports as borrowings[5][8]. ICRA and the company quote the ₹1,500 crore figure without leases[6]. We use the larger number for every ratio, because owning a lease means owing the rent.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 stands on an assumption that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest assumption under each, builds the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, and picks the one claim that decides the outcome and the date on which it will be known.

Estimate 1: the base value rests on a margin of about 50 percent in fiscal 2028

If the EBITDA margin stalls near today's 47.5 percent instead of climbing to about 50 percent, the street profit forecast of ₹4.92 fails, so the base value rests on that climb[8]. The evidence for is real: renovation spending falls from 4.2 percent of revenue in fiscal 2026 to 1.9 percent in fiscal 2027 and 1.3 percent in fiscal 2028, and the tax-rule cost eases from 2.0 to 1.7 percent[1]. The evidence against is that the 55 percent stretch hope voiced in May 2025 has been dropped while the formal 50 percent promise stands, that the June 2026 reported margin fell, and that the September 2026 quarter will still carry heavy renovation[15][2]. We will know part of the answer on 6 November 2026 and the rest in early February 2027, when the strong December quarter is reported (the date is not yet announced)[17].

Estimate 2: the 24 times multiple assumes peers do not de-rate

A group-wide fall in hotel multiples would hurt the 24 times multiple more than news about any one company. The Indian Hotels Company trades at 57.1 times earnings and Chalet at 32.9 times, so the peer group includes two richly priced names[24][25]. The four-peer median of 29.7 times is the average of the middle two names, 32.9 and 26.4, so a fall in the richest name does not move it until that name drops below 32.9 times; a fall to 30 times would lower the median only to about 28.2 times. The bigger danger is the whole group de-rating together: a 10 percent fall would take our 24 times to about 21.6 times and the base value to about ₹106. Cutting only the multiple to the 19 times used in our bear row gives ₹93.48 and an expected value of ₹98.83, 7.8 percent below the price, so multiple compression alone turns the small expected gain into a loss. A second weakness is that the split itself is unpriced: Fleur must prove a 15 percent return on capital, and a listing is not guaranteed to close the discount[2]. Evidence would arrive through the court and regulator steps during 2027, with the first shareholder meeting a visible marker.

Estimate 3: the probabilities are judgement, not measurement

The probabilities are the softest numbers in this report, and the softest of them is that bear is more likely than bull. Our reasons are the chart, the three rounds of estimate cuts in a year and a track record of slipping dates. Reasons against are July's recovery, a rating upgrade in August and a fast-growing pipeline[6][2]. The September 2026 quarter, due 6 November 2026, is the first hard test; if it shows 5 percent room rate growth and a margin of 46 percent or more against 43.0 percent a year ago, we would move 10 points of probability from bear to bull[17][8]. A useful way to read this: the probabilities are a way of saying how surprised we would be, not a prediction. If you disagree with them, change them and watch the expected value move, as the second estimate in chapter 5 shows.
