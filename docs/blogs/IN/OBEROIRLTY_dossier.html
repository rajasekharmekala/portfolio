1. What the company does

Oberoi Realty Limited (NSE: OBEROIRLTY, BSE: 533273) is a real estate developer headquartered in Mumbai. It builds premium and luxury apartments, and it also owns and runs offices, shopping malls, a hotel and two schools. By its own count it has completed 51 projects in Mumbai, and the company was founded as Kingston Properties in 1998[3][8]. The promoter group, led by Vikas Oberoi, the chairman and managing director, holds 67.70% of the shares, and none of those shares are pledged to lenders[14][8].

The company earns money in four ways. Residential sales are the largest: in FY26 they made up about 75% of revenue of ₹6,009 crore, and the rest came from rent, the hotel and property management[8]. A buyer of a new apartment pays in instalments while the tower is built, so cash arrives over three to four years. The company calls the value of apartments sold in a period its booking value or pre-sales, and it calls the cash received its collections. Both are different from revenue, which follows accounting rules and is explained later in this chapter.

The second source of income is rent, and it is the steadier one. Oberoi owns income-producing properties in Mumbai with 66.67 lakh square feet of leasable space (one lakh is 100,000): three office buildings called Commerz I, II and III, Oberoi Mall in Goregaon and Sky City Mall in Borivali. It also owns two international schools[1]. The office and mall assets were 95% occupied in September 2026 and kept 93% of their rent as operating profit[5]. In the quarter to September 2026, rent was ₹338 crore, up 16.6% from ₹290 crore a year earlier[4].

The third and fourth sources are small. The Westin Mumbai Garden City has 269 rooms, earned ₹45 crore of revenue in the September quarter and kept 36% of it as operating profit[5][4]. Property management for its own buildings brought in a further ₹39 crore[4]. Two more hotels are on the way: a Ritz-Carlton at Three Sixty West in Worli, which management now expects in the first quarter of FY28, and a hotel in Borivali that is targeted for the end of calendar 2027[7].

The company's growth plan has two parts. In Mumbai and Thane it has more than 15 towers above 60 floors under construction, and it keeps adding land through redevelopment deals (where it rebuilds an old housing society and gives the members new homes in return) and outright purchases[7]. Outside Mumbai, it launched its first Delhi-NCR project, Three Sixty North in Gurugram, around the turn of June and July 2026. The project sits on a 14.816-acre site and is expected to cost about ₹6,000 crore to build. Press reports cite about ₹16,000 crore of revenue across two phases, while the chairman said on the July call that he had mentioned ₹14,000 crore[18][16][22]. That launch changes the shape of the company, and it also brings a legal dispute that this report treats as the main risk.

Who pays, when, and why revenue follows a different clock

Customers sign a booking, pay a small part at once and pay the rest in steps tied to construction. Indian law requires developers to keep a large share of each payment in a project account that can only be used for that project, so cash from one tower cannot be moved to buy land elsewhere[23]. The practical effect is that a quarter with huge bookings brings only a small fraction of the booking value in cash in that quarter. At Three Sixty North the company collected about ₹830 crore in the September quarter against ₹7,891 crore of bookings, which is about 10.5%[7][6].

Revenue follows a third clock. For most projects the auditors confirm that Oberoi books revenue over time, in proportion to the construction cost spent so far, using the percentage of completion method[13]. A project must first pass a threshold of progress (management has put it at 25% of sales and construction), and until then revenue equals cost and no profit shows[24]. Finished units, such as those left at Three Sixty West and in Mulund, are booked only when the buyer pays in full and takes possession[22]. Three Sixty North follows a stricter rule, the project completion method, so none of its ₹7,891 crore of bookings reaches the profit and loss account until the towers are complete[6].

This is the key accounting fact for any reader. By September 2026 the company had booked ₹37,446 crore of sales in its key residential projects and had recognised ₹23,162 crore of it as revenue. The remaining ₹14,284 crore is still to come: ₹7,891 crore from Three Sixty North, which is delayed until completion, and ₹6,393 crore from older projects, which will arrive as towers are built (our subtraction from the project table)[6]. So reported profit can move for reasons that have nothing to do with demand.

What makes the company different from other developers

Oberoi sells at the top of the market, and it says its prices rise as buildings approach completion. At Oceanic in Goregaon the price moved from ₹1,50,000 to between ₹1,70,000 and ₹1,80,000 per square foot, and Three Sixty North sold at an average of ₹59,961 per square foot[7][6]. Its operating margin, the share of revenue left after running costs but before interest and tax, is unusually high for a developer: 56.94% on residential sales in the first half of FY27, against 52.21% a year before[5].

The company also keeps borrowing low. Management says it has "cracked the code" of building volume with quality, and it is building 18 towers of 60 to 65 floors at once[22]. Its credit rating of CARE AA+ with a stable outlook was reaffirmed on 7 October 2026[8].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Pre-sales (booking value): the value of apartments customers have signed up to buy in a period. It is a sale, not yet revenue and not yet cash.
  • Collections: the cash customers actually paid in the period, including rent and hotel receipts.
  • RERA: the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, which makes developers register projects and hold customer money in project accounts.
  • Percentage of completion: an accounting method that books revenue in step with construction cost spent, instead of waiting for handover.
  • Project completion method: the stricter method that books revenue only when the project is finished; Three Sixty North uses it.
  • Annuity assets: buildings that earn rent year after year, such as offices and malls.
  • Crore and lakh: Indian counting units. One lakh is 100,000, one crore is 10 million (100 lakh).
  • Committed receivables ratio: money still due from customers who have already signed, divided by the debt plus the cost left to build. Above 100% means the buyers' promised payments cover the remaining bill.
  • Writ petition: a request to a High Court to review a government decision; here it asks the court to review the licence for the Gurugram land.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Oberoi is a strong business because it combines three things: a brand that lets it charge a premium, a balance sheet with almost no net borrowing, and a rental portfolio that earns steadily whether or not apartments sell. Each point has a number behind it. Gross borrowings were ₹2,630 crore in September 2026 (the company presentation; Screener's balance sheet shows ₹2,638 crore, including small lease items) against cash and mutual funds of ₹2,864 crore, so the company holds about ₹234 crore more than it owes (our subtraction from the presentation)[4]. The credit rater CARE puts total debt at 0.16 times equity in March 2026, down from 0.21 a year earlier, and it would only change its view if that ratio stayed above 0.30[8].

The weakness is concentration and lumpiness. Almost all of Oberoi's projects are in the Mumbai region, and CARE lists this as the first weakness of the rating[8]. In the September 2026 quarter one project, Three Sixty North in Gurugram, produced ₹7,891 crore of the ₹9,150 crore of bookings, which is 86%[9][6]. Without it, first-half bookings were ₹2,308 crore, against ₹2,938 crore in the same half last year, a fall of 21% (our subtraction from the press release and the presentation)[3][6]. A company that depends on one launch for most of a year's sales is stronger than average on cash and weaker than average on predictability.

The industry sets the weather. In July to September 2026 Anarock counted 31,750 homes sold in the Mumbai region, up 5% from a year earlier, while new launches rose 27% to 37,500 homes and unsold stock across seven cities rose 12%[27]. A second data firm, PropEquity, counted 9,830 homes sold in Mumbai, down 8%, and 16,230 in Thane, down 11%[28]. The two firms use different area definitions, which is why Anarock's regional total is up while PropEquity's Mumbai and Thane counts are down (PropEquity's Navi Mumbai count rose 12% to 9,810). We read the two together as demand that is flat to soft in the core city, with new supply rising across the wider region. On 7 October 2026 the Reserve Bank raised its policy rate by 0.25 percentage points to 5.50% and moved its stance to calibrated tightening, which makes home loans more expensive[26]. Oberoi's luxury buyers may be less sensitive to loan rates than average buyers, but we have no data on how many of its sales are loan-financed, and the effect on sentiment reaches every developer.

The brand and the pricing power

Evidence of pricing power is specific. Management says it raises prices as a building nears completion and that resale prices set by its own customers lead the way: apartments resold in Borivali and Goregaon sell above the developer's price[22]. Mulund's Eternia, launched at lower prices, now sells at an average of ₹36,581 per square foot, and the Thane projects Jardin and Forestville sell at ₹25,641 and ₹19,908[6]. Pricing power is also visible in the margin: the average residential margin was 60.91% in the September quarter, and management says projects range from 43% to about 65% depending on the mix[5][22].

The brand has a limit. At Three Sixty West in Worli, the company's flagship luxury tower, bookings fell to 2 units (₹278 crore) in the September quarter, and a Nomura analyst noted on the July call that sales slowed after sharp price rises[6][22]. Management answers that it prefers to hold price and sell slowly, and that the earlier discounted sales by the former land partner have run out[22]. Both statements can be true, so we count luxury velocity as a watch item and not as a strength.

The annuity cushion

The rental portfolio is the quiet strength. The three Commerz office buildings are 96% to 100% leased, Oberoi Mall is 99% leased, and Sky City Mall is 83% leased after a year of operation[5]. Operating profit on the whole annuity business was ₹300 crore in the September quarter, so about ₹1,200 crore for a year. Motilal Oswal values these assets at a capitalisation rate of 7.5% to 8.0%, meaning it divides the annual operating profit by that rate[9]. Applied to ₹1,200 crore, that gives ₹15,000 crore to ₹16,000 crore, about a quarter of the ₹58,213 crore market value (our arithmetic)[1]. The other three quarters of the price must be earned by the apartment business, the hotels and the land bank.

Where the business is exposed

Four exposures matter. The first is the Gurugram dispute, covered in Chapter 6. The second is timing: management has missed almost every dated promise of the last two years, though the delivered results have mostly been good (Chapter 2B). The third is land cost: capital commitments not yet paid were ₹5,564 crore at 31 March 2026, up from ₹315 crore a year earlier, and the annual report does not split them by project[12]. The fourth is cash conversion, which fell from 1.46 times profit in FY24 to 0.55 times in FY26 as the company paid for land and built inventory[1][11].

None of the four is a solvency problem. Together they explain why we rate the business strong but cap the conviction at a low tier, and why the price at which we would buy matters as much as the verdict.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A developer cannot be judged on revenue growth and one valuation ratio alone, because its sales, cash and reported profit move at different times. The price-to-earnings ratio — the share price divided by the profit per share — shows what investors pay, not how the business runs. We chose five measures that run this business. Pre-sales show whether customers want what Oberoi builds. Collections and debt show whether bookings turn into cash without borrowing. Rent and occupancy measure the steady half. Residential margin shows what each rupee of revenue is worth. The launch pipeline shows whether the next set of projects will arrive on time[35].

The scoring rule gives 1.0 for a pass, 0.5 for a watch and 0.0 for a fail. A name qualifies when its weighted score is 3.0 out of 5.0 or higher and it has at most one fail. Oberoi scores 3.5 out of 5.0 with no fails, so it qualifies as PASS_WITH_WATCH. The three watches are all about timing and concentration, and none is about solvency or the level of the margin.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks whether the company passes the safety lines and which of three archetypes it fits. The inputs

Scenario values from the table below; the probabilities are our judgement and are explained row by row in that table, with 30% on the bear case because the Gurugram case is unresolved and management dates have slipped.

The range

If the bear probability were 40% (with 10% less on the bull), the weighted value would be 0.10 × 2,560 + 0.50 × 1,910 + 0.40 × 1,370 = ₹1,759. If it were 20%, with the bull at 30%, the value would be ₹1,997.

The probabilities are judgements and not measurements, so we test them in the range above: a bear probability of 40% still leaves the weighted value at ₹1,759, which is 9% above the ₹1,619 close and 12% above ₹1,577.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could fail. This chapter names the weakest, states what would show it wrong and says when we will know. It also gives the strongest case against the idea, as a red team would, and picks the single claim that the whole case hangs on.

Weakest assumption 1: the Gurugram project stays on course

Our central case assumes Three Sixty North continues to be built and that its bookings are not refunded. The legal record shows why that is not certain. The Punjab and Haryana High Court restrained new allotments on 7 July 2026 until the state planning department, DTCP, decided a complaint by Advance India Projects Limited about the transfer of the licence[17]. DTCP rejected the complaint as "without merit" in an order dated 13 August 2026, which was conveyed to the company on 17 August, and held that Licence No. 69 of 2025 and the change of developer approval of 17 June 2025 are "legally intact"[16][19][21]. On 16 September the High Court extended its restraint on further allotments to the next hearing on 25 September, after the petitioner argued that the DTCP order did not follow the court's directions[19][20]. We could not find a published outcome of the 25 September hearing, and on 19 October the chairman still called the matter sub judice, meaning before the court[7][21].

The regulator's record adds two older facts. The Haryana real estate regulator approved the project's registration on 26 June 2026, but only subject to the final outcome of a Supreme Court civil appeal (CA No. 8977 of 2014), an investigation by the CBI and the Enforcement Directorate that is under process, and other pending cases[15]. The order does not say how far each of these matters touches Oberoi's own conduct, so we flag them as conditions we cannot size. Existing sales of ₹7,891 crore and construction are not stayed, CARE calls the impact of the interim order limited, and 96% of the launched area is sold[8]. We will know more at the next High Court hearing (date not published in the sources we fetched) and at the December-quarter result in January 2027.

Weakest assumption 2: our FY28 earnings per share of ₹86.8

We assume 14% growth in FY28, against 9.6% a year over the last three years and 13.8% in Motilal Oswal's model[1][9]. The bridge in Chapter 3 shows where it comes from: about ₹1.2 from rent, ₹0.6 from hotels after our delay rule, and about ₹8.9, or 83%, from faster residential revenue recognition. The last part is a judgment, and it is the second hinge of this report after the court case. The evidence that would show us wrong is a December-quarter profit below about ₹650 crore, residential revenue in the second half that does not rise on the first-half run-rate of ₹2,133 crore, or any new delay to the Ritz-Carlton[5][7]. We will know from the result in January 2027 and the March-quarter result in May 2027. If residential recognition delivers only half of what we assume, FY28 earnings per share is about ₹82.4 and the central target about ₹1,813.

Weakest assumption 3: a 22 times multiple holds

The target uses the current 22.0 times earnings. A higher policy rate and softer sales data are the usual causes of a lower multiple for developers. The Reserve Bank raised its rate to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 and changed its stance to calibrated tightening[26]. The three-year low of the stock's multiple was 20.9, and our bear case uses 18. We will know from the Reserve Bank's next policy decision, from Anarock and PropEquity data for October to December 2026, and from whether the stock holds ₹1,569. A multiple is a price for certainty, and the certainty on offer depends on the court case and on the pace of launches. If the case clears and launches arrive, the multiple could rise toward the three-year median of 27.9. If neither happens, it could fall toward the low end of its range.

The red-team bear case

The strongest case against buying goes like this. Oberoi's profit has grown 9% a year over three years, and at 22 times earnings the stock is paying for more than that. Its first-half bookings excluding Gurugram fell 21%. Cash conversion has dropped for two years. The brand's flagship luxury tower sold two apartments in a quarter. The company has missed every promised date, and it is about to commit ₹5,564 crore to land and new projects, a number that grew nearly eighteen times in a year, with no project-wise split in the note[3][6][11][12]. If the High Court case turns against it, the company would face refunds and a damaged NCR plan while rates are rising. In that world the stock could trade at 18 times lower earnings, which is our ₹1,370, and the 52-week low of ₹1,391 would give way.

Our answer is that the bear case needs several things to go wrong at once, that the company holds net cash and an AA+ rating, and that the position size caps the damage. A 3.5% position that falls to ₹1,370 from an average entry of about ₹1,573 loses 12.9% of the position, which is 0.45% of the portfolio, and that is inside the 0.5% risk budget. A close-based stop cannot promise that outcome. A court order can arrive overnight and the stock can open below the ₹1,522 stop, so the planned stop loss of about 0.11% of the portfolio holds only if the stock trades through the stop in an orderly way. The size is what contains the gap, not the stop: even a gap straight to the bear value costs less than the budget.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, which here means: place no order today, and arm limit orders (orders that fill only at your price or lower) in a conditional band of ₹1,569 to ₹1,577 only if the trigger below fires. The support ladder in the scoring rules would on its own allow a dip purchase. The close of ₹1,619 is 1.06 average daily swings above the ₹1,569 floor, which falls in the standard band of up to 1.2, and the central-case ratio is 3.0 to 1 at the close and 6.05 to 1 at ₹1,577[35][34]. The gate result NO_MOMENTUM already ruled out a market order. We go one step further and wait, for the reason in the next paragraph.

The reason is the stress test. We cut FY28 earnings per share by 10% (to ₹78.1) and then also lowered the multiple from 22.0 to 20.9, the low of the last three years, which gives a target of ₹1,633. The highest price at which the ratio is still 2.0 to 1 is then (1,633 + 2 × 1,522) / 3 = ₹1,559, which is below the ₹1,569 floor and above the ₹1,522 stop. At ₹1,577 the ratio is 1.01 to 1, at ₹1,600 it is 0.42 to 1 and at the ₹1,619 close it is 0.14 to 1. After the earnings cut alone the ceiling is ₹1,587, and with our full estimate it is ₹1,651. Applying a trailing multiple to earnings two years ahead makes the joint test harsher than a normal forecast, so we do not treat it as the most likely outcome. We do treat it as a real one, because our own FY28 growth rests mostly on a judgment about residential revenue (Chapter 3), and a plan that fails a joint stress of this size is not a plan to start with a full position.

What would change the answer is evidence on the earnings side. If FY28 earnings per share is about ₹89.7, the joint-stress target is 0.9 × 89.7 × 20.9 = ₹1,687, the ceiling rises to (1,687 + 2 × 1,522) / 3 = ₹1,577, and the zone ₹1,569 to ₹1,577 clears the floor. FY28 at ₹89.7 needs FY27 at ₹78.7, which needs second-half profit of about ₹1,556 crore and a December-quarter profit of about ₹730 crore on last year's 47% December share. The conditional levels are therefore: stop ₹1,522, set at the 63-day low of ₹1,569 minus one average daily swing of ₹47.04 as the rule for a Stage 1 floor requires, which is 3.5% below ₹1,577 and is evaluated on closing prices; targets ₹1,910 (central), ₹2,560 (bull) and ₹1,370 (bear). The stop is not the 200-day average (₹1,686), which is above the price.

The strategy bucket is Core Alpha, because this is a low-debt, high-return compounder held for years and not a momentum trade. The conviction tier is TIER_3 (a maximum of 3.5% of the portfolio) for three reasons: Layer 2 has three watches, management's dates keep slipping, and a court case with a binary outcome is open. The table below sets size by risk and not by hope. The portfolio may lose 50 basis points (0.5%) on a TIER_3 name at the stop, and 0.5% divided by the 3.49% stop distance would allow 14.3% of the portfolio, so the 3.5% tier cap binds. If the plan is armed we would place two orders of 1.75%, at ₹1,577 and ₹1,569, with an average cost of ₹1,573.

The stop does not remove the legal tail. A close-based stop is a planned exit, not a guaranteed price. If a court order arrives overnight the stock could open below ₹1,522, and the loss would then be larger than the planned 0.11% of the portfolio (3.5% × 3.24% from the average cost to the stop). The position size is what contains the gap. A fall from ₹1,573 to the bear value of ₹1,370 would cost 12.9% of the position, or 0.45% of the portfolio, which is inside the 0.5% budget. That is why we keep the tier cap and do not size on the stop distance alone. Once the stock is owned, winners are allowed to run: a rise in the price does not by itself cause a sale, there is no mechanical trim when the RSI passes 70, the stop moves up with the rising 21-day or 50-day average, and the holding may grow up to 22% of the portfolio if the trend and the earnings hold.
