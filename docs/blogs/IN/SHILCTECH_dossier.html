1. What the company does

Shilchar Technologies Limited makes transformers at one 21-acre site in Gavasad, near Vadodara in Gujarat[3]. A transformer steps electric voltage up or down so that power can travel along a grid or run a factory. The company was set up in 1986[8]. Its shares trade in Mumbai under scrip code 531201 and, since 24 November 2025, on the National Stock Exchange as SHILCTECH[11]. The promoter family still owns 62.12 percent[15], and about 475 people work at the plant[3].

The company does not sell off-the-shelf units. Each transformer is built to a customer's order. The range runs from power transformers up to 66 kV and distribution transformers up to 33 kV, through inverter-duty units that sit beside solar inverters and generator units for wind farms, to hydro transformers up to 132 kV and furnace transformers for steel plants[3]. (A kV is a kilovolt, a thousand volts. MVA, short for megavolt-ampere, measures how much load a transformer can carry, and the company counts its plant capacity in MVA.) The largest single size it makes today is 50 MVA at 132 kV[3].

The raw materials are copper, transformer oil, a special electrical steel called CRGO, and aluminium. Together they make up 80 to 85 percent of raw-material cost[8]. In normal times management books copper or aluminium as soon as an order is signed, so it carries little metal-price risk on a signed order[5]. It buys in lower-value parts such as tanks and radiators and keeps winding, assembly and testing in-house[3]. A unit counts as revenue when it is dispatched. Custom units take 10 to 16 weeks to deliver, and a typical order runs 3 to 9 months from signature to delivery[4][8].

In FY26 the company sold ₹651.94 crore of goods. India took 51.7 percent and exports took 48.3 percent[2]. Management puts the Middle East at about 30 percent of FY26 revenue and the United States at 18 to 19 percent[5]. Domestic buyers are mostly private companies, and the company says it will not sell to state utilities[4]. The five largest domestic customers took 57 percent of domestic sales in FY26[8]. Management says export orders earn roughly 10 percentage points more profit than domestic ones[4]. That gap matters a great deal here: exports were only 34 percent of fourth-quarter sales, and management says export lifting was held back again in the June quarter[5][4].

The plant is rated at 7,500 MVA, and it dispatched about 6,000 MVA in FY26, which the company counts as 79 percent[3][5]. A second plant block of 6,500 MVA, taking the total to 14,000 MVA, is due in April 2027 at a cost the company now puts near ₹120 crore, paid from its own cash[10][5][8]. The new block can build units up to 160 MVA at 220 kV, a class the company does not make today[5].

How one order becomes profit

An order starts as a customer's specification. The company quotes a price that adds a margin to the cost of metals, oil, labour and testing. After the order is signed it books the metals, builds the unit, tests it in its own laboratory and ships it. Most export orders are sold ex-works, which means the customer pays the freight[7][4]. That matters now. When container costs rose three to five times, the cost fell on the customer, who then lifted only the minimum volume it needed[4].

Profit per unit therefore depends on three things: the metal and oil prices at the time the order is priced, how much of a later price rise the customer agrees to share, and whether the plant is full. In the June 2026 quarter the plant ran at only 60 to 65 percent, and management says only 50 to 60 percent of the metal-price rise on older orders could be passed on[4]. Both effects hit at once, which is why profit fell much faster than sales.

The industry has a seasonal pattern: management says the first quarter is always the weakest and the fourth the strongest[7]. So a weak June quarter alone proves little. What matters is whether September and December recover, and whether the margin returns toward 25 percent.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) — operating profit before financing costs, tax and the wear on assets. This report uses it before other income, as the company does.
  • EPS (earnings per share) — profit after tax divided by the number of shares. It is the number a share price is compared with.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) — operating profit after tax for each rupee of capital in use. A high figure means the business earns a lot from a small amount of capital.
  • RSI (relative strength index) — a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has moved. Below 30 is called oversold, which means the price fell unusually fast; it does not mean a bounce must follow.
  • ATR (average true range) — the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions. Distances in this report are often counted in ATRs so that a move is judged against the stock's own volatility.
  • base — a stretch of weeks in which a falling price stops and trades in a narrow range, like a floor being laid before a rise.
  • pivot — the price at the top of a base. A close above it is the first sign the base is ending.
  • breakout — a close above the pivot on higher trading volume, which suggests buyers have taken control.
  • limit order — an order to buy at a stated price or lower, never higher.
  • entry zone — the price range in which we would be willing to buy.
  • asymmetry — the gain expected in the central case divided by the loss if the stop is hit; it is also called reward-to-risk. The house floor for a Core Alpha stock is 2.0 to 1.
  • Stage 2 — the rising phase of a stock's life, with the price above a rising 50-day average that is above the 200-day average. Stage 4 is the opposite, the falling phase.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average — the average closing price over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. This report calls them the 20-day, 50-day and 200-day averages.
  • gross margin — sales less the cost of materials, as a share of sales. operating margin — operating profit as a share of sales, here before other income.
  • debt-to-equity — borrowings divided by shareholders' funds. Zero means no borrowings.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) — the share price divided by earnings per share. A P/E of 29.4 means the buyer pays ₹29.40 for each ₹1 of yearly profit.
  • free cash flow (FCF) — cash from operations less spending on plant and equipment. It is what is left to pay owners or to keep.
  • cash flow from operations — cash the business generates from its day-to-day work after tax is paid.
  • guidance — what management says it expects for the coming period. Next, chapter 2 turns to what makes the business strong or weak.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of this business is its return on the money it uses. Screener, an aggregator of filed data, shows ROCE of 50.7 percent and return on equity of 37.8 percent[1]. The company's own slide shows a lower ROCE of 43 percent for FY26, and the rating agency says more than 50 percent[3][8]. The three use different definitions of capital, and all are far above the 15 percent floor. Operating margin was 28.5, 29.6 and 29.2 percent in FY24, FY25 and FY26[3]. Peers' margins in the June 2026 quarter were 14 to 25 percent, and four of the five were between 14 and 16 percent[24][25][26][27][28]. Shilchar's FY26 margin was far above the group, but its June 2026 margin of 16.4 percent was level with it.

The balance sheet adds to the case. Borrowings are zero, cash and current investments were ₹245.95 crore at 31 March 2026 against shareholders' funds of ₹490.85 crore, and the rating agency counts ₹227 crore of free liquidity[3][8]. CARE Ratings reaffirmed CARE A with a stable outlook in July 2026, after an upgrade path of BBB+ in June 2023, A- in 2024 and A in July 2025[8][9]. Interest cover is about 436 times[8]. The company has no subsidiaries[2], and the auditor's FY26 opinion was unmodified[14].

Why has the company earned such margins? Three reasons are documented. It builds custom units for niches such as solar and wind duty, where buyers pay for fit and speed. It exports a large share, and export orders earn about 10 points more than domestic ones[4]. And it picked private buyers over state utilities, which the company says gives shorter payment cycles[4]. A broker report adds that its gross margin is about 38 percent against about 28 percent for peers, which we treat as a claim by one broker[22].

The weaknesses are just as concrete. The company is small, with ₹652 crore of sales, and the top five domestic customers are 57 percent of domestic sales[2][8]. CARE calls the industry highly fragmented and notes that profit is exposed to raw-material and currency swings, with no active hedging policy; the company gained ₹12.09 crore from currency in FY26[8]. Management itself says every transformer maker is adding capacity[5]. In the June 2026 quarter, domestic-focused peers grew sales 28 to 69 percent while Shilchar fell 15 percent[24][25][26][27][28][3]. That gap says the margin lead was partly a product of the export mix and not a permanent advantage.

Competitive position: what is proven and what is not

Proven: the company grew revenue from ₹180 crore in FY22 to ₹652 crore in FY26 and raised its operating margin from 10.8 percent to 29.2 percent over the same years[3]. The company's capacity filing of September 2025 reported 100 percent utilisation of the 7,500 MVA plant, although the dispatch-based figure for FY26 is 79 percent[10][3]. Management says no order has been cancelled, only pushed out[4].

Not proven: that exports return on their own. Management says the cause is cost and logistics and not demand, and that customers are 'lifting the bare minimum'[4]. We cannot verify that claim from outside, because the independent searches we ran for freight rates and tariff changes returned nothing in the time available. If a competitor is winning the orders instead, the order book and export share would keep falling through December 2026.

Pricing power and raw materials

Transformer oil became almost twice as dear between February and May 2026, and other commodities rose 10 to 25 percent[5]. Oil is 8 to 12 percent of the cost of a transformer[5]. Management says suppliers asked for higher prices even on material already booked, a 'force majeure' event, and that it asked customers for the same relief[5]. By August it had passed on 50 to 60 percent of the rise on older orders, and new orders were priced at current costs[4]. So the margin hole of the June quarter should narrow as old orders ship out. The pace of that narrowing is the main thing the 28 October results will show, and it decides the margin line of our scorecard.

Where the strength is thin

Three points keep the verdict from resting on quality alone. First, the stock fell with the whole power-equipment group: GE Vernova T&D India, Hitachi Energy India and Indo Tech Transformers are each 32 to 34 percent below their 52-week highs in the 23 October ledger[33], so part of the fall is a sector re-rating and not company news. Second, the company-specific part is large: sales down 15 percent in a quarter when peers grew sharply[3][24][28]. Third, a promoter sold shares near the top. Alay Shah, the chairman, sold 117,000 shares on 21 October 2025 by open-market sale, and the annual report implies a further 100,000 shares, 217,000 in all[20][2]. A news site puts the first sale at ₹4,350.26 a share, worth ₹50.89 crore[21].

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management gave specific, dated promises in the last 18 months, and the record is mixed. The company kept its corporate promises: it said in April 2025 that a bonus issue would make it eligible for the National Stock Exchange, and the bonus came on 9 June 2025 and the NSE listing on 24 November 2025[7][2][11]. It has held the April 2027 date for its new plant at every update[10][5][4].

It has missed its operating promises. FY26 sales came in at ₹651.94 crore against a target of ₹750 crore that was repeated in October 2025 as 'on track'[7][6][3]. Utilisation was 79 percent against 90 to 95 percent promised[6][3]. On 5 May 2026 management called the June quarter 'a normal quarter' and said it would do its best to keep margins near 29 to 31 percent[5]. The quarter then delivered sales down 15 percent and a margin of 16.4 percent[3]. The annual report, written in early June and circulated in July, spoke of the 'normalisation of dispatches to the Middle East'; on the August call the chairman said the crisis had lasted longer than expected[2][4]. Each cause was real, but four misses among five decided operating promises is the reason we treat the FY27 revenue target of about ₹800 crore as a stretch and not as the starting point for our estimates.

The next promise is already open. Management says FY27 revenue is 'on track', and puts it near ₹800 crore[5][4]. After ₹134.61 crore in the first quarter, that needs ₹665 crore in the next three, or ₹222 crore a quarter, against a best-ever quarter of ₹232 crore in March 2025[3][1].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report has a weakest assumption. This chapter names each one, says what evidence would show it wrong and when that evidence arrives, then gives the strongest honest case against our own view and the single claim the verdict hangs on.

Earnings estimate: the margin comes back

Our central case needs the operating margin to rise from 16.4 percent in June to about 22 percent for FY27 and 25 percent in FY28. The weakest link is pass-through. Management says it passed on only 50 to 60 percent of the metal price rise on orders executed in the spring, and that new orders are priced at current costs[4]. If new orders are priced at current costs but the export share stays low, the margin settles near the domestic level. Peers show that level at 14 to 16 percent[24][25][28][27]. Evidence against us would be a September-quarter margin under 20 percent, or an order book below ₹450 crore. We know on 28 October 2026[12]. If the margin is under 20 percent, the central case moves toward the bear case, and the weights in chapter 5 would be changed at the next ledger date.

Multiple: 29.4 times holds

The central value keeps today's multiple. Our own series puts it at the 22nd percentile of the last 30 months, so it is not high by this stock's history. But the market is in a downtrend, and the four peers in the ledger are also 32 to 38 percent off their highs[33][32]. New capacity: 6,500 MVA arrives into a market where everyone is expanding

The chairman says every transformer maker is adding capacity but that demand is large enough for all[5]. The new block can make larger units, up to 160 MVA at 220 kV. That needs customer audits, type tests and registration with the grid company. He expects three to four months after production starts to complete the first larger prototype[5][4].
