Before you read

Shilchar Technologies (NSE: SHILCTECH) is a Vadodara maker of made-to-order transformers for solar farms, wind farms, industry and exporters, and it has no debt.[1] The business looks strong, with a 43 percent return on capital and no pledged shares, but profit in the June 2026 quarter fell by half and the share price is in a downtrend.[2][6] The verdict is REJECTED for now, because the chart fails every trend test, only two of five operating measures pass, and the price has no tested floor. The idea reopens if the September 2026 quarter shows a margin of at least 21 percent and the price wins back about 4,090 rupees, and then every test is run again. The weakest link is how fast margins recover, which management has overestimated three times in a row[10][11][3].

1. What the company does

Shilchar Technologies makes transformers, the steel-and-copper units that raise or lower electric voltage so that power can travel and then be used. Its plant at Gavasad, near Vadodara, builds each unit to a customer's drawing instead of from stock.[1] The range runs from distribution units up to 33 kV, through inverter-duty units that connect solar farms and generator units for wind farms, to power transformers up to 66 kV and hydro units up to 132 kV.[2] Size is counted in MVA (mega volt-amperes). The plant can make 7,500 MVA a year, and it used about 79 percent of that in FY26, which is roughly 5,925 MVA of finished units.[1] Copper, transformer oil, electrical steel (called CRGO) and aluminium make up about 80 to 85 percent of raw material cost.[4] The company buys about 70 percent of its materials only after an order is confirmed, so it does not carry large metal stocks.[13] An order takes 3 to 9 months to execute, and a standard power transformer ships in 12 to 16 weeks.[4][11]

The customers are private-sector power developers, the engineering firms that build solar and wind parks, and industrial plants such as steel, cement, sugar and oil and gas. Management says it does no business with state utility companies and plans to keep it that way, and CARE notes that private customers give better credit terms.[3][4] Exports were 48.31 percent of FY26 turnover and went to more than 25 countries.[1] Of the ₹651.94 crore of FY26 sales, ₹337.02 crore was domestic and ₹314.92 crore was exported: ₹99.34 crore to North America, ₹195.10 crore to Europe and the Middle East, and ₹20.49 crore elsewhere.[1] No single customer was above 10 percent of revenue in FY26, against three customers in FY25.[1] Even so, the top five domestic customers took 57 percent of domestic sales, which CARE calls moderate concentration.[4] Management also says about 30 percent of FY26 revenue came from Middle East exports, so one region carried almost a third of sales.[11]

The unit economics are unusually good. Raw materials cost ₹403.65 crore on ₹651.94 crore of sales, or 61.9 percent, so the gross margin (sales minus the cost of materials, as a share of sales) was about 38 percent. Staff cost took 3.7 percent and other costs 5.0 percent, which left earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) of ₹190.42 crore, a 29.2 percent margin.[1][2] One MVA of output sold for about ₹0.11 crore in FY26 (₹651.94 crore divided by 5,925 MVA). The new plant adds 6,500 MVA for about ₹120 crore of capital expenditure (money spent on plant and machines), funded from the company's own cash.[1] At today's price per MVA, 6,500 MVA could support up to ₹715 crore of yearly sales, which is about six times the outlay. That figure assumes the plant is full and sold at FY26 prices, so it is a ceiling and not a forecast. Peers earn far less: Spark Capital puts the peer average EBITDA margin at about 15 percent against Shilchar's 29 percent.[13] Management says exports earn about 10 points more margin than domestic orders, so the mix of orders matters as much as the volume.[3]

Demand follows the building of power plants and grids. India added a record 55.29 GW of non-fossil capacity in FY26,[17] and the industry body IEEMA reports that its FY26 growth index for power transformers rose 31.6 percent.[16] The cycle still bites in three ways. First, metal prices move quickly while quotes are fixed, and on orders due in Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27 the company could pass on only 50 to 60 percent of the rise.[3] Second, freight and war can stop exports. Container costs rose three to five times in Q1 FY27 and customers lifted only the bare minimum.[3] Third, rivals are adding capacity: the annual report cites industry capacity rising from about 375 GVA in FY25 to about 690 GVA by FY28.[1] Grid spending is a long tailwind, since the national plan calls for ₹9.15 lakh crore of transmission investment by 2032.[18] Cancellation is rare because every unit is custom-built. Management said orders in the book were pushed out, not cancelled.[3] Working capital, the cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills, is the other swing factor. Receivables absorbed ₹135.77 crore of cash in FY25 and released ₹71.84 crore in FY26. Net of stock and supplier credit, working capital absorbed about ₹116.00 crore in FY25 and released about ₹37.17 crore in FY26.[1]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of this business is a niche it has worked for a long time. Shilchar moved to private-sector customers in FY13, which Spark Capital says shortened collection time and made price pass-through easier.[13] It entered renewable transformers in 2010 and now makes about 90 percent of its domestic sales there.[2][13] Export buyers need three to five years to qualify a supplier, which makes them slow to switch.[13] The plant has three parallel test lines, an impulse generator and a lab accredited by NABL (the national testing accreditation body), which supports lead times of 10 to 16 weeks depending on the unit.[2][3] CARE raised its rating from BBB+ in June 2023 to A- in 2024 and A in July 2025, and reaffirmed A (Stable) in July 2026.[4][5]

The financial evidence backs the claim. Revenue grew from ₹180.18 crore in FY22 to ₹651.94 crore in FY26, a 37.9 percent yearly rate, and profit after tax grew from ₹14.01 crore to ₹158.16 crore, an 83.3 percent yearly rate.[2] The company has had no borrowings since FY23 and held ₹245.95 crore of cash and investments at 31 March 2026.[2] The margin gap to peers is wide. In FY26 Shilchar earned about 29 percent, while Voltamp, Transformers and Rectifiers, Atlanta Electricals and Quality Power earned 15 to 19 percent.[13] Voltamp's trailing margin is 16 percent, and Atlanta's June 2026 quarter was 17 percent.[27][30]

The weaknesses are scale, dependence on exports and one site. Sales of ₹652 crore are under a third of Voltamp's ₹2,154 crore.[13] CARE rates the scale as moderate and the industry as fragmented and competitive.[4] All 7,500 MVA sits at one plant in Gavasad, so a flood, fire or licence problem would stop everything. About 12 percent of FY26 pre-tax profit was other income, including a ₹12.09 crore gain from the weaker rupee, and the company has no active hedging policy.[1][4] The June 2026 quarter showed the cost of that dependence. Exports slowed, the margin fell to 16.4 percent, and it landed at the level of domestic peers.[2][13] That is the key lesson of this chapter: the margin premium is not a fixed trait of the company. It depends on a high export share and on metal prices that can be passed on.

Governance is clean on the main tests, with two flags. The auditor, CNK and Associates, reported no key audit matters for FY26, and the company has no pledge on promoter shares.[1][6] Four of seven directors are independent.[4] The flags are these. Chairman Alay Shah cut his holding by 2,17,000 shares in FY26, from 29,26,548 shares (19,51,032 before the 1-for-2 bonus, times 1.5) to 27,09,548, and promoter holding fell from 64.01 to 62.12 percent. The annual report does not say how the shares were disposed of.[1] The company also sold ₹5.32 crore of goods to Nile Transformers, an entity controlled by relatives of directors, against ₹1.14 crore a year earlier, and ₹1.27 crore was still unpaid at year end.[1] Neither is large, but both are worth tracking. A ₹6.43 crore GST demand is disputed and not provided for.[1]

Valuation context is simple. The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) is the share price divided by a year of earnings per share (EPS), and it tells you how many years of today's profit you pay for. At 3,769.80 the P/E is 31.4 on trailing profit of ₹120.22 per share, against 33.9 for Voltamp, 32.8 for Indo Tech and 36.1 for Transformers and Rectifiers.[26][27][28][29] Management guidance (the company's own forecast of its results) is the other thing to weigh, and the track record is mixed, as Chapter 2B shows. What this means for an owner: the business is strong and the finances are safe, but the premium margin is the part you are paying for, and Q1 FY27 shows it can vanish for a quarter or two.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 compares today's level of each measure with its direction of travel over the last four to eight quarters. Technical terms are needed here. The 50-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 50 trading days, and the 200-day moving average is the same over 200 days. A base is a flat sideways stretch where a stock rests before a move, and a pivot is the top of that stretch, and a breakout is a move above that top on rising volume. Stage 2 is the Weinstein stage of a confirmed uptrend, and Stage 4 is the stage of a confirmed downtrend. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing, here ₹130.23. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold, and the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned. Asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, so 3.0 means ₹3 of possible gain for each ₹1 of possible loss. The stop is placed below the lower of the recent swing low and the two long averages, minus one ATR, which gives 3,720.00 minus 130.23, or 3,589.77.[14]
