Billionbrains Garage Ventures is the listed parent of Groww, India's largest retail investing app by active NSE clients, and most of its income is trading fees[2][23]. Our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The business is large and very profitable, but at ₹186.57 the price already assumes roughly 17 to 21 percent yearly profit growth for ten years, and analyst estimates are being cut. 1. What the company does

Groww is an investing app. A customer opens an account on a phone, adds money, and then buys shares, mutual funds, bonds, gold and silver funds, futures and options, or commodity contracts. The listed company that owns the app is Billionbrains Garage Ventures Limited. Its shares began trading on the NSE and BSE on 12 November 2025, after an offer priced at ₹100 a share[27]. By September 2026 the app had 2.4 crore total transacting users, and customers held ₹3.9 lakh crore of assets on the platforms[2]. (A crore is ten million. A lakh is one hundred thousand.)

The company earns money in four main ways. First, it charges a small fee on every trade. For futures, options and commodities the fee is ₹20 an order. For shares it is the lower of ₹20 or 10 basis points, which is 0.1 percent of the trade[3]. This fee is called brokerage, and it is the largest line: fees and commissions were 78.35 percent of total income in the year to March 2026[12]. Second, it lends money to traders who want to buy more shares than they can pay for. That product is the margin trading facility, or MTF. The customer pays interest and the shares act as security. Third, it earns interest on the cash that sits with exchanges and banks on behalf of customers, and on its own treasury. Fourth, it sells mutual funds, wealth products and IPO applications, and it runs its own asset manager[2][12].

Most of the money still comes from one product. Equity derivatives (futures and options) made up 44.8 percent of total income in the September 2026 quarter, and that share was 12.2 percentage points lower than a year earlier[2]. This drop is the central fact of the report. It happened because new exchange rules cut trading volume, not because other products grew fast enough to replace the lost fees. Chapter 2 explains the rules, and Chapter 3 shows how large the effect is.

The customers are mostly individuals who invest or trade small amounts. The company says 80 percent of new customers are acquired organically, which means without paid advertising, and the app reaches more than 97 percent of Indian postal codes[13]. On the NSE, 1,34,48,194 clients had traded at least once in the past twelve months on 30 September 2026. That is 29.16 percent of the exchange total of 4,61,20,857[22][23].

Under the parent sit a stock broker (Groww Invest Tech), a lender registered as a non-bank finance company (Groww Creditserv Technology), an asset manager (Groww Asset Management), a payments business, and the wealth business Fisdom, which Groww bought in October 2025[1][6]. The broker and the lender lend out the group's own money. That matters in Chapter 3, because lending makes the cash flow statement look weak while profit is strong.

Why a broker's balance sheet looks odd

A broker holds other people's money, so its balance sheet is much larger than its own wealth. On 30 September 2026 the group owed ₹7,607 crore to trade creditors, mostly customers and exchanges, and held ₹6,683 crore in bank balances set aside for clients and exchanges[1]. Those amounts are not the company's savings. The company's own cash and investments were ₹3,849 crore by its own definition[2], and the owners' stake in the business, called equity, was ₹11,568 crore[1].

This split decides how to read three common measures. Debt looks tiny because the company funds itself with equity, not borrowing. Operating cash flow looks weak because every rupee lent to a trader is counted as an operating outflow. And return on equity looks high because the equity is small next to the volume of business. Chapter 3 treats each of these with the right adjustment.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Relative strength index (RSI) is a meter from 0 to 100 that shows how fast a price has risen or fallen lately. Readings below 30 are called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • Average true range (ATR) is the average size of a stock's daily price swing, in rupees. A price that sits two ATR away from a level is about two normal days of movement away.
  • Earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit divided by its number of shares. It is the per-share profit that the price is compared with.
  • Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit before financing costs, tax and accounting write-offs. Groww defines it a little differently, and the report says so where it matters.
  • Return on capital employed (ROCE) is operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. It is designed for factories and shops, so for a broker we lean on return on equity instead.
  • Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by the profit per share. A price-to-earnings figure of 40 means buyers pay ₹40 for each ₹1 of yearly profit.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment. For a lender, the cash that goes out as loans muddies this figure.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash that the day-to-day business generates after tax, as shown in the cash flow statement. For Groww, loans to traders sit inside this line.
  • Debt-to-equity is borrowed money divided by owners' money. A figure of 1.0 means the business owes as much as the owners put in.
  • Net debt is borrowing minus cash. A negative figure means the company holds more cash than it owes.
  • Consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who cover the stock. Rising forecasts are a good sign, and falling forecasts are a warning.
  • Guidance is what management says it expects to happen. We check later whether it did.
  • Working capital is the money tied up in day-to-day items, such as what customers owe the company and what the company owes suppliers.
  • Dilution is the fall in each shareholder's slice when a company issues new shares, for example to staff through share options.
  • PEG is the price-to-earnings ratio divided by the yearly profit growth rate, quoted as price-to-earnings-to-growth. A PEG near 1 is a common marker of fair pricing, and 1.5 is a premium.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days. It is a slow line that short moves tend to bounce off.
  • 50-day moving average is the same average over 50 trading days, a measure of the medium-term trend.
  • 200-day moving average is the same average over 200 trading days, a measure of the long-term trend.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend in the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. Stage 1 is a flat floor and Stage 3 is a topping or pullback phase.
  • Base is a sideways stretch where a price rests before its next move. It is the platform from which a rise starts.
  • Pivot is the top of a base, the price level a rise has to clear to count as real.
  • Breakout is the move that takes a price above a base or pivot on strong volume.
  • Retest is the pullback after a breakout, when the price comes back to test the level it just cleared.
  • Stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong and the position is sold.
  • Limit order is an instruction to buy only at a named price or lower, so a fall does not catch you out and a spike does not push you into a worse price.
  • Entry zone is the price range in which we would be willing to buy.
  • Asymmetry is how much we can gain compared with how much we can lose, and reward-to-risk is that same comparison written as a ratio, for example 2.6 to 1.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Groww is the largest broker by customers, but not yet by trading. On 30 September 2026 it had 1,34,48,194 active NSE clients, against 67,91,541 for Zerodha and 67,55,711 for Angel One[22]. That is a 29.16 percent share[23]. From July to September it added about 3.24 lakh net clients, nearly 48 percent of the whole industry's gain[23]. Angel One is closing in on Zerodha for second place[21]. Reach on this scale is hard to copy, and it feeds every other product.

Many clients do not mean much trading. In the September quarter Groww held 15.4 percent of retail cash turnover and 11.0 percent of retail derivatives premium turnover[2]. Both are about half of its 29 percent client share. An analyst asked on the 14 October call why the gap is so wide. The chief financial officer said Groww's customers are newer, that turnover per customer rises as accounts age, and that the two shares may converge in about ten years[3]. So the competitive position is strong on reach and unproven on depth.

The cost position is the second strength. The group built its own technology, and the company reports an EBITDA of ₹969 crore on revenue of ₹1,501.5 crore in the September quarter, a margin of about 64.5 percent[2]. Total expenses were only 36.1 percent of total income. The screening site puts Angel One's comparable margin at 45 percent for the same quarter[17]. The caution is that compute costs for artificial intelligence and higher trading days pushed the cost of serving customers up in the same quarter, and management says part of that rise will stay[2][3].

The weaknesses are as real as the strengths. A large share of income depends on one product that regulators keep reshaping. Switching is easy for a trader, since a second account takes minutes. An analyst told management on the same call that some discount brokers had cut brokerage by half. The chief executive answered that Groww watches the customer, not competitors, and that customers stay if they feel they get more value than they pay for[3]. That is a reasonable philosophy, but it is not evidence of pricing power.

Evidence that the moat is real

Customer loyalty shows up in how few clients leave. In the June quarter Groww added 115 thousand net NSE active clients while the whole industry lost about 257 thousand[4]. Retention matters because clients who stay keep paying fees for years. The client count did dip from 13.2 million in January 2025 to 11.9 million in September 2025 after the 2024 derivatives rules, and has since recovered to 13.45 million[9][23]. Groww also leads in direct mutual funds: it says it holds 43 percent of new systematic investment plans created in FY26 and 3.4 percent of retail mutual fund assets[13].

Customers also use more than one product. Management says more than half use more than two products, and more than 5 percent use more than four[3]. A customer who has a mutual fund, a loan and a trading account is costly to win over, which supports the claim of a real moat. The evidence is management's own count, not an audited number.

Evidence that the moat is thin

Rules can erase fees overnight. A SEBI circular dated 1 October 2024 raised the contract size of index derivatives from a range of ₹0.50 to ₹1.00 million to a range of ₹1.50 to ₹2.00 million from 20 November 2024, and cut the number of weekly expiries. On 1 October 2024 SEBI also required brokers to pass on exchange charges without a markup, under its true-to-label circular[10]. The Groww offer document cites an industry study showing that daily derivatives turnover fell about 38 percent between June 2024 and June 2025, and that individual participation in futures and options fell about 36 percent[10].

A second round of rules arrived in August 2026. SEBI introduced a closing auction session for stocks that have derivatives, effective 3 August 2026[15]. Groww says its equity derivatives revenue fell 14.3 percent from the June quarter, mostly because of this change, in line with the industry[2]. On 15 October 2026 SEBI decided to settle expiring derivatives on the average price of the last 30 minutes of normal trading rather than on the auction price, from 7 December 2026[14]. That helps. It does not return the volume lost in 2024, and it does not stop future rule changes.

Lending is the third source of fragility. The margin lending book grew from ₹1,668 crore to ₹4,351 crore in a year[2]. Fast growth in loans secured by shares works well when markets rise. When markets fall quickly, the lender must sell the shares and hope the money covers the loan. Management said on the April 2026 call that most customers were still profitable after a sharp March fall, and on the October call that it now caps funding in crowded stocks, with haircuts of up to 80 percent in some cases[3][7]. A haircut means the lender advances only the remaining 20 percent of a share's value.

What a rival could copy and what it could not

A rival could copy the pricing, the screens and the tools, and some already offer similar ones. A rival could not quickly copy a customer count of 1.34 crore active clients, a lead in new mutual fund investment plans, or a ten-year-old brand that grew mostly organically[13][22]. The first group of features decides the next quarter's trading volume. The second decides the next five years.

For an owner, the point is a split verdict. The reach and cost position deserve a quality premium. The dependence on rule-sensitive fees, and the thin evidence on pricing power, deserve a limit on how much of that premium to pay. Chapter 2B puts numbers on both.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter turns the business into numbers and then tests them against the Layer 1 rules. Three adjustments come first, because Groww is a broker and a lender. Debt is tiny because the group funds itself with owners' money. Cash flow from operations is small or negative because loans to traders are counted as operations. And a broker's balance sheet holds customer money that the company does not own. Where a standard rule does not fit, the table says which equivalent we use and flags it for the reviewer.

Revenue from operations grew from ₹1,141.5 crore in FY23 to ₹4,644.6 crore in FY26, a compound rate of 59.6 percent a year. Profit for the year rose from ₹457.7 crore to ₹2,083.0 crore, or 65.7 percent a year. The screening site shows 60 and 66 percent for the same three-year growth rates[10][12][16]. FY26 was slower than the earlier years: revenue grew 19 percent and profit 14 percent[13], because SEBI's October 2024 rules cut derivatives volume. Growth then picked up. In the half year to September 2026 revenue rose 56.1 percent to ₹3,003.0 crore and profit rose 71.8 percent to ₹1,459.7 crore[1].

Two years need explaining. FY24 shows a loss of ₹805.5 crore. The offer document attributes it to a one-time tax charge of ₹1,339.7 crore on the 2024 merger that moved the group's holding company back to India, and to a one-time management bonus of ₹778.6 crore[10]. Neither is expected to repeat. FY25 profit of ₹1,824.4 crore is the first clean year.

The latest quarter shows a pause. Revenue from operations was ₹1,501.53 crore in the September 2026 quarter against ₹1,501.42 crore in the June quarter, and profit was ₹724.70 crore against ₹735.04 crore[1]. The tax rate was about 26 percent, in line with prior quarters. Revenue is up 47.4 percent on a year ago, so the business is growing. It is not growing from quarter to quarter, and that is why the price is unchanged over the past month. Press reports of the same results agree on revenue of about ₹1,502 crore and profit of about ₹725 crore[31][32][33].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter names the weakest assumption in each estimate, states the strongest honest case against our verdict, and says when each doubt will be settled. A report that cannot say how it could be wrong has not looked hard enough.

Weakest assumption 1: derivatives fees stop falling after 7 December

The central EPS estimates assume total income grows from ₹1,541 crore in the September quarter to ₹1,560 crore and ₹1,600 crore in the next two quarters. That growth needs derivatives revenue to stop falling. SEBI's circular of 15 October 2026 moves the settlement of expiring contracts back to the average price of the last 30 minutes of normal trading, from 7 December 2026[14]. Management says this should normalise activity but not lift it above normal[3].

The assumption fails if volumes stay low because traders have left, as the October 2024 rules showed. The offer document records a fall of about 38 percent in industry derivatives turnover in a year after those rules[10]. Evidence of failure would be December-quarter derivatives revenue below the September quarter's roughly ₹690 crore. We will know when the December quarter is reported, expected in mid-January 2027. The date is not announced, and past earnings calls were held on 14 October, 15 July and 20 April[3][5][7].

Weakest assumption 2: the loan book will not suffer large losses

Our profit estimates carry modest loan-loss costs. The lending books are growing fast: the margin book rose 161 percent in a year and total loans rose from ₹4,247 crore to ₹6,134 crore in six months[1][2]. The company's own provision for loss was ₹35.26 crore in the half year[1].

The weak spot is a sharp market fall. In March 2026 the market fell 16 to 17 percent in a quarter and the industry margin book fell 7 percent[6]. An analyst on the October call referred to a one-off cost in the margin book in March, and the chief financial officer said the company changed its policy after that event[3]. Evidence of failure would be an unusual loan-loss charge, a drop in the margin book while the market is flat, or any extra capital put into the lending subsidiary. We would see it in the January 2027 results.

Weakest assumption 3: institutions will absorb the early investors' shares

The estimate of the central price assumes the multiple holds at 33 times. That requires buyers to keep taking shares from early investors. The sellers are patient and price-insensitive: on the offer document's figures Peak XV paid ₹1.91 a share, Y Combinator ₹3.45, Ribbit Capital V ₹2.30 and Sequoia's growth fund ₹37.94[11]. Peak XV held 15.68 percent before its September sale[24], and Ribbit-linked entities held 10.10 percent in June[25].

The promoters' own lock-in is the next dated event. SEBI rules lock in 20 percent of post-IPO capital held by promoters for 18 months from allotment[10]. Allotment was on 10 November 2025[27], so that lock-in ends on about 10 May 2027, our calculation. The pre-IPO investors' six-month lock-in ended about 10 May 2026, and the anchor lock-ins ended in December 2025 and about February 2026, by the same calculation[10][26][28]. The 30-day promises given at each block sale have also lapsed[24][25]. Evidence of failure would be the December-quarter shareholding pattern showing foreign corporate bodies still falling while mutual funds stop buying.

Weakest assumption 4: the 33 times multiple is fair

The target multiple of 33 times FY28 earnings is a judgement, not a measured median. The stock has traded for only eleven months, so the three-year median price-to-earnings figure the scoring rules prefer is not published. We built the multiple from a PEG of 1.5 and a three-turn premium to the highest listed peer[17][18][19][20].

The assumption fails if the market decides that broking income is worth peer multiples of 20 to 30 times, whatever the growth rate. The bear case uses 25 times. Evidence would be a daily close below ₹174.24, the same rule that cancels or exits the position in Chapter 7. A multiple-only stress shows the size of the risk: keep EPS at ₹5.75 and use 25 times, which gives ₹143.75, or minus 22.95 percent against ₹186.57 and minus 19.47 percent against ₹178.50. The 2.64 to 1 ratio is a central-target ratio, not a stopped-trade expectancy. We would know on any trading day.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, and the posture is watch-only. Nothing is placed in the market today. The rule is the support ladder in the scoring file: a price 1.39 ATR above support falls in the band from 1.2 to 1.6 ATR, which allows only a limit order and a reduced size. On top of that the momentum gate reads NO_MOMENTUM and consensus estimates are falling. At the current price of ₹186.57 the reward-to-risk ratio is only 0.26 to 1, so we would not buy at market.

Activation takes four steps in order. First, the stock must close inside ₹175.50 to ₹178.50. Second, that evening we rerun the full momentum gate on a fresh ledger. Third, if any one of Test A, B or C passes, we place two limit orders for the next session, half at ₹178.50 and half at ₹176.50, for the full 2.0 percent of net worth. Fourth, any daily close below ₹174.24 cancels unfilled orders or exits a filled position. No order rests in the market before step one, and no volume condition applies to the limit order itself. Volume is part of the gate rerun.

There is one disclosed exception. If the stock closes in the zone and no test passes, we would still place only the lower order, at ₹176.50, for 1.0 percent of net worth, which is half size. The reason is the technical routing rule in the scoring file: a Stage 3 pullback that passes the business and balance-sheet tests is routed to a limit-order entry near support and not rejected. This is an entry without momentum confirmation, so it is capped at half size. The second half waits until a gate test passes. If the exception is not wanted, the sequence simply ends at step two and we keep watching.

The stop needs a plain explanation. The stop of ₹174.24 is only ₹4.26 below the zone top of ₹178.50, which is 0.65 ATR, and one ATR is a normal day's swing. So noise can stop the position out. The risk-unit rule limits the harm: at 2.0 percent of net worth a stop-out at ₹174.24 costs about 0.05 percent of net worth. The rule does not model a gap through the stop. A gap to the bear price of ₹125 would cost about 30 percent of the position, or 0.60 percent of net worth at full size. That is above the 0.35 percent risk budget, which is why the exception tier is held to half size, where the same gap costs about 0.30 percent.

The targets are ₹189.75 for the central case, ₹232.20 for the bull case and ₹125 for the bear case. The reward-to-risk ratio of 2.64 to 1 at the zone top compares the zone top with the central target. It is not an expected return and not a stopped-trade expectancy. If the stock reaches ₹189.75 we would keep half and trail the stop under the rising 50-day average, because the let-winners-run rule forbids selling only because the RSI is high. If it reaches ₹232.20 we would reassess against the FY28 earnings. If it closes below ₹174.24 we would sell the whole position and not average down.

A second path does not need a pullback. If the December quarter total income exceeds ₹1,541 crore and the gate rerun passes on a close above the 20-day average of ₹190.00 or the 50-day average of ₹192.19, the setup can become a momentum entry. Today's central target of ₹189.75 is below those levels, so no reward remains at the current targets. Targets and stop would have to be rebuilt from updated earnings before any purchase, and the entry would be a retest, not a chase.

The next heading sets out Layer 3, the step that turns a verdict into an order and a size. It has three parts. Support-aware entry posture asks how far the price is from a floor, in ATR units, and sets the entry zone and stop. Risk-unit sizing sets the position by how much is lost if the stop is hit. Let-winners-run says when a rising position may be left alone. Four labels appear in the table. CMP is the current market price. TIER_4 is the smallest active position tier, 1 to 2 percent of net worth. A basis point (bps) is 0.01 percent, so 35 bps is 0.35 percent of net worth at risk. EMA is the exponential moving average, an average that weights recent days more, and SMA is the simple moving average. The arithmetic is 35 bps divided by the 2.39 percent stop distance, which is about 14.6 percent of net worth. That is far above the 2.0 percent cap, so the cap binds and the size is 2.0 percent. For a reader, the point is that the stop is tight and the cap, not the stop, sets how much is at risk.
