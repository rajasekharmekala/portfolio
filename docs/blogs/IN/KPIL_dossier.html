Before you read

Kalpataru Projects International (KPIL) builds power lines, buildings, pipelines and water systems, and it holds ₹66,607 crore of signed work[2]. It passes every hard financial test, so we rate it a sound business, but the shares are not yet at a price we would pay[9][10]. Our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: we would place a buy order only between ₹1,255 and ₹1,295, where the gain to our ₹1,560 target is about three times the loss to our ₹1,205.90 stop. At today's ₹1,324.70 that ratio is 1.98, just under our 2.0 floor. We are wrong if stuck water-project cash does not clear, if profit margins stall, or if the shares close below ₹1,205.90.

1. What the company does

Kalpataru Projects International Limited, called KPIL here, is a contractor. A customer such as a power-grid operator or a property developer hands it a project. KPIL designs the work, buys the steel and cement, hires the crews, builds it and hands it over. The trade calls this engineering, procurement and construction, or EPC. KPIL was set up in 1981 and now works in 75 countries, with live projects in more than 30 of them[11][2].

In FY26 (the year to March 2026) the group booked sales of ₹27,143 crore and ended the year with an order book of ₹65,457 crore[2]. The order book is signed work not yet built. By 30 June 2026 it had grown to ₹66,607 crore, about 2.5 times a year of sales[11]. A crore is ten million rupees, so ₹66,607 crore is roughly ₹666 billion.

Who pays KPIL

KPIL's customers include Power Grid Corporation of India, which Crisil rates AAA, state transmission utilities, private property developers and public-sector companies[11]. In the Middle East it now qualifies to bid for work from Saudi Aramco, ADNOC, Qatar and Kuwait[3]. Government-focused projects fell from about 35% of the order book in FY23 to about 20% in FY26, and about 80% of the book now comes from public-sector companies, private firms and overseas buyers[11].

International work is 39% of the order book[11]. This matters for two reasons. Overseas jobs often earn higher margins, but they carry currency, labour and political risk. Brazil shows the downside, and Chapter 2B covers it. The water business sells mainly to state governments under the Jal Jeevan Mission, and ICRA reports that most vendors there wait more than six months for payment[29].

How KPIL earns its profit

KPIL wins a contract at a fixed or formula price and then tries to build for less. In FY26 its cost lines were materials of ₹9,397.26 crore, staff of ₹2,650.43 crore and other expenses of ₹1,940.35 crore, and sub-contractors took a further large share[4][16]. The operating profit margin was 8.8% in the June 2026 quarter, up 30 basis points on a year earlier[2]. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point.

Crisil notes that almost half of the order book has no clause passing steel or fuel price rises on to the customer[11]. KPIL hedges part of that risk and keeps contingency money in each project. A sharp rise in steel or diesel would still cut profit on those contracts.

What changed in three years

Between FY22 and FY26 KPIL spent about ₹3,000 crore on equipment, factories and tunnel-boring machines[2]. It now has six tunnel-boring machines and can make 2,40,000 tonnes of transmission towers a year[3][2]. Owning the kit lets it start many towers or pipeline spreads at the same time, which management calls a speed advantage on large contracts[5].

The balance sheet changed even more. Group debt after cash fell from ₹1,902 crore in FY22 to ₹915 crore in FY26, and the average project size rose to nearly ₹500 crore[2]. Both Crisil and India Ratings upgraded KPIL to AA+ in 2026[13][11].

The consolidated results include more than twenty subsidiaries, joint ventures and joint operations[4]. Five matter for this report. Shree Shubham Logistics is the third: KPIL has put in ₹332 crore of equity and loans, and the unit repaid its bank debt by 31 July 2026[3]. JMC Mining and Quarries is a mining subsidiary named in Crisil's consolidation list, and KPIL does not publish its figures separately. The auditor's note shows how small the unreviewed units are: eleven subsidiaries with total revenue of ₹22.45 crore and a net loss of ₹20.06 crore in the June 2026 quarter[4][11]. The fifth is the road business, led by the Kurukshetra Expressway joint venture, whose equity KPIL carries at zero[3].

Guarantees show how much KPIL stands behind its subsidiaries. At 31 March 2026 the parent had given corporate guarantees of ₹850.80 crore and bank guarantees or standby letters of credit of ₹1,158.81 crore on behalf of subsidiaries[9]. Together that is about ₹2,009.61 crore, which is not on the balance sheet.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is operating profit before the cost of equipment wear and debt. In this report it follows the company's own definition unless we say otherwise.
  • ROCE (return on capital employed) is the yearly profit before interest and tax divided by the capital the business uses. Higher means each rupee of capital earns more.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is the yearly profit divided by the number of shares. This report uses consolidated profit after tax.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is cash from operations minus spending on equipment and buildings. It is what is left for lenders and owners.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash a business generates from its work, after tax, and before it buys equipment or repays debt.
  • net debt is borrowings minus cash and bank deposits. KPIL's own figure leaves out an interest-free loan linked to a past asset sale, so we also show a wider count.
  • debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' equity. Our hard limit is 1.20 times for a company like KPIL.
  • working capital is the money tied up in unbilled work and unpaid bills, less what the company owes suppliers. In EPC it is the main use of cash.
  • guidance is the targets management gives for the coming year. We later check whether past targets were met.
  • consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow a stock. The session ledger has none for KPIL, so we use two broker notes instead.
  • capital expenditure is money spent on equipment, plants and other long-lived assets.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share. A P/E of 20 means investors pay ₹20 for each ₹1 of yearly profit.
  • relative strength index (RSI) is a 14-day gauge of how fast a price has risen or fallen. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • average true range (ATR) is the typical size of a day's price swing over 14 days. We measure distances to support in ATR units.
  • A base is a stretch of sideways trading after a fall, where buyers and sellers balance. A pivot is the price at the top of that base, and a breakout is a close above it. A retest is a later dip back to the pivot that holds.
  • Stage 2 is a rising phase in which the price sits above a rising 50-day average that is itself above the 200-day average. Stage 4 is a falling phase in which the price sits below both averages and both are falling.
  • A stop loss is a price at which we accept the idea has failed and sell. A limit order is an order to buy only at or below a chosen price, and the entry zone is the price range where we would place such an order.
  • asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the gain from the price to our target divided by the loss from the price to the stop. Our floor is 2.0 for a core holding.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

KPIL has a narrow moat. A moat is a lasting advantage that keeps rivals out. KPIL's advantage is not a patent or a brand. It is a long record of finishing large jobs, equipment it owns, and a balance sheet strong enough to bid for contracts that smaller firms cannot[11][2]. Those edges are real, but a rival with money and time can copy them, so we do not treat KPIL as a franchise with pricing power.

Where the business is strong

The power transmission franchise is the core strength. Crisil calls KPIL a market leader in transmission and distribution with a long operating record[11]. KPIL says it has completed more than 38,000 km of transmission contracts and delivered 2.8 million tonnes of towers[2]. Demand is large and visible. The National Electricity Plan for 2023 to 2032 costs ₹9.15 lakh crore, lifts the grid from 4.85 lakh to 6.48 lakh circuit-km and adds nine high-voltage direct current (HVDC) lines of 33.25 GW[26]. POWERGRID expects ₹7.9 lakh crore of transmission spending through 2035-36 to be put out to bid over three to four years, with ₹1.19 lakh crore already in the pipeline[27]. Management sees an Indian market of ₹1 lakh crore to ₹1.25 lakh crore a year for five years[3].

The order book is better than it was. Transmission and buildings and factories rose from about 60% of the unexecuted order book in March 2025 to about 75% in June 2026, and government-department work fell[11]. A new rule from the Ministry of Power sets local-content floors for HVDC converter stations that rise from 30% to 60% between 2028 and 2035[28]. That may raise the bar for foreign rivals, but KPIL is a builder, not a converter maker, so the effect on it is indirect and unproven.

The balance sheet is now a strength. KPIL reports net debt of ₹917 crore, about 0.1 times equity[2]. Crisil's adjusted figures are debt to net worth of 0.41 times and interest cover of 3.9 times in FY26, up from 2.9 times a year earlier[11]. Cash was ₹1,678 crore on 30 June 2026, of which ₹1,348 crore was free of liens, and about half of ₹3,300 crore of bank working-capital limits sat unused[11]. Both Crisil and India Ratings rate the company AA+ with a stable outlook[13][11].

Peers show how well KPIL has held up. Over one year the shares are up 3%, while KEC International is down 60%, NCC down 45%, Transrail Lighting down 41% and Techno Electric down 38%[1][31][35][32][33]. KEC's cash from operations was a negative ₹414 crore in FY26 and its borrowings were ₹5,378 crore[31]. KPIL's operating cash was a positive ₹1,534 crore in the same year[9].

Where the business is weak

Margins are thin. The group's operating profit margin was 8.3% in FY26 on the company's definition, and profit after tax was about 3.8% of sales[17][11]. Return on equity was 13.7% in the latest year and 12% on a three-year view[1]. Screener also flags that the company's cost of borrowing looks high[1]. A contractor that earns 3.8 paise per rupee of sales has little room for a bad project.

The business runs on other people's money. Crisil puts debtors, including unbilled work, at 223 days at March 2026, and total outside liabilities at 2.4 times adjusted net worth[11]. Screener shows suppliers being paid in 276 days against 113 days of debtors, which gives a cash conversion cycle of minus 94 days[1]. The annual report says trade payables are not interest-bearing and stood at ₹6,453.39 crore for the parent at March 2026[9]. If suppliers or their banks shorten those terms, KPIL would need to borrow, and the 0.1 times debt figure would not last.

Water is the sore spot. Management says billed and unbilled water receivables are about ₹1,500 crore[3]. In October 2025 management put the water receivable at ₹737 crore in Uttar Pradesh and ₹246 crore in Jharkhand, two states where payments had barely moved[7]. In FY26 KPIL collected more than ₹1,800 crore but invested about ₹2,500 crore in the business, a cash drain of about ₹700 crore[5]. ICRA expected vendor receivable cycles to fall below 60 days by September 2026, and by August the trade press still reported that contractors distrust state payment promises[29][30].

Overseas and commodity risks are real. Brazil ended in a full provision[5]. Middle East disruption, early-quarter labour shortages and supply-chain delays all hit execution in the June 2026 quarter[3]. Almost half of the order book has no clause passing input costs to the customer[11].

Ownership needs watching. Promoters hold 33.58%, down from 40.59% in December 2023[1]. Of their shares, 24.56% were pledged at 30 September 2026, which is 14,084,560 shares or 8.25% of all shares[14]. Most of that pledge sits with two promoter companies, K C Holdings and Kalpataru Constructions[15]. The pledge share has fallen from 31.51% in March 2024 and was 24.55% to 24.59% in each of the last six quarters[15]. It sits 0.44 percentage points below our 25% hard limit, which makes it a standing risk even though it is not rising.

What this means for an owner

KPIL is a solvent, improving contractor with a strong order book and a fair record of delivery, in a sector where peers have been punished. It is not a business that earns a premium multiple on quality alone, because margins are thin and cash depends on suppliers and on slow state payers. That is why we ask for a pullback before we buy. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

One detail needs care. In October 2025 management raised its FY26 revenue target to at least 25%, yet the year closed at 22% because Q4 grew only 11%[7][5].
