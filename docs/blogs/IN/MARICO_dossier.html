1. What the company does

Marico sells branded products that Indian households buy every week. The best known is Parachute, a coconut oil used mainly on hair. Saffola is the second pillar: edible oils and, more recently, oats, muesli and soya chunks. The company also sells hair oils with added ingredients (Parachute Advansed, Nihar Naturals, Hair & Care), hair serums, hair gels, male grooming products and a group of online-first brands such as Beardo, Just Herbs and Plix.[4] [1] Marico says its products reach one in three Indians. They get to shoppers through more than 10,200 stockists and distributors who serve over 58 lakh shops, plus a fast-growing online and quick-commerce channel.[15] [3] A lakh is 100,000 and a crore is 10 million.

Marico does not grow coconuts. It buys copra, the dried kernel of the coconut, from farmers and traders, presses it into oil, packs it and sells it through distributors. It also buys edible oils, rice bran oil, liquid paraffin for hair oils and plastics for bottles. It runs 14 factories, six in India and eight overseas.[13] The June 2026 quarter shows where each rupee goes. Of every ₹100 of sales, ₹53.4 went on materials and packing, ₹8.3 on advertising and sales promotion, ₹6.8 on staff and ₹10.9 on other running costs. That left ₹20.7 of operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax, which is the company's earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA).[1]

The line that moves most is the cost of materials. Copra prices roughly doubled in the year to March 2026. The chief executive said input costs rose by 100 percent and Marico raised Parachute prices by about 60 percent, which was less than the full cost, so margin fell.[3] EBITDA margin was 17.1 percent in FY26 against 19.7 percent in FY25, even though sales grew 26 percent.[13] Copra has since fallen about 35 percent from its peak, and the margin was back at 20.7 percent in the June 2026 quarter.[5] [1] This is the main cyclical risk: a coconut-oil franchise that earns well when the crop is plentiful and is squeezed when it is short. Marico is trying to lower the risk by growing parts of the business that do not depend on one crop.

That effort shows in the mix. Marico's own slide splits revenue into 'mass' categories, which are tied to commodities, and 'premium plus digital' categories. Mass was 73 percent of revenue in FY20 and 63 percent in FY26, and the company plans 56 percent in FY27 and 50 percent by FY30.[1] Foods and premium personal care were about 11 percent of India revenue in FY20 and about 23 percent in FY26; the target is about 27 percent for FY27.[1] Foods passed ₹1,000 crore of sales in FY26 and ran at over ₹1,300 crore a year in the June 2026 quarter. Premium personal care ran near ₹450 crore a year, and the digital-first brands near ₹1,100 crore.[13] [2] [3] Part of that growth was bought. Marico took stakes in Plix, Cosmix Wellness and 4700BC during FY26 and then acquired Skinetiq in Vietnam.[15]

Two concentrations matter. The first is product: Parachute coconut oil is 35 percent of India revenue and Saffola oils another 16 percent, so about half of India sales still ride on two commodity-linked franchises.[1] The second is country: Bangladesh was about 45 percent of the international business in FY26 and international was about 24 percent of group revenue, so Bangladesh is roughly 11 percent of the group by our arithmetic.[26] [15] Members of the Mariwala family and their trusts own 58.84 percent of the shares, and 2.03 percent of that holding is pledged to Tata Capital as security for personal loans of four family members.[20] [22] [17] The pledge is small, but it is a fact an owner should know. Marico Bangladesh, the listed subsidiary, had revenue of Tk 20,711 million in FY26 against Tk 16,309 million a year earlier, up 27 percent, and paid out 101 percent of its profit as dividends, which sends cash to the parent.[14]

How Marico earns money in each franchise

Parachute coconut oil is the largest franchise at 35 percent of India revenue. Marico holds 59 percent of the volume in rigid packs, meaning bottles as against small sachets.[1] In the June 2026 quarter, Parachute rigid volume grew 10 percent and value grew 23 percent. Marico cut prices by about 10 percent on large loyalty packs after copra fell, and it left the small price-point packs alone.[3] The value-added hair oils (VAHO), such as Parachute Advansed, Nihar and Hair & Care, are 18 percent of India revenue and earn a margin that management says is significantly higher than the company average.[10] VAHO grew 22 percent in value in the quarter and its market share rose 80 basis points over twelve months.[1] [2] Management says the profitable mid and premium part of VAHO grew volume close to the high teens, and it wants an almond hair-oil range at ₹100 crore a year by FY28.[3]

Saffola edible oils are 16 percent of India revenue. They sold 7 percent more by value in the June 2026 quarter but volume fell by a high single-digit percentage, because Marico stopped supplying some variants in some channels to protect a minimum profit level.[2] [3] The company chooses margin over volume here, and that choice is visible in the numbers: higher prices, fewer units. Foods and premium personal care are the growth story. Foods grew 43 percent in the quarter, but the portfolio now includes the newly bought 4700BC and Cosmix brands, so some of that growth is not organic and the company does not split it out.[2] Analysts on the call asked how those brands compete against well-funded rivals in plant protein and apple-cider-vinegar products, and the CEO answered with repeat-purchase and lifetime-value measures that the company has not published.[3]

How goods reach the shelf

Marico sells through distributors to general trade (small shops), modern trade (supermarket chains) and online. The June 2026 quarter showed double-digit growth in general trade and modern trade, and e-commerce kept growing faster.[2] Quick commerce, the apps that deliver in minutes, grew more than 50 percent in the core portfolio and is now about 5 percent of India revenue excluding digital brands. All digital channels together are over 20 percent of India revenue.[3] In traditional shops the key programme is Project SETU, a three-year plan from FY24 to FY27 to raise the number of shops Marico serves directly from about 1 million to 1.5 million.[1] [12]

Direct service matters because it tells Marico what sells and lets it set the shelf range. Management says SETU helped value-added hair oils and later the Vietnam business.[3] The company also says its distributor stock is among the lowest in packaged goods, so a price cut now reaches shoppers much faster than the eight to ten weeks it used to take.[3] A thin pipeline cuts two ways: it speeds up price moves, and it leaves little buffer if a supply chain breaks. Marico said in August 2026 that it had held supply through the energy shock by positioning raw materials, packaging and finished goods ahead of need.[3]

Who controls the company and who checks it

Saugata Gupta is managing director and chief executive. Pawan Agrawal is group chief financial officer and also runs the international business.[3] The statutory auditor is B S R & Co. LLP, and the internal auditor is Deloitte Touche Tohmatsu India LLP.[13] The founding family holds 58.84 percent, foreign institutions 22.25 percent, Indian institutions 14.11 percent and the public 4.51 percent at September 2026.[20] Mutual funds raised their stake from 8.43 percent to 9.19 percent in the September 2026 quarter, while foreign institutions cut theirs from 23.40 percent to 22.25 percent.[22]

Credit analysts are comfortable. CRISIL reaffirmed AAA/Stable on 2 September 2026 and raised the rated bank limits from ₹657 crore to ₹1,087 crore.[15] [16] The company has nil long-term debt. The CRISIL note also records that the dividend payout, normally 50 to 90 percent of profit, was about 29 percent in FY26 because cash went into acquisitions.[15] That shift in how Marico uses cash is one of the three things this report watches most closely, and Chapter 2B tests it against what management promised.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The best evidence of strength is market share. In the twelve months to June 2026, Parachute's rigid packs held 59 percent of coconut-oil volume and rank first. Marico is also first in value-added hair oils (29 percent value share), oats (42 percent), post-wash leave-on serums (44 percent) and hair gels, waxes and creams (51 percent).[1] In the June 2026 quarter Marico says over 96 percent of its business gained or held share, and Parachute rigids gained more than 400 basis points of volume share, a new high.[2] Share is hard to fake. If a brand loses its edge, rivals take shelf space first and the company discovers it later in revenue.

Pricing power was tested in FY26 and held. Marico raised Parachute prices by more than 60 percent year on year in the September 2025 quarter. After adjusting for smaller pack sizes, volumes were flat.[11] A price rise of that size normally cuts volume sharply, so the flat result tells us shoppers value the brand. In June 2026 the company passed some copra savings back with a price cut of about 10 percent on large loyalty packs, and Parachute volume then rose 10 percent, the best in 20 quarters.[3] The September 2026 quarter update says Parachute volume grew at an early-teen rate.[5] Management called 10 percent an aberration and guides to mid-single-digit volume growth for the year.[3]

Distribution and supply chain are the next edge. Project SETU lifts direct reach from about 1 million shops towards 1.5 million, which improves range and speed.[12] The CEO also says Marico's pipeline stock is thin because of AI-based demand forecasting, and that smaller coconut-oil rivals were hurt more by packaging and fuel shortages.[3] That claim comes from management and we could not check it against an outside source, but the volume share gain of more than 400 basis points is consistent with it.[2] CRISIL, an outside party, rates the company AAA and cites its leading position across categories and strong financial profile.[15]

Where the strength is thinner

Marico is still tied to commodities. About half of India revenue comes from Parachute and Saffola oils (35 plus 16 percent), and operating margin in India fell by about 310 basis points in FY26 when copra spiked.[1] [13] Saffola oils show the trade-off: revenue rose 7 percent in June 2026 but volume fell by a high single-digit percentage as the company cut low-margin variants.[2] Edible-oil costs are still rising, and the company said in August that input costs would be higher in the September quarter.[3] Kochi copra rose from US$1,625 a tonne on 3 October to US$1,696 on 24 October 2026, a rise of 4.4 percent in three weeks, which is small but points the wrong way for a margin story that depends on copra staying low.[29]

The new businesses are growing faster than they are proving their profit. Foods grew 13 percent in FY26 against an aim, set in May 2025, of at least 20 to 25 percent a year.[13] [9] Beardo has reached a double-digit EBITDA margin, but the group target of a double-digit margin across all digital-first brands is still only a target for the end of FY27.[13] [2] In October 2026 Marico paid ₹1,012.03 crore for a further 24.09 percent of Plix's parent company, taking its stake to 84.09 percent. That values the whole company at about ₹4,200 crore, or about 4.9 times its FY26 turnover of ₹864.31 crore, by our arithmetic.[18] A high price is not a mistake by itself, but it raises the bar for the profit those brands must deliver.

Rivals and the sector are not standing still. Dabur's hair-oil portfolio grew at a high-teen rate in its June 2026 quarter, and analysts on Marico's call raised pricing wars in plant protein and competition from Hindustan Unilever's Oziva and from online pharmacies.[32] [3] The Times of India reported on 7 October 2026 that the FMCG share index was down nearly 16 percent since 1 January, that analysts expect margin pressure and slower growth, and that a consultant expects the volume effect to show from the March 2027 quarter.[28] Marico said in September 2025 that about 30 percent of its India business had benefited from the GST rate cut, a boost that starts to lap in late September 2026, which makes year-on-year volume comparisons harder from here.[11]

How we weigh the strengths against the gaps

The core is strong and well evidenced; the edges are promising and costly. The core, meaning Parachute, value-added hair oils and the international hair-care business, earns the 47 percent return on capital shown in Chapter 3 and holds shares that rivals have not been able to dent. The edges, meaning Foods, premium personal care and the digital-first brands, are where Marico is spending cash on advertising (up 25 percent in June 2026) and on acquisitions.[1] [18] We therefore score share and volume as PASS, score margin and diversification as WATCH, and let the price decide whether the mix of the two is worth owning.

What this means for you: the business quality is not the open question. The open questions are how much margin comes back, and whether the stock at 53 times trailing earnings already pays for the recovery. Management also shows a habit that cuts both ways. It under-promises on the next quarter and then beats it: in May 2026 it guided to double-digit operating profit growth and reported 25 percent, and in October 2026 it said it is likely to surpass its near-term guidance across key parameters.[7] [1] [5] That habit makes the 2 November 2026 results less of a surprise than usual, because the headline numbers (double-digit revenue growth, mid-twenties operating profit growth) are already public.[5] What is not public is the margin level, the cash flow and the tax rate, so those are the lines to read first. The company issued the update on 5 October 2026, and the share closed that day at ₹790, up 1.11 percent, so the market did not treat it as a shock.[30] The May 2026 call had set the same ₹15,000 crore and high-teen EBITDA aims for the year, so August and October have so far built on them rather than changed them.[8]

The dividend change deserves a separate comment. The company says it remains committed to a strong payout under its policy, and CRISIL notes the payout was about 29 percent in FY26 because cash went into acquisitions.[13] [15] For an owner, that is a decision to trade current income for growth that Marico has bought at a high price. It may pay off, but it is a larger bet than the company's history of organic growth.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of numbers tell one story with two chapters. Revenue went from ₹9,512 crore in FY22 to ₹13,611 crore in FY26, a three-year compound growth of 11.7 percent a year from FY23 (Screener prints 12 percent).[20] Almost all of the gain came in the last two years. Revenue was flat in FY23 and fell 1.1 percent in FY24, then rose 12.2 percent in FY25 and 25.7 percent in FY26.[20] Part of the FY26 jump was price. India volume grew 8 percent, so the rest came from price increases that passed the copra spike on to shoppers, plus international and the new brands.[13]

Profit grew more slowly than sales because margin fell. EBITDA margin, which is operating profit as a share of sales, was 21.0 percent in FY24 and 17.1 percent in FY26.[20] Profit before minority interest rose from ₹1,255 crore to ₹1,813 crore, up 9.3 percent in FY26 on sales growth of 25.7 percent.[20] Earnings per share (EPS) were ₹13.57 in FY26 and ₹14.54 for the twelve months to June 2026.[20] Screener's profit row is before minority interest. The annual report shows ₹1,813 crore before and ₹1,762 crore after minority holders, and EPS ties to the second figure.[13]

Cash is the strongest part of the record. Operating cash flow was ₹2,084 crore in FY26 and free cash flow, which is cash left after buying plant and equipment, was ₹1,772 crore.[20] Over FY24 to FY26 Marico produced ₹4,834 crore of operating cash from ₹4,973 crore of profit, a ratio of 0.97. One caution: working capital days (the days of sales tied up in stock and bills, net of supplier credit) fell from 16 to minus 18 in FY26, so part of the FY26 cash came from suppliers being paid later. That helps once and will not repeat.[20]

The balance sheet carries almost no debt. Borrowings were ₹557 crore against net worth of ₹4,210 crore, and investments were ₹2,083 crore.[20] CRISIL puts cash, bank balances and investments at over ₹2,037 crore at 31 March 2026, adjusted gearing at 0.39 and interest cover at 45 times.[15] Return on capital employed (ROCE) was 47 percent and return on equity 42.8 percent. Those returns are high partly because Marico has paid out most of its profit as dividends in the past, which keeps net worth small, and the ROCE figure says nothing about how much a buyer pays for the shares.[20]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions that could be wrong. This chapter takes the weakest assumption in each, says what evidence would show it wrong and when that evidence arrives. We also state the strongest case against our own view, since that is where a mistake is most likely.

The red-team case against buying is simple. The stock costs 53 times last year's earnings for a company whose profit grew 9 percent a year over five years. The reverse DCF needs 19 to 20 percent growth for ten years.[20] [38] Margin fell 2.6 points in FY26 and management has already trimmed its FY27 margin promise from at least 200 basis points to about 140 to 150.[10] [3] It is paying about 4.9 times sales for Plix while cutting its dividend payout to 29 percent.[18] [13] The FMCG sector is down about 16 percent this year and brokers expect pressure on margins.[28] And the stock is below its falling 50-day average, with results due in five days. On that reading, buying is a bet on an event, not on a bargain.

Flaw 1: the margin recovery may stall

Our central case lifts EBITDA margin from 17.1 percent in FY26 (about 17.3 percent on restated revenue) to 18.0 percent in FY27 and 18.5 percent in FY28. That is about 90 basis points of expansion on the reported base and about 70 on the restated base. The June 2026 quarter reached 20.7 percent, but the quarter is seasonally strong, and a broker preview for September uses 17.6 percent.[1] [27] Management itself said input costs would be higher in the second quarter, because crude-linked derivatives such as liquid paraffin and polymers rose 60 to 70 percent.[3] If margin stayed flat at 17.1 percent, FY27 EPS would be ₹15.48 and FY28 EPS ₹17.29, giving a target near ₹864 at 50 times, still above the 2.0 to 1 floor but with far less cushion.

The evidence to watch is the September quarter EBITDA margin, due on 2 November 2026, and then the December quarter in early 2027. A margin below 17 percent in the September quarter would say the recovery is slower than guided. The company's update says operating profit grew in the mid-twenties and that gross margin accelerated year on year, so we expect a better print, but not a margin like June's.[5]

Flaw 2: copra and crude can move against Marico again

The margin story depends on copra staying about 35 percent below its peak. Management expects it to stay range-bound with a slight upward bias.[3] Kochi copra rose from US$1,625 a tonne on 3 October to US$1,696 on 24 October 2026, up 4.4 percent in three weeks.[29] A short rise is not a trend, but copra has an 18 to 24 month cycle, and the last cycle lasted longer than management had expected.[9] At the same time, edible oils and crude-linked inputs are rising, and Saffola's volume already fell by a high single-digit percentage as the company dropped low-margin variants.[2]

The test is the weekly price table of the International Coconut Community and Marico's own comment on the 2 November call. If Kochi copra keeps rising for several more weeks above the 24 October level, our margin path is at risk. If management announces price rises to offset costs, the risk shifts from margin to volume, which is the measure Marico has been winning on.

Flaw 3: capital allocation is changing

The payout fell from 85 percent of profit in FY25 to about 29 percent in FY26 on the annual report's basis (Screener shows 83 and 30 percent) as cash went into acquisitions, and another ₹1,012.03 crore went to Plix in October 2026.[13] [18] The deal values Plix at about ₹4,200 crore, which is 4.9 times its FY26 turnover of ₹864.31 crore by our arithmetic. A further 14.09 percent is due in July 2027 for a base price of up to ₹592 crore plus milestone payments.[18] The sellers are the founders, so the deal is a related-party transaction, which Marico says is at arm's length.[18] In May 2025 Marico said Plix had delivered a single-digit EBITDA margin for FY25, and the group aims for a double-digit digital-first margin by the end of FY27.[9] [2]

If Plix growth slows, as an analyst on the August call suggested it already has in plant protein, the company will have paid a high price for slower growth.[3] The evidence would be Plix revenue and margin disclosures and any write-down, and the first test is the 2 November results call. A second large purchase while the payout stays low would make us lower the sizing tier.

Flaw 4: valuation and the sector

Even if profit meets our estimate, the share can fall if the multiple does. Marico trades at 53.0 times earnings against a peer median of 32.9, and the FMCG index is down about 16 percent this year.[21] [28] Foreign institutions cut their stake from 24.08 percent in March 2026 to 22.25 percent in September 2026, while mutual funds raised theirs from 8.43 percent to 9.19 percent in the last quarter.[20] [22] Our bear case applies 42 times, the level at which Godrej Consumer and Hindustan Unilever trade, and gives ₹665.70.

The stock fell 4.4 percent in the week to 1 October 2026 even though it had no bad news, which shows how sensitive the price is to sector mood.[31] The evidence would be the price-to-earnings multiple falling to the low 40s on unchanged earnings. The test is the 2 November result and the reaction to it.

The multiple alone can break the case, with no earnings miss at all. Hold FY28 EPS at ₹18.75 and use 42 times, our own bear multiple: the value is ₹787.50, which is +2.32 percent from ₹769.65. Reward-to-risk to that value is 0.62 to 1 at the close and 0.82 to 1 at the top of the entry zone, both below the 2.0 floor. At 39 times, the multiple of Hindustan Unilever, the value is ₹731.25, or -4.99 percent. These are sensitivities that change one input, not a replacement for the base case. They show that the floor holds only while the market pays about 44.1 times FY28 EPS or more, and today the stock trades at 41.0 times our FY28 EPS.[36] [21]
