Before you read

Elevance Health is a large US health insurer that covers about 44.9 million medical members and earned $197.6 billion of operating revenue in 2025.[5][1] We reject it for the shortlist today, because the cash left after upkeep was only 1.59% of revenue in 2025 against a 12% floor, and the return on invested money is about 10% against a 12% floor.[1] The chart does not help: the price sits 4.8 typical daily swings above solid support, and the gain-to-loss ratio is 0.56 against a 3.5 floor. We would be wrong if medical costs fall fast enough for profit to grow 12% in 2027 and for cash to recover.[8][7]

What the company does

Elevance is a health insurer that also owns the businesses that sit around insurance. Employers, governments and individuals pay it a monthly premium; in return it pays the doctors and hospitals when members get care. At 2026-06-30 it covered 44,949 thousand medical members, 1.5% fewer than a year earlier, including 1,897 thousand in Medicare Advantage (down 15.9%) and 8,358 thousand in Medicaid (down 4.3%).[5] Medicare Advantage is the private-plan version of Medicare for people over 65; Medicaid is the joint federal and state program for lower-income households.

The company reports three engines. Health Benefits is the insurance business and earned an operating gain of $4,158 million in 2025, down from $6,243 million in 2024. CarelonRx, the pharmacy benefit manager (a middleman that negotiates drug prices and processes prescriptions), earned $2,418 million on $43.4 billion of revenue. Carelon Services, which runs care delivery and data services, earned $960 million on $28.3 billion of revenue.[1] Government programs made up 32% of 2025 revenue, up from 29% in 2023, which means the company's results depend more each year on rates that Washington and state agencies set.[1][3]

The key number to understand is the benefit expense ratio: the share of every premium dollar paid out for medical care. It was 87.0% in 2023, 88.5% in 2024 and 90.0% in 2025, and 89.7% in the second quarter of 2026.[3][2][1][7] Each point of that ratio is about $2 billion of pre-tax profit on a revenue of this size, so small moves decide the year.

What this means for you: an owner is betting on how well the company prices insurance against medical costs, not on rapid sales growth. Chapter 2 asks whether it is doing that well.

Why the business is strong or weak

Elevance is strong in scale and weak in current cost control. Scale first: with 44.9 million members and $199.1 billion of total revenue in 2025, it can spread overhead and negotiate prices that a smaller plan cannot. In Medicare Advantage, 59% of its members are in plans rated four stars or better for 2026, which matters because higher-rated plans earn bonus payments.[1][18] The company also owns the pharmacy and care-services businesses, which earned $3,378 million of operating gain in 2025 combined and did not depend on the insurance cycle in the same way.[1]

Cost control is the weakness. The Health Benefits margin fell from 4.2% in 2024 to 2.5% in 2025, and to 2.1% in the second quarter of 2026 against 3.8% a year earlier.[1][7] Second-quarter earnings per share were $6.71 against $7.72, and the first half was $14.73 against $17.33.[5][7] Management raised its 2026 outlook (adjusted EPS, meaning earnings per share, from at least $25.50 to at least $27.00), but the 2025 result was $30.29, so the 2026 guide is still about 11% lower.[8][9][10]

The regulatory story is part of the evidence. On 2026-02-27 the federal Medicare agency (CMS) told Elevance it intended to suspend enrollment and marketing in its Medicare Advantage drug plans from 2026-03-31, over how diagnosis codes from 2015 to April 2023 were resubmitted. The company booked a $935 million accrual in the first quarter, paid $342 million on 2026-05-27, and carried $593 million at 2026-06-30 with a reasonably possible range of plus or minus $320 million. On 2026-07-13 CMS wrote that the steps were complete, no sanctions would be imposed and enforcement was closed.[6][5] A separate Justice Department False Claims Act suit over risk adjustment, filed in 2020, is still in discovery, with fact discovery due 2026-08-31 and expert discovery due 2027-05-10.[5]

The sector context shows why this is hard. Medicare Advantage enrollment is 35.2 million of 64.2 million eligible people (55%) in 2026, so growth has to come from taking share, and CMS sets the payment rates the plans live on.[18][19][20] Elevance announced its 2027 plans on 2026-10-01 and open enrollment runs from 2026-10-15 to 2026-12-07, which means membership direction for 2027 will be visible within weeks.[17]

What this means for you: the franchise is intact, but its profit per dollar of revenue has been shrinking for three years. The numbers in chapter 3 show how far.

Now the cash test. Free cash flow is operating cash flow less capital spending, the cash left after the business pays for its own upkeep. Its short form, FCF, stands for free cash flow. For 2025 it was $4,290 million less $1,116 million, which is $3,174 million, or 1.59% of $199,125 million of revenue. The trailing twelve months are better, about $6.29 billion on $201.11 billion of revenue (3.13%), because the first half of 2026 had operating cash flow of $6,245 million against $3,071 million a year earlier.[1][5][21] Both are far under the 12% floor. Insurers always run thin cash margins on large revenue, so the floor is strict for this sector, but it is the rule we apply to every name.

The balance sheet at 2025-12-31 held $9,491 million of cash, $30,797 million of long-term debt plus $1,099 million of current debt and $150 million of short-term borrowings, total assets of $121,494 million and total equity of $44,026 million. Goodwill was $28,344 million, and goodwill plus intangibles was $39.5 billion, or 32.5% of assets.[1] At 2026-06-30 cash was $10,232 million, long-term debt $31,044 million, total assets $126,438 million and shareholders' equity $44,883 million.[5] Net debt is debt minus cash, the amount owed after using all cash on hand. At 2025 year-end it was $22,555 million, and with EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges) of $9,658 million, that is 2.34 times, under our 3.0 limit.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Each of our estimates has a weak assumption, and the assumption determines how much to trust it. The central case assumes the multiple stays near 13.5 to 14 times; the bull case assumes costs fall; the bear case assumes they rise. None of them is certain, and the biggest swing factor is the benefit expense ratio, which is revealed each quarter.

Fatal flaw 1: medical costs keep rising

If the benefit ratio does not come down from 89.7%, 2027 profit will fall short of the 12% target. Evidence that it is wrong: a second-quarter 2026 ratio 80 basis points higher than a year earlier, and days in claims payable at 45.4.[7] We will know on 2026-10-21 when the third-quarter report is due before the open, with the call at 8:30 a.m. EDT.[16]

Fatal flaw 2: the risk-adjustment case turns costly

The Justice Department suit over risk-adjustment diagnoses, filed in 2020, is unresolved. A large settlement would cut cash and 2027 EPS beyond what the $593 million accrual covers. Evidence: the company's own disclosed range of plus or minus $320 million on the CMS matter shows how uncertain the numbers are.[5] Expert discovery runs to 2027-05-10, so the answer is not due soon.

Fatal flaw 3: our rejection is too strict for this sector

The free cash flow floor of 12% is a rule built for software and industrial companies; no peer clears it, and the best peer reading is 5.24% (UnitedHealth). If we exempted insurers, ELV would still show the ROIC problem and the asymmetry problem, so the verdict would not change, but the reasoning would. We therefore treat the floor as correct for this process rather than argue with it.[21][22]

A fourth check is data quality. Aggregators disagree on ROIC (6.57% Finviz, 8.46% StockAnalysis, 10.3% ours for 2025), and the trailing interest expense (about $1,430 million) is our estimate. We show all three. The 3-year median multiple is N/A because we could not verify it.

What this means for you: the rejection rests on the cash and return tests, not on a forecast, so it holds even if you disagree with our price cases.
