Reading the framework

Several words in the paragraphs below are defined here first. A base is a sideways stretch in which a stock trades inside a narrow range before its next move, like a car idling at a junction. A pivot is the price level a stock has to clear to confirm that it has left its base, usually the top of that base. A breakout is a close above the top of a base, ideally on heavier trading. A retest is a return to a broken level that then holds, which the rules treat as a second chance to buy. Stage 2 is the rising phase of a stock's cycle, when the price holds above averages that are themselves rising. An entry zone is the price range in which the rules allow a buy, normally close to a support level. Price-to-earnings, shortened to P/E, is the share price divided by profit per share. EPS stands for earnings per share, the profit attributable to each share. Operating margin is the share of each sales rupee left after the running costs of the business. Guidance is the company's own forecast of what it expects to achieve in the coming period. A measured move is a price target found by adding the height of a base to the level it breaks out from.

The framework runs in three layers, and each layer is a filter that a candidate must clear before the next one is read. Layer 1 holds the hard floors: returns on capital, borrowing, cash conversion, the shape of the price chart, a momentum test and an upside-to-downside ratio. Every floor has to pass. A single failure ends the review with a REJECTED verdict, and the later layers are not read. Layer 2 would score three to five operating measures for the business. Layer 3 would decide the timing of an entry from the distance to a support level. Both are explained in their own chapter, where they are marked as not evaluated because Layer 1 failed.

The momentum gate is a code test, not an opinion. It runs on the frozen price snapshot and asks two questions. Test A asks whether the stock is already in momentum: its close should sit above a 50-day average that itself sits above a 200-day average, its relative strength against the Nifty 50 should be positive over 63 sessions, and the price should not be stretched more than 1.2 average true ranges from its base. Test B asks whether the stock is entering momentum: the 50-day average should be holding, relative strength should be turning up over 28 sessions, the price should be close to the base pivot, volume should dry up inside the base and then expand. A stock that fails both tests gets no allocation, however good the business. The thresholds are marked in the configuration file as needing calibration against live data, so they are defaults and not fitted values.

ATR stands for average true range. It is the typical daily price swing over the last 14 sessions, and it works as a ruler. For Apollo the ledger ATR is ₹222.0, which is about 2.8 percent of the price. Support is measured in ATR units because a stop placed one ATR away leaves room for an ordinary day's movement, while a stop placed closer will be hit by noise. Here the close sits 1.19 ATR above its 63-session base low of ₹7,607.5. The momentum gate uses the stricter reading of that distance, and so does this dossier.

Terms used in this report

This chapter explains each technical and financial word once, with an everyday comparison, so the later chapters can use the words without stopping to define them. The words appear in the order the framework needs them.

ROCE stands for return on capital employed. It asks how much operating profit a business earns for each rupee tied up in hospitals, machines and working stock. A figure of 17.4 percent means the business earns about 17 paise a year on every rupee it uses [1]. ROE, or return on equity, measures profit against the money shareholders have put in. The Screener header shows 21.2 percent, and its three-year average is 18 percent [1].

Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by the capital owned by shareholders. A value of 0.70 would mean borrowings equal to 70 paise for each rupee of owners' capital. The framework's limit for a non-lender is below 0.70. Lease liabilities can make this ratio look larger or smaller depending on whether they are counted as borrowings, which is the reason for the open question on this company.

Cash from operations is the cash a business brings in from running its activity, before money spent on new equipment. Comparing it with reported profit shows whether profit is backed by cash that has arrived. The three-year ratio of the two sums is the framework's cash test [1].

OPM, or operating profit margin, is the share of each sales rupee left after the costs of running the hospitals and pharmacies. In FY26 it was 15 percent [1]. P/E, or price-to-earnings, is the share price divided by profit per share. A P/E of 53.6 means investors pay about ₹53.60 for each rupee of a year's earnings per share [1].

ATR stands for average true range, the typical daily swing in price over 14 sessions. For this session it is ₹222.0. Stage 1 is the basing phase, when a stock trades sideways after a fall. Stage 2 is the rising phase, when the price holds above averages that are themselves rising. Stage 3 is the topping phase, and Stage 4 is the falling phase.

Base is a sideways stretch in which a stock trades inside a narrow range before its next move, like a car idling at a junction. Stop loss is the price at which a holding would be sold to cap the loss. Measured move is a target found by adding the height of a base to the level it breaks out from. How the framework works for this company

The framework is a set of hurdles that a company must clear in a fixed order. Layer 1 holds the hard floors, and each one is a yes or no question with a number attached. A failure at any of them ends the review with a REJECTED verdict, and the later layers are not read. The point of the ordering is that a good business cannot excuse a bad chart. If the price has not yet agreed with the business, the framework waits, however attractive the business looks on paper.

Each candidate is tested against a strategy bucket. CORE_ALPHA is for businesses expected to compound over three to five years. It needs a return on capital of at least 15 percent, with a preference above 18 percent, and it needs either a fresh base breakout or a Stage 2 trend. TACTICAL_MOMENTUM is for shorter moves of three to twelve months, and it needs a Stage 2 trend that sits above rising 50-day and 200-day averages. Apollo's business profile fits CORE_ALPHA, so it is tested against that bucket first, and the tactical test is run as well so that the reader can see both.

The layers differ in what they measure. Layer 1 measures whether the business and the price are acceptable at all. Layer 2 measures how well the operating engine is performing in measures specific to the sector. Layer 3 measures whether the entry point is close enough to support to act on. A candidate that passes Layer 1 but fails Layer 2 is a good business at the wrong moment. A candidate that passes both but sits far from support is a good business to watch rather than buy. For Apollo, Layer 1 fails, so the framework stops here and the later questions are answered only as context.

The framework also requires that each number be traceable. The price, moving averages, ATR, swing low, base low, 52-week range and momentum inputs come from the ledger for this session. Company figures come from the research record, with the source and the period attached to each. Where two sources disagree, the dossier names both and says which one it uses and why. Asymmetry and target anchoring

The upside-to-downside ratio compares how far the price could rise with how far it could fall before the stop is hit. The formula is the base target minus the close, divided by the close minus the stop. A target without an anchor is treated as unverified, and an unverified target fails the floor. The rule is strict because a target chosen to look attractive can make almost any stock appear to have room to run.

The framework accepts two anchors. The first is a forward earnings figure multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio. The second is a technical measured move from a base. Apollo's forward earnings figure and its three-year median P/E were not verified in this run. Search summaries show consensus figures and five-year P/E medians, but none of them was read in full, and a five-year median is not the three-year figure the rule asks for, so neither is used. The measured move is therefore the anchor [17][23].

Fatal-flaw red team

The red team asks what would make the verdict wrong, and the answer should be sharp. Three flaws were tested against the evidence. Each is a reason a buy could fail even if the business keeps performing well. The test here is not whether the flaw is likely, but whether the record supports it, because a flaw that the record cannot support should not change the verdict.

Flaw one: the trend has broken and may not repair

The first flaw is that the decline from the September peak is the start of a longer correction. The price sits below its 50-day average, which is falling. The regime reading is Downtrend, only one in five stocks in the breadth pool is above its 50-day average, and no sector is above its 50-day average. If the market keeps weakening, Apollo could test the 200-day average at ₹8,047.58 and then the 63-session base low at ₹7,607.5. A close below that low would begin to break the base, and the Core stop of ₹7,385.5 would then be the next line. The rules would already have rejected the name, so the flaw does not change the verdict. It makes the verdict more likely to remain right for longer.

Flaw two: the valuation assumes growth that the record only partly shows

The second flaw is that the share price already pays for the growth. At a P/E of 53.6 and a price of 11.9 times book value on the Screener header (13 Oct 2026) [1], the market is paying a high multiple for earnings that grew 33 percent a year over three years on the calculation used here, and the Screener header prints 32 percent. A business whose profit growth slows from that pace would see its multiple compress even if profit still grew. The quarter to June 2026 is a strong quarter, with sales up 20.6 percent, but the framework cannot read one quarter as a trend. The answer is that the three-year cash test still passes, and the latest quarter's profit was up more than a third. Those points carry weight, but they do not rescue a stock whose chart has not yet turned.

Flaw three: borrowing is rising and the ownership question is open

The third flaw is that the balance sheet has been carrying more debt each year. Screener's borrowings line rose from ₹4,332 crore in March 2023 to ₹5,333 crore in March 2024, ₹7,864 crore in March 2025 and ₹8,493 crore in March 2026 [1]. The debt-to-equity reading is therefore the open question: the calculation from Screener's lines gives about 0.90, which sits above the 0.70 floor, and no company-definition figure has been verified. On ownership, the promoter holds 28.02 percent and has pledged 2.91 percent of that holding as of 30 September 2026, down from 8.58 percent in September 2025 and 13.11 percent in June 2025 [2]. The pledge trend is the better sign, but the company's own annual report has not been read here, so the reason for the borrowing rise is not verified. The answer is that the pledge has fallen and the cash flow is strong, but the rising borrowing is a real flaw until the annual report explains it.

What would make the bear case wrong is a clean run of Stage 2 signals: a close above the 50-day average on heavy volume, relative strength turning positive, and a debt figure that the annual report confirms at or below 0.70. The record does not show any of these yet, so the flaws stand, and the verdict stands with them.
