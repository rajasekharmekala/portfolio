Before you read

SoFi Technologies is a digital bank that lends to consumers, and this portfolio owns no shares, only 22 call options that expire on January 15, 2027.[1][2] We reject the stock for new money and mark the options for sale, because the chart is in a Stage 4 downtrend at $14.55 and profit on tangible equity is 8.6% against a 12% floor.[3][4] The business is growing fast, with 15.8 million members up 35%, so this is a verdict on price and risk, not on a weak company.[5] We would be wrong if the October 27 report shows strong capital and credit and the stock then closes above $17.09.[6][3]

1. What the company does

SoFi began in 2011 as a company that refinanced student loans. It is now a bank holding company, which means a parent company that owns a regulated bank, and the bank, SoFi Bank, holds a national charter.[1] At the end of June 2026 it counted 15.8 million members and 24.4 million products, such as checking accounts, loans, investment accounts and credit cards, so each member holds about 1.54 products.[5] Three groups pay it. Borrowers pay interest and origination fees. Partner firms pay fees when SoFi arranges loans for them. Banks and fintech companies pay software fees to use its technology.

The largest source of money is interest. In the second quarter of 2026 net interest income was $788.2 million of the $1,218.7 million in total net revenue, which is 65% (788.2 divided by 1,218.7). The other 35% was fees and other income of $430.5 million.[4] SoFi earns interest on $47.9 billion of loans, and it funds them mostly with $45.5 billion of customer deposits. The company says deposits were more than 90% of average liabilities, and that the deposit rate was 156 basis points (1.56 percentage points) below what it pays on warehouse lines, the short-term credit lines banks use to fund loans before selling them. It puts the saving at about $712.6 million a year.[5][7]

The business runs in three segments. Lending makes personal, student and home loans and produced $724.8 million of second-quarter net revenue. Financial Services holds the money, investing, credit card, crypto and SoFi Plus products and produced $466.3 million, up 29%. Technology Platform sells processing and banking software to other companies, under the Galileo and SoFi Tech Solutions names, and produced $84.5 million, down 23% from a year earlier after one large client left the platform.[5] A newer line, the Loan Platform Business, originates personal loans for outside investors and keeps only a fee. It originated $3.1 billion of loans and added $143.3 million of revenue in the quarter, which lets SoFi grow without putting every loan on its own balance sheet.[5]

One feature of the accounts decides how this dossier is built. SoFi makes most loans to sell or to hold at fair value, so every dollar lent shows up as cash leaving operating activities. Loans held for sale rose by $7.0 billion in the first half of 2026, and operating cash flow was negative $6.2 billion for those six months and negative $8.5 billion over the last twelve months.[7][8] Free cash flow, the cash left after running the business and buying equipment, is therefore a large negative number that says nothing about the health of the lender. How the funding model changed the economics

Before it owned a bank, SoFi borrowed against its loans at warehouse-line rates. Now deposits carry about three-quarters of the balance sheet: $45.5 billion of $60.9 billion in total assets, or 74.7%.[7] Deposits grew $8.0 billion in the first six months of 2026, from $37.5 billion to $45.5 billion, and $4.5 billion of the total is brokered deposits, which are bought through intermediaries and tend to leave faster than a customer checking account would.[7][4] The lower funding cost is the main reason net interest margin has held near 5.9% while loan yields fell, and it is also the reason the stock now trades with interest rates: deposits reprice faster than loans.[9][10]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • RSI (relative strength index) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been moving; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the average daily price swing in dollars, used here to measure how far the price sits from support.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before those items; SoFi's adjusted figure also leaves out stock pay.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is a company's profit divided by its number of shares.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment, the figure the reverse DCF would grow.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying employees in shares, which this report calls stock pay.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • A base is a sideways stretch of trading that a stock builds before a new move, and the pivot is the top of that base.
  • A breakout is a move above a base or pivot on heavy volume.
  • A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut, and an entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better and does not chase the market.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).
  • Net margin is net income as a share of revenue, and net debt is debt minus cash.
  • Guidance is the company's own published forecast for a coming period.
  • Dilution is the rise in the share count from issuing new shares, which shrinks each existing owner's slice.
  • Net interest margin is the interest a lender earns on loans and investments minus the interest it pays on deposits and debt, as a share of the assets that earn interest.
  • CET1 (common equity tier 1) is the highest-quality capital a bank holds, expressed as a share of its risk-weighted assets; the regulatory minimum with buffers is 7.0%.
  • Risk-weighted assets are a bank's loans and other assets, each counted at a weight that rises with its risk.
  • Tangible book value is shareholders' equity minus goodwill and other intangible assets, a rough measure of what the bank would be worth if it stopped growing.
  • ROTCE (return on tangible common equity) is a year of net income divided by average tangible book value.
  • Charge-off is a loan the lender has written off as lost; the charge-off rate is the annualized share of average loans written off.
  • Fair value accounting means loans are carried at an estimated market price, so changes in interest rates or expected defaults move reported profit even when no loan has been sold.
  • Loan Platform Business is SoFi's service of originating loans for outside investors and earning a fee, so the loans do not sit on SoFi's balance sheet.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

SoFi's strongest advantage is cheap, sticky funding combined with a large base of members it can sell more products to. A member who already uses one product costs little to win over for a second, and the company reports that 51% of new products in the second quarter went to existing members, up from 35% a year earlier.[5] In the quarter it added 2.2 million products against 1.1 million members, the first time products grew twice as fast as members.[5] The bank charter gives it deposits, and deposits give it a funding cost the non-bank lenders do not have: Upstart and Affirm, for example, rely on partners or warehouse lines for much of their funding.[11][12]

Credit quality, the measure that matters most for a lender, is improving on the reported numbers. The annualized net charge-off rate on all loans was 1.81% in the second quarter, down from 2.12% a year earlier, and for personal loans it was 2.62% against 2.83%.[4] Management says losses on its recent personal-loan vintages, the groups of loans made from the fourth quarter of 2022 to the third quarter of 2025, are 4.68% so far with 35% of the balance still outstanding, against 6.43% for the 2017 vintage at the same age, and it holds a 7% to 8% loss tolerance.[5] We treat these as company statements that the filings support only in part, because the vintage curves are management's own analysis and not an audited disclosure.

The weak points are about price, capital and earnings quality. First, returns are low for a bank: net income of $636.3 million over the last twelve months on average tangible equity of $7.36 billion is 8.6%, while Happen, a digital bank in the same business line, reported a 15.9% return on tangible equity in its latest quarter.[4][13] Second, stock pay was $283.9 million over twelve months, 6.6% of revenue, and the share count has grown 15.9% in a year to 1,290.3 million after two public offerings, at $20.85 in July 2025 and $27.50 in December 2025.[14][4] Third, the Technology Platform segment is shrinking, and the 10-Q warns that losing one or a few top clients can matter there.[15][5]

Competition comes from every direction. The 10-K lists other lenders, larger banks that compete for deposits, brokerages, crypto platforms and technology firms offering banking software.[1] SoFi's edge is the combination of products in one app and its brand, where unaided awareness reached 10.4% in the quarter, up 190 basis points from a year earlier.[5] That edge is real but expensive: sales and marketing cost $392.4 million in the quarter, 32.2% of revenue (392.4 divided by 1,218.7), which is the price of growing members 35% a year.[4] The business is strong at gathering customers and weak, so far, at turning growth into returns that exceed what shareholders require.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of filings show a company that went from deep losses to steady profit while its balance sheet grew sixfold. Net revenue rose from $984.9 million in 2021 to $3,613.4 million in 2025, a 38.4% yearly growth rate (3,613.4 divided by 984.9 is 3.669, and the fourth root is 1.384).[23][18] Net income moved from a loss of $483.9 million to a profit of $481.3 million, and the trailing twelve months to June 2026 produced $636.3 million (481.3 plus 323.3 minus 168.4 gives 636.2 on rounded figures; the unrounded thousands are 481,320 + 323,323 - 168,379 = 636,264).[23][18][4] Two one-offs distort the middle years: a $247.2 million goodwill write-down in 2023 and a $265.3 million tax benefit in 2024.[23][18]

The standard Layer 1 tests were written for companies that sell products, so they need adapting for a bank holding company. Return on invested capital is replaced by return on tangible common equity, because a bank's capital is its equity. Free cash flow margin is not applicable: loans made for sale flow through operating cash flow, which was negative $3,742.5 million in 2025 and negative $8,502.5 million over the last twelve months, so the margin is a meaningless negative 204% (negative 8,793.0 divided by 4,305.7).[8][7] Net debt to EBITDA is not applicable because deposits are the raw material of a bank and not debt in the usual sense; we use CET1 capital and interest cover on corporate borrowings instead. The stock pay limit and the technical and reward-to-risk floors apply unchanged.

Stock pay deserves a plain explanation. SoFi pays employees partly in shares, which is a real cost because it dilutes existing owners, and it counted $283.9 million of it over the last twelve months. That is 6.6% of revenue, down from 24.3% in 2021, so the trend is good, but the level is still above the 5.0% limit.[23][8][7] The scoring rules allow up to 10% only when free cash flow less stock pay is at least 20% of revenue and the share count is falling. Neither condition holds here, since free cash flow is negative and shares outstanding rose 15.9% in a year.[4][7]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that can fail. We took the three that matter most and wrote each as a scenario in which the case for owning SoFi is wrong. All three are live: the Federal Reserve raised its rate on September 16, inflation was 3.7% in September, and the stock sits near a 52-week low ten days before it reports.[28][27][3] For each scenario we name the weakest assumption, the evidence that would show it wrong and the date we will know.

Fatal flaw 1: higher rates cost more than the fee income can replace

The assumption behind the base case is that net interest income keeps growing and the fair value marks stay manageable. The 10-Q says the balance sheet is liability sensitive: a 100 basis point rise would lower net interest income by $159.0 million over twelve months and the fair value of rate-sensitive assets by $1,040.8 million, against $92.6 million and $766.1 million at December 2025, so the exposure rose by 72% and 36% in six months (159.0 / 92.6 and 1,040.8 / 766.1).[10] The 10-year yield of 5.10% and a policy rate of 3.75% to 4.00% already sit above where the base case assumed they would settle.[29][28]

KBW warns of a rate headwind above $1 billion. The TIKR summary describes it as KBW's estimate for higher third-quarter rates before hedges, not a reported loss, and we have not seen KBW's own note.[35] The company has said that loan platform fees offset the capital cost, and the Chief Financial Officer said on September 8 that origination volumes would rise in the second half as a result.[35] The hinge is how much of the rate move is hedged and how much of the loan growth is fee-based rather than balance sheet. We will know on October 27, when the third-quarter report shows fair value changes, and again on December 8 to 9 when the Fed meets.[6][31]

Fatal flaw 2: loan growth consumes capital faster than profit creates it

The assumption is that CET1 stays well above 15% without another share sale. CET1 capital rose $639.3 million in the first half of 2026 while risk-weighted assets rose $11,448.4 million, and the ratio fell from 22.8% to 18.7%.[16][4] At the same pace the ratio would be about 17.3% at September 30 (9,432.6 divided by 54,406.6), 16.2% at December 2026 and 13.3% at December 2027, which is our extrapolation.[4] The regulatory minimum with buffers is 10.5% for total capital, so there is room, but a company that sold stock at $20.85 and $27.50 in 2025 may not wait to be forced to sell at $14.55.[14]

The evidence that would show the assumption wrong is a third-quarter CET1 ratio below 17.3%, or a new share offering. A $1.5 billion sale at $14.55 adds 103 million shares, 8.0% of those outstanding. The hinge here is the loan platform: if partners fund more of the new personal, small-business and home equity loans, risk-weighted assets grow more slowly.[5][35] The 10-Q also notes that the pool of buyers for residual interests is limited, which can push SoFi to use its own capital.[15]

Fatal flaw 3: consumer credit turns while loans are growing 30% a year

The assumption is that charge-offs stay near today's 1.81% of average loans. Personal loans are 58.9% of unpaid loan principal ($26.1 billion of $44.3 billion), and the company's own fair value default assumption for them rose to 4.77% from 4.57% in the quarter, even as realized charge-offs fell.[5] Inflation at 3.7% and a rising policy rate are the conditions in which unsecured consumer credit worsens, and fast-growing loan books are less seasoned than they look. The allowance on loans held at amortized cost is $56.5 million, or 4.29% of those loans, but most loans are carried at fair value and have no allowance, so the default assumption matters more than the allowance.[36][5]

Evidence of failure would be a quarterly personal loan charge-off rate above 3.0% (against 2.62%), or a default assumption above 5.0%. The hinge is whether delinquencies in the 2024 and 2025 vintages stay on the improving curve the company shows. We will know in the third-quarter report on October 27 and in the 2026 annual report in February 2027.[5][6]
