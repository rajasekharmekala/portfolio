SoFi is a digital bank and lender with a national charter, $45.5 billion of deposits and a software unit.[6][1] The verdict is REJECTED: the stock is in a Stage 4 downtrend, and its 8.6% return on tangible equity is under our 12% floor.[6] The business is growing fast, with revenue up 43% and members up 35%, so this call is about price and rate risk.[6] No shares are proposed, so the size is 0%, and the call would be wrong if the October 27, 2026 results lift the stock above $16.74 and the profit step-up arrives.[17] The only exposure is 22 January 2027 call options with a $25 strike, and the verdict for them is to sell all 22.

1. What the company does

SoFi began in 2011 as a way to refinance student loans, and it now describes itself as a financial services platform with three business lines: Lending, Financial Services and a Technology Platform.[1] It owns a bank, SoFi Bank, which is a nationally chartered association, and the parent company is a bank holding company.[1] At the end of June 2026 it had 15.8 million members, up 35% in a year, and 24.4 million products, up 42%, where a product is one account or loan a member has opened.[6] Think of it as a bank that lives in a phone app and tries to sell each member a second and third product.

Who pays, and for what? Borrowers pay interest on personal, student and home loans, and that interest is the largest income line: net interest income, which is interest earned minus interest paid, was $788.2 million in the second quarter, up 52% from a year earlier.[6] The average loan yielded 9.42% while interest-bearing deposits cost 3.07%, and that gap is the engine.[6] The second group of payers is fee customers: members who spend on its cards or trade in its brokerage app, partners who pay to buy loans SoFi originates, and companies that pay for processing on the Technology Platform.[6] Fee-based revenue was $472.3 million in the quarter, or 39% of the total, which matters because fee income does not need the bank to hold a loan on its balance sheet.[6]

The newest fee line is the Loan Platform Business, in which outside investors buy loans that SoFi finds and services, and SoFi keeps a fee per loan and does not fund the loan itself.[1] In the second quarter it added $143.3 million to adjusted net revenue, of which $140.9 million came from $3.1 billion of personal loans originated for third parties plus referrals.[6] Loan platform fees were $575.9 million in 2025, against $141.6 million in 2024 and $33.6 million in 2023, so this line did not exist at scale three years ago.[1] The company's own filing warns that this business is new and does not have a significant performance history, and that it has only a limited number of buyers for some of the loan pieces it sells.[1][4]

Two accounting facts shape how the numbers must be read. First, SoFi measures most of its loans at fair value, so a rise in market interest rates lowers the reported value of loans it holds, and the result flows through the income statement or equity.[4][6] Second, loans that SoFi originates to sell are counted as an operating cash outflow: cash flow from operations was negative $3.74 billion in 2025, mainly because loans held for sale grew by $5.27 billion.[1] Free cash flow, which is operating cash flow minus capital spending, therefore says nothing useful about SoFi, and this report uses return on tangible equity, capital ratios and credit losses instead.

How a loan moves through the company

A member applies in the app, and SoFi originates the loan inside SoFi Bank. It then picks one of three paths: keep the loan on the balance sheet and earn interest, sell it whole to an investor, or place it in a securitization, which means packaging many loans into bonds that investors buy.[1] Second-quarter originations were a record $14.8 billion, up 69%, and SoFi sold or transferred more than $4.1 billion of personal and home loans.[6] The choice matters because kept loans earn interest over years but tie up capital and absorb losses, while sold loans earn an upfront fee and free capital for the next loan. Chapter 2 asks which of those two economics is stronger, and what could break each.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Net interest margin (NIM): interest income less interest expense, as a share of earning assets. SoFi's was 5.98% in the second quarter.[6]
  • Adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization): profit before interest on corporate debt, taxes, depreciation, share-based pay and some one-off items, as SoFi defines it. It is higher than GAAP profit, which is profit under standard accounting rules.[6]
  • CET1 ratio: common equity tier 1 capital divided by risk-weighted assets, the main safety cushion regulators measure for a bank. SoFi's was 18.7%.[6]
  • Tangible book value: shareholders' equity minus goodwill and other intangible assets, so it counts only assets that could be sold. It was $9.47 billion, or $7.34 a share.[6]
  • Charge-off: a loan the company writes off as lost. The charge-off rate is the annualized share of average loans written off.[6]
  • Fair value: the price at which a loan could be sold today, as estimated by the company's model, which moves with interest rates and expected defaults.[4]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

SoFi's clearest strength is cheap funding. Average deposits were $41.9 billion in the second quarter at a cost of 3.07%, and the company says the rate paid on deposits was 156 basis points (hundredths of a percent) below what it pays on warehouse facilities, which are short-term credit lines from banks.[6] It puts the saving at about $712.6 million a year in interest expense.[6] Net interest margin was 5.98%, up 4 basis points from the prior quarter, and deposits rose $5.3 billion in the quarter to $45.5 billion.[6] A lender without a bank charter must lean harder on those markets, which is why the charter is the center of the SoFi case.

The second strength is growth that feeds on itself. Members rose by a record 1.1 million in the quarter, products rose by 2.2 million, and 51% of new products were opened by existing members, up from 35% a year earlier.[6] Products per member reached 1.54, an all-time high for the company.[6] The growth is not free: sales and marketing expense was $392.4 million, up 48% from $264.7 million, so the company is buying members with advertising and perks.[6] The test is whether second products come cheaper than first ones, and the cross-buy share says they do. Adjusted EBITDA still rose 44% to $357.8 million, a 30% margin.[6]

The weaknesses come in four kinds. The first is a shrinking line: Technology Platform revenue fell 23% and its accounts fell 16% to 135 million after a large client left.[6] The second is dependence on outside buyers: the 10-Q says the company has a limited number of prospective buyers for personal loan securitization residual interests, which could force it to use its own deposits or capital.[4] The third is competition on every front: its annual report lists other lenders, banks, challenger banks, brokerages, crypto platforms and software providers, and notes that many banks are larger and better known.[1] A moat here is a funding and distribution advantage, not a technology secret, so it is narrower than a software moat and can be eroded by a rate shock.

The fourth kind is interest-rate exposure on the balance sheet. SoFi calls its balance sheet liability sensitive, meaning its funding costs reset faster than its asset yields.[4] Its own table shows that a 1.00 percentage point rise in rates would cut net interest income by $159.0 million over 12 months, and a 2.00 point rise would cut it by $346.2 million.[4] The fair value of its rate-sensitive assets would fall by $1,040.8 million for a 1.00 point rise.[4] Interest rate swaps, which are hedging contracts, gained $223.5 million in the second quarter, so hedges absorb part of that fair-value swing, but the table does not net them out.[4]

What the rate sensitivity means for an owner

Net interest income of $788.2 million a quarter works out to about $3.15 billion a year, so the $159.0 million hit from a 1.00 point rise is about 5.0% of annual net interest income, which is small. The $1,040.8 million fair-value change is larger: it equals 9.4% of the $11.08 billion of equity, before hedges.[6][4] That is why a bank-like stock that is growing 40% a year can still fall when bond yields jump, and why the 10-year Treasury yield of 4.96% on October 22, 2026, from the session ledger, sits in the background of this report. 3. The numbers, in plain words

A bank-like company is read on different measures from a factory. Return on invested capital (ROIC), the profit earned on money put into the business, is replaced by return on tangible equity, because a bank's debt is mostly deposits that fund its loans and does not behave like corporate borrowing. Free cash flow (FCF) is not defined here, for the reason given in Chapter 1, and net debt to EBITDA is shown only in a narrow form that excludes deposits.

The five-year record tells a story of a company that lost money while it built a bank and has now made a profit in each of the last five quarters shown. Revenue rose from $984.9 million in 2021 to $3,613.4 million in 2025, a four-year compound rate of 38.4%, and a three-year rate from 2022 of 31.9%.[2][1] Net income swung from a loss of $483.9 million in 2021 to a profit of $481.3 million in 2025, but the profit in 2024 was lifted by a $265.3 million tax benefit, and the loss in 2023 included a $247.2 million goodwill impairment.[2][1]

A limit order is an order to buy only at a price you set or lower, and the entry zone is the range of prices where such an order is placed. We derived the zone from two numbers. Today's price is 1.15 ranges above the floor, which is the standard band, but the ladder only times an entry and cannot override a failed trend test.

The stop is placed at a structural invalidation level, which means a price that, if broken, shows the idea has failed and is not just wobbling.
