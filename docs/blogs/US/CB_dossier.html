1. What the company does

Chubb Limited is an insurance holding company with its legal home in Zurich, Switzerland. Its shares trade on the New York Stock Exchange under the symbol CB, and it belongs to the S&P 500 index[3]. The registered address in the filings is Zurich[20]. It operates in 54 countries and territories, employs about 45,000 people, and sells commercial and personal property and casualty cover, accident and supplemental health cover, reinsurance and life insurance[3]. The company reports in six segments: North America Commercial, North America Personal, North America Agricultural, Overseas General, Global Reinsurance and Life[10]. In 2025 its net premiums earned, the part of premiums that relates to cover already provided, were $53.0 billion[10].

The customer is the policyholder: a company that insures its factories and liability risks, a family that insures its house and cars, or a farmer that insures a crop. The policyholder pays a premium up front, and the insurer pays claims later. The gap between the two is why an insurer holds a large pool of other people's money, called float. Chubb's total invested assets were $175.5 billion at September 30, 2026[4]. Insurers also buy their own insurance, called reinsurance, to cap the cost of a very large loss, and that is why the company reports net premiums written, the premiums kept after paying for reinsurance. Net premiums written in the third quarter were $15.1 billion[3].

Chubb earns money in three ways. The first is underwriting, which is premiums collected less claims and running costs. The measure is the combined ratio, which is claims plus expenses divided by earned premium. A ratio under 100 percent means an underwriting profit. The property and casualty combined ratio was 84.1 percent in the third quarter and 84.0 percent for nine months, so each $100 of premium left about $15.90 of profit before tax in the quarter, or $2,061 million in all[3][4]. The second way is investing. Net investment income was $5.26 billion in nine months, up 10.1 percent because there was more money to invest and interest rates were higher[4][6]. The portfolio has an average credit quality of A, an average life of 5.0 years, a book yield of 5.2 percent and a market yield of 6.2 percent[5].

The third way is life and accident and health insurance, which made $369 million of segment income in the third quarter, up 11.4 percent[3]. Life is 14 percent of net premiums earned: $7,224 million of $53,014 million in 2025[10]. Most of it is international life insurance, plus Chubb Benefits, which sells supplemental accident, health, disability and life cover across the United States and Canada[3][10]. Life earnings do not move with storms, which helps steady the whole. The company returns a little over half of what it earns: $4.47 billion of dividends and share repurchases in nine months, which is 55.8 percent of the $8.02 billion of net income[3].

What this means for an owner: you are buying a global, diversified underwriter that has produced a combined ratio below 90 percent in each of the last five years, plus an investment portfolio that grows with premiums and interest rates[12][9]. The risk to understand is that insurers are paid for taking risk, so results swing with storms, claims inflation and the pricing cycle. The next chapters test how strong the position is, which operating measures matter, and whether today's price already pays for it.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • earnings per share (EPS) — profit divided by the number of shares. We use core operating EPS, which leaves out investment gains and losses, and we say so each time[3].
  • return on invested capital (ROIC) — profit earned on the money put into a business. For an insurer we use return on equity (profit divided by shareholders' money) and return on tangible equity instead, because insurers fund risk with equity and not with plant and equipment.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) — a profit measure used to judge debt. It fits an insurer poorly, so we lead with debt as a share of total capital and with interest cover.
  • FCF (free cash flow) — cash left after spending on equipment. For an insurer it is not meaningful: cash flow from operations rises when premiums arrive before claims are paid, and was a record $6.07 billion in the third quarter[3]. We mark FCF margin not applicable.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) — the cost of paying staff in shares. It was $400 million in 2025, which is 0.67 percent of revenue[11][13].
  • net debt — debt minus cash. For an insurer the cash belongs mostly to policyholders, so we use financial debt over total adjusted capital: 18.6 percent at September 30, 2026[7].
  • consensus estimate — the average of what analysts expect for profit per share; for CB we found one figure per year from S&P Global data on a public page[22].
  • guidance — a forecast the company publishes. Chubb gives no numeric earnings guidance; its chief executive states expectations in the quarterly release, which we test in Chapter 2B[17].
  • buyback — the company buys its own shares, which cuts the share count. Chubb has $6.0 billion of authorization left[7].
  • price-to-earnings — the share price divided by profit per share; it says how many years of current profit you pay for.
  • RSI (relative strength index) — a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has risen; above 70 is called overbought and below 30 oversold.
  • ATR (average true range) — the typical daily price swing in dollars, used here to measure distance from support in units of normal movement.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average — the average close over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days; each is a slow line that the price tends to bounce from.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend in the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend, the only chart condition that rejects a stock outright.
  • base — a sideways price shelf a stock builds before a move; the pivot is the top of that shelf, a breakout is a move above it on heavy volume, and a retest is a pullback to that level from above.
  • stop loss — the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold; a limit order only fills at your price or better, so it never chases.
  • entry zone — the band of prices where buying is planned; asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The central strength is breadth. No single line, region or risk drives Chubb: commercial insurance in North America is 38 percent of earned premium, overseas business 27 percent, personal lines 13 percent, life 14 percent, farm 5 percent and reinsurance 3 percent[10]. In the third quarter the chief executive said that a substantial majority of the underwriting businesses grew, while the rest were flat or shrinking on purpose because the price or terms were inadequate[3]. That is the behavior of an insurer that will give up premium before it gives up margin, and the combined ratio history supports it: 89.1 percent in 2021, 87.6 percent in 2022, 86.5 percent in 2023, 86.6 percent in 2024 and 85.7 percent in 2025[12][9].

The second strength is the quality of the underwriting margin. Chubb reports a current accident year combined ratio that leaves out catastrophe losses and reserve changes, which is the cleanest read of how well this year's business is priced. It was 83.9 percent in 2023, 83.1 percent in 2024 and 81.9 percent in 2025, and it was 82.3 percent for the first nine months of 2026 against 82.4 percent a year earlier[9][6]. The measure has stopped improving, but it has not worsened, even as North America Commercial moved from 80.8 percent to 82.1 percent in the third quarter because it wrote less property business[3].

The third strength is scale in investing and reserves. The company holds net unpaid claims of $69.7 billion, and its auditor lists the valuation of those reserves as the one critical audit matter, because the long-tail casualty and asbestos exposures involve high estimation uncertainty[39]. Net favorable reserve development, which means earlier claim estimates that proved too high, was $1,133 million in 2025 and $1,080 million in the first nine months of 2026[11][6]. A steady record of releases is a sign of prudent reserving, but it also flatters profit, a point we return to in Chapter 6.

The weaknesses are three. First, the commercial cycle is turning against insurers. Management described property markets in the first quarter as soft or softening, in the second quarter as overly soft in large-account and excess and surplus lines, and said soft conditions were spreading to casualty and financial lines[16][15]. North America Commercial premium fell 1.1 percent in nine months, and major accounts and specialty business fell 4.6 percent[6]. Second, growth is slowing: property and casualty premiums rose 7.2 percent in the first quarter, 3.0 percent in the second and 1.9 percent in the third[16][15][3]. Third, the balance sheet carries interest-rate risk: unrealized losses on bonds cut book value by $4.48 billion after tax in the third quarter[3].

The gap comes from a very large equity base: Chubb shareholders' equity is $73.2 billion, of which $26.0 billion is goodwill and other intangibles in Chubb's share[4]. That is why management quotes a return on tangible equity of 21.6 percent as well[3].

Governance, capital return and what insiders have at stake

Pay is tied to the same measures this report tests. The proxy statement says the chief executive's pay is 95 percent at risk, that long-term share awards vest on tangible book value per share growth and the property and casualty combined ratio, and that the annual scorecard puts 75 percent of the weight on financial results including core operating income, core return on equity and the combined ratio[14]. Insiders own 0.34 percent of the shares and institutions 83.98 percent, according to an aggregator page[23]. The company has paid a larger dividend each year for 34 years: the annual shareholder-approved dividend is up to $4.08 per share, paid as $1.02 each quarter, against $3.88 a year earlier[23][8].

Chubb is domiciled in Switzerland, which matters for two reasons. Dividends are paid by distribution from capital contribution reserves approved by shareholders at the annual meeting, and the quarterly payments follow that approval[8]. Debt is issued through a subsidiary, Chubb INA Holdings LLC, with a guarantee from the parent: in 2026 it sold $1.0 billion of 5.300 percent notes due 2036 and two Canadian-dollar tranches of C$400 million each[20][21].

What the business does not have

There is no pricing power in the usual sense. Insurance is sold in markets where capacity returns quickly when profits are high, so Chubb's edge is selection, scale and service and not a monopoly. The financial-strength ratings from S&P, AM Best, Moody's and Fitch are a business asset, but we did not retrieve the specific grades in this run, so we do not quote them[9]. There is also no free cash flow measure to lean on, and part of reported profit comes from reserve releases and private-equity marks that are not guaranteed to repeat[3].

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 separates the level of quality from the direction of travel. 5. What could happen next

A price is a forecast. The question here is what growth in profit Chubb's price of $356.25 already assumes, how analysts' estimates are moving, and where we differ from the street. The scoring file fixes a 10 percent discount rate, an 18 times exit multiple and a 10-year horizon[37].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter takes each estimate in Chapter 5 and names its weakest assumption, then states what the evidence would look like if we were wrong and when we would know. It then gives the strongest honest case against buying, even at a lower price.

Reverse DCF (-1.0 percent a year). The weakest assumption is that net income is a fair stand-in for distributable cash. Chubb keeps capital to support new premiums and regulators require it, so free cash is lower than profit. The evidence would be a payout ratio that rises above the 55.8 percent seen in nine months without a fall in capital ratios, or a debt-to-capital ratio that climbs above 20 percent[3][7]. We would know at the full-year report.

Base value ($383). The weakest assumption is the 13.0 times multiple on FY2027 EPS of $29.46. If investors pay only 12.0 times, the value is $354, close to today's price[22]. The evidence would be a falling forward multiple while earnings hold, as it did when the multiple went from 13.68 in 2021 to 10.97 in 2023[22]. The next data point is the fourth-quarter report.

Bull value ($427). The weakest assumption is that commercial premiums recover. Management has said so in two consecutive quarters and the segment fell 2.3 and 2.7 percent[15][3]. The evidence would be North America Commercial growth turning positive in the fourth-quarter report, which should appear between late January and early February 2027, since the fourth quarter of 2025 was reported on February 3, 2026[1][17].

Bear value ($288). The weakest assumption is the size of the earnings shortfall: we cut $3.26 a share and used the 2025 catastrophe level, but a single very large event, such as a major hurricane, could cost more. The evidence would be a catastrophe ratio above 6.3 points for the full year; hurricane season ends on November 30, so the fourth-quarter report settles it.

The red-team bear case. The strongest case against Chubb is that the best of the cycle is behind it. The combined ratio is near a record low, reserve releases of about 3 points are doing much of the work, large-account property premiums are down 23.8 percent, premium growth is falling and the stock trades at 12.5 times profit, which is above most peers. Reported profit would then fall for three reasons together: the cycle softens, catastrophes normalize and reserves stop releasing. On this view the stock is a good company at a fair price with a falling trend in margins.

Fatal-flaw scenario 1: the soft market becomes a price war that hurts the loss ratio

If rates fall in casualty and financial lines, as management says is starting, premiums could fall while claims keep rising with inflation. The underlying combined ratio of 82.6 percent would then rise toward 85 to 88 percent. In 2021 the reported combined ratio was 89.1 percent and 2022 was 87.6 percent, and a return there would cut earnings by roughly $3.50 a share (3.4 points on about $50 billion of earned premium, after tax)[12][9]. The warning sign is a rising current accident year ratio together with falling premiums. In the third quarter the ratio was flat (82.6 against 82.5 percent) while premiums grew 1.9 percent, so the warning has not yet sounded[3].

Fatal-flaw scenario 2: reserves turn out to be too low

Chubb has $69.7 billion of net claim reserves, mostly long-tail casualty, and the auditor flags them as the one critical audit matter[39]. Favorable development has been steady ($1,133 million in 2025), but the corporate segment recorded $306 million of adverse development in 2025 for older asbestos and environmental exposures[11]. A reserve error of just 2 percent of net reserves would be about $1.4 billion before tax, or about 2.8 points of premiums on roughly $50 billion of earned premium. The warning sign is adverse development in North America Commercial, which still showed favorable development of 0.3 points in the third quarter[6].

Fatal-flaw scenario 3: investment and interest-rate shock to book value

The company holds $172.5 billion of investments, and higher interest rates cut book value by $4.48 billion after tax in the third quarter alone, which is why reported tangible book value per share rose only 5.7 percent[3][4]. If rates rise further or credit spreads widen, book value would fall again, even though income would rise. A fall in private-equity marks would also hit profit: the company had $343 million of private-equity income in nine months[3]. The warning sign is a rising AOCI loss, now $9.5 billion, and a falling price-to-book multiple[4].
