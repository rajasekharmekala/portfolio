Before you read

Duolingo sells a mobile language-learning app and a rival English exam. It is growing fast and earns real cash: revenue rose 39% to $1,037.6 million in 2025, and cash left after capital spending has stayed above 30% of revenue for two straight years. Layer 1 is this book's set of six pass-or-fail tests: return on capital, cash margin, debt, pay in stock, price trend and payoff; Duolingo clears the first three and fails the last three. Pay to staff in stock runs more than twice the 5% ceiling, the upside-to-downside math at today's price is far under the 3.5-to-1 floor, and the 200-day average is still falling. This report therefore rejects the name at today's price and would reconsider it near $132 or if staff stock pay fell under 5% of revenue.

What the company does

Duolingo makes a free mobile app that teaches languages, plus music, chess and math. Lessons are short and gamified, with streaks, leaderboards and a mascot owl named Duo. Luis von Ahn founded the company in 2011 with Severin Hacker. Von Ahn is chief executive; Hacker is chief technology officer. The company is based in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. It had about 830 employees as of December 2024. It listed on Nasdaq on 2021-07-28.[20] The app reached 140.6 million monthly users and 58.7 million daily users in the quarter ended 2026-06-30. Those are up 10% and 23% from a year earlier.[18]

Most users pay nothing. Money comes from three sources. Subscriptions (Super Duolingo and a higher Max tier with AI conversation practice) are the largest. Advertising shown to free users is the second. The Duolingo English Test, an online rival to paper exams like TOEFL, is the third. Over 6,100 education programs worldwide accept this test. More than 5,300 of those are higher-education programs, including all of the Ivy League, MIT, Stanford, and 24 of the top 25 U.S. undergraduate programs by international enrollment.[1] In 2025, subscription revenue was $873.4 million, up 44%, mostly from more paid subscribers. Other revenue, ads plus the English test, was $164.1 million, up 17%, mostly from more daily users seeing more ads.[1] Paid subscribers reached 12.7 million at the end of the second quarter of 2026. That is 21.6% of that quarter's 58.7 million daily users. Most of the audience still pays nothing directly. Turning free users into paying ones, not finding new users, is the main lever left to grow revenue.[18]

Management's stated goal is 100 million daily users by 2028. Both the first- and second-quarter 2026 letters repeat this goal. The company says it is choosing user growth over near-term profit on purpose.[17][18] In 2025 the company said it would become "AI-first," replacing some contracted translators and writers with automation. The move drew public criticism. Some users cancelled subscriptions and broke learning streaks in protest. Reviewers said some AI-made course content lost nuance.[19] This is a reputation risk layered on top of the financial picture in later chapters.

A base is a quiet price range where a share rests before a bigger move. A pivot is the price at the top of that base. A breakout is a move past the pivot on strong volume. A retest is a pullback to the pivot after a breakout, to check the level holds.

A stop loss is a set price at which a position is sold to cap the loss. A limit order is an order to buy only at or below a set price. The entry zone is the price range where a purchase is allowed.

Why the business is strong or weak

The strength is real and has compounded for five straight years. Revenue grew from $250.8 million in 2021 to $1,037.6 million in 2025. That is a compound rate above 40% a year. The growth has not needed debt or steady cash burn. Cash from operations was positive every year in this five-year record. Free cash flow margin was 34.7% in 2025.[16] The English test gives Duolingo a moat a rival cannot copy fast. A university's trust in a testing product builds over years, not months.[1] Current User Retention Rate, or CURR, is the share of a recent active user who returns the next day. It hit an all-time high of 84% in the second quarter of 2026, up about a point from a year earlier. Management calls CURR its most important leading indicator, because small gains compound into large gains in daily users.[18]

The weakness is that much of today's growth is a growth-in-users story, not a growth-in-money story. Management says this split is deliberate. In the second quarter of 2026, daily users grew 23% and revenue grew 18%. But total bookings, the cash value of sales actually signed in the quarter, grew only 8%. Bookings lead revenue, so a bookings-to-revenue gap this wide is a warning sign for the quarters ahead. Adjusted EBITDA margin fell to 25.9% from 31.2% a year earlier.[18] Net income fell to $33.2 million from $44.8 million over the same stretch, before the one-time items chapter 3 explains. Guidance for all of 2026, raised twice this year, still calls for only 10.9% bookings growth against 16.3% revenue growth. That full-year gap is 5.4 points, narrower than the 10 points of the second quarter, but bookings still trail revenue.[17]

Free cash flow margin has stayed above 28% since 2023.[6][9][10] The 2025 net income line is misleading on its own. Operating income, the cleaner gauge of the core business, was $135.6 million. Net income was $414.1 million, a gap of $278.5 million. That gap is almost all a one-time tax item. In 2025 Duolingo released the valuation allowance it held against its deferred tax assets. A valuation allowance is a reserve a company holds when it is not yet sure it can use a tax asset. In plain words, Duolingo looks like an uptrend by the simplest measure: price above both moving averages. But the chart underneath is not confirmed. The long-term trend line has not turned up. The price has already run far above the level where fresh buying would normally start. The picture could improve on its own: as weak prices from a year ago roll out of the 200-day average, that average should start to rise within a few months even without new highs. For the signal to count as confirmed, though, the stock would also need to pull back toward its base rather than run further. Until then, a stock that fails both tests is not allocated here, however good the underlying business.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Estimate 1 depends most on the 18-times multiple. Duolingo has no steady earnings record to anchor a price-to-earnings multiple instead. The market's own implied multiple, about 17.8 times, could re-rate higher if bookings growth speeds back up. It could re-rate lower if paid-subscriber growth keeps trailing daily-user growth, as it did in Q2 2026 (17% against 23%). The evidence that would settle this is the Q3 2026 earnings release, expected around early November 2026, and whether the bookings-to-revenue gap narrows or widens.[17]

Estimate 2 depends most on the stop. A tighter stop at the 20-day average, $146.74, would cut risk to just $3.18 and lift both ratios above the floor. But that tight a stop is not the tactical rule this book uses, and using it here only to flatter the numbers would be the kind of result-shopping this framework exists to prevent. A wider stop at the 200-day average, $126.49, would raise risk to $23.43 and push both ratios lower still. The weak spot in the raw numbers is that 2025's net income and EPS are inflated by the one-time tax item in chapter 3. A reader who anchors a multiple to trailing price-to-earnings, rather than enterprise value to EBITDA, will get a misleadingly low multiple today and should avoid that approach for this name.[16]

Two further checks cut against the bull case. First, the Street is not more optimistic than this report. The average analyst target is $135.12 on one aggregator and $125.31 on another, both below the $149.92 close. At the $132.55 re-entry level, a $135.12 target gives only (135.12 - 132.55) divided by (132.55 - 130.56), or 1.29 to 1, and a $125.31 target gives a negative ratio, so even the re-underwrite trigger would not clear the 3.5-to-1 floor against those targets.[22][23] Second, insiders sold about $37.7 million of stock in the period shown and bought none.[25] Insider selling is common at companies that pay heavily in stock, so it is a weak signal, but it does not support giving the bull case more than 25%.

One limit applies to the base case. The paid-subscriber and bookings lag may come partly from the 2025 AI-first backlash described in chapter 1, when some users cancelled in protest. The data on hand do not report cancellations separately, so this report cannot tell backlash-driven cancellations from ordinary slow monetization. The base case assumes the lag is monetization, and that assumption is untested.

Competition is a second reason the monetization lag may last. Roundups of language apps name Speak, Babbel, Talkpal, Lingopie and Mondly as alternatives, so slower paid growth could reflect users choosing a substitute and not only Duolingo's pricing choices. This is secondary, qualitative context, and no figure in this report depends on it.[34]

Three events could break this thesis in either direction.

  • Bookings growth catches up to revenue growth. The gap was 10 points in Q2 2026: 8% bookings growth against 18% revenue growth. If that gap narrows in Q3 2026, it would show monetization catching up with the user base, not trailing it for good, and would support a higher multiple. The Q3 2026 letter, due around early November 2026, is the first place this would show.[17]
  • Stock-based pay keeps rising as a share of revenue. Management guides it toward roughly 15% of revenue for 2026, up from 13.2% in 2025. If that trend runs past 2026, instead of reversing once the current buyback is spent, the SBC gate failure in chapter 7 becomes a structural problem, not a temporary one. The trigger to re-underwrite on pay would then be harder, not easier, to meet.[3]
  • The 200-day average turns up on its own. Weak prices from about a year ago are rolling out of that average. It is likely to start rising within a few months, even without new highs. A reader should not mistake that mechanical shift for a real change in trend.
