Chapter 1

Seven engineers founded Qualcomm in San Diego in July 1985. Dr. Irwin Mark Jacobs and Dr. Andrew Viterbi led the spin-out from Linkabit [19]. Early revenue came from government R&D contracts and OmniTRACS, a satellite tracking tool for long-haul trucks [19]. That steady cash funded a bold bet on Code Division Multiple Access (CDMA). At the time, Europe and most carriers backed GSM for 2G cellular [19]. Jacobs argued CDMA could pack far more calls and data into scarce radio spectrum [19]. To prove the physics worked, Qualcomm even built its own CDMA phones and base stations in the mid-1990s [19].

Once CDMA proved itself in South Korea and North America, Qualcomm made a decisive pivot between 1998 and 2000. It sold its base-station unit to Ericsson and its handset arm to Kyocera [19]. That shift turned Qualcomm into a fabless chip designer (QCT) and a pure IP licensor (QTL) [19]. Core CDMA patents sat inside 3G, 4G LTE, and 5G NR standards. Qualcomm charged phone makers a share of wholesale device prices rather than a flat chip fee [19]. Critics called this model the "no license, no chips" flywheel [19].

Qualcomm has reinvented itself each time wireless cycles matured. It shed factories in 1998, survived M&A siege in 2018, and pivoted into Auto, Edge AI, and Data Center silicon by 2026.

The 2016–2018 era tested Qualcomm's board and balance sheet. Smartphone unit growth slowed, and Apple sued over royalty rates. In October 2016, Qualcomm offered $44 billion in cash for NXP Semiconductors to gain auto and industrial scale [19]. China's regulator (MOFCOM) stalled approval amid U.S.–China trade friction [19]. With the stock depressed, Broadcom's Hock Tan launched a hostile $70-per-share bid in late 2017, later raised to $82 (~$121 billion) [19]. In March 2018, the board ousted chairman Paul Jacobs after he explored a private buyout [19]. Days later, President Donald Trump blocked Broadcom's bid on CFIUS national-security grounds [19]. Qualcomm then dropped NXP, paid a $2 billion breakup fee, and bought back $30 billion in stock [19].

That close call reshaped modern Qualcomm. After settling with Apple in April 2019, Cristiano Amon became CEO in June 2021 [13], [19]. His goal was simple: never let one customer or one phone cycle control Qualcomm's fate. First, Qualcomm bought Nuvia for $1.4 billion in 2021 to build custom Oryon CPU cores [9]. Next, it grew organic Snapdragon Digital Chassis wins in cars [13]. Finally, in December 2025, it paid $2.3 billion for Alphawave Semi to enter data center interconnects [16]. Together, those moves built three non-handset growth legs while defending core mobile [9], [13], [16].

The 2018 NXP failure also taught Qualcomm's board an enduring capital-allocation lesson. Mega-mergers face geopolitical veto risk in Beijing and Washington [19]. Instead of chasing $40 billion deals, Amon favored smaller IP bolt-ons like Nuvia and Alphawave [9], [16]. That organic-plus-IP path took five years to mature. By FY2026, however, it gave Qualcomm real scale in cars, edge AI, and cloud interconnects without over-leveraging the balance sheet [1], [3], [13].

Chapter 3

Qualcomm runs a fabless chip supply chain. It outsources wafer fabrication, bumping, and most testing to outside partners [3], [22]. Key suppliers include TSMC at 3nm and 4nm nodes, Samsung Foundry, and OSAT packagers ASE Group and Amkor [3], [22]. Because Qualcomm owns no billion-dollar fabs, capital intensity stays low. FY2026 capex was $2,494 million, or 5.8% of revenue [2], [3]. That rose from $1,192 million (2.7%) in FY2025 due to data-center test gear and Alphawave [2], [3]. In return, Qualcomm signs multi-year wafer capacity deals and holds large chip inventories.

Go-to-market (GTM) distribution differs sharply by segment. In Handsets, Qualcomm sells straight to top phone makers like Apple, Samsung, Xiaomi, Oppo, Vivo, and Honor [8], [22]. It bundles processors, modems, RF front-end modules, and power chips in annual flagship deals [8], [22]. In Automotive, sales run through Tier-1 integrators (Bosch, Continental, Harman, Valeo) and direct automaker deals (BMW, Mercedes-Benz, GM) [20]. Those car programs use 3-to-5-year design-win cycles [20]. In IoT, module partners (Quectel, Fibocom, Telit) and distributors (Arrow, Avnet) serve tens of thousands of buyers. Finally, QTL collects royalties directly via OEM audits and quarterly shipment reports [8].
