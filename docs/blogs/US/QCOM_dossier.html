1. What the company does

Qualcomm is a chip designer and a patent licensor. It sells processors, modems and related chips under the Snapdragon and Dragonwing names, and it licenses its patents to the companies that build phones and other connected devices.[1][4] The company reports through two main businesses. QCT, short for Qualcomm CDMA Technologies, is the chip business. QTL, short for Qualcomm Technology Licensing, is the patent licensing business.[1] A third segment, QSI (Qualcomm Strategic Initiatives), makes minority investments in other companies, and the smaller units include a government services group and the new Data Center business.[1][4] Around 52,000 people work for the company.[1]

The customer list is short, and that is the first fact to hold in mind. In fiscal 2025 (the year ended September 28, 2025), Apple, Samsung and Xiaomi each supplied 10% or more of total revenue.[1] Qualcomm's own filings say it expects Apple to use its own modem in future devices, which it calls a significant negative for QCT revenue.[4] On the July 2026 call the finance chief said Qualcomm's share of the next iPhone launch would be materially lower than the earlier 20% estimate, and he forecast an approximately 50% fall in Apple revenue from the September quarter to the December quarter.[12] A phone maker that designs its own modem buys fewer Qualcomm chips, which is why this report treats Apple as a shrinking line and not a stable one.

QTL earns money in a simple way. Licensees pay a quarterly royalty on each device that uses Qualcomm patents, usually a percentage of the wholesale selling price, with some per-unit minimums and caps.[1] Qualcomm says its patent license agreements with key phone makers are generally long-term and expire between fiscal 2027 and fiscal 2031, so renewals are a recurring negotiation.[4] The segment is small in revenue but rich in profit. In fiscal 2025 QTL booked $5,582 million of revenue and $4,043 million of earnings before tax (EBT, profit before income tax), a 72% EBT margin, while QCT booked $38,367 million of revenue and $11,670 million of EBT, a 30% margin.[9]

Revenue is reported by the headquarters of the customer, not by where the phone is finally sold. On that basis China, including Hong Kong, was $20,340 million or 46% of fiscal 2025 revenue, the United States 24% and South Korea 21%.[1] The 10-Q lists the concentration in China, and the tension between the US and China over trade and national security, among its main risks.[4] The next table breaks revenue into the four streams that drive the story: handsets, automotive, IoT (internet of things, meaning connected devices from wearables to factory gear) and licensing.

  • Custom silicon is a chip designed for one customer's specification, here for large cloud companies.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Qualcomm's competitive position rests on three things the filings let us check. The first is a patent portfolio that every major phone maker pays to use. The second is a place in premium phone chips. The third is a growing car business with long product cycles. The licensing moat is the oldest. QTL earned a 72% EBT margin in fiscal 2025 and 73% in the first nine months of fiscal 2026. Its revenue rose 1.9% in those nine months even as handset chip sales fell.[9][4] In premium phones, Qualcomm says Snapdragon powers about 70% of Samsung's flagship devices.[12] That is the only Samsung share figure we found. In cars, the company targets $10 billion of automotive revenue by fiscal 2029 and cites a $65 billion pipeline of design wins.[10] Carmakers need long qualification, which is a high barrier to entry.[1]

The weaknesses are just as visible, and the numbers are recent. Handset chip revenue fell 20% to $5,086 million in the June 2026 quarter. Customers cut build plans after memory prices rose.[6][4] Qualcomm expects the memory shortage to keep hurting demand from several phone makers.[4] IDC forecasts that smartphone shipments will fall 16.7% in 2026, the steepest drop on record. It sees PC shipments falling 11.3%, with no meaningful memory relief before the end of 2027.[20][21] Gross margin is the share of each sales dollar left after the cost of what was sold. It fell to 53% in the June 2026 quarter from 56% a year earlier.[4] Management says higher chip-making and memory costs will keep QCT margins slightly below the historical range for now.[12] It plans double-digit price increases, which will arrive slowly because some contracts must run out first.[12]

Customer concentration has a second edge: vertical integration, which means a customer builds its own chip. The 10-K names Apple, Samsung and Xiaomi as customers that develop their own chips.[1] The 10-Q adds that Apple will increasingly use its own modem.[4] If Samsung uses Qualcomm in about 70% of flagship devices, roughly 30% is not Qualcomm's. The filings do not say how that share will change. Apple is stepping down faster than planned. So Qualcomm now says growth in non-handset revenue should replace all Apple product revenue within fiscal 2027.[12]

China, export controls and tariffs

China, including Hong Kong, is 46% of revenue by customer headquarters. That is the highest share of any country.[1] The 10-Q says US and Chinese trade actions, bans and restricted-entity lists have limited sales to some Chinese customers and suppliers.[4] Huawei is the sharpest example. In May 2024 the US Department of Commerce revoked the export license under which Qualcomm sold 4G and some other chips to Huawei. Qualcomm expects no more product revenue from it.[4] On tariffs, the filing says only that the company is monitoring trade policy and that the effects are uncertain.[4] No tariff cost figure is published, so we show none. On the July call, management said handset revenue from Chinese phone makers hit a bottom in the June quarter and would return to double-digit sequential growth in the September quarter.[12] Chapter 6 tests that claim.

Licensing and the Arm disputes

Qualcomm is in two linked court fights with Arm Holdings, whose chip designs sit under Qualcomm's processors. In the case Arm began in 2022, a Delaware jury found in December 2024 that Qualcomm did not breach Nuvia's license. It also found that Qualcomm CPUs using Nuvia designs are licensed under Qualcomm's own agreement.[1][4] On September 30, 2025 the court entered final judgment for Qualcomm. On October 1, 2025 Arm appealed to the Third Circuit.[4] Qualcomm filed its own case against Arm on April 18, 2024 and added a claim in March 2026 that Arm failed to negotiate license terms in good faith.[4] The filings give no date for the appeal ruling, so the outcome is open. A loss on appeal would put Qualcomm's custom CPU cores at risk. We list that as a fatal-flaw scenario in Chapter 6.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter attacks our own work. Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a weakest assumption. The useful question is what evidence would show it wrong, and when we would see it. The bear case is the version of events in which Qualcomm disappoints. It is the strongest honest argument against holding even 16 shares. In it, Apple revenue disappears faster than non-handset revenue grows. The memory shortage holds Android handset sales down through 2027. Data-center revenue arrives late, at low margins, with up to 25,000,000 new shares attached.[12][20][21][14] The stock would then trade near our bear value of $99.00, which is 43.7% under the close.

Fatal-flaw scenario 1: Apple leaves faster and Android does not recover

The weakest assumption behind the $12.00 earnings estimate is that growth in cars, IoT and Data Center replaces all of Apple's product revenue. Management says that growth will exceed 60% in fiscal 2027. The same assumption needs Android handset revenue to stop falling.[12] Both halves can fail. Handset revenue fell 20% in the June quarter. Apple revenue is forecast to fall about 50% from the September to the December quarter.[6][12] IDC forecasts a 16.7% fall in 2026 smartphone shipments, and no meaningful memory relief for PCs before the end of 2027.[20][21] If the December-quarter guide shows Android below the September quarter, or non-handset growth below 40%, the estimate is too high.

Fatal-flaw scenario 2: Data Center arrives late, thin and dilutive

The weakest assumption in the data-center part of the base case is the ramp. Revenue must climb from about $0.3 billion in fiscal 2026 to $5 billion in fiscal 2027, roughly seventeen times, on two custom-chip programs.[11][12] Management itself says investors want more proof that it can execute as a new entrant.[12] The Amazon deal is a collaboration with a warrant attached. The $60 billion is a ceiling on payments, not an order. Only 3,750,000 of the 25,000,000 warrant shares had vested when it was issued.[14][16] The first custom-chip revenue should carry a gross margin well below the baseline. Management puts the drag at 1.5 to 2 points on the weighted QCT margin, while operating costs rise ahead of revenue.[12] If December-quarter Data Center revenue is missing or small, both the $5 billion target and our base case lose their main support.

Fatal-flaw scenario 3: a court, a customer or a government hits the licensing and China base

The weakest assumption behind the steady licensing profit is that no large royalty agreement or patent position is lost. License agreements with key phone makers expire between fiscal 2027 and fiscal 2031, so renewals are a continuing risk. Arm's appeal in the Nuvia case is pending at the Third Circuit, and the filings give no ruling date.[4] China, including Hong Kong, is 46% of revenue by customer headquarters. The May 2024 Huawei license revocation shows how one government action can remove a customer.[1][4] None of these is a forecast. Each is a possible event that our estimates do not price. The stop of $162.05 and the 3.5% size limit exist to cap what such an event could cost us.

7. What we would do, and when

Layer 3 is the timing and sizing layer of the underwriting. It measures the distance between the price and the nearest support level in ATR (average true range) units. It then places that distance on a ladder. Within 0.6 ATR of support is the full-size band. Up to 1.2 ATR is the standard band. Up to 1.6 ATR is the starter-only band. Beyond that is a no-chase band. Our support is the 20-day swing low of $168.84. It is the lowest price of the last 20 sessions, and the stop is built from it. The close, which we also call the current market price (CMP), is $175.91. That is 1.04 ATR above support, inside the standard band. The band matters less than the arithmetic below it, because the reward-to-risk ratio fails the floor everywhere we test it.

Sizing works on risk first. Portfolio heat is the share of net worth you would lose if every position hit its stop at once. Risk-unit sizing sets each weight so that a stop hit costs a fixed number of basis points (bps, hundredths of a percent) of net worth. The fixed number depends on the conviction tier. TIER_1 is the highest conviction: 7% to 10% of net worth and 100 bps of risk. TIER_2 is 4% to 6.5% and 75 bps. TIER_3 is 2% to 3.5% and 50 bps. TIER_4 is 1% to 2% and 35 bps. TIER_0 is zero size, which means no position. Our yardstick for Qualcomm is TIER_3. Quality is high, but the asymmetry fails the floor, estimates are under pressure and the one-year beta is 2.09. So we do not give it a higher tier. The weight is the lower of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. Here that is 3.5%, against 0.50% divided by 6.28%, which is 7.96%. The 6.28% is the stop distance measured from the top of the entry zone, $172.91, where the stop is $10.86 away. Measured from the $175.91 close, which the asymmetry ratio uses, the stop is $13.86 or 7.88% away, and 0.50% divided by 7.88% is 6.35%. The ceiling of 3.5% sets the size on either basis, and that is why the head shows a 3.5% risk-unit size.

A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut. For CORE_ALPHA names the rule sets it at structural invalidation. That is the level where the chart's own structure says the thesis has failed. It is the lower of the 20-day swing low ($168.84) and the 50-day simple moving average (SMA, the plain average of the last 50 closes, $176.11), minus one ATR of $6.79. That gives $162.05. A second stop is the 63-day base low of $142.89 minus one ATR, which gives $136.10. That rule is for stocks in a Stage 1 base, and Qualcomm is in a Stage 3 pullback. So we show it and do not use it. Both stops fail the asymmetry test. On the $170.76 base value, the reward-to-risk ratio at the close is -0.37:1 with the $162.05 stop and -0.13:1 with the $136.10 stop.

The entry zone is for any future purchase. We derived it, and we did not choose it. Its top is $172.91, which is the support of $168.84 plus 0.6 ATR, the edge of the full-size band. Its bottom is $166.12, the support minus 0.4 ATR, where a close below starts to threaten the floor. At the top of the zone the stop is $10.86 away, or 6.28%. The ratio on the base value is -0.20:1, and -0.32:1 on the weighted value. At the bottom the stop is $4.07 away, or 2.45%. The ratio is 1.14:1 on the base value and 0.82:1 on the weighted value. To reach 2.0:1 from the top of the zone, the target would need to be at least $194.63. From the close it would need to be at least $203.63. Both sit above our base and weighted values. The zone top of $172.91 is also the highest price at which any future order would be placed. No buy order is placed now.

The value is $144.00, which is 18.1% under the close. The ratio at the top of the zone is -2.66:1. Second, a gap is a price that opens beyond the stop with no trades in between. A stop order cannot protect against it, and the loss is measured from the first open price. A 10% gap down, to about $158.32, would cost $527.73 on 30 shares. So the next regular-session open is the first price at which a stop could run.[13]

The let-winners-run rule protects uptrends. A stock in a confirmed uptrend, with healthy fundamentals and rising estimates, should not be trimmed only because it looks overbought or its weight has grown. Such a winner may run to 22% of net worth while the stop trails the rising 21-day EMA (exponential moving average, which weights recent days more) or the 50-day SMA. It does not apply here, for three reasons. Qualcomm is in a Stage 3 pullback, not an uptrend. Consensus earnings per share for fiscal 2027 are lower than for fiscal 2026.[22] And the position's gain is 8.39%, which is not a runaway winner.

KEEP_COMPOUNDING needs every gate passed and a Stage 2 uptrend, and this fails on both asymmetry and stage. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP needs asymmetry of 2.0 or more, and this fails. REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER needs a close under the stop, two failed Layer 2 measures or a broken thesis. Qualcomm has one failed measure and a close $13.86 above the stop.
