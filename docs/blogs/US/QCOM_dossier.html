1. What the company does

Qualcomm is a chip designer and a patent licensor. It sells processors, modems and related chips under the Snapdragon and Dragonwing names, and it licenses its patents to the companies that build phones and other connected devices.[1][4] The company reports through two main businesses. QCT, short for Qualcomm CDMA Technologies, is the chip business. QTL, short for Qualcomm Technology Licensing, is the patent licensing business. A third segment, QSI (Qualcomm Strategic Initiatives), makes minority investments, and a Data Center unit sits outside the reportable segments.[4] About 52,000 full-time, part-time and temporary workers were on the payroll at the end of fiscal 2025.[1]

The customer list is short, and that is the first fact to hold in mind. In fiscal 2025, which ended September 28, 2025, Apple, Samsung and Xiaomi each supplied 10% or more of total revenue.[1] Qualcomm says it expects Apple to use its own modem in more of its future phones, which it calls a significant negative for chip revenue.[4] On the July 29, 2026 call, the finance chief said Qualcomm's share of the next iPhone launch would be materially lower than the earlier estimate of 20%. He forecast a fall of about 50% in Apple revenue from the September quarter to the December quarter.[11] A phone maker that designs its own modem buys fewer Qualcomm chips, so this report treats Apple as a shrinking line.

QTL earns money in a simple way. Phone makers pay a royalty on each device that uses Qualcomm patents, usually a percentage of the selling price.[1] The segment is small in revenue and rich in profit. In fiscal 2025 QTL booked $5,582 million of revenue and $4,043 million of EBT, short for earnings before tax, which is a 72% margin. QCT booked $38,367 million of revenue and $11,670 million of EBT, a 30% margin.[1] One licensee was missing from that stream for a long time. The 10-K says QTL revenue has not included royalties from Huawei since the second quarter of fiscal 2025, because Huawei's license agreement expired.[1] On October 5, 2026, Huawei and Qualcomm announced a multi-year, broad patent license agreement that includes cross licenses and Qualcomm's purchase of certain Huawei US patents, and it still needs regulatory approval to close.[18] No royalty amount was published, so the revenue effect is N/A.

Revenue is reported by the headquarters location of the customer, not by where the phone is finally sold. On that basis China, including Hong Kong, was $20,340 million, or 46% of fiscal 2025 revenue. The United States was 24% and South Korea 21%.[1] The 10-Q lists the concentration in China, and the tension between the United States and China over trade and national security, among its main risks. In May 2024 the US Commerce Department revoked the export license under which Qualcomm sold 4G and some other chips to Huawei, and the company expects no more product revenue from Huawei.[4] The next table breaks the chip business into the three streams that drive the story: handsets, automotive and the internet of things (IoT, meaning connected devices from wearables to factory gear), with licensing beside them.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Qualcomm's competitive position rests on three things the filings let us check. The first is a patent portfolio that every major phone maker pays to use. The second is a place in premium phone chips. The third is a car business with long product cycles. The licensing moat is the oldest. QTL earned a 72% EBT margin in fiscal 2025, and EBT of $3,106 million on revenue of $4,252 million, or 73%, in the first nine months of fiscal 2026.[1][7][6][5] That revenue rose 1.9% while handset chip sales fell, which is what a patent stream should do when it does not depend on how many chips Qualcomm sells.[7][6][5]

In premium phones, Qualcomm says Snapdragon powers about 70% of Samsung's flagship devices, which is the only Samsung share figure we found.[11] On September 22, 2026 it launched the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 6, built on TSMC's two-nanometer process, for phones from makers including Motorola, Xiaomi and ZTE.[25] In cars, the company has raised its fiscal 2029 target to $10 billion and cites a $65 billion pipeline of design wins.[9] The July call added that Qualcomm now expects an automotive revenue run rate of about $7 billion exiting fiscal 2026, up from the $6 billion it said last quarter.[11] Carmakers qualify a chip over years, and that long cycle is a barrier to entry for newcomers.

The weaknesses are just as visible, and the numbers are recent. Handset chip revenue fell 20% to $5,086 million in the June 2026 quarter, because phone makers cut build plans after memory prices rose.[5][4] IDC, the research firm, now forecasts that smartphone shipments will fall 16.7% in 2026, the steepest drop on record, with second-half shipments down 27.2% from a year earlier. It cut that forecast from a 13.9% decline one quarter earlier and says memory prices are up more than 300% and are expected to keep rising until at least 2028.[24] Counterpoint Research, quoted by CNBC, expects a 14% drop in 2026 and a further 1% in 2027.[25] Gross margin is the share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of what was sold, and Qualcomm's fell to 53% in the June 2026 quarter from 56% a year earlier.[4] Management says memory and manufacturing costs will keep gross margins slightly below the historical range for now, and that it is raising prices by double digits, which will arrive slowly because some contracts must run out first.[11]

Customer concentration has a second edge: vertical integration, which means a customer builds its own chip. The 10-Q names Apple and Samsung as customers that develop their own chips.[4] If Samsung uses Qualcomm in about 70% of flagship devices, roughly 30% is not Qualcomm's, and the filings do not say how that share will change. Apple is stepping down faster than planned. Qualcomm now says growth in non-handset revenue of more than 60% in fiscal 2027 should replace all of its fiscal 2026 Apple product revenue.[11][5]

Competition in the new Data Center business

In data centers Qualcomm is a new entrant selling against much larger suppliers. Broadcom's quarterly revenue was $29,591 million in the quarter to August 2, 2026, up 85.5%, and AMD's was $11,536 million, up 50.1%, so both are growing in exactly the market Qualcomm is entering.[30][32] Arm, whose designs sit under Qualcomm's own CPU cores, now sells its own chips too. Arm says customer demand for its AGI CPU exceeds $2 billion across its fiscal 2027 and 2028.[33] That makes Arm both a supplier and a rival, which is part of the story behind the court fights described next.

The Arm court fights and the Huawei license

Qualcomm is in two linked court fights with Arm Holdings. In the case Arm began in 2022, a Delaware jury found in December 2024 that Qualcomm did not breach Nuvia's license, and that Qualcomm CPUs using Nuvia designs are licensed under Qualcomm's own agreement. The court entered final judgment for Qualcomm on September 30, 2025, and Arm appealed to the Third Circuit on October 1, 2025.[4] The second case is Qualcomm's own, filed in April 2024. The trial began on October 5, 2026, and on October 13 the jury found that Arm breached its architecture license agreement and intentionally interfered with Qualcomm's business with Meta, awarding $1 on each of those two claims. The jury sided with Arm on the four claims about pricing under a separate technology license.[4][19][21][20] The $2 headline understates the stakes. Judge Maryellen Noreika, not the jury, will decide whether Qualcomm may suspend royalty payments to Arm for up to five years, a remedy Futurum Group estimates at roughly $300 million a year, or about $1.5 billion in total.[21][22] That estimate is the analyst's, not a filed number, and no ruling date has been published, so the date is N/A. The first-case appeal is still pending, so a loss there would put Qualcomm's custom CPU cores at risk.[4]

China, export controls and tariffs

China, including Hong Kong, is 46% of revenue by customer headquarters, the highest share of any country.[1] The 10-Q says US and Chinese trade actions, bans and restricted-entity lists have limited sales to some Chinese customers and suppliers.[4] No tariff cost figure is published, so we show none. On the July call, management said handset revenue from Chinese phone makers hit a bottom in the June quarter and would return to double-digit sequential growth in the September quarter.[11] The September quarter has ended but its results are not yet reported, so Chapter 6 treats that claim as a test due on November 4.

It is not a reason to sell everything, because the licensing and automotive lines still earn a 73% licensing margin and a 25.7% return on capital.

The stop is a trigger, not a cap on loss

The stop at $162.44 acts on a closing price, and the sale happens at the next open. The company reports on November 4 after the close, so a bad report can open the next session well below the stop. On paper the stop costs 17 shares times $20.53, or $349, which is 0.40% of the $88,229.73 net worth. That figure holds only if the shares sell at $162.44, and a gap can break it. Take the report's own bear value of $105.00 as the price at the next open. The 17 retained shares would lose 17 times $77.97, or $1,325.49, which is 1.50% of net worth, or 150 basis points against the 50 basis point risk budget. A smaller gap of 15% to $155.52 would cost $466.65, or 0.53%. The $105.00 figure is a 12-month bear value, not a forecast of one day, and it has a 25% weight, so we treat it as a worst case and not a plan.

Is 150 basis points acceptable for the retained shares? We think so, for three reasons. The trim cuts the same worst case from 2.65% of net worth on 30 shares to 1.50% on 17. The decision point is the November 4 report itself, which Chapter 7 uses to reassess the retained shares. And the retained stake is the part that benefits if the December guide is strong. An owner who will not accept 1.50% can hold 12 shares, which is 1.06% at the bear value, or 9 shares at the 2.0% TIER_4 size, which is 0.80%. Nothing in this report describes the stop as a guaranteed fill.

Red-team bear case: a shrinking core with a call option attached

The strongest case against holding more is that Qualcomm's core is shrinking while its new business is still a promise. Earnings per share on a non-GAAP basis are expected to fall from $12.03 in fiscal 2025 to $10.48 in fiscal 2026 and to $10.19 in fiscal 2027.[8][26] Handset revenue guided for the September quarter is about $5.2 billion, 25% below $6,962 million a year earlier, and Apple revenue is set to fall about 50% in the December quarter.[11][1][5] IDC expects second-half 2026 smartphone shipments to fall 27.2% from a year earlier.[24]

The second part of the bear case is about how the company pays for returns. In nine months it returned $9,674 million against free cash flow of $6,827 million, net debt rose from $2,333 million to $6,966 million, and inventory grew by $1,798 million to $8,379 million.[5][1] Stock pay is 7.4% of revenue, close to the 8% limit, and the Amazon warrant could add up to 25 million shares at $161.26, which is below today's price. Amazon's $60 billion is a ceiling that depends on purchases and not an order.[13] Data Center margins are expected to start low, and Arm, whose designs Qualcomm uses, now sells its own data center CPU with more than $2 billion of customer demand.[11][33]

Our reply is that part of this is priced and part is managed. The reverse model needs only 5.3% growth, the licensing business is steady at a 73% margin, and the Arm jury found Arm in breach, although the remedy ruling is undated and the Third Circuit appeal remains open. The position is also cut to a size where a stop at $162.44 would cost about 0.4% of net worth if it filled at that price, which a gap can prevent, as the next subsection shows.[37][5][19][21] The bear case is a reason to be small, not a reason to sell everything. It also explains why we will not add on strength: the cushion between our middle value and the price is only 0.6%.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING, and the reason is a sizing rule, not a view that the company is broken. TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING is the nearest label, not a clean fit: its usual trigger is an extended price or a hit bull target, and here the trigger is the position ceiling and the failed reward-to-risk. It is not an endorsement to sell the rest. We hold 30 shares, 15 at Schwab and 15 at Robinhood, at an average cost of $162.29, which is 6.22% of a $88,229.73 net worth.[38]

Four terms carry the sizing, so here they are in plain words. A basis point is one hundredth of one percent, so 50 basis points is 0.5% of net worth. CORE_ALPHA is the bucket, or group, for businesses we expect to hold for years, which our policy keeps to about 35% to 40% of the US book. A tier is the size limit for one stock: it is set by how strong the evidence and the business are, and there are four, from TIER_1 at 7% to 10% of net worth down to TIER_4 at 1% to 2%. Layer 3 is the last step of the analysis. It turns the research into an entry zone, which is the band of prices where buying is planned, a stop, and a share count.

Risk-unit sizing, also called portfolio heat, caps how much of net worth a stop-out may cost. It divides a risk budget in basis points by the distance from entry to stop, and the tier limit then caps the answer. TIER_3, the third of the four tiers, allows 2% to 3.5% of net worth and a 50 basis point budget. We chose it for Qualcomm and not TIER_4 because the quality floors pass and the licensing stream is steady. We did not choose TIER_2 because the gate fails on reward-to-risk and the Layer 2 score sits on the 3.0 minimum.

The ceiling for a TIER_3 name is 3.5%, which equals about 17 shares at $182.97. So we would sell 13 shares and keep 17.

Three rules point the same way. First, the position is 178% of its ceiling. Second, the reward-to-risk is 0.05 to 1 at the close and 0.41 to 1 at the top of the entry zone, against a floor of 2.0. Third, the let-winners-run rule, which says never to trim a Stage 2 winner just for being big, does not apply. It requires intact fundamentals and rising or stable estimates, and here estimates are falling and one of five KPIs is a FAIL. That is why a chart that has moved back to Stage 2 does not change the verdict.

The first audit on October 23, 2026 reached the same verdict, with a plan to sell 14 shares at $177 and a stop of $162.05, when the chart was in Stage 3.[39] The position record still shows 30 shares, so nothing has been sold. What changed is the price, up 4.0% to $182.97, which makes the 3.5% ceiling worth about 17 shares and the trim 13 shares. It also adds the October 5 Huawei license, the October 13 Arm verdict and the October 20 job-cut memo, which the first audit's dossier did not discuss. None of these changes the arithmetic of reward-to-risk.[18][19][23]
