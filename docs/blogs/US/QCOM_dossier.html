Before you read

Qualcomm designs the chips in most Android phones and in a growing number of cars, and it collects patent royalties on phones.[1][3] We reject the stock for new money, because our rules want stock pay under 5% of sales and a chart in an uptrend, and Qualcomm has 7.4% and a pullback.[1][34] We keep the 30 shares we hold, with a hard exit if the price closes below $161.60, because licensing is steady, car chip sales grew 61% last quarter, and Amazon signed a warrant deal tied to up to $60 billion of Qualcomm purchases.[1][13] The ledger reads 4.5% yearly growth in cash flow but 9.25% once stock pay counts as a cost, so we hold and do not add.[34] We would be wrong to hold if phone chip sales keep falling about 20% a year and the first data-center revenue, due in the December 2026 quarter, does not appear[11].

1. What the company does

Qualcomm designs semiconductors and licenses patents. Its chips go into phones, cars, laptops, smart glasses and factory equipment. The chip business is called QCT and the licensing business is called QTL.[1][3] In fiscal 2025, which ended September 28, 2025, revenue was $44.3 billion: $38.4 billion from QCT chips and $5.6 billion from QTL licenses.[3][6] A small third arm, QSI, makes strategic investments and reports no revenue.[2]

Customers pay in three ways. Phone makers buy Snapdragon chips, which combine the phone's main processor with its modem, the part that connects to the mobile network. Car makers buy chips for dashboards and driver-assistance systems. Makers of industrial machines, networking gear and consumer gadgets buy a third group of chips labelled IoT, short for the internet of things.[3] Separately, phone makers pay Qualcomm a royalty for the patents inside each phone. The royalty is a share of the phone's price, up to a cap.[11]

Handsets are still the largest line. In fiscal 2025 handset chips brought in $27.8 billion, which is 63% of company revenue, against $4.0 billion from automotive and $6.6 billion from IoT.[3] Customers are concentrated. Apple, Samsung and Xiaomi each gave more than 10% of revenue in fiscal 2025, and the three largest customers or licensees were 21%, 20% and 13% of the total.[3] China, including Hong Kong, was 46% of revenue by customer location, so a trade dispute or a Chinese phone slowdown hits Qualcomm directly.[3]

Licensing is the profit engine. QTL earned $4.0 billion of pretax profit on $5.6 billion of revenue in fiscal 2025, a 72% margin.[6] Its key licenses run to dates between fiscal 2027 and 2031, and Huawei's license has expired and no longer pays.[3] Two things changed in 2026. A memory-chip shortage raised phone costs and cut phone sales, and Apple is replacing Qualcomm modems with its own, so handset revenue fell 20% in the June quarter.[1][2] In the same quarter automotive revenue grew 61% to $1.6 billion, and management now expects an annual run rate of about $7 billion by the end of fiscal 2026.[1][11]

The data-center bet

Management now says data centers are the next leg. At its June 24, 2026 Investor Day it set a target of more than $15 billion of data-center revenue by fiscal 2029, and $40 billion of revenue outside handsets, up from a $22 billion target set earlier.[10][11] It also targets adjusted profit of more than $18 per share in fiscal 2029.[10] Two custom-chip projects for large cloud companies are due to produce revenue from the December 2026 quarter, and management says data-center revenue will reach $5 billion in fiscal 2027.[11] Meta is named as a customer for a data-center processor that starts production in 2028.[12]

On September 3, 2026 Qualcomm issued Amazon a warrant to buy up to 25,000,000 Qualcomm shares at $161.26 each. A warrant is a right to buy shares at a fixed price until a set date. The shares vest in steps as Amazon signs commercial deals, places orders and buys Qualcomm server chips and services, up to a maximum of $60 billion of payments, and 3,750,000 shares vested at once.[13] Qualcomm does not yet publish data-center revenue as a dollar figure. Its quarterly report says the data-center business added $88 million of revenue compared with a year earlier, mostly from the Alphawave acquisition.[2]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest part of the business is the patent portfolio. QTL kept a 72% pretax margin in fiscal 2025 and 69% in the June quarter, and its revenue held near $1.3 billion a quarter while phone sales fell.[6][1] Phone makers sign long licenses, and the key ones run to dates between fiscal 2027 and 2031.[3] That gives Qualcomm a steady cash stream that does not depend on winning each new phone model. The risk is renewal: each expiry is a negotiation, and the 10-K warns that revenue can be lost until a new agreement is signed.[3]

In chips, the edge is low-power computing, meaning chips that run heavy AI and graphics work without draining a battery. Samsung's flagship phones use Snapdragon in about 70% of cases, and BMW named Qualcomm lead supplier of compute chips for its next driver-assistance and dashboard platforms.[11] Car programs take years to win and years to ship, so they are hard for a rival to dislodge. Qualcomm says its automotive design-win pipeline is $65 billion and that automotive revenue should reach $10 billion in fiscal 2029.[10] In the June quarter automotive revenue was $1.6 billion, up 61%.[1]

The weaknesses are concentration and cost. Apple makes its own modem, and Qualcomm expects Apple to use its own parts more over time.[2] On the July call, management said its share of the next iPhone launch would be materially below the 20% it had planned for, and that Apple revenue would fall about 50% from the September to the December quarter.[11] Handsets also suffer from the memory shortage, because phone makers cut chip orders when memory prices raise the cost of a phone.[2] The company-wide gross margin, the share of revenue left after the direct cost of making the product, fell to 53% in the June quarter from 56% a year earlier, because input costs rose.[2] Management says its price increases are in double digits, that the benefit should show in gross margin over the next couple of quarters, and that margins should then be consistent with the historical range.[11]

Competition is wide. The 10-K names Broadcom, MediaTek, Nvidia, NXP, Samsung, Skyworks and others.[3] MediaTek, the main rival in Android phone chips, reported September 2026 revenue up 1.97% from a year earlier.[31] A third-party report says Nvidia's first Windows PCs built on its RTX Spark chip launch in October 2026 with Lenovo and Acer, which competes with Snapdragon laptop chips.[30] The data-center market is led by companies that are already growing fast: in its latest quarter Broadcom's revenue grew 86% and AMD's grew 50%, as chapter 3 shows.[24][23]

The Arm dispute

Qualcomm's custom processor cores are built under a license from Arm, and the two companies have been in court since 2022. In December 2024 a jury found that Qualcomm did not breach the Nuvia license and that its chips are covered by its own license. Arm appealed on October 1, 2025.[2] On October 13, 2026 a second jury found that Arm breached its license with Qualcomm and interfered with Qualcomm's relationship with Meta, but it awarded only $2 in damages.[16] Arm says it won four of the six claims and that the verdict confirms it acted in good faith.[17] A related bench trial, decided by a judge, had closing arguments due the following week.[18] We read the verdict as lowering the chance that Arm ends Qualcomm's license, which is our inference. It pays almost nothing and leaves the 2024 appeal on the Nuvia technology open.

What the watchlist seed claimed, and what we found

The watchlist seed called Qualcomm a dominant wireless connectivity and mobile processor designer that is diversifying into AI PCs, car dashboards and IoT, with a $45 billion automotive backlog. We tested each part. Automotive is real: revenue grew 61% in the June quarter, and the company now cites a $65 billion design-win pipeline, which is signed design wins that have not yet turned into shipments, not a backlog of orders.[1][10] Diversification is real but early: handsets were still 51% of June-quarter revenue.[1] The AI-PC claim is the weakest. Nvidia's RTX Spark Windows PCs, built with MediaTek, start shipping in October 2026 and compete directly with Snapdragon laptop chips.[30] The seed said nothing about stock pay at 7.4% of revenue, the Apple exit or the memory shortage, which are the reasons the verdict is not a buy.

Why the memory shortage matters, and for how long

Management calls the shortage industry-wide and says handset sales this year will be down in the low teens, with Qualcomm's Android revenue down 20% and the profit effect above $1.50 per share.[11] It says China handset revenue hit bottom in the June quarter and will grow in double digits in the September quarter, because phone makers have used up their spare chip stock.[11] That claim will be tested when the September quarter is reported. Inventory at Qualcomm itself rose to $8.4 billion from $6.5 billion at the start of the fiscal year, which management calls a strategic advantage in a shortage and which is also cash that is not yet earning.[11][1]

3. The numbers, in plain words

From fiscal 2021 to fiscal 2025, revenue rose from $33.6 billion to $44.3 billion, a compound rate of 7.2% a year, but the path was uneven: up 31.7% in 2022, down 19.0% in 2023, up 8.8% in 2024 and up 13.7% in 2025.[9][8][3] For the twelve months to June 28, 2026, revenue was $44.1 billion, about 0.5% below fiscal 2025. That figure is our own sum: fiscal 2025 plus the first nine months of fiscal 2026 minus the first nine months of fiscal 2025.[3][1]

Profit needs care. Operating income, the profit left after all running costs and before interest and tax, was 29.2% of revenue in 2021, peaked at 35.9% in 2022, fell to 21.7% in 2023, and was 27.9% in 2025 and 23.2% over the latest twelve months.[9][8][3][1] Net income swings with tax. Fiscal 2025 carried a $5.7 billion non-cash tax charge, and the second quarter of fiscal 2026 reversed it with a $5.7 billion benefit, so the twelve-month net income of $9.3 billion is flattered.[6][4] We read operating income and cash flow first.

A few terms come before the tables. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. EBITDA, short for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, is a rough measure of operating cash profit, which we compute as operating income plus depreciation and amortization. Net debt is borrowings minus cash and marketable securities, and debt-to-equity is total debt divided by shareholders' money. Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money invested in the business. Free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operations minus spending on equipment. The operating margin is operating income divided by revenue. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share.

Several chart and trade-plan terms also need plain definitions before the tests. The Weinstein stage scale names the phase of a stock: Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend, Stage 3 is a topping or pullback phase, and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend. A base is a flat stretch of trading before a move, the pivot is the top of that stretch, and a breakout is a move above the pivot on heavy volume. The relative strength index (RSI) is a gauge from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has moved, where under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing in dollars, and we use it as a ruler for distance. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, or (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut, the entry zone is the band of prices where we would buy, and a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is weaker than the rest. This chapter names it, says what evidence would prove it wrong, and gives the date that evidence arrives. It then sets out the red-team case, which is the strongest honest argument against holding, and three ways the idea could fail outright.

Fatal flaw 1: the handset loss runs deeper and lasts longer

Handsets were 63% of fiscal 2025 revenue and 51% of the June quarter. Management has already cut its Apple outlook: Apple chips will be well below a 20% share of the next launch, and Apple revenue falls about 50% in the December quarter.[11][1] A securities analyst on the call put Apple's fiscal 2026 revenue near $7.5 billion, and management called that a fair range.[11] If the memory shortage lasts through 2027 and Chinese phone makers keep cutting orders, next year's profit could fall by the $1.50 management quantified and more, and the stock would trade near our bear value of $130.35. A close below $161.60 would take us out before that.

Fatal flaw 2: the data-center revenue slips

The upside case needs revenue from two custom-chip projects in the December quarter, a $5 billion year in fiscal 2027, and a working HBC accelerator in mid-2027. Management itself says investors want more proof that a new entrant can deliver.[11] The data center is also expected to reduce QCT gross margin by 1.5 to 2.0 points as it starts, so a delay would bring the cost without the revenue.[11] Broadcom grew revenue 86% in its latest quarter and is far ahead in custom AI chips, which sets the bar high.[24] If the December quarter shows no data-center revenue, the Amazon tranches would also be in doubt and the stock would likely trade on handset multiples.

Fatal flaw 3: legal, licensing and China risks arrive together

Arm's appeal of the 2024 verdict is open before the Third Circuit. Arm asked the court to make Qualcomm stop using, and destroy, technology developed under the Nuvia license, including processor core technology Arm says covers Qualcomm's Oryon cores.[2] The October 13 verdict lowers license-termination risk but did not touch this appeal, which stays open.[16] China, including Hong Kong, was 46% of revenue in fiscal 2025, and two customers were 23% and 20% of revenue in the June quarter.[3][2] Any of these could hit estimates at once, and licenses that expire between fiscal 2027 and 2031 must be renewed on fair terms.[3]

The red-team case in one paragraph

The strongest bear case is that Qualcomm is a handset company with a licensing annuity that is trying to buy its way into AI chips while its core is shrinking. Revenue fell 4% in the June quarter and operating income fell 41%, the gross margin dropped, inventory rose 28%, and stock pay now takes more than 7% of revenue.[1] Next year's profit estimate is below this year's, and analysts have cut it 7.75% in three months.[34] Buybacks and dividends exceeded free cash flow by about $2.8 billion in nine months, which cannot continue without more borrowing.[1] The stock has already fallen 33.8% from its high, but the multiple only looks low if stock pay is ignored.
