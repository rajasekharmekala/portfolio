1. What the company does

RLI Corp. is a specialty insurer based in Peoria, Illinois. It sells coverage that is hard to place or needs unusual skill to price. Examples are liability cover that sits above another insurer's policy, commercial buildings exposed to earthquakes, boats and cargo, homeowners' insurance in Hawaii, and surety bonds, which are promises that a contractor will finish a job or follow a rule. Of 2025 premium written before reinsurance, 60% came from the specialty admitted market ($1.2 billion), 39% from the excess and surplus market ($783 million) and 1% from specialty reinsurance ($23 million).[4] Net premium earned by segment was casualty 60%, property 31% and surety 9% of $1,614.3 million.[4]

Customers rarely buy from RLI directly. The company says it distributes through wholesale and retail brokers, independent agents, carrier partners, underwriting agents and direct digital platforms.[4] It has 1,193 employees, whom it calls associates, with an average tenure of 8.1 years, and it shares ownership through an employee stock ownership plan that pays out in company shares based on profitability.[4] The reason this matters for an investor is simple: the people who price the risk own part of the outcome, and management says it does not give underwriters top-line budgets.[3]

An insurer earns money in two ways. The first is underwriting income, which is premium collected minus claims paid and costs. The second is investment income, because premium arrives first and claims are paid later, so the company invests the cash in between (the float). In 2025 RLI's underwriting income was $264.2 million on a combined ratio of 83.6, and net investment income was $159.7 million.[7][4] In the first nine months of 2026, underwriting income was $176.5 million on a combined ratio of 85.9, and net investment income rose 15% to $135.1 million.[1][2]

The combined ratio is the score to watch. It adds claims and expenses and divides by premium, so anything below 100 is an underwriting profit. RLI's annual combined ratio has stayed between 83.6 and 86.8 for five years.[4] That steadiness is real, but it hides a very uneven mix. In 2025 the property segment earned $219.1 million of the $264.2 million underwriting income (83%) on 31% of premium, while casualty, 60% of premium, earned $15.9 million (6%) and surety earned $29.2 million (11%).[4] Chapter 2 asks how durable that mix is, and Chapter 6 shows what happens to the stock if it slips.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio is claims plus expenses divided by premium earned; below 100 means an underwriting profit.
  • Underwriting income is premium earned minus claims and settlement costs, acquisition costs and operating expenses. It excludes investment income.
  • Excess and surplus lines (E&S) is the market for risks that standard insurers decline; it is less regulated on price and policy wording than the admitted market.
  • Reserves are the money an insurer sets aside for claims it expects to pay on policies already written.
  • Prior-year (reserve) development is the change in those estimates for older years. Favourable development, or a reserve release, adds to profit today.
  • Catastrophe losses are claims from large events such as hurricanes and earthquakes.
  • Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding; for an insurer it is the closest thing to a net worth per share.
  • Accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI) is the unrealised gain or loss on bonds held for sale; it moves book value but not net earnings.
  • Operating earnings are net earnings without investment gains and losses; RLI also excludes income from its minority investee Prime.
  • Surety is a guarantee that someone else will perform an obligation, such as completing a building contract.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

RLI's competitive strength is a habit more than a product. It has produced an underwriting profit for 30 consecutive years, has raised its regular dividend for 51 consecutive years, and has returned more than $1.3 billion to shareholders over five years through dividends and buybacks.[1] In February 2026 AM Best raised the financial strength rating of the RLI group to A++ (Superior) from A+, citing balance sheet strength, historically positive reserve development, strong underwriting controls and a quality reinsurance program.[20] A rating like that matters in specialty insurance because brokers place large or unusual risks only with carriers they trust to pay.

The sceptic's version of the same facts is that RLI is a good underwriter in a business with few lasting advantages. Insurance is a product that buyers can swap on price. The 10-K says about 2,600 companies sell property and casualty cover in the United States, and management says competitors from managing general agents to standard carriers are adding capacity.[4][3] Over the five years to 2025, RLI's annualised total return to shareholders was 8.1%, against 14.4% for the S&P 500 and 18.0% for the S&P 500 property and casualty index.[4] Quality of the business and return to the owner are different things, and the second depends on the price paid.

Where the edge is real

The first real edge is discipline about premium. The Chief Operating Officer says RLI does not set top-line budgets for underwriters and lets business leave when pricing misses return targets.[3] The record fits that claim: in 2025 gross written premium grew 1% while the combined ratio fell to 83.6, and in the third quarter of 2026 gross written premium grew 2% on an 86.2 combined ratio.[7][1] A company that chased growth would show the reverse pattern.

The second edge is a well-protected balance sheet. The 10-K models a one-in-100-year hurricane at a gross loss of $416 million but a net loss of only $50 million, and a one-in-100-year California earthquake at $295 million gross and $31 million net, against shareholders' equity of $1.78 billion at the end of 2025.[4] For 2026 RLI bought $150 million less catastrophe limit because it had cut its exposure and the market was softening.[8] Statutory surplus, the capital regulators measure at the insurance subsidiaries, was $1.96 billion at September 30, 2026, up 6.4% from year-end.[1] Debt is 14.9% of capital and the nine-month interest cover (earnings before tax and interest, divided by interest) is about 35 times, our calculation from the filing.[1][2][31] This is why Chapter 3 finds no solvency problem.

The third edge is the investment portfolio. Fixed income is 73.4% of invested assets, averages AA- in credit quality and has a duration of 4.6 years, and new purchases in the third quarter earned an average yield of 5.5%, nearly 100 basis points above the book yield.[2][3] That reinvestment gap is why net investment income grew 13% in the quarter while premium grew 2%.[1] It is the best-supported source of earnings growth in the story.

Where it is thinner than it looks

The first weak point is how much of the profit comes from one segment and one source. Property delivered 86% of nine-month 2026 underwriting income ($152.1 million of $176.5 million) with a combined ratio of 59.6, and casualty delivered 6% with a combined ratio of 98.7.[2][31] Property is the line now under the most pressure. Excess and surplus property premium fell 17% in the third quarter, management says renewal pricing fell by double digits on second renewals, and its rates are about back to 2022 levels.[3] A 60 combined ratio is exceptional, and it is also the kind of margin that attracts new competitors.

The second weak point is reserve releases. In the first nine months of 2026, favourable development on prior-year reserves added $98 million to pre-tax profit, against $74 million a year earlier, and it was 55% of the $176.5 million of underwriting income.[2][31] Take that out and the nine-month combined ratio is about 93.7 rather than 85.9, compared with 90.1 in 2025 by the same method, our calculation.[2][31] In casualty the releases ($36 million) were larger than the segment's whole underwriting income ($10 million), so before releases casualty ran at about 103.4, and the 10-Q reports adverse development in personal umbrella.[2][31] Reserve releases are real accounting profit and have appeared every year, but they do not come from this year's sales.

The third weak point is growth. Premium written rose 3% in nine months, with strength in personal umbrella (up 22% in the quarter, rates up 8%) and a $9 million start from a new landlord-insurance partner, offset by E&S property down 17%, E&S casualty down 10% and commercial transportation down 9%.[3][2] Growth that depends on a few lines is fragile, and umbrella carries the adverse development just mentioned.

A softening market, not yet a soft one

Insurance runs in cycles. In a hard market, prices rise and capacity falls. In a soft market, prices fall as capital floods in. Management's own description of the present state is that RLI is still in a softening market and that it is not a soft market, and that it can still bind business at rates and terms it is comfortable with and earn a double-digit return on.[3] In January the Chief Operating Officer warned that friendlier reinsurance renewals could soften the market further.[8] Cheaper reinsurance helps RLI's own costs for 2027, and management expects a positive result at the January 1 renewal.[3] It also hands every competitor the same relief.

Our reading of the moat is therefore narrow. The moat is underwriting judgement, a reputation that lets RLI price risk, and a balance sheet that survives a bad year. It is not a cost or technology advantage, and it gives little pricing power once capacity returns. What this means for an owner is that the company is likely to keep making underwriting profits through a soft cycle, which it has done for three decades, but earnings per share will probably fall as releases and property margins normalise. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Insurance accounts need a different reading from a factory's. Revenue includes investment gains and losses that have nothing to do with selling policies. Net earnings move with stock markets, and book value moves with bond prices. Free cash flow is not meaningful because customers pay premium before claims are settled, so operating cash flow rises whenever the company grows and falls when claims are paid. We therefore use premium earned, operating earnings per share, the combined ratio, book value per share and debt as a share of capital. Three cases appear in this report. The base case is our most likely outcome, the bull case is a better one and the bear case is a worse one.

The headline in the third-quarter release is a book value increase of 10% from year-end 2025. The balance sheet in the same release shows book value per share of $18.49 against $19.35, a fall of 4.4%.[1] Both are consistent. The $2.00 special dividend and $0.52 of ordinary dividends were paid in between, and management's 10% adds back dividends and buybacks. By our arithmetic, adding back dividends alone gives 8.6%.[3][31] We use the balance sheet figures throughout, and we note where management's headline differs.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

The red-team bear case is the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion. Our conclusion is that RLI is a good company at a full price, so the red-team argument has two parts. The first is that the price is too high even for a good company: 3.45 times book value and 23 times 2027 earnings for a business whose written premium is growing 3%. The second is that the quality itself is overstated: more than half of underwriting profit comes from releasing old reserves, and the lines that are growing are the ones with weaker margins.

The bear also has a record to point to. The stock fell from $82.42 at the end of 2024 to $55.31 at September 30, 2026, a 33% decline, and the five-year annualised return of 8.1% trails the market's 14.4%.[9][1][4] Three flaws could each be fatal to the thesis, and each can be tested with data in the next two quarters.

Fatal flaw 1: the profit is partly borrowed from old reserves

RLI books claims reserves for every policy and later revises them. When it revises down, profit rises. This has happened every year in our data: $106.5 million in 2022, $95.3 million in 2023, $84.1 million in 2024, $87.4 million in 2025 and $98 million in nine months of 2026.[11][10][9][7][2] If those releases were a sign of cautious reserving, they will continue. If they were a sign that current-year loss picks are too low, the releases will shrink and later reserve reviews could even turn adverse.

The warning sign is casualty. Casualty releases of $36 million exceed the segment's $10 million of underwriting income, personal umbrella has had adverse development, and management says it is holding loss picks for umbrella until it has more data.[2][3] Before releases, casualty's combined ratio is about 103.4. If releases stop, 2027 earnings fall to about $2.25 and the stock would be at 28 times that figure, not 22.[31] This is the flaw that could take the stock to our bear value of $45.50.

Fatal flaw 2: the property margin is competed away

Property is 31% of premium and 86% of nine-month underwriting income.[4][2][31] The profit rests on a 59.6 combined ratio in a line where E&S property premium fell 17% in the third quarter (total property premium fell about 1%), renewal prices fell double digits on repeat accounts, hurricane rates were down 15% and earthquake rates down 12% in the fourth quarter of 2025, and management says rates are about back to 2022 levels.[3][8] New managing general agents and standard carriers are offering larger limits than RLI will.[3]

Property earns about $500 million of premium a year (nine months of 2026: $376.7 million). A combined ratio of 70 instead of 59.6 on that premium would cut property's underwriting income by roughly $52 million, about 16% of the $327 million of 2027 pre-tax operating earnings in our base case, our calculation.[1][31] The tail event is a big catastrophe. The 10-K models net losses of $50 million for a one-in-100-year hurricane and $31 million for a one-in-100-year California earthquake, which is small against $1.78 billion of equity.[4] That limits a disaster but does not protect against a slow squeeze.

Fatal flaw 3: book value is more sensitive to markets than the rating suggests

AM Best praised RLI's balance sheet and also flagged its equity-heavy investing and its earthquake exposure.[21] Equity securities are $948.3 million, 19.3% of invested assets and 56% of shareholders' equity, and bonds carried an unrealised loss of $291.8 million at September 30.[2][31] A 20% fall in the equity portfolio would cost about $1.63 a share after tax, or 8.8% of book value, our calculation. Book value per share fell 4.4% in nine months even though the company earned $303.9 million, because of the special dividend and rising interest rates.[1][31]

Higher yields also help, because new money is invested about 100 basis points above the book yield at 5.5%.[3] The two effects offset in income but not in book value. A stock priced at 3.45 times book is more exposed to a fall in book than one priced at 1.5 times. Debt, now $297 million after the March notes, adds to this.[2] Leverage is modest at 14.9% of capital, but the company chose to raise it while paying out capital, and the next downturn will test that choice.
