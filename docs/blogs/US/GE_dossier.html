1. What the company does

GE Aerospace designs, builds and repairs jet engines. Its engines power an installed base of about 50,000 commercial and 30,000 military aircraft engines, and servicing that base is roughly 70% of revenue.[1] The company used to be General Electric, a conglomerate. It spun off GE HealthCare on 3 January 2023 and GE Vernova, the power business, on 2 April 2024, and what remains is a pure aerospace company.[2] General Electric also did a 1-for-8 reverse stock split that took effect on 2 August 2021, so price quotes from before that date are one-eighth of their split-adjusted equivalents.[13][20] The price history behind this report has 501 daily bars, about two years, so it starts after the April 2024 spin-off and needs no further adjustment for that event.

Two kinds of customers pay GE. Airplane makers such as Boeing and Airbus buy new engines, which GE books as equipment revenue. Airlines, leasing companies and maintenance shops buy spare parts and shop visits, which GE books as services revenue. In the first nine months of 2026, equipment revenue was $10,794 million and services revenue was $26,739 million.[1] A shop visit is the scheduled overhaul of an engine taken off an aircraft. Engines need one every few years for as long as they fly, which is why the service business lasts for decades.

The business model is like a printer company that sells the printer cheaply and earns its profit on the ink. In the nine months to September 2026, GE booked $10,794 million of equipment revenue against $11,049 million of equipment cost, a gross loss of about 2.4%. The same period had $26,739 million of services revenue against $14,642 million of services cost, a gross margin of about 45.2% (our calculation).[1] Signed service contracts add to the picture: of $212,084 million in remaining performance obligations (RPO) — contracted revenue not yet delivered — $178,747 million is services, and only 12% of that services backlog is due within one year, so the revenue is spread over many years.[1]

GE reports two segments. Commercial Engines & Services (CES) holds the airliner engines, mainly the LEAP and the GEnx and GE90 wide-body engines, and their aftermarket. LEAP is built by CFM International, a 50/50 joint venture with Safran of France.[4] Defense & Propulsion Technologies (DPT) makes military engines such as the F414 and F404, plus the Avio Aero and Unison propulsion and additive-manufacturing businesses. CES supplied $28,992 million of the $37,533 million of adjusted revenue in the first nine months of 2026, or 77%, and DPT supplied $10,126 million.[1][4]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position rests on the installed base. GE says it has the industry's largest fleet, about 80,000 engines, with more than 2.3 billion flight hours of operating history.[6] An airline cannot swap one engine maker's parts into another's engine, and the repair data and parts supply sit with the maker. That is why shop-visit work flows back to GE and its partners for decades.

The evidence for demand is in the order book. Total remaining performance obligations were $212,084 million at 30 September 2026, up 11% from $190,564 million at the end of 2025.[1] Commercial services orders rose 23% in the third quarter and 30% over nine months.[6] For the full year management now expects 2026 adjusted revenue growth of about 19–20% and, for 2027, low-double-digit commercial services growth.[4][5]

Defense adds a second leg. DPT's remaining performance obligations rose 22% to $31,431 million, and defense book-to-bill (new orders divided by revenue, where above 1 means the order book is growing) was 1.0 in the third quarter and 1.5 for the year to date.[1][6] The segment's margin is lower than commercial, at 13.1% for nine months 2026, so defense steadies growth more than it lifts profit.[1]

Several weaknesses are real. New engines are sold below cost, which means fast equipment growth presses on margins. LEAP service margins still sit below the fleet average, and management expects them to match the total services portfolio only by 2028.[7] The GE9X, GE's engine for the Boeing 777X, is expected to add about $300 million of extra losses in 2027 compared with 2026, and management expects 2027 margins to stay in line with 2026.[6] Supply is the other limit: the 10-Q says supply chain constraints will keep affecting production and deliveries, and that inflation will persist.[1]

Tariffs, fuel prices and airframe production rates

On tariffs, the 10-Q says the United States agreed zero-for-zero aerospace tariffs with the EU, UK, Japan and Korea in 2025, and that after a 2026 Supreme Court ruling on International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) tariffs, GE requested refunds and received part of what it had paid.[1] Tariff cost is therefore easing, not rising, in the filings we read.

Fuel is the bigger worry today. On the October call an analyst put Brent crude at $111, and Safran's chief executive said kerosene had doubled from its pre-crisis level.[6][18] GE's own commentary is that airlines are keeping aircraft flying and that retirements of the older CFM56 engine are running at about 1.5–2% of the fleet, below the 2–3% it expected at the start of the year.[6]

For airframe makers, GE said Boeing has more than 30 engine sets on and off the wing and that GE is working to help Boeing reach a rate of 10 aircraft a month for the 787.[6] We did not find a quoted Boeing 737 or Airbus A320 monthly rate in the pages we retrieved, so we state none.

Disclosed legal and safety matters

The 10-Q names two significant legal matters. The first is borrower litigation at Bank BPH, a Polish mortgage portfolio now in discontinued operations, where GE estimates total losses of $1,658 million, down from $2,334 million at year-end 2025.[1] The second is environmental and worker-exposure claims, with reserves of $2,222 million.[1]

The Air India crash of 12 June 2025 (Flight AI-171, a Boeing 787-8) is the one safety matter in the public record. It happened in 2025, and the Indian investigation bureau said on 12 June 2026 that its final report would follow international review.[29] A press report from July 2026 says the draft final report is expected in October 2026, and that the delay came from analysis of engines sent to the United States.[30] We searched the text of the 2025 annual report and the third-quarter 10-Q and found no mention of the accident, so no filing quantifies a liability. That makes the draft report a dated event risk, not a measured one, and we draw no conclusion about its cause.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. We do not buy at the close of $285.64 (the current market price, or CMP). We allow a limit order, which fills only at our price or better, with a ceiling of $283.00 and a floor of $278.37. The rule behind the verdict is the technical routing rule: only a Stage 4 chart disqualifies a good business, and a stock in a weak chart that passes the business tests is routed to a limit-order zone. The triggers that change the decision are a fill in the zone, a full Test C pass, or a trade at or below $269.24.

Several terms need plain definitions before the levels. Portfolio heat is the share of net worth we would lose if the stop were hit. Risk-unit sizing sets the position so that this loss stays inside a risk budget: weight equals the smaller of the tier cap and the budget divided by the stop distance. A tier is a conviction rank that sets that cap and budget; TIER_1 is the highest, and TIER_0 means zero size. We use TIER_3 as the yardstick here, which allows 2% to 3.5% of net worth and a 50 basis-point budget (a basis point, bps, is one hundredth of a percent), because the business is excellent but the chart and price are not.

The support used is $278.37, the 63-day base low and 20-day swing low, which was set by the 23 October session itself.[16] The ledger puts the close 0.80 ATR above it, in the standard band of the support-distance ladder (0.6 to 1.2 ATR). The stop rule for a Stage 1 base is the base low minus 1.0 ATR, which gives $278.37 - $9.13 = $269.24. The Stage 2 rule uses the lower of the swing low and the 50-day simple moving average (SMA, the average closing price over a set number of days) minus 1.0 ATR, which gives the same $269.24, so we do not need to choose between them. This is a structural invalidation stop, meaning the level where the idea that the base holds is wrong. We apply it as one rule: a stop order that sells the whole position when the stock trades at or below $269.24, during the session. We do not wait for a close. A wider alternative stop under the 52-week low ($268.91 minus 1.0 ATR) would be $259.78.

Reward-to-risk (asymmetry) is the target minus the entry, divided by the entry minus the stop. We set the order ceiling at $283.00, which is lower than the $283.85 edge of the full-size band (278.37 + 0.6 x 9.13). The reason is the stress test below. At the ceiling the ratio is (328 - 283.00) / (283.00 - 269.24) = 3.27 to 1, with a stop distance of 4.86% of the entry. At the zone floor of $278.37 it is 5.44 to 1, with a stop distance of 3.28%. At the CMP it is 2.58 to 1 with a 5.74% stop distance. On the probability-weighted value of $318.50 the ratios are 2.58 (ceiling), 4.40 (floor) and 2.00 (CMP). The probability-weighted ratios are lower than the central ones because $318.50 is below the $328 target. With the wider $259.78 stop, the central ratio at the ceiling falls to 1.94, below the 2.0 floor, so we use the tighter stop and accept a higher chance of being stopped out.

Stress tests of the ratio. The nominal gate is the 2.0 floor on the nominal weights, and the entry clears it. A sensitivity is a different question: does it still clear if our probabilities are wrong? At the old $283.85 ceiling a 20/50/30 split gave $311.60 and only 1.90 to 1, which fails. The ceiling and floor work like this. A limit order fills at the first price at or below $283.00, so it fills inside the zone in nearly every case. It can fill below the $278.37 floor only if the stock gaps down through the whole zone. We therefore place the order for one session at a time, and only after a same-day check (next paragraph). If the stock trades below $278.37 before the order fills, cancel it and rerun the report, because the support has failed. If a gap fills it below $278.37 anyway, the stop of $269.24 stays as the exit, we add nothing, and the report is rerun before the next session. The series of daily orders ends on 31 December 2026, a date we chose, and is then cancelled and rerun.

Same-day check before any fill. On the day of the order, before it is entered, confirm five things. First, the price is inside the zone or no more than 1% above the ceiling, and has not traded below $278.37. Second, the momentum gate and trend are rechecked on that day's data, including the exact Stage 4 condition. Third, support, ATR, stop and size are recomputed from that day's prices, and the stop distance and ratio are still at or above the floor at the ceiling. Fourth, the event calendar is clear: no earnings report within about 10 trading days, and no GE9X decision or Air India report landing that week. Fifth, cash is available as described below. If any item fails, do not place the order, and cancel any order already working.

Gap risk and the exit. A stop does not cap a gap.
