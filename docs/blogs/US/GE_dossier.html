1. What the company does

GE Aerospace designs, builds and repairs jet engines. Its engines power about 80,000 aircraft in service, and the company says that fleet has flown more than 2.3 billion hours.[15] Roughly 50,000 of those engines are commercial and 30,000 are military.[1] Until 2024 the business sat inside General Electric, a conglomerate. The shares of GE Vernova, the power business, were distributed to shareholders on 2 April 2024, and what is left is one aerospace company.[1] The company also still carries a run-off insurance book and a mortgage portfolio in Poland (Bank BPH), which are legacy items rather than part of the engine business.[3]

Think of it as the razor and the blade. The company sells a new engine to an aircraft maker, often at thin margins or even at a loss, and then earns money for 20 to 30 years as the engine comes back for overhauls (called shop visits) and spare parts. In the third quarter of 2026 the company booked $3,924 million from selling equipment and $9,361 million from selling services, so services were about 70% of equipment-plus-services sales.[3] Services are also 84% of the $212.1 billion of contracted work still to be delivered ($178.7 billion of it), which is why the order backlog matters more than any single quarter.[3]

Who pays? Aircraft makers such as Boeing and Airbus pay for new engines. Airlines, aircraft lessors and independent repair shops pay for overhauls and spare parts. Governments pay for military engines, and defense is the smaller segment: $3,469 million of the $13,285 million adjusted revenue in the third quarter, with a 13.7% profit margin against 27.9% in the commercial segment.[6] A large part of the commercial engine business runs through CFM International, a 50/50 joint venture that builds the CFM56 and the newer LEAP engine.[6] GE is also the only engine supplier on Boeing's 777X, with more than 1,300 GE9X engines on order.[15]

The business rises and falls with flying. More flights and longer trips wear engines faster, which brings them into the shop sooner. In the third quarter, departures were up only about 1% while widebody flying grew faster, and management expects departures to grow again in 2027.[15] The new-engine side follows aircraft production, and management says aircraft makers' order books are full through the end of the decade.[15] The main outside shock right now is fuel: jet fuel in the US Gulf Coast region has almost doubled to $4.34 a gallon from $2.19 a year earlier since the Iran war began in February, and Delta cut its 2026 profit outlook because of it.[25] Demand itself is strong: Delta's September-quarter operating revenue was $20.2 billion at a 7.2% operating margin.[26] Fuel does not hurt GE directly, but it can make airlines trim schedules, which slows the work that feeds GE's services profit.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Aftermarket: the repair, overhaul and spare-parts work done after an engine is sold. It is where most of GE's profit is made.
  • Shop visit: an engine comes off the aircraft and goes into a workshop for overhaul. Each visit is a large, scheduled sale.
  • LEAP: the newer narrow-body engine built by CFM International for the Airbus A320neo and Boeing 737 MAX. It is young, so most of its repair revenue is still ahead.
  • GE9X: the engine for the Boeing 777X. GE loses money on each early unit and cannot book the full revenue until the FAA certifies an upgraded seal.
  • Long-term service agreement: a contract in which GE services an airline's engines for many years at agreed prices. These make up most of the services backlog.
  • Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. When shares are bought back, EPS rises even if profit does not.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after the business pays for its plant and equipment. It is the money available for dividends, buybacks and debt.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the core business collects. Capital expenditure is the cash spent on plant, equipment and software that last for years. Free cash flow is the first minus the second.
  • Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. A high number means each dollar of capital earns a lot.
  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is a rough measure of the cash a business earns from operations.
  • Net debt is borrowings minus cash. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given to staff in shares, which is a real cost to owners.
  • Operating margin is operating profit divided by sales. Guidance is management's published forecast for the year ahead. Book-to-bill is new orders divided by sales in the same period; above 1.0 means the backlog is growing.
  • Buyback is when a company buys its own shares, which shrinks the share count.
  • Asymmetry and reward-to-risk are two names for the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss. A ratio of 2 to 1 is $2 of reward for every $1 of risk, measured from the buy price to the target and to the stop.
  • The entry zone is the price range in which we would place a buy order. Stage 2 is the rising phase of a stock's price cycle, and Stage 4 is the falling phase.
  • A base is a flat stretch of trading that forms a floor. A breakout is a move above its top, and the pivot is the price level that marks that top.
  • Adjusted versus GAAP: GAAP is the standard accounting result; adjusted figures strip out the insurance run-off, gains on investments and one-off costs. The report shows both and says which one it uses.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence of a moat is the installed base. About 80,000 engines are in service, and every one of them will need parts and overhauls from the company that designed it.[15] Engines are certified on one aircraft type for many years, and an airline cannot easily swap one maker's engine for another. The LEAP powers the Boeing 737 MAX and many Airbus A320neo aircraft, and the 777X uses only the GE9X.[15][1] The contract backlog shows the result: $212.1 billion of remaining performance obligations, against $190.6 billion at the end of 2025.[3]

Pricing power is the second pillar. In the third quarter, commercial segment profit rose 21% on higher services volume and price, and spare-parts revenue grew more than 20%.[6] Spare parts are scarce across the industry, and the CFO said the company entered the fourth quarter with about 95% of its revenue in backlog, so customers wait for GE rather than shop around.[15] The moat is not airtight. CFM International renewed an agreement with the International Air Transport Association that commits it to an open aftermarket for the CFM56 and LEAP, which leaves room for independent repair shops and parts sellers.[9] Management also notes that spare-part delinquency, meaning late deliveries to customers, rose in the third quarter.[15]

The weaknesses are real and specific. New engines are a drag: management expects the GE9X to cost about $300 million more in 2027 than in 2026, and it still cannot recognize revenue on GE9X units shipped with the upgraded seal until the FAA certifies it, which it hopes happens by the end of 2026.[15][3][16] The LEAP is still young, and a LEAP shop visit brings in about half the dollars of a CFM56 visit today, so profit growth from LEAP depends on the fleet ageing.[15] Supply is the other constraint. Priority suppliers have raised material input for ten quarters in a row, but quarterly LEAP deliveries grew only 4.7% in the third quarter (535 against 511 engines) after 26% growth over nine months.[15][3]

On balance the business is strong and its competitive position is secure. The weak points show up in margins and timing, not in demand. Chapter 2B scores five operating measures one by one, and Chapter 3 tests whether the financial record agrees with them.

Where the moat could wear thin

A moat is only as good as the customer's alternatives. For commercial engines, the company says airlines may have a choice between its engines and those of other manufacturers, and a large peer, RTX, appears in the peer table in Chapter 3.[1][27] For defense, the main risk is budget timing rather than a competitor, because most defense revenue flows through the US Department of War budget or equivalent foreign budgets.[3] A final risk is concentration in two aircraft makers: Boeing and Airbus are the main customers, and a Boeing production problem would delay engine shipments even if airline demand stayed strong.[1]

5. What could happen next

A price is a bet on the future. A reverse discounted cash flow model (reverse DCF) works backward from today's price to find the growth rate the market is already counting on. If that rate is high, the stock can disappoint even when the company does well. We ran it with the scoring file's parameters for US stocks: a 10% discount rate, a terminal value of 18 times free cash flow (FCF), and a ten-year projection. The cash flow is the 12-month operating cash flow less capital spending, $7,992 million, and the price is the market value of $311.2 billion (1,033.35 million shares at $301.13).

Our own result is that the price assumes FCF growth of 14.3% a year for ten years, which would lift FCF from about $8.0 billion to about $30.5 billion by 2036. That is a high bar. Analysts forecast 18.56% a year of EPS growth over three years and 10.63% a year of revenue growth, so the bar is close to the three-year forecast, but it has to hold for ten years, not three.[20] The ledger holds no reverse DCF or consensus block for GE, so the two consensus rows in the table below read N/A. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 can fail, and here we name the weakest assumption in each, say what evidence would show the failure, and say when that evidence arrives. We also state the strongest honest case against buying, which is the red-team view, and then the single claim that carries the most weight. The sections that follow take the estimates in order.

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF

The weakest assumption is the cash flow base. The reported operating cash flow of $9,425 million includes working-capital swings: in 2026 progress payments and contract liabilities have been a source of cash, and if customers pay less in advance, cash would fall short of earnings.[3][15] The model also excludes the $11.75 billion CPP deal, which will use cash and add debt. The evidence that would show a problem is free cash flow conversion falling below 100% in the fourth quarter, and we will know when the full-year number arrives in late January 2027.

Weakest assumption in the EPS estimate

The weakest assumption is that services grow at a low double-digit pace on flat margins. Management said the low double-digit number carries some level of conservatism, but fuel at $111 a barrel and trimmed airline schedules argue for caution.[15] The evidence is the fourth-quarter report and 2027 guidance, due in late January 2027. A second weak point is the tax rate: 2026 guidance assumes about 16%, and a higher rate would cut EPS directly.[15] A third is CPP, which is not in the EPS: its financing cost could take up to about $0.46 a share and its profit is unpublished.

Weakest assumption in the 30 times multiple

The multiple is the weakest of all, and the 2.0 to 1 floor depends on it. It needs the market to keep treating GE as a premium compounder while the 10-year Treasury yield, which was 4.99% at the 27 October close in the ledger's macro block (the ledger's 28 October close for the same series is 5.05%), stays high and the group is de-rating.[20] Howmet's next earnings date is 29 October, which the vendor page calls confirmed, and TransDigm's is 11 November, which the vendor page calls estimated.[29][28] Weak reports from either would move the group multiple before GE reports. A fall to 28 times removes the entire base-case gain.

The red-team bear case

The strongest case against buying starts with the multiple. HEICO at 44.8 times lifts the four-peer mean to 31.8. Without it the mean is 27.5, and the median of the four is 29.1. At the median the target is $290.5, and the reward-to-risk ratio at the zone top is only 1.19 to 1. At 27.5 times the target is $274.7, below the zone.[27][28][29][30] The red-team stress case combines that 27.5 times with a $0.50 EPS miss: 27.54 times $9.475 is $261, a fall of 6.4% from the $278.75 zone midpoint. We have added it to the scenario table at a 25% weight.

The second point is the stock's reaction. It fell 3.7% after beating on four quarters straight, which tells us the beats are expected and the next one must be larger to matter.[23][22] Add the fuel shock, which has doubled airline fuel costs, and a $11.75 billion acquisition in the second half of 2027 that cash and new debt must fund, while cash has already fallen to $8.8 billion.[25][3] The balance sheet also carries $34.1 billion of insurance liabilities that standard leverage measures leave out, and 71% say-on-pay support suggests owners are uneasy about management pay.[3][13] If both fuel and the multiple move against GE, the bear value is $209, a 31% fall from the close and 25% from the zone midpoint, and a gap through the stop could lose more than planned.

Reaching $279.00 needs a 7.3% fall from $301.13, and a fall that large is likely to arrive with a peer report or a group de-rating, which is exactly the event that lowers the target. The zone bottom of $278.50 is also only 13 cents above the $278.37 swing low, so a fill at the bottom and a move of 14 cents lower would break the support.
