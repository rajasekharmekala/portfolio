1. What the company does

Vistra sells electricity in two ways, and the two halves feed each other. First, it owns the plants. The fleet totals 43,641 megawatts: 26,989 MW of natural gas, 8,743 MW of coal and lignite, 6,448 MW of nuclear, 1,274 MW of solar and batteries, and 187 MW of fuel oil[1]. Second, it sells power to people. Its retail brands, led by TXU Energy, serve about 5 million customers in 16 states and Washington, D.C., including about 2.6 million in Texas[1]. A household pays a monthly bill. A power grid operator pays for electricity delivered and, in the eastern United States, for a promise to keep plants available, which is called capacity.

Here is how the money moves. Vistra sells output from its plants into wholesale markets, mainly ERCOT in Texas and PJM in the Mid-Atlantic and Midwest. It locks in prices ahead of time through hedges, which are contracts that fix a price today for power delivered later. By early August 2026 it had hedged about 100% of 2026 output, about 94% of 2027 and about 72% of 2028[6]. Retail works as a natural offset, which the company calls a fundamental advantage of its integrated model[1]. When wholesale power gets expensive, the plants earn more but retail margins squeeze, and the reverse is also true. Retail is seasonal: management says the second and fourth quarters are typically the strongest for retail[22]. The nuclear plants also earn a federal production tax credit of up to $15 per megawatt-hour from 2024 through 2032, which puts a floor under their earnings[5].

Revenue was $17,738 million in 2025 and about $19,212 million over the twelve months to June 2026 (the 2025 figure plus the first half of 2026 minus the first half of 2025)[1][5]. The company's own yardstick is Ongoing Operations Adjusted EBITDA, which strips out accounting gains and losses on hedges that have not yet settled. That figure was $5,912 million for 2025 and $3,261 million for the first half of 2026, up from $2,589 million a year earlier[6][8]. The gap between this number and reported net income matters. In 2025 net income was only $944 million, because $808 million of the hedge loss was on paper and expected to reverse when the hedges settle[8].

Growth now comes from five moves. Vistra bought 2,600 MW of gas plants from Lotus in October 2025[8]. It agreed in January 2026 to buy Cogentrix, about 5,500 MW of gas plants, for a net price of about $4.0 billion; the deal is waiting to close, expected late in 2026 after FERC approval in August[12][5]. It signed 20-year contracts to sell nuclear power: 1,200 MW from Comanche Peak to a large investment-grade customer (the company's results release names Amazon Web Services) and 2,609 MW from three PJM plants to Meta[20][8][19]. It is building two gas units of 860 MW in West Texas[8]. And it committed up to $1.0 billion to Helix, a data-center infrastructure company formed with KKR, NVIDIA and the Kuwait Investment Authority, where Vistra is the preferred power supplier[21][6].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The first strength is scale with mixed fuels. Vistra says it runs the second-largest competitive nuclear fleet in the country[10]: 6,448 MW across four sites[1]. Nuclear plants run almost all the time, so they earn money when the grid is tight. In the first half of 2026 the PJM-region nuclear units ran at a 91.0% capacity factor, meaning they produced 91.0% of the maximum possible output, and the Texas units ran at 92.9%[5]. Gas plants add flexibility, and the coal and lignite plants add another 8,743 MW of dispatchable capacity.

The second strength is contract visibility. Long contracts change how much of the nuclear output is sold at market prices. The Comanche Peak agreement covers 1,200 MW for 20 years with delivery starting in the fourth quarter of 2027 and reaching full size by 2032[20]. The Meta agreements cover 2,609 MW: 2,176 MW of existing output from Perry and Davis-Besse, plus 433 MW of future uprates (added output from upgrading the units)[19]. Together that is 3,809 MW, equal to 59% of today's nuclear fleet capacity. The company says the operating part adds about 8% to 10% to free cash flow before growth, and the uprates another 5% to 7%[19]. These are company claims. They are not yet visible in the financial statements, since deliveries start between late 2026 and 2027, and the CEO has said the uprate work is likely to come after 2028[36].

The third strength is that the price has been supported by capacity auctions. In July 2026 Vistra cleared 10,924 MW in the PJM auction for the 2028/2029 delivery year at $325.00 per megawatt-day[18]. FERC accepted an extension of the price cap and floor in April 2026, so the auction price is bounded on both sides for now[25]. A cap protects customers, but it also caps Vistra's upside, so the benefit is stability, not growth.

The weaknesses are just as concrete. Vistra is a price-taker: it cannot set the price of power, and the market has been moving against its Texas business. On the August call the finance chief said ERCOT forward prices were meaningfully lower than the ones behind the 2027 outlook, and that 2027 adjusted EBITDA would trend toward the lower end of the $7.4 to $7.8 billion range[22]. Debt is also high. Reported borrowings were $19,595 million at 2026-06-30 against $435 million of cash[5], with liquidity of $6,295 million and the commodity-linked credit line extended to 2027-09-29 on 2026-09-30[6][14], and Cogentrix plus the redemption of $1.0 billion of preferred stock add to that[12][15]. Finally, the political risk is rising: Texas has effectively paused new data-center approvals while it audits the queue[26].

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 shows what the company earned and spent over five years, then tests it against the Layer 1 rules. Two accounting facts shape every figure. First, Vistra reports GAAP profit, which includes gains and losses on hedges that have not settled yet. When the two disagree, this report weights cash flow and adjusted EBITDA for trend, and GAAP operating income for return on capital.

The first table covers 2021 to 2025 and the twelve months to 2026-06-30. The 2022 loss came mainly from unrealized hedging losses that reversed in 2023[11].
