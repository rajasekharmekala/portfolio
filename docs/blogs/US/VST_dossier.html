1. What the company does

Vistra is a power company based in Irving, Texas. It owns about 44,000 megawatts (MW) of plants. It sells the power two ways: to retail customers under brands such as TXU Energy, and to the wholesale grid.[1] The retail arm serves about 5 million customers. About 2.6 million of them are in Texas. In 2025 it sold 139,139 gigawatt-hours (GWh) of power.[1] The fleet burns natural gas, lignite and coal. It also runs six nuclear units at four sites, 6,448 MW in all. Comanche Peak is in the ERCOT grid and the other three sites are in PJM.[1] Vistra says it runs the second-largest competitive nuclear fleet in the country. We did not rank the industry ourselves.[9]

Vistra earns money three ways. Retail margin is the gap between what customers pay and what the power costs. Energy margin is what the plants earn when they sell at market prices. Capacity payments are fees that grid operators pay plants for being ready when demand peaks. PJM, which runs the grid across 13 states and the District of Columbia, is one such operator.[17] To protect cash flow, Vistra sells most of its future output in advance. This is called hedging. On August 3, 2026 it had hedged about 100% of 2026 volumes, about 94% of 2027 and about 72% of 2028.[6] Power prices rose after many hedges were placed. Accounting rules therefore make Vistra book paper losses on them today. Paper hedge losses of $808 million are one reason 2025 net income was only $944 million while adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) was $5,838 million.[1] Interest, depreciation and tax explain the rest. This report always says which of the two numbers it uses.

The newest growth story is long contracts with very large power users. In September 2025 Vistra signed a 20-year power purchase agreement (PPA) for 1,200 MW from Comanche Peak. A PPA is a fixed-term contract to sell electricity. The company's 8-K calls the buyer a large investment-grade company. The 10-K names it as Amazon Web Services (AWS). Delivery starts in the fourth quarter of 2027 and reaches full volume by 2032.[11][1] In January 2026 Vistra signed 20-year PPAs with Meta for 2,609 MW from its PJM nuclear plants. That includes 433 MW of planned output increases, called uprates.[12] Vistra says the Comanche Peak deal adds about 8% to 10% to adjusted free cash flow before growth. Free cash flow is the cash left after running the business and buying assets. The Meta deals add about 8% to 10% for existing output and 5% to 7% for the uprates.[11][12] The contract prices are not published, so we cannot check those figures. A 200 MW Oak Hill solar plant in Texas also carries a PPA with AWS.[8]

Most growth so far has come from purchases. Vistra bought Energy Harbor on March 1, 2024. That added three nuclear sites and about one million retail customers.[1][9] In October 2025 it bought 2,600 MW of gas plants from Lotus. On December 31, 2025 it agreed to buy Cogentrix, ten gas plants of about 5,500 MW. The net price is about $4.0 billion after expected tax benefits. That is about 7.25 times the 2027 adjusted EBITDA the company expects Cogentrix to add.[1][13] Vistra Vision is the legal entity that holds the combined nuclear and retail businesses and some renewable and storage plants.[1] The filings give no stand-alone Vistra Vision EBITDA, so we cannot isolate its share. The East segment holds the PJM nuclear plants. Its adjusted EBITDA rose from $1,001 million in 2023 to $2,017 million in 2024 and $2,282 million in 2025.[9][1] In June 2026 Vistra agreed to sell three gas plants. It also committed up to $1 billion to Helix, a KKR-managed fund that invests in artificial-intelligence infrastructure.[5]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Adjusted EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, adjusted by the company for paper hedge gains and losses and other items. It is a non-GAAP measure, meaning US accounting rules do not define it.
  • Adjusted free cash flow before growth (FCFbG) is Vistra's own measure of cash from operations. It leaves out working-capital swings and margin deposits and is after maintenance spending but before growth spending.
  • Capacity factor is the share of a plant's maximum possible output that it actually produced. A nuclear plant that ran all year with no outage would score 100%.
  • Unrealized mark-to-market is the paper gain or loss on hedges that have not yet settled. It moves net income but not cash today.
  • Nuclear production tax credit (PTC) is a federal credit of up to $15 per megawatt-hour for existing nuclear plants. Vistra sells it for cash.
  • Reverse DCF is a calculation that starts from today's price and solves for the yearly growth rate that would justify it.
  • ERCOT and PJM are the grid operators for most of Texas and for 13 states plus the District of Columbia. Each runs its own market rules.
  • Reward-to-risk is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, measured from the planned entry price.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Vistra's strongest asset is the mix of plants and customers under one roof. The nuclear units are licensed into the 2040s and 2050s. Comanche Peak Unit 1 runs to 2050 and Unit 2 to 2053. Perry runs to 2046 after a July 2025 renewal.[1] Vistra says its PJM contracts support a further 20-year license extension for all four PJM nuclear units.[8] The plants run well. Nuclear capacity factors in 2025 were 95.4% in Texas and 90.8% in the East. In the summer 2026 heat the company reported commercial availability of 97% or more across the fleet.[1][6] In 2025 the Texas and East segments made 52,262 GWh from nuclear, about 110,625 GWh from gas and 42,178 GWh from lignite and coal (our sums of the segment tables). Nuclear was about a quarter of that output.[1] Nuclear plants also earn the federal production tax credit. That credit fell from $545 million in 2024 to $220 million in 2025.[1]

Contracts and hedges make cash flow steadier than the stock's swings suggest. Hedges cover about 94% of 2027 volumes.[6] The AWS and Meta deals put 3,376 MW of existing nuclear output under 20-year contracts. That is 1,200 MW at Comanche Peak plus 2,176 MW at Perry and Davis-Besse. It is 52% of the 6,448 MW nuclear fleet (our calculation).[11][12][1] PJM capacity prices have sat at a cap. They were $269.92 per MW-day for 2025-26, $329.17 for 2026-27, $333.44 for 2027-28 and $325.00 for 2028-29. Vistra cleared 10,924 MW in the 2028-29 auction.[1][17][5] At the cap that is about $1,296 million a year of gross capacity revenue. That is our calculation: 10,924 MW times $325 times 365 days. It is not a profit forecast.

The weak points are leverage, policy and execution. PJM's price cap and floor is called a collar. It covers four auctions, and 2028-29 was the third. The next one, for 2029-30, is scheduled for December 2026. The PJM release we read describes no cap for auctions after that.[17] PJM cleared 6,831 MW short of its reliability need. That supports prices today. PJM also planned to ask FERC to approve a one-time backstop buy of new supply in September 2026.[17][18] In Texas, power at the ERCOT North Hub averaged $32.01 per MWh in 2025, up from $25.89 in 2024. Senate Bill 6 of 2025 makes some large users next to plants cut load in emergencies. That can limit what a plant can promise a data center.[1] On execution, the January 2025 Moss Landing battery fire and the November 2024 Martin Lake Unit 1 fire cost capital and output. Martin Lake Unit 1 returned to service in February 2026.[1]

Nuclear plants carry licence and fuel risk of their own. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) can modify, suspend or revoke licenses and impose civil penalties, and new NRC rules could raise spending.[1] On fuel, Vistra says it has nuclear fuel contracted for all refueling needs through 2030 without any additional Russian deliveries.[1] Neither risk is large today. Both are the kind that matter only once they happen. Peers tell a similar story. Constellation guided to adjusted operating earnings of $11.50 to $12.50 per share for 2026. Talen guided to adjusted EBITDA of $2,025 million to $2,225 million. NRG reaffirmed its 2026 guidance and is pursuing a 1.2 gigawatt gas plant for a data-center customer in Texas.[24][26][25] Vistra's 2026 adjusted EBITDA guidance is more than three times Talen's. Vistra also owns a large retail arm, so it carries weather and retail-margin risk that a pure generator does not. A skeptical owner should check each quarter whether the next big contract goes to existing plants like these or to new supply built for the buyer.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Our method has three layers. Layer 1 tests solvency and financial floors (Chapter 3). Layer 2 scores the business on the measures that matter for its own industry (this chapter). Layer 3 sets entry timing and size (Chapter 7). Net debt, which is interest-bearing debt minus cash, is one of the measures. For a power producer the Layer 2 measures are plant uptime, how much output is sold ahead, how much is under long contracts, how much debt it carries, and how steady the retail arm is.

Each measure has a PASS, WATCH or FAIL boundary written before we looked at the number. PASS is 1.0 point, WATCH 0.5 and FAIL 0.0, and we score the unrounded value. The result is 3 PASS and 2 WATCH. That is 4.0 points out of 5.0 with no FAIL. The Layer 2 rule asks for at least 3.0 out of 5.0 with at most one FAIL. The layer therefore passes with watch items, labelled PASS_WITH_WATCH. The two watch items are linked. The company is buying assets with debt while retail earnings dipped in the first half. A weak second half would hit both at once.

Fatal flaw 2: financing and collateral stress

Debt-funded growth is tolerable at 2.94 times on basis B and uncomfortable above 3.2 times, which is our rejection line. The company issued $2.25 billion of notes in January 2026. It issued $1.5 billion of junior subordinated notes in September 2026. Retained proceeds do not raise net debt, because cash rises with debt. Spent proceeds do.
