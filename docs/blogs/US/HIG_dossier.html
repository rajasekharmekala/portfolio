1. What the company does

The Hartford is the holding company for a group of insurers whose oldest unit, Hartford Fire Insurance Company, was founded in 1810.[8] It sells three things. It sells insurance to businesses, from corner shops to multinational firms. It sells car and home insurance to families, including a program for members of AARP. And it sells group benefits to employers, such as disability, life and paid-leave cover for their staff.[8][2] About 19,200 people work there, and in 2025 the company booked $28.4 billion of revenue.[8][5] The company is no longer called The Hartford Financial Services Group: it was renamed in February 2025, and the ticker is unchanged.[24]

Business Insurance is the biggest piece. It earned $13.9 billion of premium in 2025, which is 57.8 percent of the company's $24.0 billion of earned premium, against $6.4 billion (26.7 percent) for Employee Benefits and $3.7 billion (15.5 percent) for Personal Insurance.[8][5] Premium is the price a customer pays for protection, and earned premium is the part of it that has been used up as time passes. How an insurer makes money

An insurer earns money two ways, and the second is easy to forget. The first is underwriting: collect more in premium than is paid out in claims and costs. The standard measure is the combined ratio, which is claims plus operating costs divided by earned premium, so a ratio below 100 is an underwriting profit. For the whole property and casualty business the ratio was 81.1 in the third quarter, which looks superb but includes a one-time $497 million reserve gain; the underlying ratio, which strips out storms and old-reserve changes, was 89.6, so each $100 of premium left about $10.40 before investment income.[2][1]

The second source is investing. Premiums arrive first and claims are paid months or years later, so the company holds claim reserves of $47.7 billion and invests the money in the meantime.[2] That pool is called float. In the third quarter, net investment income was $850 million before tax, up 13 percent from a year earlier, on invested assets of $64.7 billion.[1] Investment income matters here because it did not come only from interest: $156 million of it was profit from private funds, called limited partnerships, which is more than the $91 million of a year ago and is the least predictable line in the income statement.[1]

Who pays, and how the product reaches them

Most business customers buy through independent agents and brokers, who shop several insurers and earn a commission. That makes the agent a second customer: if The Hartford is slow to quote or priced too high, the agent sends the business elsewhere.[8] The company's answer in small business is speed. Management says 78 percent of small-business submissions can now be bound with no human touch, and that a survey firm ranked its small-business digital experience first for the eighth year in a row, with a 20-point lead.[14] Small business had 1.73 million policies at September 30, up 5.4 percent from 1.64 million a year earlier, and 84 percent of them renewed.[2] Personal Insurance sells through a direct channel and through independent agents, and the agent channel is the growth plan: agency written premium was $227 million in the quarter against $189 million a year ago, while direct premium fell.[2]

Employee Benefits sells to employers through brokers. It carries a different kind of risk from property insurance. A disability claim can last for years, so the insurer must predict how many workers will claim and how long they will stay out. Fully insured premiums were $1.66 billion in the quarter, and more than 90 percent of the book renewed.[1]

What the company does with its profit

The Hartford earns more than it needs to keep its insurers well capitalized, so it returns the excess. In 2025 it returned $2.2 billion, of which $1.6 billion was share repurchases and $592 million dividends.[4] In the third quarter it returned $638 million, and the board raised the quarterly dividend 12 percent to $0.67 a share, payable January 5, 2027 to holders of record on December 1, 2026.[1] Over the nine months to September 30 the company bought back $1.4 billion of stock (10.1 million shares), and $4.37 billion of authorization remains, which is about 12.5 percent of the current market value of $34.9 billion (the close of $130.04 times 268.3 million shares).[3][2][30] A buyback is a company buying its own shares, so the share count falls and per-share profit grows faster than total profit.

What changed in 2026

Three events reshape the company. First, in June The Hartford agreed to sell Hartford Funds, its mutual-fund unit, to Wellington Management. The sale is worth an estimated $1.9 billion in present value, of which $300 million is cash at closing and the rest is paid out of the combined business's cash flows over seven years; the deal was expected to close in the first quarter of 2027, though management said in October that it could close as early as December 1.[10][15] Hartford Funds now sits in discontinued operations, so core earnings no longer include it, and 2025 profit figures in this report are shown both ways where it matters.[3] Second, on October 13 the company closed the purchase of Equitable's employee-benefits business, which adds about $500 million of annual premium; the price was not disclosed.[12][13] Third, the board named President A. Morris Tooker to succeed Christopher Swift as chief executive on March 1, 2027, with Swift becoming executive chair.[11]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio: claims plus operating costs divided by earned premium; below 100 means an underwriting profit.
  • Underlying combined ratio: the combined ratio with storm losses and changes to old reserves removed, so it shows a normal year.
  • Written and earned premium: written premium is the price of policies sold in a period; earned premium is the part of it used up as time passes.
  • Catastrophe losses (CATs): losses from named events such as tornadoes, hail, hurricanes and wildfires.
  • Prior accident year development: the change in the estimated cost of claims from earlier years. Favorable means earlier estimates were too high.
  • Float: premiums held and invested between collection and claim payment.
  • Core earnings: profit after tax before realized investment gains and losses and one-off items such as reinsurance gains; core earnings ROE divides it by average equity excluding unrealized bond gains and losses.
  • Book value per share excluding AOCI: equity per share before the paper gains or losses on bonds, which swing with interest rates.
  • Limited partnerships (LPs): private funds the insurer invests in; their returns are lumpy and reported with a lag.
  • Workers' compensation: insurance that pays medical bills and lost wages when an employee is hurt at work.
  • Reinsurance and adverse development cover: insurance that insurers buy so another company pays part of a loss; an adverse development cover pays if old claims cost more than reserved.
  • Asbestos and environmental (A&E) reserves: money set aside for old liability policies that still produce claims decades later.
  • Reverse DCF: a discounted cash flow worked backwards to find the growth rate that today's price already assumes.
  • Price-to-book: the share price divided by book value per share; a common yardstick for insurers.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A good insurer is one that can write business at a profit through a full price cycle, not only in the good years. The test has three parts: does it price risk better than rivals, are its reserves honest, and can it survive a bad year without damaging its capital. The Hartford passes the first test in small business, passes the third clearly, and is on a watch list for the second. That is why the Layer 2 score in the next chapter is a pass with watch items, and not a clean pass.

The strongest evidence of an edge is small business. The segment wrote $5.5 billion of premium in 2024 and, in management's words, has extended a decade-long run of annual underlying combined ratios below 90.[17] In the third quarter its underlying combined ratio was 85.2, which is 4.6 points better than a year earlier, and the 20-point digital lead and the 78 percent no-touch rate explain how it keeps costs low.[1][14] The edge is real but it is not unlimited: management said that new business in small commercial went flat for the first time in roughly twenty quarters, because rivals are competing harder.[14]

The second strength is the balance sheet. The insurers carry A+ ratings from A.M. Best, AA- from S&P and Aa3 from Moody's, all on stable outlook.[2] Debt is $4.4 billion, or 16.1 percent of capital excluding unrealized bond losses, and the earnings-to-fixed-charges ratio is 23 to 1.[2] The bond portfolio is high quality: 73 percent of fixed maturities are US government, AAA, AA or A rated, 22 percent are BBB, and under 5 percent are below investment grade.[2] Private funds are $6.4 billion, or 9.8 percent of invested assets, which is where the volatility sits.

Where the competitive position is thin

The first weakness is the price cycle. Across the industry, commercial price increases are slowing. The Hartford's Business Insurance renewal price rose about 4.0 percent in the quarter (5.3 percent excluding workers' compensation), down 40 basis points from the prior quarter, and management said it does not see the stability that some rivals describe.[14] Peers show the same picture: Travelers' net written premium was flat, Chubb's North America commercial premium fell 2.7 percent (up 1.7 percent excluding large-account property and some underwriting changes), Cincinnati's grew 2 percent and W. R. Berkley's grew 1.3 percent.[25][26][27][29] When prices rise more slowly than claim costs, margins shrink, and The Hartford's Business Insurance underlying ratio has already moved from 88.1 in the fourth quarter to 90.1 in the third.[2]

The second weakness is Personal Insurance auto. Policies in force fell to 967,000 from 1,091,000 a year ago, down 11 percent, because the company raised prices about 14 percent in 2025 to repair margins and is now facing intense competition for new customers.[2][14] The margin repair worked: the auto underlying combined ratio was 96.0 in the third quarter against 97.9 a year earlier, and homeowners ran at 71.5.[1] But a shrinking auto book means the segment cannot grow its way into higher profit, and the agency channel that is meant to replace lost direct sales is still small.

The third weakness is a leadership change: Christopher Swift, who ran the company for nearly 13 years and led its turnaround, hands over on March 1, 2027.[11] The board's choice, Mo Tooker, has been president and says strategy is unchanged, so continuity is likely, but a new chief executive in a softening market is a risk we weigh in Chapter 6.

Reserves: how honest are the numbers?

Reserves are the insurer's estimate of what claims it still has to pay, and they are the most judgment-heavy number in the accounts. The Hartford's record has two blemishes. In the fourth quarter of 2024 it strengthened general liability reserves by $130 million and said it believed it had addressed the trend.[17] In 2026 it added another $70 million in the first quarter and $46 million in the second, $116 million in all, and it also added $26 million for commercial auto liability in the second quarter.[2] On the other side, it released $167 million of workers' compensation reserves in the first nine months of 2026, even as it reports a rise in workers' compensation claim frequency.[2][14] Management says its third-quarter review found no need to change booked reserves.[14]

Asbestos and environmental reserves are the old tail. Net A&E reserves were $1.56 billion at September 30.[3] In September the company settled its dispute with a Berkshire Hathaway insurer over its adverse development cover: it cut the recoverable from $1.437 billion to $1.120 billion, received $1.12 billion in cash, and booked a $497 million before-tax gain that is excluded from core earnings.[3][1] The cover had already been used up, since a $165 million A&E increase in the fourth quarter of 2025 went through core earnings after it, so the commutation changes the timing of cash more than the risk.[4] For an owner the point is that reported net income of $4.59 billion over the last twelve months is a poorer guide to earnings power than core earnings of $3.80 billion.[2]

How The Hartford compares with its peers

Against the five peers in Chapter 3, The Hartford has a mid-range return on equity (17.9 percent against 14.9 percent at Chubb and 22.1 percent at Travelers) and the lowest price-to-book after Cincinnati and Chubb.[1][25][26] Its mix is broad: a small-business franchise, an employee-benefits arm and a personal-lines book under one roof. Diversity helps in a bad year for one line. It also means the company is rarely the best at any single thing that investors screen for, such as the combined ratio at Chubb or Travelers.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Three standard Layer 1 tests do not fit an insurer, so we use the financial-company equivalents that the scoring rules allow.[31] The first is ROIC (return on invested capital), which we replace with return on equity. The second is the free cash flow margin. It means little here because an insurer collects premium before it pays claims. The Hartford's operating cash flow was $5.19 billion in nine months, but part of that cash is owed to future claimants.[3] A third test uses EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) to measure net debt, which is debt minus cash. That is not how insurers are judged. We use debt as a share of capital and interest cover instead. We also quote EPS (earnings per share), which is profit divided across all shares.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a weak assumption, and this chapter takes the three that matter most. For each one it states the assumption, the evidence that would show it is wrong, and when we will know. This is the red-team case against our own view. Where we say the business is sound, it argues the business is deteriorating; where we say the price is fair, it argues the price is too high.

Assumption 1: the commercial margin is stabilizing

The weakest assumption is that the workers' compensation jump is a one-time step in 2026. Management says frequency has risen across industries, regions and injury types, which fits a systemic cause such as healthcare costs, and it says it will work price into 2027 filings.[14] But management says it is constrained by the filing process in small business, workers' compensation prices outside California are only slightly positive, and the Business Insurance renewal price has already slowed to 4 percent.[14] If frequency keeps rising, 4 points of loss ratio on a book the size of middle and large is a long-running cost, not a one-quarter catch-up.

What would show it wrong: the Business Insurance underlying ratio above 90.5 in the fourth quarter, middle and large above 97, or another workers' compensation reserve increase. We would know at the fourth-quarter results (early February 2027 on the company's usual pattern; a third-party date of February 4 could not be confirmed from a fetchable page) and the first-quarter results in April 2027.[4][2]

Assumption 2: core earnings are a clean base

The second weak assumption is that $3.80 billion of core earnings is a clean base, with nothing in it that will not repeat. Two items argue it is flattered. About $0.58 a share over nine months comes from private-fund income above last year's pace, and the company released $167 million of workers' compensation reserves while claims frequency rose.[2] General liability and commercial auto needed $142 million of strengthening in 2026, which suggests the casualty reserve position is not as comfortable as the releases imply.[2] Hartford Funds, which contributed about $200 million of core earnings a year until it was reclassified, is also leaving. The $300 million of cash at closing and later payments over seven years are the replacement.[10][5]

What would show it wrong: a private-fund yield below 5 percent in the fourth quarter, reported core favorable development turning negative outside A&E, or a larger asbestos charge. The A&E reserve of $1.56 billion has no cover left, so any increase falls straight into earnings.[3] We would know with the fourth-quarter results and the annual reserve study.

Assumption 3: the multiple holds near its median

The third weak assumption is that investors keep paying about 9.7 times earnings and 1.9 times book for an insurer whose price increases are slowing. In 2022 and 2023 the stock was valued at 1.76 and 1.58 times book at year-end, and it traded at 8.5 times forward earnings at the 2023 year-end.[23][19] With the 10-year Treasury at 4.99 percent, a competitive commercial market and a softening price cycle, investors have alternatives.[30] The buyback also depends on price: at about 1.5 times book it adds value, and at 2.0 times book it adds less.

What would show it wrong: price-to-book below 1.5 times (about $104 on reported book value), a stock that stays below the 200-day average through the fourth-quarter results, or an announced slowdown in repurchases after the Equitable purchase and the Hartford Funds proceeds. We would know with each quarterly report and the January reinsurance renewals.

The decision is REJECTED. The rule is the technical routing rule in our scoring file.[31] A Stage 4 breakdown is a Layer 1 disqualifier, whatever the quality of the business. The 50-day average is $133.15 and fell 3.0 percent in 21 days. The 200-day average is $135.39. A second rule leads to the same label. Our floor for a Core compounder is 2.0 to 1. The ratio is the gain to the base value divided by the loss to the stop.[31] The base value is $132.80 and the stop is $117.75. At the $130.04 close the ratio is 0.22 to 1. The entry zone is the band of prices where we would buy. We set ours from $120.50 to $123.50, which is the support low plus about 1.2 ATR.[30] At $123.50 the ratio is 1.62 to 1. It reaches 2.0 to 1 only at $122.77 or lower. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and that is the only way we would buy here. Here is which label each route gives. The Stage 4 label gives REJECTED. The asymmetry floor on its own also gives REJECTED, because our routing rule sends a stock to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT only when it clears the floor at its entry zone, and The Hartford does not (1.62 to 1 against 2.0 to 1). So the label is REJECTED either way, and the action is the same: no capital today. The price is also in the no-buy band, 3.62 ATR above support.

On bucket and archetype, the watchlist row said TACTICAL_MOMENTUM. That label came from the peer-discovery seed.[32] We change it to CORE_ALPHA with the COMPOUNDER archetype. A tactical position needs momentum. The Hartford fails all three momentum tests, and its relative strength is -13.2 percent.[30] The case is a multi-year bet on a 17.9 percent return on equity and on book value growth, which fits the core bucket. The 2.0 to 1 core floor therefore applies. The stop goes below the 63-day base low minus one ATR.[31] That is $120.42 minus $2.66, or $117.76, which we round to $117.75.

The next table is Layer 3, the part of the process that decides how much to buy, where to buy and where to get out. It uses a tier, which is a conviction grade from TIER_1 (highest) to TIER_4. TIER_0 means no position. Each tier has a risk budget and a size range in the scoring file.[31] A basis point is one hundredth of a percent. A risk budget of 50 basis points means we accept losing 0.5 percent of net worth if the stop is hit. Position size is the risk budget divided by the distance to the stop, capped at the tier maximum. The Hartford is TIER_0, so its weight is zero and the table commits no capital.

An example shows the arithmetic. Suppose a later review grades the stock TIER_3, which has a 50 basis-point budget and a size range of 2 to 3.5 percent, and buys at $123.50. The stop is 4.66 percent away. A budget of 0.5 percent divided by 4.66 percent gives 10.7 percent. The TIER_3 maximum is 3.5 percent, so the size would be 3.5 percent.[31] In the table, the stop rule is quoted in the scoring file's shorthand. In plain words, the stop sits one ATR below the 63-day low for a base, or below the lower of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average for an uptrend. For The Hartford we use the 63-day low of $120.42 minus $2.66. The sizing line means the smaller of the tier cap and the budget divided by the stop distance.

The last row is the let-winners-run rule. A winning position is not trimmed only because the RSI passes 70 or the weight passes 15 percent. We trail the stop to the rising 21-day or 50-day average, and allow the weight to grow to 22 percent. The Hartford is not held, and the positions file shows no holding (net worth $83,424.97).[33]
