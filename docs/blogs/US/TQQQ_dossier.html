1. What the company does

TQQQ is the ProShares UltraPro QQQ. An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a basket that trades on a stock exchange like a share. ProShare Advisors runs TQQQ, and its stated goal is three times (3x) the daily performance of the Nasdaq-100 Index, before fees and expenses.[1][2] If the index rises 1% in a day, the fund aims to rise 3%. If the index falls 2.50%, as the QQQ fund that holds it did on October 16, 2026 (close $709.80), the fund aims to fall 7.50%. TQQQ closed down 7.60%, a shortfall of 0.10 points against that target.[12][15][17][18] The promise covers one day only. In the issuer's own words, the fund does not seek 3x for any period other than a day.[2] The fund began trading on February 9, 2010. Its May 2026 financial statements list one share split, a 2-for-1 on November 20, 2025, and no reverse split, so every price in this book is split-adjusted.[12][6]

The fund does not hold the index as a plain basket of shares. It gets most of its 3x exposure from total-return swaps, which are contracts with banks in which the bank pays the fund the index's gain or loss in return for a financing fee.[2] At May 31, 2026 the fund had open swaps with ten banks covering $101.94 billion of notional exposure (the face amount the contracts are measured on), plus 8,655 Nasdaq-100 E-Mini futures contracts with $5.26 billion of notional value.[6] Net assets were $39.75 billion on that date, and the notes to the statements put derivative notional at 247% of net assets.[5][6] The rest of the exposure comes from direct holdings of the index's largest stocks and a money-market fund.[13] Each evening the fund resets its exposure to 300% of its net assets. A simple picture: a driver whose cruise control is reset every evening to three times the speed reached that day. The reset is what makes the fund useful for a one-day bet and costly for a long hold.

The buyers are traders who want a magnified one-day bet on large technology and growth shares. ProShares charges a management fee of 0.75% a year. With other expenses the total is 0.94%, and a contractual waiver of 0.16 points cuts it to 0.78% through September 30, 2027.[2] The annual report's cost table also shows a net expense ratio of 0.78%. It prints a dollar figure of $134 for a $10,000 investment beside it, and the two do not match (0.78% of $10,000 is $78), so we rely on the 0.78% ratio, which the prospectus fee table confirms.[5] The fund's own web page and its June fact sheet still describe the waiver as running through September 30, 2026, so those two documents are older than the September 28, 2026 prospectus.[1][7] Two larger costs sit outside that table. The first is the swap financing charge. The floating rates on the ten swaps ran from 4.47% to 4.92% at May 31, 2026, and they are paid on the borrowed two thirds of the exposure.[6] The second is volatility drag, explained in Chapter 2. Our own measurement from prices: over the last 250 trading days TQQQ returned 31.19%, while compounding the daily 3x target would have returned 44.41%. That shortfall of 13.2 points is 9.15% of the compounded value, and it covers fees, financing and tracking gaps (our arithmetic).[17][18]

The index behind the fund is the Nasdaq-100: 100 of the largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq, weighted by market value. Its list is re-evaluated every December, and weights are adjusted in March, June and September.[2] Nasdaq's factsheet for September 30, 2026 shows 101 securities, a technology weight of 68.01% and a top ten that adds up to 46.86% (our sum), led by NVIDIA at 8.36%, Apple at 7.38% and Microsoft at 5.78%.[9] QQQ closed at $709.80 on October 16, 6.96% below its 52-week high of $762.86.[15][45] This book treats TQQQ as the framework requires for leveraged funds: it studies the index, the price trend and the decay risk, and it does not analyse the fund as a company. The measures this book uses

Several measures come up again in later chapters, so here they are in plain words. The relative strength index (RSI) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has been moving, and below 30 is called oversold. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing in dollars. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash a business has left after paying for equipment. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is operating profit before borrowing costs, taxes and the wear on equipment. Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money put into the business. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given to staff in shares. Net debt is debt minus cash. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share.

The chart terms are these. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. A base is a flat floor that a price builds before a move, and the pivot is the top of that floor. A breakout is a move above the pivot. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better. An entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the potential gain divided by the potential loss: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). A measured move is a target taken from a chart distance, such as a return to a recent average. A consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Leveraged ETF — a fund that aims to return a multiple (here 3 times) of one day's move in an index.
  • Daily reset — the fund rebuilds its 3x exposure at every close, so gains and losses compound day after day.
  • Swap — a contract with a bank that pays the fund the index's gain or loss in return for a financing fee.
  • Counterparty — the bank on the other side of a swap. If it fails to pay, the fund can lose the gain it is owed.
  • Volatility drag — the slow loss a daily-reset fund suffers when the index swings up and down, even if the index ends where it began.
  • Net asset value (NAV) — the value of everything the fund owns, per share, calculated at the close.
  • Nasdaq-100 — the index of 100 large non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq that TQQQ follows; QQQ is the Invesco fund that holds it.
  • Notional — the face amount a swap or futures contract is measured on, which is much larger than the cash the fund puts up.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A fund has no brand or factory, so its strength is how well it does the one job it promises, and how it compares with rivals doing the same job. On both counts TQQQ is sound and large. It is the only ETF that targets 3x the daily return of the Nasdaq-100, with $37.06 billion of assets on the data page against $14.66 billion for the 2x version (QLD), $7.18 billion for Direxion's 3x S&P 500 fund (SPXL) and $5.61 billion for ProShares' 3x S&P 500 fund (UPRO).[7][12][19][20][21] Its stated expense ratio of 0.78% is lower than all three of those rivals (0.84% to 0.89%), and far above the 0.18% of QQQ, the fund that holds the index without leverage.[12][15][19][20][21] It also trades about $3.89 billion of shares a day over the last 63 sessions (our arithmetic), more than eight times the next-busiest 3x fund in our peer set.[17][24][25][26] Its edge over rivals is size, liquidity and cost, not a different product.

The index it follows has a record of strong earnings, but the fund is only as good as three habits: it must match the daily target, it must survive a falling market, and its owner must understand that the daily reset eats value when the index zigzags. The sections below take each in turn.

Strength: the fund does what it says each day

The strongest evidence is the daily match. On October 14, 15 and 16, QQQ moved -1.97%, -0.13% and -2.50%, so a perfect 3x fund would have moved -5.90%, -0.39% and -7.50%. TQQQ moved -5.95%, -0.40% and -7.60%.[17][18] Over the last 250 trading days the average daily gap from the 3x target was -0.04 percentage points, the average absolute gap was 0.07 points and the worst gap was 0.73 points, on June 22, 2026. The daily correlation with the index was 0.9996 and the slope of the fund's daily moves against the index was 2.97, close to the promised 3. These are our measurements from closing prices.[17][18] The issuer reports the same result: for the year to May 31, 2026, the fund had an average daily statistical correlation of over 0.99 to three times the index return.[5] A fund that tracks this closely has no hidden tracking problem. Whatever loss an owner suffers comes from the design, not from sloppy execution.

Weakness: the daily reset eats value when the index is choppy

The prospectus states the weakness plainly. Over any period longer than one day the fund's return can be higher or lower than 3x the index, and the issuer says this difference may be significant.[2] Its own table shows a fund that loses 17.1% in a year when the index ends flat at 25% volatility, and 52.8% when the index ends flat at 50% volatility, before any fees.[2] Volatility here means how widely the index swings up and down day to day, measured as an annual percentage. QQQ's volatility was 19.84% over the last 250 sessions, 18.33% over the last 20 and 21.82% over 500; the index's five-year volatility to May 31, 2026 was 22.60%.[18][2] At 19.84%, the daily reset costs about 11.1% a year when the index ends flat (the factor e to the power of minus three times the volatility squared, our arithmetic), and the swap financing and fees cost about another 9.15% a year. FINRA warned in 2009 that daily-reset funds typically are unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them for longer than one trading session, and the SEC chair repeated the concern in 2021.[42][43]

Real prices show what that does. From October 17, 2025 to October 16, 2026, QQQ rose 17.53% (price only) and TQQQ rose 31.19%. Simple tripling of the index gain would give 52.59%. Compounding each day's tripled return gives 44.41%, so compounding drag was 8.2 points. The fund's actual result was another 13.2 points below that figure, which is the cost of fees, swap financing and tracking gaps. In plain terms, the fund captured about 59% of the tripled gain over a year in which the index trended.[17][18] The drag is mild when the index trends smoothly and large when it zigzags. Chapter 3 turns this into a break-even number.

What a 3x fund does in a drawdown

A drawdown is the fall from a peak to a later low. A 3x fund falls about three times as fast as the index on the way down, and the daily reset also makes it harder to climb back. Between December 16, 2024 and April 8, 2025, QQQ fell 22.69% and TQQQ fell 58.30%, from $46.65 to $19.45. It took 162 trading days to regain the December 16 price, on August 12, 2025 (our arithmetic from daily closes).[17][18] After a 58.30% fall a holder needs a gain of 139.8% to get back to even, because 1 divided by 0.417 is 2.40. In calendar 2022 the Nasdaq-100 fell 32.97% on a price basis and TQQQ lost 79.03%; its worst quarter, to June 30, 2022, was -58.60%.[9][3][2]

The present fall follows the same pattern at a smaller size. QQQ closed at a high of $759.66 on October 6, 2026 and fell 6.56% to $709.80 by October 16; TQQQ fell 19.04% over the same days, from $84.25 to $68.21, and is 21.80% below its 2026 closing high of $87.22 set on June 2 (our arithmetic).[17][18] The issuer adds a warning that matters at the extreme: if the index approaches a 33% loss at any point in the day, an investor could lose the entire investment, and a swap counterparty may close a swap out during the day after a dramatic move.[2] A day like that has not happened in the data we read. The worst TQQQ day in two years was -18.31% on April 4, 2025, when QQQ fell 6.21%, and the fund moved more than 5% in either direction on 82 of 500 sessions.

The number

How it was calculated

A daily-reset 3x fund is worth about its starting value times the index's price ratio cubed, times a decay factor of e to the power of minus three times the volatility squared times the years, times one minus the yearly cost times the years. With 19.84% volatility and 0.25 years the decay factor is 0.9709, and the cost factor is 1 - 0.0915 x 0.25 = 0.9771. Setting the product to one gives a price ratio of the cube root of 1 / (0.9709 x 0.9771), which is 1.0177. This is the standard approximation and our simplification, not a number from the fund.

The inputs

Volatility of 19.84% from 250 daily closes of QQQ.[18] A yearly cost of 9.15%, measured as the shortfall of TQQQ's 250-day return (31.19%) against the daily-compounded 3x return (44.41%).[17][18] The start price of $709.80.[15]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, the evidence that would show it wrong and when we will know. It then gives three fatal-flaw scenarios, the strongest cases against our own conclusion and the single claim the conclusion hangs on.

Estimates 1 and 2: the volatility and cost assumptions

We assumed QQQ volatility of 19.84% a year and a holding cost of 9.15% a year. Neither is stable. QQQ's volatility was 18.33% over the last 20 sessions, 19.84% over 250 and 21.82% over 500, all from daily closes.[18] At 30% volatility the three-month break-even rises from +1.77% to +3.07% and the base case falls from $74.08 to $71.32. The cost estimate comes from one year of prices and includes swap financing, which floats with short-term rates, and the Federal Reserve has raised them and signalled more.[38][44] The evidence that would show us wrong is realized volatility above 30% or below 15% for a month, or a visible widening in the daily gap. We will know within 20 trading days, and the next Federal Reserve decision is October 28.

Estimates 3 and 4: the scenario prices and weights

The $762.86, $742.52 and $661.14 prices come from chart levels in the ledger, not from a valuation, and the 25%, 50% and 25% weights are a statistical convention, not a forecast.[45] A twelve-month valuation anchor, forward earnings times a median multiple, was not available because no source we fetched gives a median multiple for the index. Estimate 5: the reverse DCF

The 10.48% figure assumes that QQQ's trailing P/E of 29.05 is a fair measure of look-through earnings and that earnings are a fair stand-in for free cash flow. Both are weak. The P/E is a vendor number with no stated method, and earnings include items that are not cash.[15] Amazon's free cash flow was negative over the last twelve months, so a cash-flow measure for the index would be lower than the earnings measure. The evidence that would show the number wrong is an index-level free cash flow figure from Nasdaq or Invesco, which neither publishes. We will know when the third-quarter reports land by the end of November.

Fatal flaw 1: the stop sits inside one week of ordinary noise

The $65.78 stop is $2.43 below the close, which is 0.74 of one normal day's swing. It corresponds to QQQ falling about 1.2% from $709.80, to roughly $701. In a simulation of QQQ with 19.84% volatility and no drift, compounded into TQQQ, a close below $65.78 occurs within 5 sessions about 51% of the time, within 10 sessions about 64% and within 21 sessions about 76%. A bootstrap from the last 500 daily QQQ returns gives about 47%, 62% and 74%. Both runs are our arithmetic. TQQQ moved more than 5% in a day on 82 of the last 500 sessions, and its worst day was -18.31% on April 4, 2025.[17][18] A stop can also be jumped overnight or inside one session, and then the loss is larger than planned. The honest reading is that this stop will probably be hit soon, whatever the index does over the next quarter. Treat it as a line where the idea is wrong, not as a guarantee of the exit price.

Fatal flaw 2: rates rise and the financing bill rises with them

TQQQ pays a floating rate on about two thirds of its exposure. At May 31 those rates were 4.47% to 4.92% on a policy rate that was lower than today's.[6][38] The Federal Reserve raised its target range to 3-3/4 to 4 percent on September 16 and indicated another rise is to come.[44] The 10-year Treasury yield was 5.10% on October 16 and hotter-than-expected inflation reports that week showed price pressure beyond energy.[40] Each 1 point rise in short-term rates costs the fund about 2 points a year, and higher rates also lower the multiples that investors pay for growth stocks, which are the index's main weight. This is the scenario in which a profitable index and a falling fund coexist.

Fatal flaw 3: the AI financing chain breaks inside a concentrated index

The index's top ten companies are 46.88% of QQQ, and many of them depend on the same chain of AI building: chips, networking, cloud and the debt that pays for data centers.[16] On October 16 CoreWeave fell about 12% and, in Reuters' words, its bonds sold off sharply, pushing yields above 15%, and Reuters reported that some investors in a large AI developer were weighing a lower valuation for its coming share sale.[40] A failure at one large borrower, or a sharp cut in AI spending by a big customer, could move NVIDIA, Micron, AMD and other chip names together. A 3x fund holding about 24% of its net assets in NVIDIA-like exposure, and more in the other chip names, would fall through any stop. This is the scenario in which the index's good earnings do not protect the fund.

The framework has two stop rules. The looser one, for long-term core holdings, puts the stop a full ATR below the lowest support. This is a short-term vehicle, so the tighter rule applies: the 20-day swing low of $67.43 less half of one ATR ($1.65) gives a stop of $65.78. Sizing for new money is zero. A tier is a conviction level that caps position size, and tier 0 is our lowest, with a target weight of zero, so the sizing formula returns 0.0% by design.
