1. What the company does

ProShares UltraPro QQQ is an exchange-traded fund, a basket that trades on a stock exchange all day like a share. Its job is narrow. Each day it tries to deliver three times that day's move in the Nasdaq-100 index, before fees and costs. [1] If the index rises 1% in a day, the fund aims to rise about 3%. If the index falls 1%, the fund aims to fall about 3%. The promise covers one day only. For any longer stretch the return can differ from three times the index return, and the prospectus says the gap may be large. [1] That is why we do not treat the fund as a company. It has no products, customers or profits of its own, so this book looks at the index it follows, the fund's price trend and the decay risk.

The fund gets its 3x in three ways. On May 31, 2026, the end of its fiscal year, it held stocks worth 30% of net assets, long swaps on the Nasdaq-100 worth 257%, and Nasdaq-100 E-Mini futures worth 13%, for 300% in total. [2] A swap is a contract in which a bank pays the fund the index return and the fund pays the bank a floating interest rate on the borrowed amount. A future is a standard contract on the index that trades on an exchange. The sponsor's daily holdings file for October 27, 2026 lists ten swap contracts with a combined $90.56 billion of exposure, one E-Mini future worth $4.09 billion, and a stock book of $15.49 billion (our sums). [5] Divided by about $36.85 billion of net assets (518.75 million shares times the $71.04 close of that day, our estimate), that is 245.7% in swaps, 11.1% in futures and 42.0% in stocks, or 298.8% in all. [14] [5] [25] Every day the fund resets its exposure back to three times its net assets, so it buys after a rise and sells after a fall. [1] That daily reset is the source of the decay that hurts the fund in choppy markets.

The sponsor, ProShare Advisors LLC, charges a management fee of 0.75% and other expenses of 0.19%, a gross cost of 0.94%. A contractual waiver of 0.16% brings the net cost to 0.78% through September 30, 2027, and the sponsor may recoup waived amounts within five years. [1] The expense ratio is not the whole cost. The fund also pays financing on the money it borrows through swaps. At May 31, 2026 the ten swap rates ranged from 4.47% to 4.92% and averaged 4.672%. [3] As a rough guide (our calculation), borrowing about two dollars for each dollar of net assets at 4.672% costs about 9.34% a year, so the all-in carry is about 10.12% a year before any trading result. The fund also earns interest on the cash it holds, which is why only the borrowed two dollars are charged: it reported $242.5 million of interest income in fiscal 2026. [3] The Treasury bills in the October 27 file imply a short-term rate near 4.07% a year (our calculation from a $1.1 billion face value valued at $1,088,943,834), so today's financing may be a little cheaper than the May rates, and at 4.1% the carry would be about 9.0% a year. What the daily reset does to your return

Take a plain example (our arithmetic). The index rises 10% one day and falls 10% the next. It ends the two days at 0.99 of where it started, a loss of 1%. The 3x fund rises 30% and then falls 30%, so it ends at 1.30 times 0.70, which is 0.91, a loss of 9%. That is nine times the index loss, not three. The more the index zigzags, the larger this gap, and a flat index in a volatile year still loses money. The prospectus table shows a fund return of -17.1% over a year when the index ends flat with 25% volatility. [1]

Volatility means how widely the index's daily returns scatter. The prospectus gives the index's annualized volatility as 22.60% over the five years to May 31, 2026, and 27.39% in its highest May-to-May year. [1] At 22.60% the daily reset costs the fund about three times the variance a year (3 x 0.226 x 0.226 = 15.3%, our arithmetic), which we call volatility decay in this book. In a strong straight-line rally the same reset works in the fund's favor: the fund returned 142.72% in the year to May 31, 2026, against 43.09% for the index. [2] Decay is a cost of choppy markets, and it turns into a bonus when the index runs without pausing.

Who sits on the other side of the contracts

The ten swap banks are Bank of America, Barclays, BNP Paribas, Citibank, Goldman Sachs International, J.P. Morgan Securities, Morgan Stanley International, Nomura, Societe Generale and UBS. [3] The financial statements show $101.94 billion of notional exposure and $13.69 billion of gain owed to the fund at May 31, 2026. [3] Counterparty risk means a bank might fail to pay what it owes. The fund holds collateral against nearly all of it, and only $64.30 million was uncollateralized at that date, which is 0.16% of net assets (our calculation). [3] [2] The contracts can be ended at will by either side without penalty, and the prospectus warns that a bank may close one out in the middle of a day after a sharp index drop. [3] [1] The fund would then have to rebuild exposure with other contracts, possibly at worse prices. What this means for an owner: the counterparty risk is small in size but not in kind, because the same event that triggers a close-out is the one that hurts the fund most. Plain-English glossary for this report

  • relative strength index (RSI) — a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has been moving; under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought.
  • average true range (ATR) — the average size of the price's daily swing, in dollars; here $3.34 for the fund and $11.03 for QQQ.
  • earnings per share (EPS) — a company's profit divided by its number of shares; the fund has none, but the companies in its index do.
  • free cash flow (FCF) — the cash a business has left after running itself and buying equipment; a fund has none of its own. A free cash flow margin is that cash as a share of sales.
  • return on invested capital (ROIC) — the profit a company earns on each dollar put into the business; the index companies have it, the fund does not.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) — profit before financing and accounting charges; it is used here only for the index companies' net debt, which is their borrowings minus their cash.
  • stock-based compensation (SBC) — the cost of paying staff in shares; it is not on the pages we could fetch, so it is shown as N/A.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio — the price of a share divided by the profit behind it; a trailing P/E uses the past year's profit and a forward P/E uses the coming year's forecast.
  • 50-day moving average — the average closing price over the last 50 trading days, which smooths out daily noise. The 20-day and 200-day versions work the same way over 20 and 200 days; we write them as the 20-day, 50-day and 200-day averages.
  • Stage 2 — the rising phase of a price cycle, with the price above rising averages; Stage 4 — the falling phase, with the price below falling averages. Stage 3, between them, is a top or a pullback.
  • base — a stretch of sideways trading after a fall or rise, where a new trend may start; pivot — the price at the top edge of a base; breakout — a close above that edge on heavy trading.
  • stop loss — a price at which you sell to cap a loss; limit order — an order that fills only at your chosen price or better; entry zone — the price range where a new purchase would be considered.
  • asymmetry and reward-to-risk — the expected gain divided by the loss if the stop is hit; the floor for a tactical idea in this book is 2.5 to 1.
  • guidance — a company's own published forecast; capital expenditure — cash spent on plants, equipment and data centres; consensus estimate — the average of analysts' forecasts for a number.
  • Daily reset — the fund rebuilds three times exposure to the index every day, which makes multi-day returns differ from three times the index return.

The case for the fund is simple and strong. It is the only ETF designed to deliver 3x the daily return of the Nasdaq-100, it is the largest geared fund in its peer group, and it gives that exposure through a single ticker in a brokerage account. The index is a quality hurdle too, though a narrow one. The Nasdaq-100 holds 100 of the largest non-financial companies listed on Nasdaq, picked by rules rather than a committee. [9] [10] Its top 10 holdings were 46.65% of the QQQ ETF on October 28, 2026, led by NVIDIA at 8.16%, Apple at 7.87% and Microsoft at 6.13%, with Micron at 4.50% and Amazon at 4.24%. Nasdaq limits the damage with weight caps: if any company's weight exceeds 24% it is cut to 20%, and if the names above 4.5% add up to 48% or more, that group is cut to 40%. [9]

The weaknesses are structural. The fund's return over months depends on the path of the index, not only its end point, and the 2022 drawdown shows it: from November 19, 2021 to December 28, 2022 the QQQ ETF fell 35.6% while the fund fell 81.8% (our calculation from daily closes). [25] [26] A fund that loses 82% needs a 450% gain to recover. The prospectus warns that if the index approaches a 33% loss at any point in a day, you could lose your entire investment. [1] Financing is a second weakness: the swap rates of 4.47% to 4.92% are a cost that does not appear in the 0.78% expense ratio, and the daily reset adds decay when volatility is high. [3] Over the last 12 months the fund returned 21.5% while three times QQQ's return was 43.9%, a gap of 22.4 points that Chapter 3 splits into decay and financing. [25] [26]

Money flows tell a third story. In fiscal 2026 the fund took in $23.83 billion from new shares and paid out $35.73 billion on redemptions, a net outflow of $11.90 billion, while net assets still rose $15.34 billion because trading gains were $27.43 billion (our arithmetic from the statements). [3] Holders were net sellers into the rally, which fits a product that investors use for short spells. Cash distributions are small but taxable: $0.07162 per share (ex-dividend March 25, 2026), $0.17123 (June 24) and $0.15601 (September 23), or $0.48 over the last year, 0.67% of the price. How the fund compares on strengths and weaknesses

Weigh the strengths first. The fund is liquid (about $3.76 billion traded in the session, our calculation), cheap for its class and well tracked. Some plain-English definitions before the numbers. CMP means current market price, here $72.36. A basis point (bps) is one hundredth of a percent, so 50 bps of your $88,229.73 net worth is $441.15. Risk-unit sizing sets a position's weight from the loss you will accept if the stop is hit: weight equals the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. A conviction tier is the size class. TIER_3 has a ceiling of 3.5% of net worth and a 50 bps risk budget, and we keep the fund there because it qualifies on Layers 1 and 2 while its chart fails the gate and its expected value is negative; TIER_0 would mean zero size. Portfolio heat is the sum of the stop losses across all positions, capped at 12% of net worth. Structural invalidation is the price at which the chart's support is broken and the idea is wrong. An EMA (exponential moving average) and an SMA (simple moving average) are averages of recent closes, and the EMA gives recent days more weight.
