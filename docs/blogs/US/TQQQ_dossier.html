1. What the company does

ProShares UltraPro QQQ is an exchange-traded fund, a basket that trades on a stock exchange all day like a share. Its job is narrow. Each day it tries to deliver three times that day's move in the Nasdaq-100 index, before fees and costs.[1] If the index rises 1% in a day, the fund aims to rise about 3%. If the index falls 1%, the fund aims to fall about 3%. The promise covers one day only. For any longer stretch the return can differ from three times the index return, and the prospectus says the gap may be large.[1] That is why we do not treat the fund as a company. It has no products, customers or profits of its own, so this book looks at the index it follows, the fund's price trend, and the decay risk.

The fund gets its 3x in three ways. On May 31, 2026, the end of its fiscal year, it held stocks worth 30% of net assets, long swaps on the Nasdaq-100 worth 257%, and Nasdaq-100 E-Mini futures worth 13%, for 300% in total.[2] A swap is a contract in which a bank pays the fund the index return and the fund pays the bank a floating interest rate on the borrowed amount. The financial statements list ten banks and rates from 4.47% to 4.92%.[3] A future is a standard contract on the index that trades on an exchange. Every day the fund resets its exposure back to three times its net assets, so it buys after a rise and sells after a fall.[1] That daily reset is the source of the decay that hurts the fund in choppy markets.

The sponsor, ProShare Advisors LLC, charges a management fee of 0.75% and other expenses of 0.19%, a gross cost of 0.94%. A contractual waiver of 0.16% brings the net cost to 0.78% through September 30, 2027.[1] The expense ratio is not the whole cost. The fund also pays financing on the money it borrows through swaps. As a rough guide (our calculation), borrowing about two dollars for each dollar of net assets at an average swap rate of 4.67% costs about 9.3% a year, so the all-in carry is about 10.1% a year before any trading result.[3] Buyers need the index to rise enough every year just to pay for that.

Size and liquidity are not the problem. Net assets were $39,747,278,039 on May 31, 2026, up from $30,774,299,036 on November 30, 2025.[2][4] A market-data page lists $37.55B in assets and 508.60M shares outstanding; the page does not date those two figures, and they imply about $73.83 a share against the $66.29 close, so we treat them as approximate and use the audited figure for size.[14] The ledger shows 51,344,000 shares traded in the session, which at $66.29 is about $3.40 billion (our calculation). The sponsor calls the fund the world's largest leveraged ETF, and says it is the only ETF that targets 3x the daily returns of the Nasdaq-100.[5][6] The real risk is how the fund behaves when the index swings, which the next chapters measure.

What the daily reset does to your return

Take a plain example (our arithmetic). The index rises 10% one day and falls 10% the next. It ends the two days at 0.99 of where it started, a loss of 1%. The 3x fund rises 30% and then falls 30%, so it ends at 1.30 times 0.70, which is 0.91, a loss of 9%. Nine times the index loss, not three. The more the index zigzags, the larger this gap, and a flat index in a volatile year still loses money. The prospectus table shows a fund return of -17.1% over a year when the index ends flat with 25% volatility.[1]

Volatility means how widely the index's daily returns scatter. The prospectus gives the index's annualized volatility as 22.60% over the five years to May 31, 2026, and 27.39% in its highest May-to-May year.[1] At that level the daily reset costs the fund about 3 times the variance a year (3 x 0.226 x 0.226 = 15.3%, our arithmetic), which we call volatility decay in this book. In a strong straight-line rally the same reset works in the fund's favor: the fund returned 142.72% in the year to May 31, 2026, against 43.09% for the index.[2]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • relative strength index (RSI) — a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has been moving; under 30 is called oversold, over 70 overbought.
  • average true range (ATR) — the average size of the price's daily swing, in dollars; here $3.52 for the fund.
  • earnings per share (EPS) — a company's profit divided by its number of shares; the fund has none.
  • free cash flow (FCF) — the cash a business has left after running itself and buying equipment; a fund has none of its own.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio — the price of a share divided by the profit behind it; a trailing P/E uses the past year's profit and a forward P/E uses the coming year's forecast.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average — the average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 trading days, which smooths out daily noise.
  • Stage 2 — the rising phase of a price cycle, with the price above rising averages; Stage 4 — the falling phase, with the price below falling averages; Stage 3 is a top or pullback between them.
  • base — a stretch of sideways trading after a fall or rise, where a new trend may start; pivot — the price at the edge of a base; breakout — a close above that edge on heavy volume.
  • stop loss — a price at which you sell to cap a loss; limit order — an order that fills only at your chosen price or better; entry zone — the price range where a new purchase would be considered.
  • asymmetry and reward-to-risk — the expected gain divided by the loss if the stop is hit; the floor for a tactical idea in this book is 2.5 to 1.
  • Daily reset — the fund rebuilds three times exposure to the index every day, which makes multi-day returns differ from three times the index return.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the fund is simple and strong. It is the only ETF that targets 3x the daily return of the Nasdaq-100, it is the largest geared fund in its peer group, and it gives that exposure through a single ticker in a brokerage account.[5][6] Net assets of $39.7 billion at fiscal year end mean the fund is unlikely to close for lack of assets.[2] On the market-data pages (which do not date their asset figures), the five peers we checked were all smaller than TQQQ's roughly $37.55B: SOXL at $23.26B, QLD at $14.57B, SPXL at $7.20B, TECL at $5.87B and UPRO at $5.59B.[14][18][19][20][21][22] Its net expense ratio of 0.78% is below the peer median of 0.87%.[1][18][19][20][21][22] Tracking is tight: the fund reports an average daily correlation above 0.99 to three times the index, and our own test on 251 sessions gives a slope of 0.9909 and a correlation of 0.9996.[2][25][29]

The index is a quality hurdle too, though a narrow one. The Nasdaq-100 holds 100 of the largest non-financial companies listed on Nasdaq, picked by rules rather than a committee.[9][10] Its top 10 holdings were 46.59% of the QQQ ETF on October 23, 2026, led by NVIDIA at 8.13%, Apple at 8.08% and Microsoft at 6.15%.[17] The fund's own report shows information technology at 58.9% of exposure.[2] A narrow index makes the fund a bet on a handful of AI and chip names. Nasdaq limits the damage with weight caps: if any company's weight exceeds 24% it is cut to 20%, and if the names above 4.5% add up to 48% or more, that group is cut to 40%.[9]

The weaknesses are structural. The fund's return over months depends on the path of the index, not only its end point, and the 2022 drawdown shows it: from November 19, 2021 to December 28, 2022 the QQQ ETF fell 35.6% while the fund fell 81.8% (our calculation from daily closes).[25][29] A fund that loses 82% needs a 450% gain to recover. The prospectus warns that if the index approaches a 33% loss at any point in a day, you could lose your entire investment.[1] Financing is a second weakness: the swap rates of 4.47% to 4.92% are a cost that does not appear in the 0.78% expense ratio, and the daily reset adds decay when volatility is high.[3]

Cash distributions are small but taxable. The fund's recent distributions were $0.15601 per share (ex-dividend date September 23, 2026), $0.17123 (June 24, 2026) and $0.07162 (March 25, 2026).[14] The sponsor warns that distributions for this fund may be higher than those of most ETFs.[1] Regulators have long said that geared funds are complex products designed for a daily objective; FINRA's 2009 notice describes them as highly complex financial instruments that are typically designed to achieve their stated objectives on a daily basis.[28] The sector's closure rate is also a risk to track: a headline listed on a market-data page says leveraged ETFs are being shut down at a record pace, though the fund's own size makes closure of this one remote.[14]

How the fund compares on strengths and weaknesses

Weigh the strengths first. The fund is liquid (about $3.40 billion traded in the session, our calculation), cheap for its class, and well tracked. Its net assets rose by $9.0 billion between November 30, 2025 and May 31, 2026, which reflects price gains and any new money; the reports do not split the two.[2][4] Then the weaknesses: it has a negative carry of about 10% a year, it can lose 8% or more in one day (the fund fell 8.52% on October 22, 2026 and 7.60% on October 16, 2026), and it is tied to one index whose largest names move together.[15] The balance is that the fund is a good tool and a poor holding: it works well for short spells with a plan and badly as a place to wait.

5. What could happen next

A company chapter would solve for the growth rate that today's price already assumes, using the company's free cash flow. That is called a reverse discounted cash flow, and it is not defined for a fund: a fund owns swaps and futures, not a business that earns cash, so there is no cash flow to work from. In its place we show the look-through valuation of the Nasdaq-100, which the fund tracks three times over. Analyst estimates of earnings per share are also not defined for the fund, and we found no dated index-level revision history, so we mark revisions N/A instead of estimating them.[17]

For the six-month cases we need a formula for the fund's price. The fund's multiple over a period is (1 + index return) cubed, times e to the power of minus three times the variance times the number of years, times a carry factor for financing and fees. We set the period at six months (October 23, 2026 to April 23, 2027), the carry at 10.12% a year and the volatility by case. The formula reproduces the prospectus table at seven points to the tenth of a percent, for example -75.8% for a one-year index loss of 20% at 50% volatility.[1][3] We picked six months because the plan in Chapter 7 limits the holding period to six months.
