1. What the company does

GraniteShares 2x Long NVDA Daily ETF is an exchange-traded fund, a basket that trades on a stock exchange all day like a share. Its job is narrow. Each day it tries to deliver two times the percentage move in NVIDIA common stock, before fees and expenses [1]. It began trading on December 13, 2022 and sought 1.5 times until January 22, 2024, when the objective became 2 times [5][10]. If NVIDIA rises 1% in a day, the fund aims to rise about 2%. If NVIDIA falls 1%, the fund aims to fall about 2%. The promise covers one day only, and the sponsor says plainly that the fund should not be expected to give two times the return for longer periods [1][2].

The fund does not hold NVIDIA shares. It gets its exposure from total-return swaps, which are contracts in which a bank pays the fund the NVIDIA stock return and the fund pays the bank a floating interest rate plus a spread on the notional amount [1][3]. At the end of each trading day the exposure is set to about 200% of net assets, and the fund expects to post between 25% and 50% of its assets as collateral [1]. On June 30, 2026 the schedule of investments showed nine swap counterparties, a total swap value of $8,115,494,329 against net assets of $4,051,374,858, and Treasury bills of 323.95% of net assets [3]. The swap value is 200.3% of net assets (our calculation), which is the daily reset at work: the fund rebuilds two times exposure every evening.

The cost has three layers, and the new prospectus dated October 28, 2026 changes the first. The management fee is 0.99%, other expenses are 0.63% and the total is 1.65%, or 1.64% after a small waiver; the other expenses include 0.60% of interest expense on margin posted by the swap banks [1]. Last October's page figure of 1.05% is the older basis, and a market-data page still shows it [10]. The sponsor's fee cap of 1.15% now runs to December 31, 2027, but it excludes interest and swap costs, and the prospectus estimates the indirect swap cost at 1.45% for the year to June 30, 2027 [1]. The third layer is the drag we measured from daily prices: after doubling NVIDIA's daily returns the fund lost about 9.7% a year (our calculation, Chapter 3) [19][20].

Size and trading are not the problem. Net assets were $4,051,374,858 on June 30, 2026 and a market-data page lists $3.61 billion on October 28, which we treat as approximate because it does not date the figure [2][10]. The ledger shows 6,919,782 shares traded on October 28, which at the $32.15 close is about $222.5 million (our calculation) [37]. The real risks are how the fund behaves when NVIDIA swings, who sits on the other side of the contracts, and how the sponsor treats funds that shrink. The next chapters measure each one.

What the daily reset does to your return

Take a simple two-day case. NVIDIA rises 10% on day one and falls 9.09% on day two, so it ends flat. The 2x fund gains 20% and then loses 18.18%, which turns $100 into $98.18 (our calculation). The fund lost money because the second-day loss hit a larger balance. Repeat that over many choppy days and the gap grows. The prospectus grid says a flat year with 25% volatility costs the fund 6.0%, and 100% volatility costs it 63.3% [1]. Volatility is how widely the daily moves scatter. NVIDIA's own five-year volatility was 52.84% in the prospectus and 37.5% over the last twelve months in our calculation [1][20].

What follows for you: a rally with calm days pays more than 2x, a choppy flat market bleeds, and a long slide compounds downward. The last five months show it. From May 14 to October 6, 2026 NVIDIA rose 1.5% while NVDL fell 6.6%, and from October 6 to October 28 NVIDIA fell 10.0% and NVDL fell 20.0% (our calculation) [19][20]. That is why this book sets a time limit and a stop instead of asking you to hold and wait.

Who sits on the other side of the contracts

Counterparty risk means a bank might fail to pay what it owes. The fund holds swaps with nine counterparties, but not evenly: Clear Street, LLC carries notional of $1,973,347,649 and an unrealized gain of $3,105,370,746, which is 76.65% of net assets (our calculation) [3]. The prospectus lists counterparty risk among the principal risks, and the fund says credit support annexes under master netting agreements limit credit risk [1][3]. Chapter 3 shows the table and Chapter 6 counts the loss if that bank fails at the wrong time.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • relative strength index (RSI) — a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has been moving; under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought.
  • average true range (ATR) — the typical size of the price's daily swing in dollars; for NVDL it is $1.82.
  • earnings per share (EPS) — a company's profit divided by its number of shares; the fund has none, but NVIDIA does.
  • free cash flow (FCF) — the cash left after a business pays for its operations and equipment; the fund has none of its own.
  • stock-based compensation (SBC) — pay given to employees in shares, a real cost to owners.
  • return on invested capital (ROIC) — profit earned on the money put into the business.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) — a rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average — the average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 trading days, which smooths out daily noise.
  • Stage 2 and Stage 4 — the Weinstein stage scale, where Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 the confirmed downtrend; Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase between them.
  • base, pivot and breakout — a base is a stretch of sideways trading, a pivot is the price at its upper edge, and a breakout is a close above that edge on heavy volume.
  • stop loss, limit order and entry zone — a stop loss is the price at which you sell to cap a loss; a limit order fills only at your chosen price or better; an entry zone is the price range where a purchase would be considered.
  • asymmetry and reward-to-risk — the expected gain divided by the loss if the stop is hit; the floor for this book's turnaround and tactical ideas is 2.5 to 1.
  • daily reset — the fund rebuilds two times exposure to NVIDIA every day, which makes multi-day returns differ from two times NVIDIA's return.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for NVDL is size and structure. Among the five funds that double NVIDIA each day, it is the largest by far: $3.61 billion on October 28 against $441.64 million for Direxion's NVDU, $341.96 million for T-Rex's NVDX, $37.00 million for Leverage Shares' NVDG and $4.29 million for ProShares' NVDB [10][13][14][15][16]. Scale matters because small leveraged funds get closed. The fund also tracks well. Over 695 sessions since the objective became 2 times, a regression of the fund's daily returns on NVIDIA's gives a slope of 1.9864 and a correlation of 0.9996 (our calculation) [19][20]. It traded about $222.5 million on the last session (our calculation from the ledger) [37].

The weaknesses are structural. The page fee of 1.05% ties NVDX for the highest among the five, and the prospectus now states 1.64% once swap-bank interest is counted, against page figures of 0.76% for NVDG, 0.92% for NVDU and 0.95% for NVDB [1][10][13][14][15][16]. Financing and decay come on top of the fee: over the last 252 sessions the fund returned -0.57% while NVIDIA gained 12.48%, so twice NVIDIA's return would have been 24.95% and the shortfall is 25.5 points (our calculation) [19][20]. The fund is also a single-stock bet. The prospectus says its exposure is concentrated in the semiconductor industry and lists NVIDIA's customer concentration among the underlying risks [1]. NVIDIA's 10-K says one direct customer was 22% of fiscal 2026 revenue and another was 14%, and its latest 10-Q says one direct customer was 16% of second-quarter revenue [22][24].

Gap risk is the third weakness. The prospectus warns that a fund of this kind could lose more than its net assets if NVIDIA fell more than 50% in a day, and that a bank may close a swap right after a dramatic move [1]. NVDL fell 33.78% on January 27, 2025, the day NVIDIA fell 16.97%, and it fell 17.14% on February 27, 2025, the day after NVIDIA's report (our calculation) [19][20]. The fund's maximum fall from peak to trough in our data was 67.55%, from June 18, 2024 to April 4, 2025. A fall of that size needs a gain of 208% to recover, so a holder who sits through it is not rescued by time [19].

Regulators have said the same thing for years. FINRA's 2009 notice says inverse and leveraged ETFs that reset daily are typically unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them for longer than one trading session [18]. That statement is the right frame for this book: NVDL is a tool for a short, planned trade and not a place to wait.

What the sponsor's record says

The sponsor, GraniteShares Advisors LLC, runs a trust with many daily-reset funds, and its record has two sides [1][3]. On NVDL itself the record is clean: the fund has had two forward splits (6-for-1 after the close of March 12, 2024 and 3-for-1 effective June 26, 2026) and no reverse split [3][6][19]. A third portfolio manager, Jeroen Visser, joined in September 2026 beside Jeff Klearman and Ryan Dofflemeyer [1]. Elsewhere the record is harder. The trust's 2x Short NVDA fund needed a 1-for-25 reverse split after the close of November 4, 2024 and lost 75.07% in fiscal 2025 and 53.87% in fiscal 2026 [3]. Other funds had 1-for-20 reverse splits after the close of January 10, 2025, August 14, 2025 and May 1, 2026, and a 1-for-10 on May 1, 2026 [3]. In 2026 the sponsor also closed three funds because their assets stayed small, and its 2x Long LCID fund was liquidated in July after its net asset value turned negative [7][8][9].

A reverse split is what a sponsor does when a fund's price has fallen so far that it needs fewer, larger shares. It does not change the value of your holding, but it tells you the fund lost most of its value first. NVDL is far from that, with a price of $32.15 after two forward splits. The point is that this sponsor closes and reshapes small leveraged funds quickly, which is a reason to prefer the large NVIDIA fund and a reason not to hold it longer than planned.

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could be wrong. The central case ($35.37) assumes NVIDIA's earnings base grows 11.6% by April and volatility stays near 38%; its weakest part is volatility, because 45% volatility lowers the weighted value to $33.40. The bull case ($49.89) assumes a higher multiple; its weakest part is that the multiple has been falling, and we would know by the November 17 report if the stock rises instead of falling. The bear case ($18.22) assumes an 11.0 multiple; it may be too mild, because the fund can lose 33.78% in a day. The weighted value is only as good as its weights, and the 30% bear weight is a judgment from the regime, not a model.

The strongest honest case against this idea is the red-team view. The edge is thin: a weighted value 5.3% above the close sits beside a measured carry of 9.7% a year and a stop that, in our data, was touched in 62% of 25-session windows and 77% of 126-session windows (our calculation) [19]. About half of the six-month windows touched that level and still ended above their start, so a stop at this distance will often sell a fund that later recovers. The central reward also depends on delivered earnings: a 10% shortfall against fiscal 2028 EPS leaves the central price near $28.65, almost no gain from $28.60. The underlying also carries its own tail risks: $279 billion of supply commitments against $134.4 billion of trailing cash from operations, one customer at 16% of second-quarter revenue, and earnings flattered by $23.7 billion of equity gains [24]. Fatal flaw 1: a gap through the stop

The stop is a closing rule at $25.96, acted on at the next open. A stop does not protect against a gap, a day when the price opens far below the last close. NVDL fell 33.78% on January 27, 2025, 19.95% on April 19, 2024 and 18.98% on September 3, 2024, and it fell 17.14% the day after the February 2025 report (our calculation) [19][20]. The prospectus warns that a fund of this kind could lose more than its net assets if NVIDIA fell more than 50% in a day [1]. What would show this wrong: nothing before it happens. The cost of the risk is the size, and the plan cuts to 30 shares before the report, which roughly halves the loss. The two bases differ: the 60-share figures assume 30 shares filled at $28.60 and 30 at $27.80 (a blended $28.20 a share), and the 30-share figures use $28.60 a share, the higher of the two limits, as a conservative bound: step 2 of Chapter 7 keeps the shares bought at $27.80, so the actual 30-share losses would be slightly smaller.

Fatal flaw 2: a long, choppy sideways market

If NVIDIA goes nowhere for six months with 37.5% volatility, our formula gives a fund loss of 11.6% before the stop, or about $196.56 on 60 shares (22.3 bps). The prospectus table shows the same effect: a flat year at 25% volatility gives -6.0% and at 50% gives -22.1% [1]. Some plain-English definitions before the numbers. Layer 3 is the third part of the underwriting: where to enter, how much to buy and when to leave. CMP means current market price. A basis point (bps) is one hundredth of a percent, so 35 bps of your net worth is the loss budget for one position. Risk-unit sizing sets a position's weight from the loss you will accept if the stop is hit: weight equals the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. Portfolio heat is the sum of those planned losses across the book, capped at 12%. A conviction tier is the size class: TIER_1 is the largest and TIER_0 means zero size. We place the fund in TIER_4, a 1% to 2% micro-satellite with a 35 bps budget, because Layer 2 scores only 3.5 of 5.0, the chart is weak and one bank carries most of the swaps. An SMA is a simple moving average and an EMA an exponential one, which weights recent days more. Structural invalidation means the price level where the reason for the trade is broken.
