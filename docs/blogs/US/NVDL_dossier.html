1. What the company does

GraniteShares 2x Long NVDA Daily ETF is an exchange-traded fund, a basket that trades on a stock exchange all day like a share. Its job is narrow. Each day it tries to deliver two times the percentage move in NVIDIA common stock, before fees and expenses[1]. It began trading on December 13, 2022 and sought 1.5 times until January 22, 2024, when the objective became 2 times[5][10]. If NVIDIA rises 1% in a day, the fund aims to rise about 2%. If NVIDIA falls 1%, the fund aims to fall about 2%. The promise covers one day only, and the sponsor says plainly that the fund should not be expected to give two times the return for longer periods[1][10].

The fund does not hold NVIDIA shares. It gets its exposure from total-return swaps, which are contracts in which a bank pays the fund the NVIDIA stock return and the fund pays the bank a floating interest rate plus a spread on the notional amount[10][3]. At the end of each trading day the exposure is set to about 200% of net assets, and the fund expects to post between 25% and 50% of its assets as collateral[1]. On June 30, 2026 the schedule of investments showed nine swap counterparties, a total swap value of $8,115,494,329 against net assets of $4,051,374,858, and Treasury bills of 323.95% of net assets[3]. Value of 200.3% of net assets (our calculation) is the daily reset at work: the fund rebuilds two times exposure every evening.

The cost has three layers. The first is the fee. The management fee is 0.99%, other expenses are 0.06%, and the total is 1.05%, with a cap of 1.15% through December 31, 2026[1]. The second is financing. The prospectus estimates the indirect cost of the swaps at 3.00% for the fiscal year to June 30, 2026, and the fund's own statements show $25,783,317 of interest expense that lifted the expense ratio from 1.02% to 1.61%[1][3]. The third is the drag we measured from daily prices: after doubling NVIDIA's daily returns, the fund lost about 9.8% a year (our calculation, Chapter 3)[20][21].

Size and trading are not the problem. Net assets were $4,051,374,858 on June 30, 2026[2]. A market-data page lists $3.51 billion and 130.57 million shares for October 2026, but those two figures imply $26.88 a share against the $30.91 close, so we treat them as approximate and use the audited figure for size[11]. The session ledger shows 6,531,300 shares traded on October 23, which at the $30.91 close is about $201.9 million (our calculation). The real risks are how the fund behaves when NVIDIA swings, who sits on the other side of the contracts, and how the sponsor treats funds that shrink. The next chapters measure each one.

What the daily reset does to your return

The prospectus gives a plain example. NVIDIA rises 5% on day one and falls 4.76% on day two, so it ends flat. The 2x fund gains 10% and then loses 9.52%, which turns $100 into $99.52[1]. The fund lost money because the second-day loss hit a larger balance. Repeat that over many choppy days and the gap grows. The prospectus table says a flat year with 25% volatility costs the fund 6.0%, and 100% volatility costs it 63.3%[1]. Volatility is how widely the daily moves scatter. NVIDIA's own five-year volatility was 53.5% in the prospectus[1] and 37.6% over the last twelve months in our calculation[21].

What follows for you: a rally with calm days pays more than 2x, a choppy flat market bleeds, and a long slide compounds downward. That is why this book sets a time limit and a stop instead of asking you to hold and wait.

Who sits on the other side of the contracts

Counterparty risk means a bank might fail to pay what it owes. The fund holds swaps with nine counterparties, but not evenly: Clear Street, LLC carries notional of $1,973,347,649 and an unrealized gain of $3,105,370,746, which is 76.65% of net assets (our calculation)[3]. The prospectus says the fund's swap terms may let a counterparty close out a swap immediately after a dramatic move in NVIDIA[1]. Chapter 3 shows the table and Chapter 6 counts the loss if that happens at the wrong time.

  • earnings per share (EPS) — a company's profit divided by its number of shares; the fund has none, but its underlying, NVIDIA, does.

  • stock-based compensation (SBC) — pay given to employees in shares, a real cost to owners.
  • return on invested capital (ROIC) — profit earned on the money put into the business.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) — a rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average — the average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 trading days, which smooths out daily noise.
  • Stage 2 — the rising phase of a price cycle; Stage 4 — the falling phase, with the price under falling averages.
  • base — a stretch of sideways trading; pivot — the price at the edge of a base; breakout — a close above that edge on heavy volume.
  • stop loss — a price at which you sell to cap a loss; limit order — an order that fills only at your chosen price or better; entry zone — the price range where a purchase would be considered.
  • asymmetry and reward-to-risk — the expected gain divided by the loss if the stop is hit; the floor for this book's tactical ideas is 2.5 to 1.
  • Daily reset — the fund rebuilds two times exposure to NVIDIA every day, which makes multi-day returns differ from two times NVIDIA's return.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for NVDL is size and structure. Among the five two-times NVIDIA funds we checked, it is the largest by far: about $3.51 billion in assets on a market-data page (an approximate figure, see Chapter 1), against $487.10 million for Direxion's NVDU, $389.46 million for T-Rex's NVDX, $36.30 million for Leverage Shares' NVDG and $4.77 million for ProShares' NVDB[11][14][15][16][17]. Scale matters because small leveraged funds get closed. The fund also tracks well. Over 692 sessions since the objective became 2 times, a regression of the fund's daily returns on NVIDIA's gives a slope of 1.9864 and a correlation of 0.9996 (our calculation)[20][21]. And it traded about $201.9 million in the last session (our calculation from the ledger).

The weaknesses are structural. The fee of 1.05% is above the peer median of 0.935% (NVDG 0.76%, NVDU 0.92%, NVDB 0.95%, NVDX 1.05%)[1][14][15][16][17]. Financing and decay come on top of the fee: the fund returned 7.49% over the last 252 sessions while NVIDIA gained 17.02%, so twice NVIDIA's return would have been 34.04% and the shortfall is 26.5 points (our calculation)[20][21]. The fund is also a single-stock bet. The prospectus says its exposure is concentrated in the semiconductor industry and lists NVIDIA's customer concentration among the underlying risks[1]. NVIDIA's 10-K says one direct customer was 22% of fiscal 2026 revenue and another was 14%[23].

Gap risk is the third weakness. The prospectus warns that a fund of this kind could lose more than its net assets if the stock moves more than 50% in a day, and that a bank may close a swap right after a dramatic move[1]. NVDL fell 33.78% on January 27, 2025, the day NVIDIA fell 16.97%, and it fell 17.14% on February 27, 2025, the day after NVIDIA's report (our calculation)[20][21]. The fund's maximum fall from peak to trough in our data was 67.55%, from June 18, 2024 to April 4, 2025[20]. A fall of that size needs a gain of 208% to recover, so a holder who sits through it is not rescued by time.

Regulators have said the same thing for years. FINRA's 2009 notice says inverse and leveraged ETFs that reset daily are typically unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them for longer than one trading session[19]. That statement is the right frame for this book: NVDL is a tool for a short, planned trade and not a place to wait.

What the sponsor's record says

The sponsor, GraniteShares Advisors LLC, advises a trust of thirty-nine series, and its record has two sides[3]. On NVDL itself the record is clean: the fund has had two forward splits (6-for-1 effective March 13, 2024 and 3-for-1 effective June 26, 2026) and no reverse split[3][6][20]. Elsewhere it is not. The trust has done reverse splits on the 2x Short NVDA fund (1-for-25, November 1, 2024), the 2x Short TSLA fund (1-for-20, January 10, 2025), the 2x Short COIN fund (1-for-20, August 14, 2025), and on April 30, 2026 the 2x Long MSTR (1-for-20), 2x Long MARA (1-for-10) and 2x Long SMCI (1-for-20) funds[3]. In 2026 it also closed three funds because their assets stayed small, and its 2x Long LCID fund was delisted in July after its net asset value turned negative[7][8][9].

A reverse split is what a sponsor does when a fund's price has fallen so far that it needs fewer, larger shares. It does not change the value of your holding, but it tells you the fund lost most of its value first. NVDL is far from that, with a price of $30.91 after two forward splits. The point is that this sponsor closes and reshapes small leveraged funds quickly, which is a reason to prefer the large NVIDIA fund and a reason not to hold it longer than planned.
