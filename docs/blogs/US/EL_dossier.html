Before you read

The Estée Lauder Companies (EL) makes and sells prestige skin care, makeup, fragrance and hair care under brands such as Estée Lauder, Clinique, M·A·C, La Mer and TOM FORD.[1] We rate the stock REJECTED for now: the turnaround is real, with adjusted operating margin up from 8.0% to 11.2% in fiscal 2026.[2] But at $108.82 the reward-to-risk ratio is 0.08 to 1 on our anchored target, and 0.59 to 1 at the top of the dip-buy zone, far under the 2.5 floor.[2][18] The ratio would reach 2.5 only at about $93.73, and the first test of the case is the first-quarter report on November 2, 2026, before the open.[13][20] The call is wrong if margins keep beating guidance and the market keeps paying more than 32 times next year's profit.

1. What the company does

Estée Lauder sells beauty products at the high end of the price range, which the industry calls prestige. The company operates as a wholesaler. Its products are sold through department stores, duty-free retailers, specialty-multi retailers, online pure players, upscale perfumeries and pharmacies, and top-tier salons and spas, and it also runs freestanding stores and brand websites.[1] The products are sold in approximately 150 countries and territories under brands that include Estée Lauder, Clinique, M·A·C, La Mer, Jo Malone London and TOM FORD.[2] In fiscal 2026, the year to June 30, 2026, net sales were $15,049 million.[1] The five product categories add up to $15,061 million before a small adjustment for restructuring returns: skin care $7,338 million, makeup $4,276 million, fragrance $2,779 million, hair care $565 million and other $103 million.[2]

Skin care is the largest category at 48.7% of that total (our calculation), and it is also the one that earns the money: it made $1,416 million of operating income before restructuring charges, while makeup lost $70 million and hair care lost $4 million.[2] Fragrance is the growth engine. Its organic sales, which are sales with currency changes and acquisitions removed, grew 10% in fiscal 2026, led by Le Labo, TOM FORD and KILIAN PARIS.[2] The company says it now has six billion-dollar brands, after adding Jo Malone London and TOM FORD in fiscal 2026.[2]

Geography matters as much as category. The company reports four regions. The Americas were $4,463 million (29.6% of the regional total), Europe, the U.K., Ireland and emerging markets (called EUKEM) were $3,794 million, Asia/Pacific including global travel retail was $3,746 million, and mainland China was $3,058 million, or 20.3%.[2] Travel retail, which means sales to travelers through duty-free retailers, was 15% of reported sales, and online was a record 34%, 3 points higher than a year earlier.[17] Mainland China and travel retail were the source of the 2023 to 2025 slump, which is why they are tracked closely in this report.[10][6]

The profit structure explains why the stock can move so much on small changes in sales. Gross margin, the share of each sale left after the direct cost of making the product, is 75.5%.[2] But selling, general and administrative costs, which pay for advertising, store staff, promotions and head office, were $9,685 million, or 64.4% of sales (our calculation).[1] That leaves a reported operating margin of 5.2% and an adjusted operating margin of 11.2%. Because so much cost is fixed in the short run, a few points of extra sales falls mostly through to profit, and a few points of lost sales does the opposite. That is the operating leverage the turnaround depends on.

What the turnaround programme is

Two names describe the repair job. Beauty Reimagined is the strategy to restore growth and share. The Profit Recovery and Growth Plan (PRGP) is the cost programme behind it, which management says should deliver about $1.2 billion of gross annual benefits, at the top of its earlier $1.0 billion to $1.2 billion range, and remove a net 10,000 positions.[2] The money saved is meant to pay for more advertising and store support, called consumer-facing investment, which rose 7% in fiscal 2026.[2] The company also reorganised its regions in fiscal 2026. Mainland China is now reported on its own, and global travel retail sits inside Asia/Pacific, which makes the China recovery easier to track.[6]

A reader should know one more strategic fact. In March 2026 the company confirmed it was in talks about a merger with the Spanish perfume group Puig, and on May 21, 2026 both sides ended the talks.[15][14] Management said it was confident as a standalone company. A new deal approach cannot be ruled out, and it would change the investment case, so chapter 6 treats it as a risk.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Estée Lauder's strength is its brand portfolio. It holds a spread of brands. The Ordinary is priced to attract new consumers, while La Mer is described in the 10-K as a leading global luxury skin care brand, which lets the company sell at several price points and across all four categories.[2][1] Innovation supports that: 23% of fiscal 2026 sales came from new products.[2] The 75.5% gross margin, which is 150 basis points higher than a year earlier despite $102 million of tariff costs, shows brands that can charge for their products.[2] In the latest quarter the company reported share gains in several markets: value share in mainland China, Japan, Korea and Western Europe, and volume share in the U.S.[2]

The weaknesses are just as clear and are the reason the shares fell from about $237 in June 2022 to $66 in the last year.[21][33] Sales fell from $17,737 million in fiscal 2022 to $14,326 million in fiscal 2025, and adjusted earnings per share (EPS) fell from $7.24 to $1.51 over the same span.[11][6][12] Management pointed to weak sentiment among Chinese consumers and weak retail sales trends in Asia travel retail.[10][9] The fiscal 2026 plan also called for tighter monitoring of inventory held by retailers, a significant reduction in discounts and less exposure to reseller activity, which suggests problems that had to be unwound.[6] That is part of why the organic sales decline of 8% in fiscal 2025 was so far below the 1% fall to 2% rise forecast a year earlier.[6][10]

Acquisitions are the third weak point. The company paid $2,550 million in fiscal 2023 to buy the TOM FORD brand, at a total cost of $2,578 million, and it later wrote $773 million of that brand's value off in fiscal 2025.[11][2] Goodwill and other intangible write-downs were $1,286 million in fiscal 2025 and $471 million in fiscal 2024, which is why equity fell from $5,585 million to $3,806 million.[1][11] For an owner this means that reported profit understates the recent cash earned, but also that earlier management teams overpaid for growth that did not arrive.

Evidence that the repair is working

Four quarters in a row show organic growth: 3% in the first quarter, 4% in the second, 2% in the third and 5% in the fourth of fiscal 2026, and every region grew for the year.[5][4][3][2] Mainland China grew 9% organically and the company says it beat the market each quarter. Asia/Pacific grew 4%, helped by Korea, Hong Kong and Hainan travel retail.[2][17] The Americas grew only 1% for the year, with North America turning positive in the fourth quarter.[2][17] Adjusted operating margin rose by about 300 basis points or more in every quarter, and the company now guides to 12.7% to 13.5% for fiscal 2027.[2]

Cash tells the same story with a lag. Free cash flow (FCF), which is operating cash flow minus capital spending, was $1,316 million in fiscal 2026 against $670 million a year earlier, and capital spending fell to $457 million from $919 million two years before.[1] Inventory is $1,999 million, down from $2,920 million in June 2022, so the stock-up that hurt earlier years has been worked off.[1][11]

Where the advantage is thinner

Three limits stand out. First, makeup, 28.4% of sales, was flat and lost money before restructuring, and sales declines at Bobbi Brown and Too Faced held the category back.[2] Second, the Americas, the largest region, grew 1% while L'Oréal's North America grew 6.3% over nine months, so the company is still behind L'Oréal, the largest peer in our comparison, there.[2][28] Third, prestige beauty depends on Chinese and travelling consumers, and the company says disruptions from the conflict in the Middle East cut fourth-quarter sales by about 1 point.[2] The next chapter turns these observations into scored operating measures.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has changed since the worst of the slump. Stéphane de La Faverie became chief executive on January 1, 2025, taking over from Fabrizio Freda.[8] The record under the old team was poor: the fiscal 2025 outlook issued in August 2024 forecast organic sales between a 1% fall and a 2% rise, and the company withdrew it by October and cut its dividend in half.[10][8] The result was an 8% fall in organic sales and adjusted EPS of $1.51 against a promised $2.78 to $2.98.[6]

The record since then is better. For fiscal 2026 the company started with a deliberately wide range of 0% to 3% organic growth and $1.90 to $2.10 of adjusted EPS, and delivered 3% and $2.51.[6][2] Adjusted EPS beat the Wall Street average estimate in all four quarters of fiscal 2026: $0.32 against $0.18, $0.89 against $0.83, $0.91 against $0.65 and $0.39 against $0.32.[18] Two cautions apply. First, a beat against a low bar after a bad year is easier than a beat against a high one, and the bar has now been raised: consensus fiscal 2027 EPS of $3.33 sits 92% of the way up the $3.10 to $3.35 guidance range (our calculation).[18][2] Second, fiscal 2026 adjusted EPS includes a $0.07 benefit from tariff refunds, which will not repeat.[2]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 puts numbers behind the story. EL's fiscal year ends on June 30, so fiscal 2026 means the twelve months to June 30, 2026. The five years show a full cycle: a strong fiscal 2022, a fall through fiscal 2025 when sales, profit and cash all shrank, and a recovery in fiscal 2026.[11][1] The reported numbers follow US accounting rules (GAAP). The company also publishes adjusted figures that remove restructuring charges, write-downs and legal settlements, and the tables label which is which.

Two points stand out before the tables. First, reported profit and adjusted profit are far apart. Reported operating income was $780 million in fiscal 2026 against adjusted operating income of $1,687 million, a gap of $907 million.[2] The gap is not a one-off: the company has booked restructuring and other charges every year, rising from $55 million in fiscal 2023 to $481 million in fiscal 2025 and $813 million in fiscal 2026 (the income-statement line; the release's $823 million adjustment also counts small amounts booked in cost of sales and net sales).[11][1][2] Management says the restructuring programme is now fully approved and that total charges will land slightly above $1.7 billion, so the charges should fall in fiscal 2027, but they are real cash costs and the adjusted figures leave them out.[2]

Second, the dividend was cut, and the cash that remains covers it by a wider margin than before. Dividends declared fell from $2.64 per share in fiscal 2024 to $1.71 in fiscal 2025 and $1.40 in fiscal 2026. The quarterly payment was halved to $0.35 in October 2024.[1][8] Free cash flow covered the dividend 2.6 times in fiscal 2026 (our calculation). But the company guides fiscal 2027 operating cash flow down to $1.3 billion to $1.4 billion. With capital spending at about 4% of sales, that leaves about $0.7 billion of free cash flow against about $0.5 billion of dividends.[2]

The balance sheet is stable but not strong. Debt was $7,306 million against $3,498 million of cash, so net debt is $3,808 million, down from $4,396 million a year earlier.[1] Standard and Poor's rates the debt A- and Moody's A3, both with a negative outlook, and total debt is 66% of total capitalization because earlier write-downs shrank equity.[1] A $500 million 3.150% note falls due on March 15, 2027, and the company has $3,500 million of committed credit lines, so near-term refinancing is not a concern.[1]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in chapter 5 has a weakest assumption, and a good decision names it before money is committed. The case below argues against our own verdict as hard as the evidence allows. Fatal flaw 1: the November 2 report is priced in
