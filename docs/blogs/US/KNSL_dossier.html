1. What the company does

Kinsale Capital Group sells insurance that other insurers decline. Standard carriers will not write some risks, such as a restaurant with a poor claims history, a contractor working on condominiums, or a home in a wildfire zone. These risks go to a separate part of the market called excess and surplus lines, or E&S. Kinsale writes only E&S business. It was founded in 2009.[3] At the end of 2025 it had 720 employees, all of them at its headquarters in Richmond, Virginia.[9]

Its customers are small and mid-sized businesses, and they do not buy from Kinsale directly. A local agent hands the account to a wholesale broker, and the broker shops it around to carriers like Kinsale. Three brokers produced 47.6% of 2025 premium: RSG Specialty at 18.8%, AmWINS at 17.1% and CRC at 11.7%.[9] That concentration is a real dependence, and Chapter 6 comes back to it.

The typical policy is small. The average premium per policy in 2025 was $13,400, and 70.7% of premium came from casualty cover (claims from injuries and damage to other people) while 29.3% came from property cover (damage to buildings and contents).[9] Small policies matter because large accounts attract the most competition and the most price cutting, while small ones are less contested.

The company earns money in two ways. The first is underwriting profit: premium collected minus claims paid and costs. The second is investment income. Premium arrives first and claims are paid later, so Kinsale invests the cash in between. That pool of money, mostly unpaid claims and unearned premium, was $3.5 billion at the third quarter call.[20] Net investment income was $169.4 million in the first nine months of 2026, up 21.1%.[1]

The main score is the combined ratio. It is claims plus expenses, divided by premium. Below 100% means an underwriting profit. Kinsale's third quarter ratio was 75.4%, made of a 53.4% loss ratio and a 22.0% expense ratio, which means it kept about 24.6 cents of each premium dollar before investment income.[1] The five peers in Chapter 3 report ratios between 82.5% and 90.8%.

Why the premium is a poor guide to profit here

Premium comes in two forms in the filings. Gross written premium is what customers are charged. Net written premium is what Kinsale keeps after paying reinsurers (other insurers that take part of the risk) for protection. In the third quarter, gross premium was $462.9 million, down 4.8%, and net premium was $399.1 million, down 1.5%.[1] Net fell by less because Kinsale chose to keep more risk when it renewed its reinsurance on June 1, 2025.[18] By the third quarter of 2026 it was keeping 86.2% of its gross premium, against 83.3% a year earlier.[20]

Keeping more risk changes the accounts in two ways. Profit rises because Kinsale no longer hands part of every premium to reinsurers. The expense ratio also rises, because the commission that reinsurers used to pay Kinsale shrinks. Management says the trade is positive overall: the higher net commission cost is more than offset by greater underwriting and investment income.[19] Read the expense ratio with that in mind. The next chapter asks whether the business is strong enough to justify the price.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • E&S (excess and surplus lines) is the market for risks that standard insurers decline. These insurers are not bound by the same rate and form rules, so they can price each risk on its own merits.
  • Gross written premium is the total premium charged to customers in a period. Net written premium is what remains after the part paid to reinsurers.
  • Combined ratio is claims plus expenses divided by premium. Below 100% means the insurer made an underwriting profit, and each point below 100% is one cent of profit per premium dollar.
  • Reserves are money set aside for claims that have happened but are not yet paid. If the reserves turn out too low, the insurer must charge the shortfall to profit later.
  • Float is premium that an insurer holds and invests before it pays claims. It is mostly unpaid claims plus unearned premium.
  • Return on equity (ROE) is a year of profit divided by average shareholders' equity. For an insurer it plays the role that return on invested capital plays for other companies.
  • Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding. It is the accounting value of each share, and for an insurer it is the usual yardstick for price.
  • Catastrophe losses are claims from large single events such as hurricanes, wildfires and storms. They are reported separately because they vary so much from year to year.
  • earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. Operating earnings exclude the swings in the value of investments, so they show the insurance business more cleanly.
  • stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given in shares or options. It is a real cost to owners because it creates new shares.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit before borrowing costs, taxes and non-cash charges. We use it only to test borrowing, as the scoring rules require.
  • return on invested capital (ROIC) is profit divided by the money invested in a business. For an insurer we use return on equity instead, because borrowing is small and the equity is the capital.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment. For an insurer it is mostly premium waiting to be paid out as claims, so it says little and we mark it not applicable.
  • Net debt is borrowing minus cash. It matters when debt is large. Here borrowing is small, so we measure debt against total capital instead.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash that comes in from running the business. For an insurer it rises with premium and falls as claims are paid.
  • Buyback means the company buys its own shares in the open market, which shrinks the share count and raises each remaining owner's slice.
  • Dilution is the rise in share count when a company issues new shares, for example to pay staff. It makes each existing share worth a smaller slice.
  • Guidance is a company's own forecast or target. We check later whether the company met it.
  • Consensus estimate is the average forecast from the analysts who follow a stock. We use it to see what the market expects.
  • Price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share. It tells you how many years of current profit you pay for.
  • Stage 2 is the rising part of a stock's life cycle, when the price is above a rising 50-day average and a rising 200-day average. Stage 4 is the falling part, when the price is below both and both are falling. A stock in the third stage, as this one is, is either topping or pulling back.
  • Base is a stretch of weeks or months when a stock moves sideways after a decline. A pivot is the price at the top of that sideways range, where a rise through it would signal a new advance.
  • relative strength index (RSI) is a 0 to 100 reading of how fast a price has moved. Under 30 is called oversold and over 70 is called overbought.
  • average true range (ATR) is the typical size of one day's price swing, measured over 14 days. It is the ruler we use to measure distances on a chart.
  • A retest is a return of the price to a level it recently bounced from, to see whether that level holds a second time.
  • Stop loss is a price at which you sell to limit a loss. A limit order is an order to buy at a chosen price or lower, so you never pay more than you decided.
  • Entry zone is the band of prices at which we would start buying. Asymmetry and reward-to-risk mean the same thing here: the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Kinsale's strength is cost. In 2025 its expense ratio was 20.8%, and management says many competitors run in the mid-30s or higher.[5][18] The peer data bear this out: W. R. Berkley's expense ratio was 28.6% in the third quarter of 2026 and Palomar's was 48.8% in the second quarter.[21][24] A lower cost base lets Kinsale price below rivals and still earn a profit, and it matters most when competition is hot. The proof is the combined ratio: 75.4% in the latest quarter and 76.1% over nine months, against 77.5% a year earlier.[1]

The second strength is the market. Kinsale reports that from 2001 to 2024 the domestic professional surplus lines composite, an industry series from A.M. Best, grew direct premium by 10.0% a year against 4.7% for property and casualty insurance as a whole, with a loss ratio of 68.7% against 73.6%.[9] So the E&S market has been a faster-growing and more profitable pond than the one standard insurers fish in. A company that is the cost leader in that pond has a built-in advantage.

The third strength is the way it works. Management says it owns one core operating system that it custom-built, it focuses on small accounts at an average premium of $13,400, and it uses more than 100 artificial intelligence agents and skills across underwriting, claims and software work, according to management.[9][18][20] Underwriters get to a quote quickly, and each quote draws on 17 years of Kinsale's own claims data.[20] The pay plan points the same way. The proxy ties executive pay to underwriting profit, combined ratio and operating return on equity.[11]

The cases against the business are the next three paragraphs. One is competition, one is the long tail of casualty claims, and one is how much of the premium comes through a few brokers.

What is weakening: growth

Premium growth has gone from fast to negative. Gross written premium grew 42.4% in 2023, 19.2% in 2024 and 5.7% in 2025, then fell 3.5% in the first nine months of 2026.[5][6][7][1] The cause is clear in the filings. The Commercial Property Division fell 26.8% in the third quarter and 29.8% over nine months, because large property accounts attract heavy competition, including from standard carriers.[1][5] The second quarter was similar: gross premium fell 5.0% and net premium fell 1.4%.[4] Casualty premium still grew 0.9% in the third quarter and 3.0% over nine months, and excluding Commercial Property gross premium was down 0.5% in the quarter.[1][20]

There are two bright spots in the same data. New-business submissions rose 6% in the quarter, and 10% excluding Commercial Property.[20] Kinsale is seeing more accounts, but it wins fewer of them at its prices, and it is also shifting toward smaller accounts. Policies of at least $100,000 are about 0.7% of policies but about 12% of premium, and they have lower new-business hit rates and weaker renewal retention than smaller accounts.[20] Renewal retention itself is steady in the very low 70% range.[18] So demand is intact. Price and large-account competition are what hold growth back.

What is weakening: price and the market cycle

Insurance prices move in cycles. When profits are high, new capital arrives and prices fall. Management says Kinsale's pricing tracked the Amwins pricing index, which fell 3.1% in the third quarter after a 5.9% fall in the second.[20] The Q3 pace is slower, but prices are still falling in commercial property. Competitors say the same. Arch Capital described a market that is "increasingly uneven" in its October release.[23]

The main protection is that Kinsale earns a 75.4% combined ratio, so it has a wide cushion. A margin that thick can absorb about 24 points of deterioration before the business loses money on underwriting. That is why we do not treat a soft market as a reason to avoid the stock. We treat it as the reason the stock is not growing, and so as the reason the price deserves care.

What is weakening: reserves and the long tail

Reserves are Kinsale's estimate of claims it has not yet paid. They rose 15.0% in nine months, from $2,890.9 million to $3,324.3 million, while net earned premium rose 7.5%.[1][5] Higher retention explains part of this, since Kinsale now carries more of each claim itself. But casualty is 70.7% of premium, and casualty claims can take a decade to settle.[9] A company can look very good for years and then add to its reserves.

So far the signs are good. Favorable development, meaning old claims came in cheaper than reserved, was $62.8 million (3.9 points) in 2025 and $57.8 million (4.5 points) in the first nine months of 2026.[5][1] The 10-K says the 2025 favorable total came from $70.9 million of releases on accident years 2020 to 2024, offset in part by adverse development in construction liability.[9] This is why the reserve measure in the next chapter is scored WATCH and not PASS.

What is weakening: people and brokers

The Chief Financial Officer, Bryan Petrucelli, notified the company on October 19, 2026 that he will retire on March 2, 2027.[12] The company says he has played an integral role since its founding in 2009. He joins the board effective October 22, and Christopher Tangard, the chief accounting officer since March 2024, takes over as Chief Financial Officer on the retirement date.[3] That is an orderly handover, and the CFO has been part of the culture. It is still a change of the person who signs the numbers, in the middle of a falling-growth year.

Three brokers send 47.6% of premium, so a decision by one of them to steer business elsewhere would hit growth quickly.[9] This risk has not shown up so far. Kinsale appointed 12 new wholesale brokers in the third quarter, and a unit named Aspera added 124 retail brokers, according to the call summary.[20]

What this means for an owner

Kinsale is a very good business in a slow patch. Its margin, cost advantage and return on equity are best in class, and none of the weaknesses above has yet dented them. What has changed is growth, and a company priced for growth gets a lower valuation when growth stops. That is the question Chapter 2B puts into numbers: which five measures run this business, and did management do what it said it would do?

Our $373 base uses a 24% long-run return on equity. Weakest assumption in the scenario weights: that the margin cushion survives a soft market

We assign 30% to the bear case and assume a combined ratio of about 85% in it. A 75.4% ratio is unusual, and soft markets erase unusual profits. Berkley's ratio is 90.8% and RLI's is 86.2%.[21][22] If Kinsale's drifted to 85% with premium shrinking, earnings per share would fall to about $17, as in our bear case. If it drifted to 90%, earnings per share would be nearer $14 and the stock, at 14 times that, would be near $196.

The cushion is also thinner than 75.4% suggests. Management says its price tracks the Amwins index, and the Q3 index decline of 3.1% was smaller than the 5.9% fall in Q2.[20] One more quarter of a smaller decline would be an early sign the cycle is bottoming.

The red-team bear case: what the strongest honest argument against our WAIT verdict says

The case against waiting is that Kinsale has repeatedly looked expensive on book value and been wrong. The stock trades at 4.2 times book now, and it traded at much higher multiples in 2023 and 2024, when StockAnalysis's year-end forward multiple was 24.4 and 26.6 times, against 16.3 times now.[14] On a trailing price-to-earnings of 15.6 times, a company with a 76% combined ratio and a 25% return on equity looks cheap. The company is also buying back about 3.9% of its market value a year.[2][29]

The bear case against owning it is the opposite. Premium is falling in a business that was priced for 10% to 20% growth. Consensus has no earnings growth in FY2027. If growth does not return, a fair multiple is near 14 to 16 times earnings of $17 to $22 a share, or about $240 to $350. At the close, the stock already trades near the top of that range.

A third attack is on the entry itself. The one passing momentum test needs the price at or above the 20-day average, and our zone is below it. A price-only fill in a falling market can happen after that test has stopped passing. We accept this attack. The plan in Chapter 7 is therefore a conditional one: no resting order, and a fresh check of support and of the 2.0 to 1 floor before any purchase.

We weigh the arguments this way. The case for owning has real evidence behind it (margin, return, buybacks), but the price already reflects it. The case against has real evidence too (growth, flat estimates), but the margin cushion is thick. The balance is a stock that is fairly priced today, which is why we wait for a lower price and do not reject the company.

The plan is conditional. We do not leave a resting order in the market, because a price-only fill can happen after the one passing momentum lane has stopped passing. Test C2 needs the price at or above the 20-day average ($352.59), and the zone is below that line. If the stock closes inside $319.00 to $323.00, we run three checks before buying at the next open. A touch of $323.00 without those three is a price-only fill, and we do not buy it.

The next heading, Layer 3, turns the view into a plan: when to buy, where to exit and how large to go. The risk budget is the share of net worth we accept losing if the stop is hit. For TIER_3, the tier we assign because growth is negative and earnings are flat even though the business is excellent, the budget is 0.50% (50 basis points, where a basis point is one hundredth of a percent). The tier cap is the largest share of net worth the tier allows, 3.5% for TIER_3. Stop distance is the gap between entry and stop as a share of the entry price, here 4.79% at $323.00. The weight is the smaller of the cap and the budget divided by the stop distance. Here that is the cap, since 0.50% divided by 4.79% is 10.44%. Portfolio heat is the total risk budget of all open positions, which the scoring rules hold to 12% or less. If a position is opened and it works, the let-winners-run rule applies: we trail the stop under the rising 21-day exponential average or the 50-day simple average, and we do not trim only because the weight rises or the RSI passes 70.
