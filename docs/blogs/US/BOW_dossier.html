Before you read

Bowhead Specialty sells hard-to-place liability insurance to businesses. It has agreed to be bought for $34.00 a share in cash by American Family, its partner and 14.3% owner.[1][18] The stock closed at $34.13 on October 29, which is 13 cents above the price holders will get, so a new buyer has no upside and faces about a 14% fall if the deal fails.[38][39] We rate it REJECTED because the reward-to-risk ratio, the gain you could make against the loss you could take, is 0.07 to 1 at the best price we could trade, against a floor of 2.0 to 1.[39] The call is wrong only if a rival bid lifts the price above $34.00, and the stockholder vote on November 13 is the first test of that[4].

1. What the company does

Bowhead sells insurance that most standard insurers will not write. Its customers are businesses that need large amounts of liability cover. The 10-K names construction, distribution, manufacturing, real estate, public-entity and hospitality risks for Casualty, financial institutions and not-for-profit organisations for Professional Liability, and hospitals and senior-care providers for Healthcare Liability.[18] When a customer is sued, Bowhead's policy pays the defence costs and any settlement up to the limit it sold. The company was founded in September 2020 by Stephen Sills, listed on the New York Stock Exchange on May 23, 2024, and had 296 employees at the latest count.[4][29] It sells through four divisions: Casualty, Professional Liability, Healthcare Liability and Baleen Specialty, a digital unit that writes general liability for construction and real-estate risks that are hard to place.[18]

The money comes from two places. The first is underwriting: the company collects a premium up front and later pays claims and costs, and it keeps the difference if premiums exceed what goes out. In 2025 net earned premium was $491.7 million and the combined ratio, the share of each premium dollar spent on claims and costs, was 96.5%, so underwriting left only a thin margin.[18] The second source is investing the premiums between the day they arrive and the day claims are paid. That pool of held-back money is called float. Net investment income was $57.8 million in 2025, or 86% of the $67.3 million profit before tax, which means this business earns most of its profit from its bond portfolio and not from underwriting alone.[18][39]

Casualty is the engine. It produced 63.8% of 2025 gross written premium and 67.0% of the second-quarter 2026 total, and it grew 32.5% in the second quarter against 0.6% for Professional Liability.[18][13] About 80% of premium is written on an excess and surplus lines (E&S) basis, which means the cover is outside the admitted market's rate and form rules and can be repriced quickly.[18] California, Florida and Texas together supplied 39.8% of 2025 premium, so a court-cost trend or a catastrophe in one large state matters more than the national average suggests.[18][39]

What changed in August 2026 is the stock, not the business. On August 2 the company agreed to merge with a subsidiary of American Family Mutual Insurance Company, S.I., a mutual-owned group that the proxy calls the nation's 12th-largest property and casualty insurance group, at $34.00 in cash per share.[4][1] Management will stay in place and the Bowhead name will continue.[1] From that date the share price has followed the contract. The rest of this book therefore asks two questions at once: is the business worth the price, and what happens to a holder if the contract does not complete?

Why the policies carry another company's name

Bowhead does not yet have an insurance rating strong enough to write policies on its own paper. The 10-K says so directly: the company originates business on American Family's paper through its managing general agency, and then reinsures 100% of it to its own insurer, Bowhead Insurance Company, in exchange for a ceding fee.[18] Think of it as renting a bigger company's licence and credit rating while keeping the underwriting profit. The rent has been 2.75% of the net premiums assumed. It rose to 3.25% on May 23, 2026 and rises to 5.0% on May 23, 2027.[18]

That arrangement shaped the sale. American Family also capped the amount of business Bowhead could write on its paper, and the proxy says the cap was likely to be reached by the end of 2026. The two sides agreed the terms of a higher cap in mid-March, and the Wisconsin insurance regulator approved the amendment on April 30, 2026.[10] That April approval of the cap amendment is a separate filing from the change-of-control decision discussed in Chapter 5. A growing company that depends on a partner's paper, pays a rising fee and faces a cap has a built-in reason to sell to that partner. The consequence for an owner is that the sale removes a ceiling on the business, and the buyer, a partner since 2020, knew the numbers better than any rival bidder could.

Who owns the shares now

On the October 12 record date there were 32,945,158 shares outstanding. American Family owned at least 4,700,928 of them, about 14.3%. Gallatin Point, the investment firm that backed the company before its listing, owned about 27.2% and has told the company it will vote in favor.[4][39] Together those two holders control about 41.5% of the shares, which leaves roughly 58.5% held by the public, or 19.3 million shares worth about $658 million at the October 29 close.[39][38]

Two points follow. First, the vote needs a majority of all shares and also a majority of the shares held by everyone except American Family and the four directors the proxy names as recused, so the public must approve with its own votes, not only the insiders.[4] Second, the float is small and held largely by funds that trade deals. On October 29 only 73,147 shares changed hands, about $2.5 million, and that was 0.31 times the 20-day average.[38][39] A thin float is harmless while the deal holds. It becomes a liquidity problem if the deal breaks and sellers rush out together. Chapter 2 turns from who owns the shares to how good the business is.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio is claims plus all costs divided by premium earned; below 100% means underwriting made money.
  • Float is premium collected before claims are paid; insurers invest it and keep the income.
  • E&S (excess and surplus lines) is insurance sold outside the regular state-regulated market for risks standard insurers decline.
  • Ceding fee is the price Bowhead pays American Family for lending its paper and rating.
  • Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by shares, the accounting net worth per share.
  • Return on equity (ROE) is net income divided by average equity; we use 12% as the floor for an insurer.
  • Merger spread is the gap between the deal price and the market price; a negative spread means the stock trades above the offer.
  • Appraisal rights let a holder who votes no ask a Delaware court to set the fair value of the shares instead of taking the offer.
  • Reverse DCF asks what growth rate today's price already assumes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business is real. Premium grew 24.0% in 2025 and 26.4% in the first half of 2026, against a plan of about 20%.[18][13][17] The expense ratio fell from 33.5% in 2022 to 29.8% in 2025 and 28.5% in the first half of 2026, which shows the company is spreading fixed costs over a larger book.[20][18][13] Return on equity reached 13.9% in the first half of 2026, up from 12.2% a year earlier.[13] A buyer with the most information, American Family, chose to pay 2.4 times diluted book value, which is a vote of confidence in the franchise.[5][13]

The case against is also specific. The combined ratio is 95.6%, and the other specialty insurers in our peer set report between 75.4% and 89.5%.[13][33][32][34] Bowhead's loss ratio has climbed from 61.1% in 2022 to 66.7% in 2025 as casualty became a larger share of the mix.[20][18] Most of its profit comes from investment income, and the company has not yet shown a track record of releasing reserves, which is the usual sign that early loss picks were cautious. The sale price, a premium of only 11% to the pre-announcement close, sits near the bottom of the 9% to 43% range (median 27%) that Ardea measured across the precedent insurance deals in the proxy.[5][6]

Where the edge is real

The first edge is that Bowhead sits in the one corner of commercial insurance where prices are still rising. Marsh's third-quarter 2026 index shows US casualty rates up 6% while global property rates fell 10% and global composite rates fell for a ninth straight quarter.[31] The 10-K says excess casualty drove most of the Casualty division's 2025 growth, so the company is growing into rising prices, not fighting falling ones. That matters because growth in a falling-price market usually means taking worse risks.

The second edge is scale in a lean organisation. Operating expenses grew 2.1% in the second quarter while net earned premium grew 20.8%, which cut the operating-expense ratio by 3.4 points.[13] The third edge is investment income, which rose 37.6% to $18.8 million in the quarter on a portfolio yielding 4.7%, rated AA- on average with a duration of 3.3 years.[13] A short, high-grade portfolio is the right shape for an insurer that cannot afford a mark-to-market shock, and the accumulated other comprehensive loss of $6.8 million at June 30 shows the damage so far has been small.[13]

What this means for an owner: the franchise is growing profitably in a favourable line, and the company has shown it can cut its cost ratio on schedule. The thinner parts of the case come next, because they are the reasons the buyer's price is not a bargain and why the standalone stock traded as low as $21.21 on March 20.[5][29]

Where it is thinner than it looks

Underwriting income, the profit before investment income, was $14.9 million in 2025 against $18.2 million in 2024, even though net income rose 40.6%.[18] Investment income did the lifting. That is acceptable while rates are high and the portfolio grows with premium, but it means earnings depend on how much float the company can keep adding, and growth of 20% or more a year may not last as the book matures.

The reserve book is young. Gross reserves are $1,318.6 million against equity of $472.4 million, and reinsurers owe the company $466.2 million (reinsurance recoverable, net), which equals 98.7% of its equity.[13][39] The five largest reinsurers hold 86.1% of that figure, led by RenaissanceRe at 30.0% and Endurance at 23.3%, all rated A or better.[14] Casualty claims take years to settle: in the first half of 2026 the company paid $70.2 million on prior accident years against opening net reserves of $748.2 million, so most of the reserve has never been tested by cash.[14] Prior-year development was a small $0.8 million adverse, which the company explains as an accounting effect of extra premium, not a reserve increase, but it also shows no favourable releases.[13][14]

The partnership costs rise. The ceding fee goes from 2.75% to 5.0% of net premiums assumed within two years, and the second-quarter acquisition-cost ratio already rose 1.7 points partly for this reason.[18][13] Debt also changed: the $150 million of 7.75% notes issued in November 2025 lifted second-quarter interest expense to $3.3 million from $0.3 million a year earlier.[27][13] Higher cost lines like these are part of why return on equity has been only 13% to 14% on a full-year basis since 2024 (13.6% in 2024 and 13.1% in 2025), although the first half of 2026 is up to 13.9% from 12.2% a year earlier.[18][13]

What the sale price says about the business

The proxy gives an unusually clear view of what other buyers thought. Between June 1 and June 6, 2026, the company's adviser called 11 likely buyers; none engaged on a deal and only one signed a confidentiality agreement. Some potential buyers told the adviser the shares were already properly valued, or overvalued, by the market.[5] The board also says it negotiated a $3.00 increase over the buyer's first offer, a gain of more than 9%, and believes $34.00 is the highest price American Family would pay.[5] The board cited a softening market and heavy competition in directors-and-officers and errors-and-omissions cover as reasons to sell.[5]

For you as an owner or prospective buyer, the point is that a price of about 2.4 times book value does not look like a lowball bid, and there was no rival interest at a higher price in the only test the board ran. The contract price is therefore a fair summary of what the market will pay today, and the stock now sits slightly above it. The next chapters check whether the operating numbers back the sale price, and then whether anything is left to earn for a buyer at $34.13.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 asks whether the operating engine is healthy, whatever the share price does. For a property and casualty insurer the useful gauges are different from those for a software or industrial company. Free cash flow (FCF) is meaningless because premiums arrive years before claims are paid, and return on invested capital is replaced by return on equity. FCF is the cash left after spending on the business, and an insurer's cash flow is inflated by money it owes to policyholders. We chose five indicators that between them cover price, cost, growth, safety and profit: the combined ratio, premium growth, the expense ratio, reserve quality and return on equity. Each is scored PASS (1.0), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0), and the layer qualifies at 3.0 of 5.0 or more with no more than one FAIL.[39]

The five were picked because they are the ones a casualty underwriter or a rating analyst would ask for first. The combined ratio says whether the company is paid enough for the risk. Premium growth says whether it can find business at that price. The expense ratio shows whether growth is bringing scale. Reserve quality tests whether today's profit is real or borrowed from the future, which matters most for slow-paying casualty claims. Return on equity says whether all of this produces a return above the 12% hurdle we use for insurers. Bowhead's scores come from the second-quarter 2026 filings, the latest published before the third-quarter report due on November 3.[13][14][22] The scorecard that follows is the layer in table form. It gives each indicator, the rule we score it by, the latest reading and the score, so you can see at a glance which parts of the engine are strong and which are only adequate. Look first at the two PASS rows, growth and cost, and then at why the three WATCH rows stop short of a pass.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

An estimate is only as good as its weakest assumption. Chapter 5 rests on three: that the deal is very likely to close, that a higher bid is unlikely, and that a failed deal would leave the stock near $29.40. We test each one by writing down how it could be wrong, what we would see first, and when we would know.[39] The three flaws below are the most credible ways to be wrong, and the table after them lists dates.

Fatal flaw 1: the deal breaks and the price gaps down

The deal still needs two decisions: the stockholder vote on November 13 and the Wisconsin insurance regulator's approval.[4] Either could fail. A material adverse change at Bowhead before closing would also let the buyer walk. A failure would not be orderly. With a daily range of about 19 cents and a thin float, a break could reprice the stock in a day, from $34.13 toward $30.59 and possibly lower.[38][5]

A stop-loss order does not protect against that. A stop at $33.53 would trigger on a gap and fill at the new, lower price, so the true risk is the gap, not the 60 cents between the close and the stop. That is why the stress test uses the break value of $29.40 and not the stop. At a $33.68 entry the gain to $34.00 is $0.32 and the loss to $29.40 is $4.28.[39]

What would show the flaw is real: a request from a regulator for conditions, a proxy adviser's recommendation against the deal, a stockholder suit that wins an injunction, or an amended agreement. We would see these first in the stock itself, as a close below $33.53 on volume well above the usual 236,000 shares a day, or in a filing. The date to watch is November 13, with the Wisconsin approval and a Q3 report on November 3 on either side.

Fatal flaw 2: nothing improves, and time works against a buyer above the offer

The simplest way to lose money here is to do nothing wrong at all. The stock is $0.13 above the price the buyer will pay, and if the merger closes on schedule, the shares convert to $34.00 in cash. A buyer at $34.13 gives up $0.13 a share, or about 2.2% a year if closing happens on December 31, and is tied up in a stock that cannot go higher on its own.[39] If closing slips toward the April 2, 2027 outside date, the same $0.13 loss is spread over about five months of tied-up money instead of two.[9]

The one hope for a buyer above the offer is appraisal. A holder who does not vote for the merger and who perfects appraisal rights under Delaware law can ask a court to set the fair value of the shares, and the court may award more or less than $34.00.[9] That is a legal process with a cost and a long timetable, not a plan, and the proxy lists it as a right and does not promise a higher result. The 11% premium is low relative to the 9% to 43% range of past insurance deals, which gives a litigant something to argue, but the board has an adviser's fairness opinion and a separate majority-of-the-minority vote to point to, and the outcome of a court's valuation is not predictable.[6][5]

What would show the flaw is real is a rival bid or a filing about appraisal demands. The stock rising above $35.07, the ledger's 52-week high, is a warning signal that prompts a re-check, not proof of a bid. We would know by the vote date, November 13. Until then, the price above the offer is an unexplained premium, and we do not pay it.

Fatal flaw 3: the standalone value is lower than we assume

The $29.40 break value uses 12 times the Street's $2.45 for 2027. That figure assumes the earnings growth the Street expects in 2027, 21.8% above 2026, happens without a buyer's support.[38] Several things could go wrong. The combined ratio is 95.6% and the loss ratio has risen with the casualty mix. The company has no history of reserve releases, and casualty claims take years to settle. The ceding fee to American Family rises to 5.0% in May 2027, which adds cost if the company remains independent.[13][14][18]

If the deal broke because of something about Bowhead itself, such as reserves or a regulator's concern, the multiple would be lower than 12 times. Ardea's illustrative price-to-book range of 0.89 to 1.90 times applied to a $13.80 book value (the adviser's first-quarter figure; the latest diluted figure is $14.12) gives $12.28 to $26.23, and the March low of $21.21 was about 1.5 times today's diluted book value.[6][29][13][39] The softening market the board cited, with Marsh showing global property rates down 10% and US financial and professional lines up only 1%, could also hold the multiple down.[5][31]

What would show the flaw is real is a third-quarter combined ratio above 98%, any reserve charge, or the company cutting its growth plan. The third-quarter report on November 3 is the first test. A result near 95.6% with premium growth above 20% would support the 12 times assumption, while a miss would make the bear case worse than the $29.40 we show.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, and the rule behind it is the reward-to-risk floor. A Core name must offer at least 2.0 of reward for every 1.0 of risk at the price we would pay. The entry zone is the price range in which we would be willing to buy. Bowhead offers -0.22 at the $34.13 close, because the target is the $34.00 contract price and the close is above it. At the best entry we could plan, $33.68, the ratio is 2.13 against the technical stop, but the stop cannot protect against a deal break, and under the break stress the ratio is 0.07. The probability-weighted value of $33.80 is also below the close.[39][38] The verdict changes only if the terms of the deal change or the deal ends, and the levels that would do that are in the triggers below.
