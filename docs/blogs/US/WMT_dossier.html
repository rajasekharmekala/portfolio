Walmart is a global retailer with $713.2 billion of revenue in the year to January 2026, from about 10,900 stores and a fast-growing online business[1][9]. We conclude REJECTED for the shares and would sell the account's three call options, even though the business is healthy and advertising sales rose 38% in the July quarter[6]. The stock closed at $119.89 on October 23, 2026, which is 42.21 times trailing earnings, and our base value is $117.50[22]. 1. What the company does

Walmart sells everyday goods to about 280 million customers a week through more than 10,900 stores in 19 countries and through its websites and phone apps.[1] It employed about 2.1 million people at January 31, 2026.[1] Its financial year ends on January 31, so fiscal 2027 runs from February 2026 to January 2027, and the newest filed quarter is the one that ended July 31, 2026.[18] In November 2025 the company announced that its shares would move from the New York Stock Exchange to Nasdaq on December 9, 2025, with the same ticker, and its October 2026 filing lists Nasdaq, so the $119.89 close used in this book is a Nasdaq close.[15][16][22] Think of the company as a very large, low-margin seller of food and household goods that is slowly adding higher-margin side businesses.

Most of the money is simple: shoppers pay for groceries, general merchandise, health and wellness products and fuel. At Walmart U.S., grocery was $285.5 billion of $483.0 billion of net sales (59%) in fiscal 2026.[1] Net sales are the value of goods sold, and the company reports membership fees and other income, $6.75 billion in fiscal 2026, as a separate line.[1] Members pay yearly fees to Sam's Club and Walmart+. Advertisers pay to reach shoppers: the company says global advertising grew 46% to nearly $6.4 billion in fiscal 2026, including VIZIO, a business Walmart acquired.[9][12] Outside sellers pay commissions and fulfilment fees on the online marketplace, and in the July quarter nearly half of that business used Walmart's fulfilment services.[20]

The profit is thin. Gross profit, which is net sales minus the cost of the goods sold, was $171.0 billion in fiscal 2026, or 24.2% of net sales.[1] Operating income was $29.8 billion, 4.2% of revenue.[1] A change of one-tenth of a percentage point in that margin is worth about $0.7 billion a year, which is why small mix shifts matter. Advertising and membership add profit without adding stock to shelves, but the company does not publish a profit margin for advertising, so we do not state one.[9]

Online sales are counted broadly. The company includes store-fulfilled pickup and delivery orders, where a customer orders on the phone and a store packs and hands over the goods, plus advertising and fulfilment services.[1] By our addition of the three segment figures, eCommerce net sales were about $150.4 billion in fiscal 2026, 21.3% of net sales, up from about $120.9 billion, a rise of 24.4%.[1] In the July 2026 quarter eCommerce was 24% of net sales and grew 23%.[7] The segment table shows where the money is made. It also shows that Walmart U.S. earns a wider margin than the other two, which is why its comparable sales are the first number we look at.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Comparable sales (comp sales) means sales at stores and clubs open at least a year, including their online orders; Walmart reports the figure without fuel because fuel prices swing.
  • Constant currency removes the effect of exchange-rate moves, so growth shows the business and not the dollar.
  • Adjusted EPS (earnings per share) is profit per share after removing items management calls unusual, such as gains and losses on investments in other companies.
  • Free cash flow is cash from operations minus the money spent on property and equipment; Walmart defines it the same way.
  • Return on invested capital is operating profit after tax divided by the money tied up in the business.
  • Net debt is borrowings, including finance leases, minus cash; we leave out operating leases unless we say so.
  • Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share; it says how many dollars investors pay for each dollar of yearly profit.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Walmart competes on price, choice and convenience, and its filing calls price leadership a cornerstone of the business.[1] The numbers back that up. In the July quarter Walmart U.S. comparable sales rose 2.6% even though pharmacy price cuts took about 125 basis points (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point) off the figure, and excluding health and wellness the rise was 3.4%.[6][7] Transactions rose 1.5%. At Sam's Club, comparable sales without fuel rose 4.4% on transactions up 7.0%.[6] More customers buying more often is the cleanest sign that prices and assortment are working.

The stores are also the delivery network. Store-fulfilled delivery grew about 43% in the July quarter, and roughly 37% of store-fulfilled orders arrived in under three hours.[7] Online sales added about 510 basis points to Walmart U.S. comparable sales.[6] In our reading this is the real moat: a rival with no store near the customer cannot match the speed at the same cost. The company also says its marketplace sales rose 52% and its Walmart Connect advertising rose 43% without VIZIO.[7][6]

The July quarter profit jump needs care. Consolidated operating income rose 28.8%, but only 17.4% on the adjusted constant-currency basis, and the company received about $2.9 billion of tariff refunds in the quarter, which it booked as a cut to cost of sales.[6][5] The gross profit rate rose 96 basis points to 25.4%, and the company says tariff refunds were the main driver.[7] The chief financial officer asked investors to read the second and third quarters together because the refunds are being spent on lower prices in the second half.[6] So the quarter shows a business that is healthy, but it overstates the steady-state profit.

A moat is also a cost. Capital spending is guided to about 4.0% of net sales for fiscal 2027, up from 3.5% in February, and six-month spending was $14.2 billion against $11.4 billion a year earlier.[6] Walmart U.S. operating expenses grew faster than sales, and the company blames claims costs, depreciation and associate healthcare costs.[6] Those costs are what the automation spending is meant to pay back, and the payback is the thing we cannot yet see in the cash flow. The next chapter's scorecard tests it.

Where the moat is thinner

The margin is thin, which makes the stock sensitive to small errors. Operating income was 4.2% of revenue in fiscal 2026 and 4.4% over the last twelve months by our calculation.[1][6] The peers show the range: Costco 3.79%, Kroger 2.98% and Target 6.04% operating margins on the Yahoo Finance figures.[28][32][30] Walmart is not the cheapest retailer to operate, and it is not the best earner per sale, so the premium valuation rests on growth and mix rather than on a margin lead.

Three outside forces could reverse the gains. First, tariffs: less than one third of what Walmart sells in the United States is imported, and management is directing the refund money into price cuts in the second half.[5][6] Second, pharmacy prices fell under a new maximum fair price regulation, in force since January 1, which took about 125 basis points off Walmart U.S. comparable sales.[6][7] Third, on October 20, 2026 the company began selling Walmart+ and Sam's Club Plus together for $120 a year, almost $100 less than members were paying for the two separately; management says the cost does not materially change the fiscal 2027 outlook, and it does not say what it does to fiscal 2028.[19] Membership fee revenue grew 17% in the July quarter, so this is a trade of near-term fees for more members, and the result will show in the November and February reports.[6]

For fiscal 2026 it first guided to net sales growth of 3.0% to 4.0%, adjusted operating income growth of 3.5% to 5.5% and adjusted EPS of $2.50 to $2.60 in February 2025.[12] It raised the outlook in August and November 2025, and delivered revenue growth of 5.1% in constant currency, adjusted operating income growth of 5.4% and adjusted EPS of $2.64, above the original range.[11][10][9] Capital spending was the exception: it guided to 3.0% to 3.5% of net sales and spent $26.6 billion, which is 3.77% of net sales by our calculation.[12][1]

How it was calculated

We took twelve-month operating income of $32,280 million, taxed it at the fiscal 2026 effective rate of 24.4%, and divided by the average of invested capital at July 2026 and July 2025. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is weaker than the rest, and each could be wrong in a direction that changes the decision. Because the verdict is REJECTED on price and cash flow, the strongest challenge to it comes from the upside: the case that Walmart earns a higher multiple than we allow. The bear case, our $88.50 scenario, is the opposite challenge, and it matters mainly for the calls.

The three scenarios below are the most likely ways to be wrong, stated as strongly as the evidence allows. After them, a table lists each estimate, its weakest assumption, the evidence that would show it wrong, and the date it will be known.

Flaw 1: the multiple does not compress

Our base value assumes the multiple falls from 42.4 times adjusted earnings to 37.9. That is a judgement built from three data points. Costco, the other membership-led retailer, trades at 48.33 times, so investors do pay up for this model, and Walmart's low beta makes it attractive as a shelter in a market that is in a correction.[27][23] If the multiple holds at 42.4 times on our $3.10, the value is $131.44, which is 9.6% above the close.

Even then the reward-to-risk ratio at $119.89 is 0.57 to 1 against a floor of 2.0, so the verdict would not change on this flaw alone. It would change the calls' arithmetic more: $131.44 is still below the $145 strike.

Flaw 2: advertising and membership lift earnings faster than we model

Advertising grew 38% in the July quarter and membership fees 17%, and the company does not publish their profit margins, so the part of EPS growth they drive is unknown to us.[6] If they push fiscal 2028 EPS to the analyst high of $3.56, the value at 37.9 times is $134.92, 12.5% above the close. A third-quarter EPS above $0.64 and a fiscal 2027 outlook raised above $2.87 would be the first evidence.[24][6]

The October 20 membership bundle works against this flaw in the short run. It lowers the price of the two memberships, and the company says the cost does not change its fiscal 2027 outlook, but it has said nothing about fiscal 2028.

Flaw 3: the bear case is worse than we modelled

The July quarter's profit gain depended on about $2.9 billion of tariff refunds, and management is spending the refunds on lower prices in the second half. If the consumer weakens as well, and pharmacy cuts and higher claims costs persist, the earnings that consensus expects for fiscal 2028 could be 10% too high. At 28 times, just above the 27.4 times proxy of fiscal 2024, and EPS of $2.95, the value is $82.60, which is 31% below the close.

This is the flaw that hurts the calls most, and it is the reason we want them sold before the November 19 report. It would not hurt a share buyer at $105.50 as much, because the stop at $99.67 limits the loss, but a gap through the stop would not be protected, as the Chapter 7 stress shows.
