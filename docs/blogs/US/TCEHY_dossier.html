1. What the company does

Tencent is a Chinese internet company that earns money in four ways. The largest group is value-added services, which means games plus paid social features. Advertising inside Weixin, its video feeds and its search comes second. Payments, wealth products, consumer loans and cloud computing form the third group, and a small fourth group holds the rest. Revenue for 2025 was RMB751.8 billion, up 14%, and revenue for the second quarter of 2026 was RMB204.8 billion, up 11%[5][8]. The company reports in Chinese yuan (RMB), so every figure in this dossier is in RMB unless it carries a dollar sign.

Weixin is the centre of the business. Its 1,439 million monthly users open it to chat, pay, shop, watch short video and run small apps[8]. The same users see the ads, play the games and use the payment tools, so each new service costs little to add. Tencent also owns or publishes big game franchises such as Honour of Kings, Delta Force and VALORANT, and international games earned RMB77.4 billion in 2025, up 33%[5]. Games and ads together explain most of the gross profit. The cloud and payments units are large but earn lower margins.

The story changed in 2026. Tencent is buying far more computing power for its own AI models and for customers who rent it. Capital expenditure (capex) is money spent on servers, chips and data centres, and it reached RMB52.8 billion in the second quarter, up 176% from a year earlier[8][9]. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after paying for the business and for assets such as servers, and it was negative RMB13.8 billion in that quarter, the only negative one among the five quarters we read[8][5][6][7][11]. Management says the spending is for model training, inference for its AI products and rented computing capacity[35]. That spending is the main reason the share price has fallen while the older businesses keep growing.

What you own is an American depositary receipt (ADR), a US-traded certificate that stands for a share held abroad. Citi's program page lists the ratio as one ordinary share per ADR, so the ADR price and the Hong Kong share price describe the same slice of the company[22]. Tencent is not an SEC registrant, so no 10-K or 10-Q exists for it. Its audited reports sit on HKEXnews and tencent.com, and every filing figure in this dossier comes from those pages[1][10]. US government notices and market-data pages fill the rest of the evidence.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest asset is the Weixin network. Its 1,439 million monthly users are 2% more than a year earlier, which is slow growth for a mature app. Time spent keeps rising, though: total time on Video Accounts, the short-video feed inside Weixin, grew more than 20% year on year in the second quarter[8]. That matters because advertisers pay for attention, and Marketing services revenue rose 22% to RMB43.6 billion[8]. Tencent credits an AI model that picks which ad each user sees and a campaign tool called AIM+, which handled about 30% of marketing spend in the first quarter[7].

Games are the second pillar. Domestic games revenue rose 17% to RMB47.3 billion, and Delta Force and VALORANT PC reached record average daily players[8]. Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of delivering the service, divided by revenue. The games and social segment earned a 64% gross margin in the second quarter, up from 60%, because internally developed games carry high margins[10]. For the whole group the figure was 58%[8]. International games are the weak spot. Their revenue fell 0.8% in RMB, or rose 4% at constant exchange rates, as stronger Wuthering Waves and VALORANT PC sales were offset by lower Supercell revenue[8].

The price of staying strong is rising. Tencent now reports its new AI products as a separate line. Without them, non-IFRS operating profit was RMB86.1 billion in the second quarter, up 19%. With them it was RMB75.6 billion, up 9%[8]. The gap of RMB10.5 billion (our subtraction) is the quarterly cost of building those products. Operating margin is operating profit divided by revenue. On the non-IFRS measure it was 37% against 38% a year earlier, and 42% before the new AI products[8]. Marketing services gross margin slipped to 57% from 58% as depreciation and running costs for AI computing rose[10].

The weak points are user growth and spending discipline. Weixin grew 2%, QQ, the older chat app, lost 2% to 520 million users, and fee-based subscriptions fell 2% to 259 million[8]. Social networks revenue grew only 0.8%[8]. Management also warned on the second-quarter call that it may steer more cash to computing and potentially less to buybacks[35]. A strong moat protects the core. It does not guarantee that new AI spending earns a return.

The Pentagon's 1260H list: what it does and does not do to a US holder

The Defense Department's notice in the Federal Register of 10 June 2026 lists Tencent Holdings Limited and says it is 'indirectly affiliated with the PLA'[16]. The first listing came in January 2025[17]. Tencent answered then that it is neither a Chinese military company nor a military-civil fusion contributor, called the listing a mistake, and said it would start a reconsideration process and take legal action if needed[18]. The 2026 notice repeats the listing and explains how a listed entity can ask for reconsideration[16].

A listing is not a sanction. A law-firm review of the 2026 update says a designation does not, by itself, impose sanctions or prohibit commercial transactions with the entity[19]. Its practical effect is on Pentagon purchasing: from 30 June 2026 the Defense Department may not buy from a listed entity[19]. Tencent's own 2025 statement says the list does not stop anyone other than the Defense Department from dealing with it, including trading its securities[18]. So a US retail investor can still buy, hold and sell the ADR today.

The risk is the next step. The same law-firm review warns that listed companies face a higher chance of being added to the Treasury's separate NS-CMIC list, and that US persons are generally barred from buying or selling the securities of companies on it[19]. We found no notice adding Tencent to that list and no new US purchase restriction aimed at Tencent in the Federal Register notices and law-firm alerts we could read, so 'none found' describes our search, not a guarantee. Alibaba, listed in the same June 2026 round, sued in federal court on 23 June[20]. We found no sign that Tencent has sued, and N/A (no public record found) is the honest status of any Tencent court challenge.

What this means for you: the list does not change the financial numbers in this dossier, but it fattens the left tail. It is one reason the bear case in chapter 5 uses a low valuation multiple. If Treasury added Tencent to the NS-CMIC list, US brokers would probably have to stop purchases and the thin OTC market for the ADR could become harder to trade. We would treat any such notice as an immediate trigger to review the position, which chapter 7 states.

China's rules for games and advertising

China's games regulator published its September 2026 approvals on 30 September: 209 titles, of which 206 were domestic and 3 imported[36]. A steady monthly flow of licences points to routine approvals rather than a freeze, but a licence is a regulator's permission and not a right, so the risk never disappears. On advertising, Tencent says its ad load, the share of screen time given to ads, remains much lower than peers'[5], and we found no new rule aimed at platform advertising in the sources we read. For a US holder, the practical point is that China policy and US policy both act on the same share price. The next chapters test whether the business can absorb a shock from either.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter shows five years of revenue, profit, cash and debt, then tests them against the Layer 1 rules in our scoring file. A few terms first. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before the accounting and financing charges, and it is a rough guide to cash earnings. EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares. ROIC (return on invested capital) is the profit earned on each unit of money put into the business. SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying staff in shares. Net debt is borrowings minus cash, so a negative figure means net cash. Dilution is the rise in share count when a company issues new shares. The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio is the share price divided by profit per share.

Revenue rose from RMB554.6 billion in 2022 to RMB751.8 billion in 2025, a compound rate of 10.7% a year, and operating profit more than doubled from RMB110.8 billion to RMB241.6 billion[1]. Operating margin climbed from 20.0% to 32.1% and gross margin from 43.1% to 56.2% over the same period (our calculation from the reported lines)[1]. Profit attributable to shareholders grew only 6.1% a year over the three years, because 2022 included large investment gains and 2023 was a low year at RMB115.2 billion[1]. Non-IFRS operating profit, which removes investment gains and share-based pay, grew 25% a year, from RMB143.2 billion to RMB280.7 billion[1]. That non-IFRS line is the cleaner view of how the operating business performed.

Cash generation tells a similar story until 2026. Operating cash flow rose from RMB146.1 billion in 2022 to RMB303.1 billion in 2025[4][1]. Free cash flow on a cash-flow-statement basis (operating cash flow minus payments for property, equipment, intangibles and land use rights) was RMB190.2 billion in 2025, or 25.3% of revenue (our calculation)[1]. Tencent's own definition also deducts media content and lease payments and gave RMB182.6 billion, or 24.3% of revenue[5]. Over the last four quarters the company-defined figure fell to RMB135.4 billion, or 17.2% of revenue, because first-half capex payments were RMB107.2 billion against RMB57.5 billion a year earlier (our sum of the cash-flow lines)[10].

The balance sheet is strong but moving the wrong way. Net debt of RMB14.8 billion at the end of 2022 became net cash of RMB107.1 billion at the end of 2025[3][1]. Net cash then dropped to RMB58.2 billion at 30 June 2026 after RMB41.6 billion of dividends, RMB22.3 billion of buybacks and the capex surge[8][10]. Diluted shares fell from 9,696 million in 2021 to 9,244 million in 2025, a 4.7% drop, so dilution has been negative and each remaining share owns more of the company[4][1]. Share-based pay was 3.4% of 2025 revenue on the equity-settled measure and 4.2% on the total expense line[1]. Tencent also holds stakes in other companies worth RMB487.2 billion for listed holdings at fair value, plus RMB387.9 billion of unlisted holdings at book value; these sit outside free cash flow and act as a cushion we do not count[8].

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 has two parts: static levels (how good the business is today) and rate of change (which way each number moves). The hard red lines are binary: a net debt to EBITDA ratio of 4.0 times or more with interest cover below 2.0 times, a Stage 4 breakdown with Test C failing, or fraud and going-concern warnings. Stage 4 is the Weinstein stage scale's confirmed downtrend, where the price sits below a falling 50-day average that sits below a falling 200-day average, and Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on assumptions. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, says what evidence would show it is wrong, and says when we will know. It then gives the red-team case, which is the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion. A conclusion that cannot survive its best opponent should not drive a sale.

Fatal flaw 1: the AI spending does not pay back on our timetable

The weakest assumption behind the base value of $54.39 is that FY2027 earnings per share reaches RMB30.23, a 4.9% rise on 2026, even though the cost of computing hits the income statement through depreciation after it hits cash[24]. Management describes the new AI business as 'a lump sum that we need to invest in order to get our compute' and says it will measure the spending against cash, investments and 'a prudent level of debt capacity'[35]. Evidence that this assumption is wrong would be a third-quarter capex figure above RMB52.8 billion, another negative free cash flow quarter, or operating profit excluding the new AI products growing more slowly than the 19% reported for the second quarter[8]. Fatal flaw 2: US policy moves from a procurement list to investment limits

The weakest assumption behind every scenario is that the 1260H listing stays a defense-purchasing list that does not touch private investors. A law-firm review notes that listed companies face a higher chance of landing on the Treasury's NS-CMIC list, which generally bars US persons from buying or selling the securities[19]. Evidence that the assumption is wrong would be a Federal Register or Treasury notice naming Tencent, or a broker notice restricting purchases. We cannot size the price effect from financial statements, and we did not find a source that states the wind-down terms for a Tencent listing, so we do not rely on one. We will know only when a notice appears; the Defense Department updates the list at least once a year[16], and Alibaba's June 2026 lawsuit may show how courts treat such listings[20].

Run notes and gate exceptions

PDF extraction. Tencent's annual reports, results releases, interim report, presentation and several notices are published as PDF files. The fetch tool used in this run returns PDF bytes without extracting text, so we downloaded each PDF and extracted its text with the pypdf library before caching it. Web pages were fetched with the fetch tool and cached as returned.
