1. What the company does

Tencent is a Chinese internet company that earns money in three main ways. Users pay it for games and digital content, advertisers pay it to reach those users, and businesses pay it for payments and cloud services[8]. Everything sits on top of Weixin, the chat and life app that connects people to friends, shops, games, video and payments. In June 2026 the combined audience of Weixin and WeChat was 1,439 million people a month, up 2% from a year earlier[8].

Think of Weixin as a shopping mall where Tencent owns the entrance. People walk in to talk to friends, then browse short videos, play a mini game or pay a bill without leaving the app. Advertisers rent space in the mall. Game players and music fans pay for items and subscriptions. Shops, banks and cloud customers pay Tencent fees for tools. This is why one app can feed four different revenue lines.

Games are the largest single piece of the value-added services line. Domestic Games earned RMB47.3 billion in the June 2026 quarter, up 17%, led by Delta Force, VALORANT and the new title Roco Kingdom: World. International Games earned RMB18.6 billion, down 0.8% as reported but up 4% once currency moves are removed, because certain Supercell games earned less[8]. Marketing Services earned RMB43.6 billion, up 22%, helped by AI that picks which ad each user sees[8]. FinTech and Business Services earned RMB60.3 billion, up 9%, with cloud revenue rising on AI demand[8].

Tencent reports in Chinese yuan (RMB) under IFRS, the international accounting standard, and files its annual report and results with the Hong Kong exchange. Its US-traded shares are an unsponsored ADR, a certificate issued by banks such as Citi without Tencent running the program[16]. We found no 10-K or 20-F for Tencent, so the primary sources in this book are HKEXnews filings and tencent.com investor pages. Every currency figure in this book is shown in RMB as filed, and a US dollar line is added using one rate: 6.6861 RMB per dollar from the Federal Reserve on 16 October 2026[21].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The first strength is reach. Weixin and WeChat had 1,439 million monthly users in June 2026 against 1,411 million a year earlier, which is slow growth but on an audience that already covers most of China[8]. Reach lets Tencent test new products inside an app people already open every day. Video Accounts, the short video feed inside Weixin, grew total user time by more than 20% in the quarter, and Mini Shops, Mini Games and Mini Programs sit in the same app[8][5].

The second strength is money earned from that attention. Marketing Services grew 19% in 2025 to RMB145.0 billion and 22% in the June 2026 quarter, and management credits better AI ad targeting and a growing share of ads that end in a purchase inside Weixin[5][8]. Gross margin, the share of revenue left after direct costs, rose from 53% in 2024 to 56% in 2025 and was 58% in the June 2026 quarter[5][8]. Rising margin on rising revenue is the sign of a business that gets paid more for each user.

The third strength is a games portfolio that does not depend on one hit. Delta Force and VALORANT PC reached lifetime highs in average daily players in the June 2026 quarter, and Roco Kingdom: World ranked first among new mobile titles in China so far this year by daily players and by gross receipts[8]. China's game regulator approved 206 domestic titles in September 2026 and 1,571 in the first nine months, about 23% more than the same period of 2025, so the licence queue is open for now[33][34]. The same source notes that new licences stopped around April 2021 and restarted in April 2022, which shows the queue can close[34].

The weak spots are real. QQ, the older chat app, fell 2% to 520 million monthly users, and paid VAS subscriptions fell 2% to 259 million[8]. Social Networks revenue grew only 0.8%. International Games shrank 0.8% in reported terms. New AI products pulled the non-IFRS operating margin down to 37% from 38%, while the margin without them rose to 42% from 39%[8]. Most important, capital expenditure, meaning money spent on buildings and computers, rose 176% to RMB52.8 billion in one quarter[8]. The business is therefore strong in its core and being asked to fund a very large new AI bet at the same time. The next chapter scores that trade-off with five measurable tests.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means checking what management said it would do against what it did. In March 2025 Tencent said it intended to carry out a buyback of at least HKD80 billion during 2025; a buyback is a company buying its own shares[2]. It bought back about 153.4 million shares for HKD80.0 billion, exactly at the floor it set[5]. It also raised the annual dividend to HKD5.30 per share for 2025 from HKD4.50[1].

The same March 2025 presentation said capital expenditure would rise toward the low teens as a share of revenue[11]. Actual 2025 spending was RMB79.2 billion, or 10.5% of revenue, so Tencent spent less than it signalled in 2025 and then spent far more in 2026[5][1]. Capital expenditure in the first half of 2026 was RMB84.7 billion, already above all of 2025, and 21% of first-half revenue[8][7]. The June 2026 call added that management may steer more cash to capital spending and potentially less to buybacks, and that the AI build is a lump sum over this year and next[12].

The record is therefore mixed in a useful way. Promises about shareholder returns were kept to the letter. Promises about spending were kept in 2025 and then outrun in 2026, and management itself says buybacks may slow. First-half 2026 buybacks were HKD24.5 billion (HKD7.6 billion plus HKD16.9 billion) compared with HKD80.0 billion for all of 2025[7][8][5].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above rests on assumptions that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest assumption in each estimate, gives the bear case against our own view, and says when the evidence arrives. The bear case, in one line, is that a high-quality business can still be a poor holding when its owners are also its biggest spenders and a government blacklist limits who can buy it. Our modeled bear price covers an earnings and multiple miss only; years of near-zero free cash flow and a future US purchase restriction are tail risks we describe but do not put in a price.

Fatal flaw 1: the AI build does not pay back

The weakest assumption in the base case is that consensus 2027 earnings of RMB30.59 per share survive a much larger cost base. Capital expenditure was RMB84.7 billion in six months, and depreciation on the new equipment will reach the income statement for years[8][7]. Management says the model-building part is a lump sum and that some computing it ordered months ago could be sold at more than a 30% profit, which is encouraging but is a statement, not an audited result[12].

If external demand for computing fades, Tencent would own expensive equipment with lower returns. The evidence arrives in the 2026 third-quarter report on 2026-11-18 and the 2026 annual results in March 2027 (company calendar; the board notice has not been published as of 24 October)[18].

Fatal flaw 2: US policy closes the ADR route

The US Defense Department's June 2026 notice lists Tencent as indirectly affiliated with the People's Liberation Army, and Tencent has been trying to get off the list since it was added in 2025[13][14]. A law passed in the 2026 defence bill requires a report within two years on whether 1260H companies belong on the Treasury list that bars US persons from buying or selling their publicly traded securities[15].

This is a tail risk, not a base case, and we assign no number to it. But it is the one risk that could make an owner's problem unfixable, because a forced sale by US holders would hit the ADR line while the Hong Kong line is unaffected.
