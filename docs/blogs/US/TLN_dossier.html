1. What the company does

Talen Energy is an independent power producer. That means it owns power plants but does not sell electricity to homes. It sells three things into the PJM grid, which serves all or parts of 13 states and the District of Columbia: energy (the megawatt-hours it generates), capacity (a payment for standing ready to run when the grid is short), and smaller grid-support services.[3] The company says it owns about 15.7 gigawatts of plants, including 2.2 gigawatts of nuclear power.[1] One gigawatt is a thousand megawatts.

The nuclear plant is the centre of the story. Talen owns 90% of the 2.5 gigawatt Susquehanna plant in Pennsylvania, which made about 17 terawatt-hours of carbon-free power in 2025 at an all-in cost of about $27 per megawatt-hour.[3] In March 2024 Talen sold the data-center campus beside the plant to AWS (Amazon Web Services) for gross proceeds of $650 million, and it now sells power to AWS under a power purchase agreement (PPA), a long contract that fixes who buys the power and how much.[3] The deal moved to a front-of-the-meter structure in April 2026. That means the power now flows through the grid rather than over a private wire.[2] The contract covers up to 1,920 megawatts through 2042, has minimum commitments, and ramps up to full volume no later than 2032.[3] The filings describe the price only as anticipated premium prices and give no dollar figure, so we cannot test it.[3]

The other plants earn money from the market. PJM runs an auction three years ahead in which plants are paid to be available. For the 2028/2029 delivery year Talen cleared 10,180 megawatts at $325 per megawatt-day, about $1,208 million of capacity revenue.[14] Two Maryland plants, Brandon Shores and H.A. Wagner, earn fixed payments of $145 million and $35 million a year under reliability-must-run agreements that keep them open until May 31, 2029, and PJM has asked to extend them to 2031.[2] Talen also benefits from the federal nuclear production tax credit under the Inflation Reduction Act, and says its hedge percentages include that credit.[3][1]

Talen has grown fast through purchases, and that is why its history is hard to read. It bought the Freedom and Guernsey gas plants (about 2.8 gigawatts) in November 2025 for $3.8 billion in cash, and it bought the Lawrenceburg, Waterford and Darby plants in June 2026 for $3.5 billion, paid as $2.6 billion in cash and 2,399,998 new shares worth $927 million.[5][2] Total output rose from 17.0 terawatt-hours in the first half of 2025 to 29.7 in the first half of 2026.[1] The company also emerged from bankruptcy in the second quarter of 2023, so its statements split 2023 into two periods that are not comparable, and no source publishes a three-year median valuation for the stock, which has a short history since the reorganization.[6]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • PJM is the grid operator for 13 states and the District of Columbia. It runs the markets where Talen sells energy and capacity.
  • Capacity payment is money paid to a plant for being ready to run, whether or not it runs. PJM sets the price in an auction held about three years ahead.
  • PPA stands for power purchase agreement, a long contract to sell power at agreed terms. Talen's AWS contract is the main example.
  • RMR means reliability-must-run: a plant that would close is paid fixed fees to stay open because the grid needs it.
  • Adjusted EBITDA is the company's own profit measure, adjusted by Talen for items such as price swings on hedges and trust-fund gains. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of cash profit before debt costs and plant wear.
  • Adjusted FCF is Talen's measure of free cash flow (FCF), the cash left after running the business, buying equipment and paying interest and taxes. Talen's version excludes growth spending, so it can be higher than the figure from the cash flow statement.
  • EPS means earnings per share, the company's profit divided by its share count.
  • SBC means stock-based compensation, the cost of paying staff in shares or share-linked awards.
  • ROIC means return on invested capital, the profit earned on the money invested in the business.
  • RSI is the relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has been moving; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price swing in dollars. Here it is $18.92, about 5.8% of the share price.
  • ASR is an accelerated share repurchase: a company pays a bank up front and the bank delivers shares over time.
  • Spark spread is the gap between the power price and the cost of the gas needed to make it. A wider spread means a gas plant earns more.
  • Basis is the price gap between one location and a hub. Talen's Pennsylvania zone has sold at a discount to the main PJM West hub, and that discount widened.
  • Net leverage is net debt (debt minus cash) divided by adjusted EBITDA. The company's target is 3.5 times.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest part of the business is low-cost, hard-to-replace supply in a grid that is short of it. In July 2026 PJM cleared its 2028/2029 auction with 6,831.3 megawatts less supply than its own reliability requirement, and the reserve margin was 14.4% against a 20% target.[26] Every price in that auction cleared at the cap of $325.00, and PJM's simulation without the cap came out at $554.72.[26] A plant that already exists, already has a grid connection and runs at low cost is worth more in that setting, and Susquehanna fits that description at about $27 per megawatt-hour all-in.[3]

Location adds to the case. Management says its plants sit mainly in the PPL zone of Pennsylvania and in AEP Ohio, where data-center demand is building, and that PPL has about twice as much generation as load.[18] The same call shows the other side: the price discount of the PPL zone to the PJM West hub widened from about $9 to about $20 per megawatt-hour, which cuts what Talen earns on each unit it sells there.[18] Management says each $1 of basis improvement is worth about $1 of adjusted free cash flow per share, so the discount is a real swing factor in both directions.[18]

The contract book is thin compared with the size of the fleet. Management says contracted margin is about 10% today and rises to 35% as the AWS campus ramps between 2028 and 2030, and it hopes another two gigawatts of long contracts could lift that toward 60%.[18] Talen reports a pipeline of about 4 gigawatts of land and data-center contracting options but the filings we read show no second contract of AWS's size.[1] Until it does, most cash flow depends on market prices and PJM rules, and hedges cover only about 70% of 2027 and 30% of 2028 output.[1]

The weaknesses are debt, rule risk and quality of earnings. Debt rose from $3.0 billion at the end of 2024 to $9.6 billion in June 2026 because Talen paid for two acquisitions with borrowed money.[3][2] PJM's capacity price cap of $325 per megawatt-day was extended for the 2028/2029 and 2029/2030 years, and FERC (the federal energy regulator) has pending proceedings on co-located loads and a new backstop auction.[2][27] Reported profit is swollen and then cut by items that are not cash: a $501 million stock-award charge in the fourth quarter of 2025 and a $324 million loss on hedges in the first half of 2026.[5][1] Chief executive Mac McFarland will retire on January 1, 2027, and president Terry Nutt, formerly the chief financial officer, takes over.[15][16]
