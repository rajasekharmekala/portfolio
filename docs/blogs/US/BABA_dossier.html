Before you read

Alibaba runs the Taobao and Tmall online marketplaces and Alibaba Cloud, and its shares trade in New York as BABA, each American depositary share (ADS) standing for eight ordinary shares.[1] We rate it REJECTED because the chart is in Stage 4, the confirmed downtrend, and free cash flow over the last twelve months was negative at -6.9% of sales.[1][2] Cloud revenue grew 45% in the June quarter, but marketplace fees fell 7% and capital spending reached RMB 67.7 billion.[1] Our base value is $98.25 against a $99.64 close, so the true reward-to-risk is negative. The call is wrong if cloud profit and cash flow improve faster than we model, which the September-quarter report, expected near 24 November 2026 but not confirmed, would show first[23][24].

1. What the company does

Alibaba earns money in three ways. First, merchants pay it to reach shoppers on Taobao and Tmall. That income is called customer management revenue, the fees merchants pay the marketplaces, and it was RMB 343,867 million in fiscal 2026, which is 33.6% of group revenue by our calculation.[2] Second, it sells and delivers goods itself: quick commerce (Taobao Instant Commerce, Freshippo and other on-demand delivery), cross-border retail through AliExpress, and wholesale through 1688 and Alibaba.com.[1][2] Third, it rents out computing power through Alibaba Cloud, and since the June 2026 quarter that business is reported together with T-Head, its own chip design unit, as AI Cloud and Compute Services.[1]

The 2026 reorganisation matters for reading the numbers. The company now reports four segments, and it recast the prior-year quarter to match, so growth rates in the table below compare like with like. The e-commerce segment still produces nearly all of the profit: RMB 39,749 million of the RMB 27,329 million group adjusted EBITA, because the AI segments and other units lose money. Adjusted EBITA is the company's own profit measure before share-based pay, intangible amortisation and one-off items.[1] The AI Labs and Applications segment lost RMB 13,861 million in the quarter, against a loss of RMB 3,224 million a year earlier, and management says the loss came from AI investment and the higher inference cost, the computing bill for answering user requests, of the Qwen app.[1]

The legal shape of the company explains several risks later in this report. Alibaba Group Holding Limited is a Cayman Islands holding company that does not run the business itself. Because Chinese law restricts foreign ownership of internet businesses, it controls them through variable interest entities (VIEs), contracts with Chinese-owned companies, and its own filing says those contracts have not been tested in a court of law.[2] The ADS ratio is eight ordinary shares per ADS. The shares have been listed in Hong Kong since 2019 and became a primary listing on 28 August 2024, so the stock trades on two exchanges.[2] Directors are nominated by the Alibaba Partnership, a structure that Hong Kong rules classify as weighted voting rights, although each share has one vote.[1]

What the money is being spent on

The company calls its plan a full-stack AI strategy: its own chips, cloud, models and applications. Capital expenditure, the money spent on data centres and servers, was RMB 67,678 million in the June quarter, up 75% from RMB 38,676 million a year earlier, while cash flow from operations was only RMB 22,945 million.[1] The gap was covered by cash on hand, bank borrowing of RMB 11,005 million net, and then by the HK$80 billion share placement that closed on 26 August 2026, which the company said it will spend on computing capacity (about 60%) and hyperscale data centres and cloud upgrades (about 40%).[1][15] The point for an owner is that the business is funding growth from outside, not from its own cash flow, and Chapter 3 measures how large that gap is.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The first strength is the marketplace engine. Customer management revenue was RMB 343,867 million in fiscal 2026, up 5%, and the company says the rise came mainly from a higher take rate, the share of each sale that the marketplace keeps.[5] The number of 88VIP members, its highest-spending shoppers, rose by double digits to about 64 million by 30 June 2026.[1] That base is why the company can still fund new bets from this profit pool. The warning in the same filings is direction: in the June quarter customer management revenue fell 7%, and only rose 1% when the contra-revenue effect of a new merchant programme is removed, down from like-for-like growth of 8% in the March quarter.[1][5] A company whose core fee income slows from +8% to +1% in one quarter, while it spends heavily elsewhere, has less room for error.

The second strength is cloud scale. Revenue from AI Cloud and Compute Services rose 45% to RMB 48,437 million, and revenue from AI-related products alone was RMB 12,376 million, the twelfth quarter in a row of triple-digit growth.[1] The company cites a research firm, Omdia, for a 38.1% share of China's AI cloud market, which is a company-reported ranking we did not verify against the Omdia report.[1] Profit is following: segment adjusted EBITA rose 133% to RMB 5,628 million, an 11.6% margin by our calculation, against 7.2% a year earlier.[1] Management says compute demand will keep outstripping supply, and that its T-Head chips now serve more than 650 outside customers.[18][1]

The weakness is what it costs to compete for everyday shopping. Sales and marketing expense rose from RMB 144,021 million to RMB 245,023 million in fiscal 2026, from 14.5% to 23.9% of revenue by our calculation, while income from operations fell 64% to RMB 50,150 million.[2] The China E-commerce Group's adjusted EBITA fell 44% to RMB 107,509 million for the year, and for the March 2026 quarter management said that, excluding the loss from quick commerce, that group's profit would have been stable.[2][19] Quick commerce revenue grew 47% in fiscal 2026 and 45% in the June quarter, so the business is winning orders, but the company does not publish its profit or loss, and the stated aim is overall profitability in fiscal 2029.[2][1][18] In the June quarter the subsidies partly moved into a contra-revenue line, which lowered reported sales and marketing expense to 17.7% of revenue from 21.5%, so the expense ratio improved while the fee revenue line was held down.[1]

The fourth point is the risk list, which is heavier than for a US company. The US Department of Defense published a list of Chinese military companies that includes Alibaba on 8 June 2026 (the Federal Register notice followed on 10 June). The European Commission fined it under the Digital Services Act, and the company booked a provision for EUR 550 million in the June quarter. And its 20-F lists tariffs, the VIE structure and audit-inspection rules among the risks.[9][1][2] None of these has yet stopped the business from operating, but each can raise costs or limit who may own the shares, and Chapter 6 treats them as scenarios.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record on investment promises is good: it said it would spend heavily on AI and cloud, and it did, ahead of its own plan. The record on returns to owners is less steady. It repurchased about 1.2 billion shares in each of fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2025, then almost stopped buying, and in August 2026 it raised new equity at HK$112.70 per share.[2][14] A reader can fairly call this a change in priorities, since management says it will adjust to market conditions.[18] The prices paid in the last buyback months, US$11.97 to US$16.79 per ordinary share, bracket the US$14.43 per share that the placement price equals at the company's June rate, so buying back and then selling new shares was not a trade across very different prices.[5][14][1]

Management gives no numeric earnings guidance, so the test of its forecasting is the Street's. Non-GAAP earnings per ADS (profit per ADS before share-based pay, investment gains and one-offs) missed the Street estimate in each of the last four quarters: RMB 4.36 against 5.78 in September 2025, 7.09 against 10.94, 0.62 against 5.74, and 8.52 against 10.82 in June 2026.[21] That is the Street's miss, not a broken promise by the company, but it shows that analysts keep underestimating how much investment is running through the profit and loss account.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of audited results show a business that was throwing off cash through fiscal 2024 and is now investing more than it earns. Revenue grew from RMB 853,062 million in fiscal 2022 to RMB 1,023,670 million in fiscal 2026, a compound growth of 5.6% a year over the last three years, which is slow for a company priced as a growth story.[3][2] Part of the slowdown is sales of businesses: the company says that without Sun Art and Intime, which it disposed of, fiscal 2026 revenue would have grown 11% rather than 3%.[5] Gross margin, revenue less cost of revenue as a share of sales, rose from 36.8% to 39.8%, then slipped to 38.2% in the last twelve months as cloud, which costs more to serve than marketplace fees, became a larger share of sales.[3][2][1]

Profit tells a more complicated story. Income from operations, profit from running the business before interest and investment gains, peaked at RMB 140,905 million in fiscal 2025 and fell to RMB 50,150 million in fiscal 2026, and to RMB 30,323 million over the last twelve months, a 2.9% margin.[2][1] Net income held up far better, RMB 105,904 million in fiscal 2026, because interest and investment income was RMB 87,512 million, mostly gains on investments the company holds, which is not repeatable.[2] So a trailing earnings multiple of 22.7 times, our calculation at RMB 6.7851 per dollar, flatters the stock, and the multiple on the company's own non-GAAP earnings is 32.9 times.[1][5]

Cash is where the change shows most. Cash flow from operations fell from RMB 199,752 million in fiscal 2023 to RMB 76,213 million in fiscal 2026, while capital expenditure rose from RMB 32,087 million (fiscal 2024) to RMB 126,063 million.[3][2] Free cash flow, the cash left after buying equipment, turned negative for the first time in this five-year window, at RMB -46,609 million in fiscal 2026 and RMB -72,464 million over the last twelve months.[2][1] In the June quarter alone, capital expenditure of RMB 67,678 million was about three times cash flow from operations of RMB 22,945 million.[1]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 of our framework is the test of static financial levels and of the rate of change. The levels are a return on invested capital (ROIC, profit earned on each dollar put into the business) of at least 12%, a free cash flow margin of at least 12%, net debt (borrowings less cash) below 3.0 times EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), and stock-based compensation (SBC, the cost of paying staff in shares) below 8% of revenue. The hard red lines are net debt of 4.0 times EBITDA or more with interest cover below 2.0 times, a Stage 4 chart with no base, and an accounting-fraud or going-concern warning. Alibaba passes the two balance-sheet floors comfortably because it has net cash, fails the two profitability floors, and fails the Stage 4 red line, so the gate fails. The trajectory column matters because a Turnaround archetype needs at least two improving signals, and the table counts them.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that can fail. This chapter names the weakest one for each estimate, gives the bear case against our own conclusion, and states the evidence and date that would settle each question. The bear case is the strongest honest argument that the verdict is right for the wrong reason, or wrong in a direction we have not weighted.

Fatal flaw 1: the capital-spending cash never comes back

The bull case needs the AI and cloud equipment to repay itself in about three years. Management gave that figure itself, and said it expects to shorten the payback to 2.5 years as gross margins rise.[18] Capital spending in the last twelve months was RMB 155,065 million, and purchase commitments for co-location, bandwidth, licensed content and marketing rose from RMB 84,226 million to RMB 200,062 million in a year, which means the fixed cost base is already committed.[2] If demand slows or chip costs rise, the company would hold a large set of costs with falling prices. The June release itself said higher chip component prices contributed to the rise in spending.[1] Evidence that this is failing would be a cloud margin that stalls below 10%, or a free cash flow outflow that stays above RMB 40,000 million a quarter for two more quarters.

Fatal flaw 2: the old profit engine erodes faster than the new one grows

Marketplace fees are the profit source that pays for everything else. They fell 7% in the June quarter, and the company's own like-for-like measure slowed from +8% to +1% in one quarter.[5][1] Quick commerce is gaining orders and share, but management says it will reach overall profitability in fiscal 2029, three years out, so subsidies must continue.[18] The e-commerce segment's adjusted EBITA of RMB 39,749 million is what holds group profit up.
