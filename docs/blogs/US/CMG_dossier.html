1. What the company does

Chipotle sells burritos, burrito bowls, tacos, quesadillas and salads made from fresh ingredients, and it cooks and serves them in restaurants it owns. At June 30, 2026 it owned 4,074 restaurants in the United States and 112 abroad, and a further 15 abroad were run by partners [5]. That ownership is unusual: the company says it is the only restaurant company of its size that owns and operates all of its restaurants in the United States, Canada and Europe [6]. Because it owns the stores, every dollar of sales is Chipotle's revenue, but every dollar of food, wage and rent cost is also Chipotle's cost.

Revenue was $11,925.6 million in 2025, up 5.4 percent, and $12,423.8 million over the twelve months to June 30, 2026 (our calculation from the annual and quarterly filings) [1][5]. Growth comes from two sources. Comparable restaurant sales, the change in sales at restaurants open at least 13 months, was a decline of 1.7 percent in 2025 and a gain of 2.2 percent in the second quarter of 2026 [1][6]. New restaurants add the rest. In the first half of 2026, comparable sales added $79.9 million of revenue against $434.0 million from restaurants opened in 2025 and 2026 (our sum of the two new-restaurant lines) [5]. So the company is growing mainly by building, and the quality of its existing stores is the open question.

A guest orders in the restaurant, through the app or website, through a delivery service, or in a Chipotlane. A Chipotlane is a drive-through pickup lane for orders placed ahead of time. Digital orders were 38.3 percent of food and beverage revenue in the second quarter of 2026, up from 35.5 percent a year earlier, and 80 of the 100 restaurants opened in that quarter had a Chipotlane [6]. Management expects 350 to 370 openings in 2026, around 80 percent of company-owned openings with a Chipotlane, and says North America can hold at least 7,000 restaurants [6][20].

Each sales dollar is split between four restaurant costs and what is left. Food was 29.7 percent of revenue in the second quarter of 2026 and labor 25.0 percent, both higher than a year earlier [6]. What remains after food, labor, rent and other restaurant costs is the restaurant-level operating margin. It was 25.2 percent in the second quarter, down from 27.4 percent [6]. That margin pays for head-office costs, depreciation and tax, so it is the best single measure of how profitable a Chipotle restaurant is.

Who pays Chipotle, and why they pay it

The customer is a person buying a meal, and the pitch is fresh food made in front of them. The company's own words for its 2026 plan, called the Recipe for Growth, name four drivers: menu innovation, the Chipotle Rewards program, hospitality in each restaurant and group orders [6]. Bloomberg's reporting adds the other side: guests who had grown weary of high-priced bowls triggered sales declines in recent quarters [22]. What this means for an owner is that Chipotle's pricing power is real but finite. The company raised menu prices 2.1 percent in 2025 and only 1.3 percent in the first half of 2026 [1][5], and it now wants traffic, not price, to carry growth. Chapter 2 tests whether that is working.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Comparable restaurant sales: the change in sales at restaurants open at least 13 full months. It strips out the effect of opening new ones.
  • Transactions: the number of orders. When transactions rise, more guests are visiting. When only the average check rises, price is doing the work.
  • Restaurant-level operating margin: revenue less food, labor, rent and other restaurant costs, as a share of revenue.
  • Chipotlane: a drive-through lane used only to pick up digital orders.
  • Guidance: guidance is the forecast management gives investors for the year ahead, such as expected sales growth and number of new restaurants.
  • Buyback: a buyback is the company buying its own shares in the market, which shrinks the share count.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Chipotle's competitive position rests on three things: the economics of each new restaurant, the control that comes from owning every store, and a large digital share of sales. The evidence for the first is strong. Management says new restaurants reach about 80 percent of the sales of an average older restaurant and that year-two cash-on-cash returns are about 60 percent, meaning each new store earns back roughly 60 cents a year per dollar invested [20]. The company opened 334 restaurants in 2025, which management called a record number [8], and the owned count reached 4,186 at June 30, 2026, up 9.0 percent from 3,839 a year earlier (our calculation) [8][5]. These returns are management's statement on a call and not an audited figure, so we treat them as strong but unverified.

The weak side is the existing restaurant base. Comparable sales fell 1.7 percent in 2025 and transactions fell 2.9 percent [1]. Average restaurant sales slipped from $3.213 million to $3.104 million, a drop of 3.4 percent [1]. Some of that is the arithmetic of new stores that sell less than old ones, and management says the new openings take about 100 basis points (one percentage point) off comparable sales [20]. The rest is lost traffic. In the second quarter of 2026 transactions turned up 1.0 percent, but that came against a comparison of minus 4.9 percent a year earlier, so part of the gain is an easy comparison and not new strength [5].

The newest evidence is mixed and recent. The company raised its 2026 comparable sales outlook in July from about flat to the low single digits [6][7], and the stock closed more than 12 percent higher the next day [19]. Since then the news has turned. TD Cowen cut its fourth-quarter comparable sales estimate to 1 percent from 4 percent, against 2.9 percent for the consensus estimate, the average of analyst forecasts, citing a weak start to October [22]. The company itself warned in its quarterly filing that it had seen low-single-digit headwinds to sales trends from food safety concerns and geopolitical developments [5]. We weight the analyst view cautiously, because it rests on alternative data, meaning third-party tracking and not Chipotle's own figures [25], but it points the same way as the company's own warning.

What the food-safety events did

Two food-safety stories hit the sector this summer. The first was a cyclospora outbreak linked to lettuce, which made guests avoid salads and greens across the industry. Chipotle's chief financial officer said sales softened by about 200 basis points in the second half of July [18], and the company built that into its third-quarter forecast of about 1 percent comparable sales growth [20][21]. The second was a salmonella outbreak traced to imported jalapeños. Chipotle pulled the peppers on August 4 and said its forecast did not include any financial impact [12][21]. The shares fell nearly 10 percent that day [21]. The CDC closed the investigation on October 2 with 488 cases in 34 states and no deaths, and it named Chipotle Mexican Grill and QDOBA among the places where sick people had eaten [17].

The consequence is that the company's forecast carried one scare and left out the other, and the October softness analysts describe may include both. The third-quarter report on October 28 is the first hard data on how large the damage was [21][16].

How Chipotle compares with its peers

Among restaurant peers Chipotle sits in the middle on price and third on size. At $27.52 it trades at 25.5 times trailing earnings, against 20.18 for McDonald's and 28.37 for Wingstop [29][31][34]. Its operating margin of 15.27 percent is far below McDonald's 45.72 percent, which reflects a franchise model that collects fees and does not run stores, but above Starbucks at 10.37 percent [29][31][32]. The gap matters for the risk: because Chipotle owns every location, soft sales flow straight to its own profit, while a franchisor's fee income is cushioned. That is the strength and the weakness of the model in one sentence.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk compares what management promised with what happened. The comparison favors the opening plan and the cash returns, and it does not favor the sales forecasts. For 2025, the company guided in February to comparable sales growth in the low to mid single digits [11], cut that to about flat in July [10], and cut it again to a decline in October [9]. The year ended at minus 1.7 percent [8]. For 2026 the direction reversed: about flat in February and April, then low single digits in July [8][7][6]. A forecast raised in July is a good sign, but it was raised just before the cyclospora and salmonella news and before the October slowdown, so it is the next promise at risk.

Capital allocation shows the other side of discipline. In 2025 Chipotle spent $2.42 billion on buybacks at an average $42.54 a share, and in the first half of 2026 it spent a further $1.35 billion at an average $34.35 [1][5][20]. Against today's $27.52 close those purchases are underwater by 35 percent and 20 percent. Cash and investments fell from $2.29 billion at the end of 2024 to $0.77 billion at June 30, 2026, and the company still has no borrowings [1][5][20]. This is an honest reading of the record: management kept its promise to return cash, and the price paid was too high in hindsight.
