1. What the company does

Chipotle owns and operates restaurants that sell burritos, burrito bowls (a burrito without the tortilla), quesadillas, tacos and salads. The first restaurant opened in Denver in 1993.[1] At the end of 2025 the company owned 3,938 restaurants in the United States and 104 abroad, and it counted 14 more run by partners.[1] By June 30, 2026 the company-owned total had reached 4,186, with 15 partner-operated restaurants.[13] Chipotle manages the United States in 11 regions but reports one business segment, and all of its revenue comes from sales in its restaurants.[1] Guests pay at the counter, through the app or website, or through delivery apps, and digital orders were 38.3% of food and beverage sales in the second quarter of 2026, up from 35.5% a year earlier.[12]

Revenue in 2025 was $11,925.6 million. Food and beverage sales were $11,866.1 million, and a small delivery-fee line added $59.6 million.[3] Growth comes mostly from opening new restaurants. In 2025 restaurants opened in 2024 and 2025 that are not yet in the comparison group added $481.8 million and $327.0 million of sales, while the restaurants already in the group cut sales by $191.3 million.[2] The company owns and operates every restaurant in the United States, Canada and Europe, so it keeps the restaurant profit that a franchisor would hand to franchisees. It uses partners in the Middle East, and on July 16, 2026 it opened its first Mexico restaurant with the operator Alsea.[31]

The unit economics are the core of the investment idea. Restaurant-level operating margin, which is sales less food, labor, occupancy and other restaurant costs as a share of sales, was 25.4% in 2025 against 26.7% in 2024.[25][3] Average restaurant sales were $3.104 million in 2025.[2] A new restaurant cost about $1.5 million to build in 2025, or about $1.3 million after landlord payments, so a typical restaurant sells roughly two dollars a year for each dollar invested.[2] A Chipotlane is a drive-through lane used only to pick up digital orders. The company opened its 1,500th on September 29, 2026, and 257 of the 334 restaurants it opened in 2025 had one.[30]

Management calls its plan Recipe for Growth. It has five parts: protect the core menu and service, push menu news and new occasions, modernize the business with technology and a relaunched rewards program, expand globally, and develop talent.[1] The stated long-term goal is 7,000 restaurants in the United States and Canada.[30] For 2026 the company expects 350 to 370 openings and about 80% of new company-owned restaurants with a Chipotlane.[12] The next report is the third-quarter 2026 release on Wednesday, October 28, 2026, which the company says it will issue at about 4:10 p.m. ET, with a call at 4:30 p.m. ET.[26][27]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Comparable restaurant sales: the change in sales at restaurants open at least 13 full months; it removes the effect of new openings.
  • Transactions (traffic): the number of orders in comparable restaurants; the part of sales growth that is not price.
  • Average check: sales per order; it rises with menu prices and with what guests choose to add.
  • Restaurant-level operating margin: restaurant sales less restaurant costs, as a share of sales, before head-office costs and depreciation.
  • Average restaurant sales: trailing twelve-month sales of restaurants open at least 12 months; the industry calls it average unit volume.
  • Chipotlane: a drive-through lane for picking up digital orders only.
  • Partner-operated restaurant: a Chipotle restaurant run by another company under a license or franchise agreement.
  • Recipe for Growth: the name of management's five-part strategy.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Chipotle's strength is the economics of one restaurant. The company earned a 25.4% restaurant-level margin in 2025 on $3.1 million of average sales, and it reports no borrowed money and a $500.0 million revolving credit line that was undrawn.[25][2] By our arithmetic, return on invested capital (ROIC) was about 20% when leases are treated as debt, which is the cautious way to count it, and a data vendor shows 21%.[36] The second strength is control: because the company owns its restaurants, it controls menu, price and staffing in every one of them.[1] The third is the guest relationship, with 36.7% of 2025 sales ordered digitally and a rewards program that management is relaunching as part of its strategy.[1]

The weakness is that the same-store engine stalled. In 2025 comparable restaurant sales fell 1.7%, because transactions fell 2.9% while the average check rose 1.2%.[2] Average restaurant sales fell from $3.213 million in 2024 to $3.104 million in 2025, and they were $3.102 million in the second quarter of 2026.[2][13] Costs rose faster than sales. The operating margin, the share of sales left after all running costs, was 15.7% in the second quarter against 18.2% a year earlier, and restaurant-level margin was 25.2% against 27.4%.[12] Management blamed beef and freight inflation, more protein and produce used per order, and higher pay and bonuses.[12]

Peers show how much room there is. In the same quarter CAVA's same-restaurant sales rose 9.0% with traffic up 5.3%, Starbucks' comparable sales rose 7.9%, Shake Shack's rose 3.5% and McDonald's rose 1.3% worldwide and 0.8% in the United States.[32][35][33][34] Chipotle's 2.2% is fourth of the five, ahead only of McDonald's and well below CAVA and Starbucks.[12] Customers have many places to spend a lunch budget, so Chipotle's moat is brand and operating discipline rather than a patent or a network effect. That kind of moat holds only while guests keep coming, which is what the traffic line tests.

Event and management risks are real. On August 4, 2026 the company disclosed that public health authorities were investigating a salmonella outbreak in the food-service supply chain, that it had removed jalapeños from its restaurants as a precaution, and that its guidance did not include any financial effect.[20] The second-quarter 10-Q adds that the company had recently seen low-single-digit headwinds to comparable sales that it ties to food-safety concerns and geopolitical events.[10] The executive team has also turned over: the legal and marketing chiefs left in January 2026, a new chief brand officer started on June 1, and a chief digital officer was named.[24][23] The board added an independent director with restaurant industry experience on September 14, 2026 and now has 11 directors, 10 of them independent.[22]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chipotle's sales grew from $7.55 billion in 2021 to $11.93 billion in 2025, a compound rate of 12.1% a year, and reached $12.42 billion in the twelve months to June 30, 2026.[8][3][12] The growth rate has changed shape, though. Sales rose 14.4%, 14.3% and 14.6% in 2022, 2023 and 2024, then 5.4% in 2025, when comparable sales turned negative. The first half of 2026 grew 8.4% on new restaurants and a small comparable-sales gain.[3][12]

Profit rose faster than sales until 2024. Operating margin, the share of sales left after all running costs, climbed from 10.7% in 2021 to 16.9% in 2024, then slipped to 16.2% in 2025 and 14.7% over the latest twelve months.[8][3][12] Net margin, profit after every cost as a share of sales, moved from 8.7% to 13.6% and back to 11.4%. Earnings per share (EPS) were $0.46, $0.64, $0.89, $1.11 and $1.14, so the four-year compound rate is 25.6%, but the twelve months to June 2026 are $1.08, below 2025. We divided the 2021 figure by 50 because the 50-for-1 stock split happened in 2024.

Cash tells a steadier story. Operating cash flow rose from $1.28 billion to $2.11 billion over 2021 to 2025, and the company spent most of its cash on buybacks, $2.43 billion in 2025 alone.[8][3] Cash and investments fell from $2.29 billion at the end of 2024 to $0.77 billion at June 30, 2026 because of those buybacks, while shareholders' equity fell from $3.66 billion to $2.20 billion.[3][13] There is no borrowed money, but operating lease liabilities, the present value of rent owed, were $5.42 billion at June 30, 2026.[13]

The number

Chipotle's return on invested capital (ROIC), meaning profit after tax for each dollar of capital put into the business, was about 20.5% over the last twelve months when leases are counted as capital.

How it was calculated

We took twelve-month operating income, subtracted tax at the 24.1% twelve-month effective rate, and divided by the average of invested capital at December 31, 2025 and June 30, 2026, where invested capital is equity plus lease liabilities less cash and investments.

The inputs

Operating income $1,820.1M and tax rate 24.1% from the 2025 10-K and the 2026 release; equity $2,830.6M and $2,199.8M; lease liabilities $5,075.8M and $5,419.2M; cash and investments $1,246.3M and $774.9M. Net operating profit after tax is $1,381.8M and average capital is $6,752.1M.[3][12][13]

The range

Leaving leases out, average capital is only $1,504.6M and the figure is 91.8%, which the buybacks inflate by shrinking equity. A data vendor shows 20.99%.[36] If operating income fell another $300M, ROIC would be about 17%, still above the 12% floor.

If the number is off

Each $100M of operating income is worth about 1.1 points of ROIC. The floor of 12% would need operating income below about $1.07B, so this test is not the weak point; the margin trend that drives it is.

The number

Free cash flow (FCF) over the last twelve months was $1,569.0 million, or 12.6% of sales, which clears the 12% floor by 0.6 of a point.

How it was calculated

We added 2025 operating cash flow to the first half of 2026 and subtracted the first half of 2025, then did the same for capital spending, and divided the difference by twelve-month revenue of $12,423.8M.

The inputs

Operating cash flow $2,113.9M + $1,332.0M - $1,118.4M = $2,327.5M; capital spending $666.3M + $397.6M - $305.4M = $758.5M.[3][13]

The range

If operating cash flow stays at $2,327.5M and capital spending rises to the planned $834.1M, FCF is $1,493.4M, or 12.0%. A 10% drop in operating cash flow would give $1,260.7M, or 10.1%, and a 10% gain would give $1,726.2M, or 13.9%.

If the number is off

The 12% floor passes only if cash flow holds while capital spending grows 25%, which is why we call this pass thin.[2] A second sensitivity from the red-team review: the first half of 2026 gained $125.3M of cash from income tax payable and receivable (+$111.9M against -$13.4M a year earlier). If that tax timing does not repeat, twelve-month FCF would be about $1,443.7M, an 11.62% margin, below the 12% floor; this is a stress assumption, not a forecast.[13] The shared ledger shows $1.116B for the same measure; we could not reproduce it from the filings and explain the difference in the notes.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above rests on two assumptions: that restaurant margin stops falling, and that the stock keeps a forward multiple near 23.55. The weakest of the two is the margin assumption, because margin has fallen year on year in each of the last seven quarters.[12][14][15] This chapter gives three ways the thesis could fail, the strongest bear argument, the single claim everything hangs on, and when we will know.

Fatal flaw 1: a food-safety event hits traffic

On August 4, 2026 the company disclosed a salmonella investigation in the food-service supply chain and removed jalapeños from its restaurants; it said its guidance did not include any financial effect.[20] The 10-Q says recent sales headwinds of low single digits are tied in part to food-safety concerns, and that results could be materially hurt if the trends worsen in the third quarter or later.[10] If traffic turns negative again, comparable sales go back below zero, the +1.0% transactions line disappears, and the 2026 guide would be at risk. The evidence would be third-quarter comparable sales below +1%. We will know on October 28, 2026.

Fatal flaw 2: margins keep falling

Beef and freight inflation, higher pay and bonuses, and the cost of more restaurants all push the margin down, and the 2026 capital spending plan of $834.1 million is 25% above 2025.[12][2] Each point of restaurant margin on 2027 sales is worth about $0.087 a share, so a margin that stays at 25.2% instead of recovering would cut 2027 EPS to about $1.31 or less. The evidence would be third-quarter restaurant-level margin below the 24.5% of a year earlier. We will know on October 28, 2026.

Fatal flaw 3: the traffic recovery is thin and price-led

Second-quarter comparable sales of +2.2% were 1.0% transactions and 1.2% average check, so over half the gain is price.[12] Average restaurant sales are $3.102 million, 3.5% below 2024, and a new restaurant costs about $1.3 million net, so weaker new-unit sales lower the return on every opening.[13][2] The evidence would be transactions falling below zero or average restaurant sales dropping under $3.09 million. We will know on October 28, 2026 and again with fourth-quarter results in early 2027.

Red-team bear case

The strongest bear case is that the market is not mispricing a great business, it is repricing a mature one. Same-store sales have been at or below zero in most quarters since the start of 2025 (-1.7% in 2025, then +0.5% and +2.2% in the first two quarters of 2026), so most growth comes from opening restaurants, 350 to 370 planned for 2026 with $834.1 million of capital spending, and the twelve-month EPS of $1.08 is below 2025.[2][12] Management also bought $2.49 billion of stock in 2025 at about $43.82 a share and has cut cash and investments from $2.29 billion to $0.77 billion since the end of 2024, which leaves little cushion if sales slip.[5][13] A restaurant chain growing sales 9% with falling margin and falling returns on capital can reasonably trade at 22 to 24 times earnings, not 45 times, and a Stage 4 chart says sellers are still in control.
