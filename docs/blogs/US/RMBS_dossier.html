1. What the company does

Rambus is a fabless chip company, which means it designs chips and pays other firms to build them. It sells memory interface chips that sit on the memory modules inside servers. Those chips are the registering clock driver (RCD), the multiplexed versions used on faster modules, the power management integrated circuit (PMIC), the serial presence detect hub and the temperature sensor.[1] A memory module is the small circuit board that holds a server's memory chips, and the interface chips keep the data and timing clean at very high speeds. The customers are memory module makers, server makers and cloud companies, reached through a direct sales force and distributors.[1]

Rambus also sells silicon IP, which means reusable chip-design blocks that other chip makers build into their own products. The list includes controllers for high-bandwidth memory (HBM), graphics memory, PCIe and CXL links, plus security blocks.[1] The third source of money is patent licensing. Companies that include AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Nvidia, Qualcomm, Samsung and SK hynix have licensed Rambus patents, usually under agreements that run up to ten years with fixed, variable or mixed payments.[1] Royalty revenue and contract revenue come from these licences and design projects, and the 10-K warns that they vary from period to period.[2]

The mix is close to half chips and half licensing. In the second quarter of 2026 product revenue was $99.2 million, royalties were $84.2 million and contract and other revenue was $24.0 million, for $207.4 million in all.[7] The table puts that quarter next to the same quarter a year earlier and the last three full years. How the chip business works and what is next

The Rambus chip line is built on DDR5, the current server memory standard. Management said it exited 2025 with a mid-40% share of the registering clock driver market, which is the company's own claim.[27] We could not verify that share, because the outside estimates we found disagree by a wide margin, so we treat it as unverified.[1] In the second quarter of 2026 management said new chips, mainly the power management chip, were still a low double-digit share of product revenue and should reach a mid double-digit share by the end of the fourth quarter.[25] The filings report one product revenue line, so the exact split between the clock driver and the new chips is not published.[2]

The next products are the module chipsets for MRDIMM, a memory module design that doubles the data rate by multiplexing two ranks, and for LPDDR5X SOCAMM2, a compact server module.[1][8] Management values the MRDIMM serviceable market at about $600 million, expects the ramp to start in earnest in 2027, and said the fourth-quarter 2026 contribution would be minimal.[27][25] On the IP side, Rambus launched an HBM4E controller and PCIe 7 switch IP, and said it won a design with a tier 1 U.S. hyperscaler for next-generation HBM.[8][7][25]

Who the customers are

Revenue is concentrated. In 2025 the top five customers made up about 66% of revenue, and two of them were 23% and 18% on their own.[1] In the first half of 2026 the top five were 64%, and in the second quarter alone they were 60%.[5] The 10-K does not name these customers. By location of the contracting party, South Korea was $329.3 million or 46.5% of 2025 revenue, Singapore $163.8 million, the United States $124.1 million and other places $90.5 million.[1] Samsung and SK hynix are Korean memory makers and appear on the patent licensee list, but the filings do not say which customers are A and B, so we do not assign those names.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • DDR5 is the current generation of server memory; each generation moves data faster than the last.
  • RCD (registering clock driver) is the chip on a memory module that buffers commands and clocks so many memory chips can work together.
  • PMIC (power management integrated circuit) is the chip on a module that turns server power into the exact voltages memory needs.
  • MRDIMM is a faster memory module that doubles bandwidth by multiplexing two ranks of memory.
  • HBM (high-bandwidth memory) is stacked memory placed next to AI processors; Rambus sells controller IP for it.
  • Silicon IP means reusable chip-design blocks sold under licence.
  • Fabless means the company designs chips and relies on outside factories to make them.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for Rambus rests on three things: standards-based chips with few suppliers, a patent royalty stream that pays for research, and a cost structure that turns growth into cash. The 10-K names Monolithic Power Systems, Montage Technology, Renesas and Texas Instruments as chip rivals, and Cadence and Synopsys as IP rivals.[1] Memory interface chips follow industry specifications, so the buyer picks from a short list of approved suppliers, and the product has to work at the speed of each new generation. That is a real barrier, but it is shared with Montage and Renesas, so it is not a monopoly.

The financial evidence of strength is in the margins. Total gross margin was 79.6% in 2025, and operating margin was 36.8%, while research and development took 26.5% of revenue.[1] Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of selling the product, shown as a share of revenue. Licensing carries almost no direct cost, which lifts the blend. The chip line alone had a 60.1% gross margin in the second quarter of 2026, and management says its model is 60% to 65%.[7][25]

Where the business is strong

Growth is real. Product revenue rose 40.9% in 2025 and 21.9% in the second quarter of 2026, and the company guided third-quarter product revenue to $110 million to $116 million, which would be about 21% above the $93.3 million of a year earlier at the midpoint.[2][7][10] The balance sheet has no borrowings and $824.9 million of cash and marketable securities, against $163.7 million of convertible notes at the end of 2021.[7][4] Cash generation is strong: cash flow from operations, the cash a business produces before buying equipment or financing, was $360.0 million in 2025.[1]

Where the business is weak

The weak points are concentration, lumpy licensing and a rival with scale. Top-five customers were about 66% of 2025 revenue, and Korea was 46.5%.[1] Royalty revenue fell from $74.0 million to $69.6 million between the first quarters of 2025 and 2026 before rising to $84.2 million in the second quarter, and the company guided third-quarter royalties to $69 million to $75 million, a sequential drop.[8][7] Montage Technology reported a 69.8% gross margin on 19.5% revenue growth for the first quarter of 2026 under China Accounting Standards, which suggests a competitor may have more room to cut prices than Rambus does on chips, although the accounting bases differ.[22]

Since the first quarter of 2026 Rambus has also been responding to a U.S. federal grand jury subpoena in a criminal investigation by the Department of Justice Antitrust Division.[6][5] The filings give no further detail, no accrual and no estimate of cost, and the legal proceedings item says the company is not party to any material pending proceeding.[5] We treat this as an open, unquantified risk and do not guess its scope. Finance leadership also changed: the chief financial officer resigned effective February 27, 2026, an interim officer served until Sumeet Gagneja started on April 29, and the chief accounting officer retired effective September 8.[13][14][12]

The number

We estimate Rambus produced $299.5 million of free cash flow in the twelve months to June 30, 2026, a 39.6% margin, and that after counting SBC as a cost it was $243.5 million, a 32.2% margin.

How it was calculated

Free cash flow is cash flow from operations minus capital expenditure. We took fiscal 2025 operations cash flow of $360.0 million, added the first half of 2026 ($144.5 million) and subtracted the first half of 2025 ($171.8 million), which gives $332.7 million. We did the same for capital spending ($26.8 million plus $20.7 million minus $14.4 million, or $33.2 million). The difference is $299.5 million.[1][5]

The inputs

Fiscal 2025 operating cash flow was $360.019 million and capital expenditure was $26.842 million.[1] First-half 2026 figures were $144.458 million and $20.706 million, and first-half 2025 figures were $171.773 million and $14.379 million.[5] Twelve-month revenue was $756.331 million, which is fiscal 2025 plus the first half of 2026 minus the first half of 2025.[1][7] Stock-based compensation was $54.267 million plus $27.339 million minus $25.616 million, or $55.990 million.[1][5]

If the number is off

A lower base raises the growth the price already assumes. The reverse DCF in Chapter 5 moves from 10.1% a year on $299.5 million to 12.8% a year after SBC. A free cash flow margin below 12% would fail the Layer 1 floor, which is far from the current reading.

The number

We estimate return on invested capital at 31.6% if the cash pile is excluded, and 14.4% if it is included.

How it was calculated

After-tax operating income divided by invested capital. After-tax operating income is the twelve-month operating income of $268.6 million times one minus the twelve-month tax rate of 18.6% ($54.9 million tax on $294.6 million of pre-tax income), which is $218.5 million. Invested capital is equity plus lease and software-licence liabilities, with and without subtracting the $824.9 million of cash and securities.

The inputs

Operating income, tax and pre-tax income come from the filed income statements.[1][7] June 30, 2026 equity was $1,466.659 million and lease liabilities were $21.803 million. Software-licence liabilities were $28.182 million and cash and securities were $824.948 million.[7]

The range

Using average capital for December 2025 and June 2026 instead of the June figure moves the cash-excluded result by less than one point, to about 32%. Either way the result is above the 12% floor.

If the number is off

The cash-included figure has a thin cushion. If after-tax operating income fell about 17%, from $218.5 million to about $182 million, with capital unchanged, ROIC would drop to 12% and breach the floor. On the cash-excluded basis it would take a fall of about 62%. Operating income fell 1.5 margin points in the latest quarter, so we treat the cash-included result as a pass with limited headroom.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter attacks our own estimates. The weakest assumption in the 2028 earnings estimate is that product revenue keeps growing in the mid-teens while chip gross margin holds at or above 60%. Management said on the first-quarter call that supply had not improved, and on the second-quarter call that lead times were lengthening and that it was building inventory ahead of ramps that depend on server platforms launching on time.[27][25] The weakest assumption in the multiple is that investors will still pay 22 times earnings for a business whose revenue is concentrated in a few customers. The weakest assumption in the reverse DCF is that $299.5 million is a fair base, when first-half operating cash flow fell 15.9% year over year.[5]

Our bear case, which we call the bear case and not a red team, is that these assumptions fail together. A memory-maker customer cuts orders after a build year, the MRDIMM ramp slips beyond 2027, and royalty revenue steps down as licences expire, while a fine or settlement from the Justice Department matter lands. That outcome is worth about $60.32 in our scenario table, which is 29.6% below the close.

Fatal flaw 1: the Justice Department criminal investigation

Rambus disclosed that it has been responding since the first quarter of 2026 to a federal grand jury subpoena in a criminal investigation by the Antitrust Division.[6][5] The filings do not say what the subject is, whether the company is a target, or what it might cost, and the 10-K filed in February did not mention it.[1] If the matter led to fines, damages, changes to how the chips are sold or customer pushback, the effect could be larger than any one quarter's operating income. We cannot size it, so we do not model it. We would rerun this dossier if charges, an accrual or a customer reaction appears.

Fatal flaw 2: a handful of customers and a Korea-heavy order book

Top-five customers were about 66% of 2025 revenue, and customers A and B were 23% and 18%.[1] South Korea was 46.5% of 2025 revenue and 46.5% of first-half 2026 revenue, which is the same share to one decimal place.[1][5] If a large customer or a Korean memory maker slows orders, or if licence agreements renew at lower terms, revenue could fall much faster than costs, which would pull the 35% operating margin down quickly. A related tax point is that after a September 2025 ruling the company reserved $82.7 million of Korean withholding-tax refund claims it no longer expects to recover, and it reduced a $114.9 million receivable and the matching payable to zero with no expense effect.[1]

Fatal flaw 3: supply tightness, inventory and a competitor with room to cut prices

Inventory is $74.8 million, up 69.7% in six months, and equal to 172 days of chip cost of sales.[7] Management says the build is planned.[25] If end demand softens, the stock could need to be written down, and chip margins sit just above the 60% bottom of the model. Layer 3 is the third layer of our underwriting, the layer that decides timing and size. It measures how far the price is above the nearest support, in units of the average true range (ATR). The ladder reads this way: within 0.6 ATR is the full-size band, up to 1.2 ATR is the standard band, up to 1.6 ATR is a starter-only band, and anything beyond is a no-chase band. Our support is the 20-day swing low of $84.43, the lowest price in the last 20 sessions. The close, which we also call the current market price (CMP), is $85.65, only 0.22 ATR above it, so the ladder band is full size. The stop uses the same swing low, so there is no second support level to explain, and the 63-day base low of $80.00 is the deeper floor.

The verdict is REJECTED, and its rule is the hard red line in our scoring file: the Stage 4 condition with Test C failing. Chapter 4 checked each part with the ledger's numbers. The ladder band and the quality layers do not override it. The conviction tier is TIER_0, which is zero size. The tiers run from TIER_1, the highest conviction with 7% to 10% of net worth and a risk budget of 100 basis points (bps, hundredths of a percent), to TIER_4 with 1% to 2% and 35 bps. We do not give a higher tier.

Risk-unit sizing sets a position so that a stop hit costs a fixed number of basis points of net worth. The weight is the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. Portfolio heat is the share of net worth that would be lost if every stop were hit at once.
