1. What the company does

Regeneron invents antibody medicines. An antibody is a protein the body already makes to find and block a target, and Regeneron designs fully human versions that doctors give by injection or infusion. The company says it has about 50 product candidates in clinical testing, many of them new uses for medicines already on sale.[1] Its marketed medicines treat eye disease (EYLEA and EYLEA HD), allergic and inflammatory disease (Dupixent and Kevzara), cancer (Libtayo, Lynozyfic and Ordspono), high cholesterol (Praluent) and rare conditions.[2]

Money reaches Regeneron in three ways. In the six months to June 30, 2026, revenue was $7,896.1 million. Net product sales that Regeneron books itself were $3,176.9 million, collaboration revenue from partners was $4,354.7 million, and other revenue, mostly royalties, was $364.5 million.[1] Sanofi was the largest partner by far at $3,779.4 million, and Bayer was second at $563.5 million.[1] Dupixent is the clearest example of the partner model. Sanofi sells it and records the sales, and Regeneron books its share of the profit. In the United States the two companies split Dupixent profit equally, and outside the United States Sanofi's share starts at 65% and falls to 55% as sales rise.[2] Regeneron's profit share was $3,483.4 million in the six months, which is 44.1% of total revenue (our calculation from the two cited figures).[1]

One accounting detail explains why profit is about to rise faster than sales. Until the middle of 2026, part of Regeneron's Dupixent profit went to repay what Sanofi had earlier funded for development, a running total called the Sanofi Development Balance. The company reported that the balance was fully repaid by the end of the second quarter of 2026 and will no longer reduce Sanofi collaboration revenue from the third quarter.[1] On October 1, 2026 the two companies also widened the partnership. Sanofi will pay $1.0 billion up front and up to $7.0 billion more in milestone payments for four new long-acting antibodies that Regeneron invented, and the profits on those products will be shared equally.[8][9]

Regeneron also sells EYLEA, a drug injected into the eye to treat wet age-related macular degeneration and diabetic eye disease. Regeneron records United States sales. Bayer records sales elsewhere and Regeneron books a share of Bayer's profit. EYLEA HD is a higher-dose version that needs fewer injections, and it is the product Regeneron is steering patients toward as copies of the original EYLEA reach the market.[1][3] The next chapter explains how that transition is going.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Antibody: a protein made by the immune system that locks onto one target, which drug makers copy and refine into medicines.
  • Collaboration revenue: money Regeneron books from partners such as Sanofi and Bayer, mostly its share of profit on products the partner sells.
  • Biosimilar: a near copy of a biologic medicine that can be sold once the original's protection ends or is overcome, usually at a lower price.
  • Non-GAAP: company-adjusted figures that leave out items such as stock-based pay, so they run higher than the audited accounting (GAAP) figures.
  • Free cash flow — operating cash left after capital spending — (FCF): cash from operations minus spending on equipment and buildings; here it follows the company's own definition.
  • Stock-based compensation (SBC): pay given as shares or share awards, which costs shareholders through a larger share count.
  • Return on invested capital (ROIC): after-tax operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business.
  • Earnings per share (EPS): profit divided by the number of shares.
  • Average true range (ATR): the typical daily price swing of a stock, used here to measure distance from support and to place the stop.
  • Relative strength index (RSI): a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has moved; readings near 30 mean fast selling and near 70 fast buying.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Regeneron's strength is the rate at which its laboratories turn science into medicines. Dupixent, which Regeneron and Sanofi built together, is used by more than 1.5 million people across nine indications, according to Sanofi.[9] Regeneron's own January 2026 release put active patients at over 1.4 million.[6] Its weakness is the other side of the same fact. Profit leans heavily on one partnered product. The original EYLEA is losing ground to competitors. American drug-price policy and several lawsuits can move results in ways the company does not control.

The competitive position is therefore a split verdict. The Dupixent engine is growing fast, the eye franchise is shrinking more slowly than it did in 2025, and the oncology and rare-disease lines are small but growing. The sections that follow take each in turn, and each claim carries a source.

Dupixent and the Sanofi profit share

Dupixent global sales, recorded by Sanofi, were $5,998.2 million in the second quarter of 2026, up 38% from $4,344.6 million a year earlier. For the six months they were $10,878.3 million, up 36%.[1] Regeneron's share of the profit was $2,032.6 million in the quarter against $1,282.1 million (+58.5%), and $3,483.4 million for the six months against $2,300.2 million (+51.4%).[1] Profit grew faster than sales because Dupixent's costs do not rise in step with its sales.

New uses keep widening the market. In April 2026 the FDA and the European Commission approved Dupixent for chronic spontaneous urticaria in children aged 2 to 11. This is a skin condition with hives, and the children still had symptoms on antihistamines.[1] The FDA has also approved it for allergic fungal rhinosinusitis, and Japan has approved it for bullous pemphigoid.[2] Not everything worked. The companies withdrew the European application for bullous pemphigoid. Phase 3 studies in a skin itch condition called lichen simplex chronicus missed their main goals. Regeneron and Sanofi also stopped developing itepekimab, a second antibody for lung disease.[2] Dupixent faces named rivals in atopic dermatitis, including Ebglyss from Eli Lilly, Rinvoq from AbbVie and Nemluvio from Galderma.[3]

The concentration is the main risk. Regeneron's Sanofi profit share alone was 44.1% of revenue in the first half of 2026 (our calculation), so a slowdown in Dupixent would show up in the results almost one for one. The October 2026 expansion of the Sanofi agreement adds long-acting follow-on antibodies that could extend the franchise. The new products are at an early stage, and the lead IL-13 antibody is in a Phase 1 study.[8][2] We have not verified Dupixent's patent expiry dates, so they are not part of this analysis.

The eye franchise: EYLEA HD, EYLEA and the copy-product fight

In 2025, United States sales of EYLEA and EYLEA HD together fell 27% to $4,385 million from $5,968 million. EYLEA HD rose to $1,637 million, and the original EYLEA fell to $2,748 million.[6] The decline has slowed. In the second quarter of 2026 the two products together sold $1,008 million against $1,147 million, a fall of 12.1%. For the six months they sold $1,950.0 million against $2,190.3 million, a fall of 11.0%.[1] EYLEA HD is growing (+52.1% for six months) while the original EYLEA is shrinking (-40.6%).[1]

Three pressures explain the original EYLEA's fall. The company names lower sales volumes from competitive pressure and the move of patients to EYLEA HD, and a lower net selling price.[1] In 2025 it also named loss of share to compounded bevacizumab, a lower-cost off-label alternative, because of patient affordability limits.[6] The named competitors are Genentech and Roche's Vabysmo, which is a branded rival, and Amgen's Pavblu, an aflibercept copy that is listed among the marketed copy products.[3] Regeneron has also added reasons to prefer EYLEA HD. The FDA approved it for retinal vein occlusion and a monthly dosing option in November 2025. In April 2026 it approved dosing intervals of up to every 20 weeks.[6][1]

The court record has been mixed but is moving in Regeneron's favor in the United States. The court denied Regeneron's request for a preliminary injunction against Amgen in September 2024, and the Federal Circuit affirmed that on March 14, 2025.[2] On July 7, 2026, the court granted Regeneron's motion to dismiss most of Amgen's defenses and counterclaims in the 2025 lawsuit, with one exception about delay in prosecuting the patent.[2] An independent case summary describes the dismissed claims as antitrust counterclaims that alleged fraud on the patent office.[16] The merits of the patent claims continue.[16] Outside the United States the picture is weaker. A European patent opposition division invalidated the '049 patent on April 21, 2026, and a Munich appeals court suspended enforcement of a preliminary injunction against Sandoz in February 2026.[2] A reader may ask whether other large companies, such as Pfizer, are also in court with Regeneron over EYLEA. The 10-Q's legal note names Amgen in the United States, Sandoz and Sam Chun Dang in Europe, and Sam Chun Dang and Optus in South Korea, and it does not name Pfizer.[2] We therefore report no Pfizer outcome. Pfizer appears in the 10-K only as the maker of Cibinqo, a rival eczema drug.[3]

A manufacturing point matters for margins. The company says gross margin on net product sales fell in the second quarter. The cause was unabsorbed costs from a temporary interruption of bulk production at its Limerick, Ireland facility. Production returned to normal levels at the end of the quarter.[1] The EYLEA HD pre-filled syringe, which the company had expected the FDA to decide in the second quarter of 2026, is now described as possible by the end of 2026.[6][2]

Oncology, rare disease and the pipeline

Libtayo, a cancer immunotherapy, had global sales of $489.4 million in the second quarter of 2026, up 30% from $376.5 million. For the six months it sold $927.6 million, up 40%.[1] The FDA and the European Commission approved it in October and November 2025 as the first immunotherapy for high-risk cutaneous squamous cell cancer after surgery.[6] Praluent, the cholesterol antibody, sold $265.6 million worldwide in the quarter against $222.0 million.[1] Lynozyfic, a bispecific antibody for multiple myeloma, sold $16.5 million in the second quarter.[1] Ordspono (odronextamab), a bispecific antibody for lymphoma, is in a pivotal study in B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma and in further lymphoma settings.[2] The filings do not publish separate sales for Ordspono, so we show that as not available.[1]

The 2026 pipeline has delivered approvals and failures. Otarmeni, a gene therapy for a form of inherited deafness, was approved in April 2026, and Regeneron has agreed to provide it free in the United States.[15][2] Pasatru (garetosmab) was approved on August 19, 2026 for a rare disease in which muscle turns to bone, twelve days before its goal date of August 31.[13][14] The cancer antibody fianlimab, which blocks a protein called LAG-3, did not reach statistical significance in a Phase 3 melanoma study against pembrolizumab, reported in May 2026.[1] The adjuvant melanoma study of the same drug passed two planned interim reviews and continues to a final result, which the company expects in the fourth quarter of 2026.[2] Cemdisiran, an RNA drug for generalized myasthenia gravis, has an FDA target action date in November 2026 under priority review.[1]

The obesity and gene-editing programs are early. Regeneron is testing trevogrumab, a muscle-preserving antibody, with semaglutide. It expects more Phase 2 data in the second half of 2026. It also plans to start a Phase 3 program for olatorepatide, a GLP-1 and GIP drug licensed from Hansoh, in the same period.[2] The FDA removed its clinical hold on the Phase 3 trials of nexiguran ziclumeran, the gene-editing treatment developed with Intellia.[2] The second-quarter collaboration revenue table lists Sanofi, Bayer and an other line, and no Roche collaboration line, so Roche appears in the filings as a competitor through Vabysmo rather than as a revenue partner.[1][3]

Pricing policy and legal exposure

In April 2026 Regeneron signed agreements with the American government. The company agreed to sell certain products it wholly owns to Medicaid at prices no higher than prices benchmarked to other developed countries. It agreed to price certain future medicines on the same basis. It will offer Praluent through the TrumpRx.gov purchasing platform and provide Otarmeni free. In return its products will not face tariffs under the Section 232 pharmaceutical investigation through January 2029, provided it keeps its commitments to invest in American manufacturing and research.[2] One analyst firm quoted by a trade publication said Praluent is a small fraction of revenue and the revenue effect should be incremental at most.[15]

The Inflation Reduction Act is the second price channel. Medicare price negotiation for the first group of Part B drugs is set to start in 2028, according to Regeneron's 10-Q.[2] The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services announced 15 drugs for that cycle on January 27, 2026, and none of the 15 on its list is a Regeneron product by our reading of the list.[17] We have not verified when Dupixent or Libtayo could first be eligible in later cycles, so the long-run Medicare risk stays open.

Three legal matters carry dollar or conduct risk, and one of them is in Regeneron's favor. In the case Regeneron filed against Amgen over Praluent, a jury sided with Regeneron on nine of ten counts on May 15, 2025. It awarded Regeneron $135.6 million in compensatory damages and $271.2 million in punitive damages.[2] Amgen has asked the court to overturn the verdict or order a new trial, and the 10-Q reports the post-trial motions as heard with no ruling.[2] The exposure for Regeneron is therefore losing or cutting an award it has not yet collected, not paying one. The Department of Justice civil complaint filed on June 24, 2020 alleges violations of the federal Anti-Kickback Statute and False Claims Act in connection with Regeneron's support of patient-assistance charities and remains pending.[2] The Amgen EYLEA litigation described above continues in the Northern District of West Virginia.[2]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of filings show a business that peaked in 2021, reset, and is now growing again. Net product sales were $12,117.2 million in 2021 and $6,893.7 million in 2022, so any growth rate that starts in 2021 is misleading, and we use 2022 as the base for three-year rates.[4] From 2022 to 2025 revenue grew at 5.6% a year, from $12,172.9 million to $14,342.9 million, and GAAP net income grew at 1.3% a year, from $4,338.4 million to $4,504.9 million (our calculation).[4][3] Those are modest rates, and 2025 itself was almost flat with revenue up 1.0%.[3]

The more useful measure is operating income, because 2025 net income was lifted by $946.1 million of gains on securities and a tax rate of 13.9%, itself helped by the release of liabilities after an IRS audit settled.[3][6] Operating income fell from $4,738.9 million in 2022 to $3,577.9 million in 2025, an operating margin drop from 38.9% to 24.9%, because research and development rose from $3,592.5 million to $5,850.2 million, or from 29.5% to 40.8% of revenue (our calculations).[4][3] Regeneron spends about forty cents of every revenue dollar on research, which is the cost of owning a pipeline and the reason margins sit below those of some peers.

Growth has now turned. Revenue for the six months to June 30, 2026 was up 17.8% and operating income up 15.9%, so the operating margin held near 24.5% against 24.9% a year before.[1] Over the 12 months to June 2026 we calculate revenue of $15,534.7 million, operating income of $3,843.1 million and free cash flow of $3,760.4 million, which is 24.2% of revenue. Those figures sum the 2025 annual result, add the first half of 2026 and subtract the first half of 2025.[3][1] The company's non-GAAP results, which leave out stock-based pay and some other items, run higher: non-GAAP earnings per share were $14.29 in the second quarter against GAAP earnings per share of $12.23.[1]

The balance sheet is the quiet strength. At June 30, 2026 Regeneron held $17,822.9 million of cash and marketable securities against $1,986.6 million of long-term debt and $720.0 million of finance lease liabilities, a net cash position of $15,116.3 million.[1] An independent data page shows $15.12 billion of net cash, which agrees.[19] That is about $147 a share, or 18.2% of the market value at the October 23 close (our calculation from the cited share count of 102,954,988).[2]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could be wrong, and the honest test is what evidence would show it. We name the weakest assumption in each estimate, then give the strongest argument against buying the stock, three scenarios that would break the thesis, and the single claim everything else depends on.

The weakest assumption in the corrected reverse DCF is that the whole $15.1 billion of net cash belongs to shareholders. Some of it supports operations and a $2 billion plant plan, and part is held as marketable securities that move with markets, so the implied growth rate would be higher if less cash were treated as spare. We will see the next cash balance with the third-quarter report on October 30, 2026.[1][6][22] The weakest assumption in the base target is the 2027 earnings figure of $62.70, which sits only 6.55% above 2026 and below the annualized second-half run rate, so it may be stale in either direction. The 2027 guidance in the fourth-quarter release is the next check; that release was published on January 30 in 2026 and on February 4 in 2025, so we treat the 2027 date as an estimate until the company confirms it. The weakest assumption in the probability-weighted value is the 25% bear weight, which could be too high or too low, and it will be known by October 2027.

The bear case, stated as strongly as we can, runs as follows. The stock is 6.3% below its 52-week high after a 28% rise in 2026 earnings estimates in 90 days, so good news is in the price. Profit depends on one partnered product whose sales grew 38% in the second quarter, and growth like that tends to slow. United States sales of the two eye drugs together fell 27% in 2025 and 11% in the first half of 2026, with new competitors and a patent fight that is not fully won. The company's own 2026 record includes a failed melanoma trial, a withdrawn European filing and a lowered gross margin forecast. And it has agreed to price certain products by reference to other countries, with a federal civil case still open.[21][1][2]

Regeneron's Sanofi profit share was 44.1% of first-half revenue, so a slowdown in Dupixent growth from 38% toward 15% would remove most of the earnings upside in the 2026 and 2027 estimates.
