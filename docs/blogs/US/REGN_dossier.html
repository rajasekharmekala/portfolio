Chapter 1

Regeneron Pharmaceuticals began in 1988 in Tarrytown, New York [20]. Dr. Leonard Schleifer, a neurologist and pharmacologist, wanted new disease-modifying drugs for brain disorders [20]. He teamed up with Dr. George Yancopoulos, a molecular immunologist from Columbia University [20]. Most biotech startups license one outside molecule from a university. Instead, Schleifer and Yancopoulos built an internal drug-invention engine [5], [20]. The firm ran for nearly twenty years without a commercial hit. Early trials failed, including Axokine in ALS during the 1990s. Management responded by shifting talent and cash into two core platforms [5], [20]. First, chimeric "receptor traps" fuse human receptor parts to antibody tails. Second, VelocImmune uses engineered mice with human immune genes to make fully human antibodies fast [5], [20].

That 20-year build created four clear cycles that explain how Regeneron grows capital today. Phase 1 (1988–2010) focused on platform building, funded by early stock sales and R&D deals with Sanofi and Bayer [20]. It peaked with the 2008 FDA approval of Arcalyst (rilonacept) for rare inflammatory syndromes, proving receptor traps worked in humans [20]. Phase 2 (2011–2019) brought rapid sales growth. In November 2011, Eylea (aflibercept 2 mg) launched in the U.S. for wet AMD and diabetic macular edema (DME) [12], [20]. Zaltrap (2012) tested VEGF-trap cancer biology, and Praluent (2015) proved VelocImmune's speed in PCSK9 cholesterol care [12], [20]. The biggest turning point came in March 2017 when the FDA approved Dupixent (dupilumab) with Sanofi, opening type 2 immunology across skin and lung disease [12], [13]. By 2019, annual revenue reached $6.56 billion and GAAP net income hit $2.12 billion [21].

Phase 3 (2020–2022) tested Regeneron's speed during COVID-19. While peers searched outside libraries, Regeneron used VelocImmune to isolate and test REGEN-COV (casirivimab/imdevimab) in months [5], [20]. REGEN-COV lifted total revenue from $8.50 billion in 2020 to $16.07 billion in 2021, with GAAP net income of $8.08 billion [21]. Revenue then reset to $12.17 billion in 2022 after Omicron variants led the FDA to revoke its Emergency Use Authorization [21]. Crucially, management treated the 2021 windfall as a one-time cash reserve, not permanent earnings. They banked the cash instead of buying overpriced late-stage peers. That restraint left Regeneron with a strong balance sheet and steady internal R&D when COVID sales faded.

Phase 4 (2023–2026) marks Regeneron's shift into a multi-franchise compounder facing its first major biosimilar test. On defense, legacy 2 mg Eylea faced Roche's Vabysmo and Amgen's biosimilar Pavblu (aflibercept-ayyh) [8], [9], [11]. Regeneron responded by moving clinics to high-dose Eylea HD (8 mg). It offers dosing up to every 20 weeks in wet AMD and DME, plus approval in retinal vein occlusion (RVO) [8], [11]. An October 2025 FDA Complete Response Letter at Catalent Indiana delayed the Eylea HD prefilled syringe [3], [10]. Even so, Eylea HD jumped 62% YoY to $699 million in Q3 2026 [3]. Meanwhile, Dupixent hit $17.81 billion in FY2025 global net sales (+26% YoY) and $6.5 billion in Q3 2026 (+35% YoY) on COPD and CSU growth [2], [3]. Libtayo also crossed a $2.2 billion annual run rate in Q3 2026 [3]. In Q2 2026, Regeneron finished repaying its development balance to Sanofi [3], [12]. That payoff ended a 20% profit-share deduction and drove a 68% YoY jump in Sanofi collaboration revenue in Q3 2026 [3], [12].

Phase 4 also expanded Regeneron's lab output beyond standard monoclonal antibodies. The engine now builds T-cell bispecifics, small interfering RNA (siRNA) drugs, and complement therapies [15], [16]. Recent FDA approvals include Lynozyfic (linvoseltamab) in multiple myeloma, Ordspono (odronextamab) in lymphoma, Livsayis (cemdisiran) in generalized myasthenia gravis, and Pasatru (garetosmab) in FOP [3], [15], [16]. These wins prove VelociSuite now delivers across several drug classes [3], [15], [16]. In addition, the 2026 Sanofi deal expansion co-funds four long-acting immunology antibodies to build a direct bridge into the 2030s [3].

Chapter 3

Regeneron sells its drugs through two distinct U.S. channels [17], [18]. The first is physician buy-and-bill for clinic-given biologics like Eylea, Eylea HD, Libtayo, Lynozyfic, and Ordspono. The second is the specialty pharmacy / PBM benefit for self-injected drugs like Dupixent, Praluent, and Kevzara [17], [18]. Knowing how both pipes work is vital. Channel rules—not just clinical data—drive working capital, net pricing, and biosimilar defense.

In the buy-and-bill eye channel, Regeneron ships Eylea and Eylea HD to large national distributors [17], [18]. Key partners include McKesson, CuraScript SD (Evernorth/Cigna), and Cencora [17], [18]. They keep vials cold at 2°C–8°C and deliver them straight to retina clinics and hospitals. Clinics buy the drug upfront and take inventory risk. They then bill Medicare Part B (roughly 55%–60% of wet AMD patients) or private plans under permanent J-codes at ASP plus 6% (4.3% after sequestration) [19]. Clinics earn a percentage markup on ASP. When 2 mg biosimilars like Amgen's Pavblu cut price, they shrink the clinic's dollar spread per shot [8], [9]. By contrast, Eylea HD's separate 8 mg J-code protects both clinic economics and dosing intervals [8], [9]. Buy-and-bill still carries third-party filling risks. In October 2025, an FDA inspection warning at Catalent Indiana triggered a Complete Response Letter (CRL) that delayed the Eylea HD prefilled syringe (PFS) [10], [11]. Regeneron is now qualifying backup fill-finish sites [10], [11].

By contrast, Dupixent and Praluent move through specialty pharmacies like CVS Specialty, Accredo, and OptumRx [17]. Pharmacy Benefit Managers (PBMs) demand rebates off list price (WAC) for preferred formulary tiers. Drugmakers also fund copay cards to help patients clear high deductibles. Sanofi runs global Dupixent sales and rebate contracting [2], [12]. As a result, Regeneron's reported Sanofi collaboration line ($5,884.0 million in FY2025) arrives net of rebates and selling costs, making it a very clean profit stream [2], [12].

On supply, Regeneron runs an internal manufacturing network across three core sites [6]. Tarrytown, New York houses corporate HQ, VelociSuite R&D, and early clinical supply. Rensselaer, New York runs huge mammalian cell bioreactors that make bulk drug for Eylea, Dupixent, and Libtayo. Limerick, Ireland serves as its European biologics plant [6]. Owning bulk drug capacity drove an 81% FY2025 product gross margin (82.7% total gross margin TTM) [2], [6]. It also brought in $642.4 million from Sanofi and $139.7 million from Bayer in FY2025 supply paybacks [2], [6]. Capital spending rose to $898.4 million in FY2025 from $755.9 million in FY2024 to expand Rensselaer, Tarrytown, and backup filling lines [2]. Working capital stays tight because distributors pay in 30 to 60 days while bioreactor runs span months. Thus, FY2025 operating cash flow ($4,978.9 million) easily beat GAAP net income ($4,504.9 million) [2].
