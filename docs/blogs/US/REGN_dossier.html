1. What the company does

Regeneron is a biotechnology company that invents, develops, manufactures and sells antibody medicines. An antibody is a protein the body already makes to find and block one target, and Regeneron designs fully human versions that doctors give by injection or infusion. The company says it has about 50 product candidates in clinical development, many of them new uses for medicines already on sale.[2] Its marketed medicines include Dupixent for allergic and inflammatory disease, EYLEA and EYLEA HD for eye disease, Libtayo for cancer, Praluent for cholesterol, Kevzara for arthritis, Lynozyfic for multiple myeloma and Evkeeza for a rare cholesterol disorder.[2] Most of the value sits in two of them. Dupixent is the profit engine, and the EYLEA franchise is the older cash source that is now shrinking.

Money reaches Regeneron in three ways, and the difference matters because each way carries a different cost. In the six months to June 30, 2026, revenue was $7,896.1 million. Net product sales that Regeneron books itself were $3,176.9 million, collaboration revenue from partners was $4,354.7 million, and other revenue, mostly royalties, was $364.5 million.[3][2] Sanofi was the largest partner at $3,779.4 million, which is 47.9% of revenue, and Bayer was second at $563.5 million (our calculation from the cited figures).[2] The table that follows shows each line next to the same period of 2025.

The Dupixent partnership and the repayment that just ended

Dupixent is the clearest example of the partner model. Sanofi sells the drug and records the sales, and Regeneron books its share of the profit. In the United States the two companies split Dupixent profit equally, and outside the United States Sanofi's share starts at 65% and falls to 55% as sales rise.[3] Regeneron's profit share was $3,483.4 million in the six months, which is 44.1% of total revenue (our calculation).[3]

One accounting detail explains why profit should rise faster than sales from here. Until mid-2026, Regeneron repaid Sanofi for development spending that Sanofi had earlier funded, and the repayment was deducted from the profit share. In the second quarter Regeneron's share before the deduction was $2,285.6 million, the deduction was $253.0 million, and the reported share was $2,032.6 million.[3] As of June 30, 2026 that balance was fully repaid, and it no longer reduces collaboration revenue from the third quarter.[3][2] Management called it a meaningful step-up in collaboration profits.[2] After tax, the $253.0 million is worth about $1.98 a share each quarter (our calculation at the 15.5% tax rate and 108.0 million diluted shares the company uses).[2]

On October 1, 2026 the two companies widened the partnership. Sanofi will pay $1.0 billion up front and up to $7.0 billion more in milestone payments for four new long-acting antibodies that Regeneron invented, and profits on those products will be shared equally.[9][10] The companies also settled their earlier dispute over Sanofi's information and audit obligations.[9] Regeneron said at the same time that its third-quarter results will include an acquired research charge of about $22 million before tax, or about $0.18 a share.[9] The existing Dupixent profit split does not change.[10]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Antibody: a protein made by the immune system that locks onto one target, which drug makers copy and refine into medicines.
  • Collaboration revenue: money Regeneron books from partners such as Sanofi and Bayer, mostly its share of profit on products the partner sells.
  • Biosimilar: a near copy of a biologic medicine that can be sold once the original's protection ends or is overcome, usually at a lower price.
  • GAAP and non-GAAP: GAAP is the audited accounting standard; non-GAAP is the company-adjusted version that leaves out items such as stock-based pay, so it runs higher.
  • Acquired IPR&D: acquired in-process research and development, an expense booked when Regeneron pays up front for rights to someone else's drug candidate.
  • Reverse DCF: a discounted cash flow model run backwards, which starts from the share price and solves for the growth the price assumes.
  • Target action date: the date the FDA has set to decide on a drug application; a priority review is a faster review track for drugs the agency sees as significant.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Regeneron's strength is the rate at which its laboratories turn science into medicines. Dupixent, which Regeneron and Sanofi built together, is used by more than 1.5 million people across nine conditions, according to Sanofi.[10] Its weakness is the other side of the same fact. Profit leans heavily on one partnered product, the original EYLEA is losing ground to cheaper alternatives, and American drug-price policy and several lawsuits can move results in ways the company does not control. The competitive position is therefore a split verdict, and each part has its own evidence.

The financial result of that split is visible in the half-year numbers. Revenue grew 17.8% to $7,896.1 million, operating income grew 15.9% to $1,936.4 million, and the operating margin, which is operating income divided by revenue, held near 24.5% (our calculations from the cited statements).[3] Growth came from one engine, so the report asks two questions in every section: how long can Dupixent keep growing, and can EYLEA HD fill the gap that the original EYLEA leaves.

Dupixent: the engine and its limits

Dupixent global sales, recorded by Sanofi, were $5,998.2 million in the second quarter of 2026, up 38% from $4,344.6 million a year earlier. For the six months they were $10,878.3 million against $8,010.2 million, up 35.8%.[2] Sanofi reported the same quarter as €5.2 billion, up 37.6%.[27] Regeneron's profit share grew faster than sales, $2,032.6 million against $1,282.1 million, so its share of Dupixent and Kevzara sales rose to 33% from 29%.[3] That is operating leverage: the drug's profit grows faster than its sales.

Growth will not stay at 38%. Sanofi's management said it expects Dupixent growth to moderate in the second half as new uses annualize and comparisons get tougher.[29] New uses keep widening the market. The FDA and the European Commission approved Dupixent for chronic hives in children aged 2 to 11 in April 2026, and the companies reported that the FDA approved it for allergic fungal rhinosinusitis, a sinus condition.[2][3] Not everything worked. The European application for bullous pemphigoid, a blistering skin disease, was withdrawn, and Phase 3 studies in lichen simplex chronicus, a skin itch condition, missed their main goals.[3]

The long-run limit is patent time. The 10-K lists a United States composition-of-matter patent for Dupixent that expires on March 28, 2031.[4] We have not verified any extension or later protection, and the company does not publish a loss-of-exclusivity date, so we treat the early 2030s as the horizon over which the new long-acting antibodies must take over. That is why the October 1 Sanofi expansion matters: the new antibodies target the same pathways and share profit equally.[10]

The eye franchise: EYLEA HD, EYLEA and the copy-product fight

United States sales of EYLEA and EYLEA HD together fell 27% in 2025 to $4,385 million.[7] The decline has slowed. In the second quarter of 2026 they sold $1,008 million against $1,147 million, a fall of 12%, and for the six months $1,950.0 million against $2,190.3 million, a fall of 11.0%.[2] EYLEA HD is growing (+52.1% for six months, $1,064.7 million against $700.0 million) while the original EYLEA is shrinking (-40.6%, $885.3 million against $1,490.3 million).[2] The company blames lower volumes from competition and the move of patients to EYLEA HD, plus a lower net selling price.[2]

Legal fights decide how fast copies arrive. The court denied a preliminary injunction against Amgen in September 2024 and the Federal Circuit affirmed that on March 14, 2025. On July 7, 2026 the court granted Regeneron's motion to dismiss most of Amgen's defenses and counterclaims in the 2025 lawsuit, except one about delay in prosecuting the patent.[3] An independent case summary calls the dismissed claims antitrust counterclaims that alleged fraud on the patent office.[16] The patent claims on the merits continue. Outside the United States the picture is weaker: the company withdrew a German injunction request against Sandoz in 2026 after an appeals court suspended enforcement.[3]

Two operating points sit alongside the legal ones. A temporary interruption of bulk manufacturing at the Limerick, Ireland plant pushed down the margin on product sales in the first half, and production returned to normal levels by June 30.[3][2] The EYLEA HD pre-filled syringe, which the company had expected the FDA to decide in the second quarter, is now described as potentially approved by the end of 2026.[3] We could not find a new FDA date, so we show that as not published.

Cancer, rare disease and the pipeline

Libtayo, a cancer immunotherapy, had global sales of $489.4 million in the second quarter, up 30% from $376.5 million, and $927.6 million for the six months, up 40.2%.[2] On October 25, 2026 Regeneron reported longer-term data from the C-POST trial of Libtayo after surgery for high-risk skin cancer: a 37% lower risk of death, with a hazard ratio of 0.63. The statistical range, 0.36 to 1.11, includes 1.0, so the survival result is promising but not conclusive.[30] That trial is why Libtayo has an adjuvant label, which is the use after surgery.

The 2026 pipeline has delivered approvals and failures. Otarmeni, a gene therapy for inherited deafness, was approved in April.[2] Pasatru (garetosmab) was approved on August 19, twelve days before its August 31 goal date.[13][14] The cancer antibody fianlimab did not reach statistical significance in a Phase 3 melanoma study against pembrolizumab, reported in May.[2] Cemdisiran, an RNA drug for generalized myasthenia gravis, has a November 2026 FDA target action date under priority review.[2] Regeneron also extended its supply agreement for the Ebola antibody Inmazeb, adding about $270 million of value through 2031.[31]

Pricing policy and legal exposure

In April 2026 Regeneron agreed with the United States government to sell certain wholly owned products to Medicaid at prices benchmarked to other developed countries, to list Praluent on the TrumpRx platform and to provide Otarmeni free, and in return it received relief from tariffs under the Section 232 pharmaceutical investigation.[15][3] Analysts quoted by BioSpace said Praluent is a small part of revenue and the effect should be incremental at most.[15] The Inflation Reduction Act is the second price channel, with negotiated prices for the first Medicare Part B drugs starting in 2028.[4] We have not verified when Dupixent or Libtayo could first be selected, so that long-run risk stays open.

Three legal matters carry dollar or conduct risk. In the Praluent case against Amgen, a jury sided with Regeneron on nine of ten counts, with $135.6 million of compensatory and $271.2 million of punitive damages. Amgen's post-trial motions were heard on August 27, 2025, and the 10-Q reports no ruling, so Regeneron has not collected the award.[3] A civil complaint that the Department of Justice filed on June 24, 2020 alleges Anti-Kickback and False Claims Act violations over support for patient-assistance charities, and it is still pending.[3] The Amgen EYLEA litigation above continues. What this means for an owner: the business is strong where it is partnered and growing, and weaker where it sells alone against copies, which is why Chapter 2B scores both sides separately.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of filings show a business that peaked in 2021, reset, and is now growing again. Revenue was $16,071.7 million in 2021 and $12,172.9 million in 2022, so any growth rate that starts in 2021 is misleading and we start the clock in 2022. From 2022 to 2025 revenue grew at 5.6% a year, to $14,342.9 million, and GAAP net income grew at 1.3% a year, from $4,338.4 million to $4,504.9 million (our calculations).[5][4] Those are modest rates, and 2025 itself was almost flat with revenue up 1.0%.

Operating income is the cleaner measure, because 2025 net income was lifted by $946.1 million of gains on securities and a tax rate of 13.9%.[4] Operating income fell from $4,738.9 million in 2022 to $3,577.9 million in 2025, an operating margin drop from 38.9% to 24.9%, because research and development rose from $3,592.5 million to $5,850.2 million, or from 29.5% to 40.8% of revenue (our calculations).[5][4] Regeneron spends about forty cents of every revenue dollar on research. That is the cost of owning a large pipeline, and it explains why margins sit below those of some peers.

Growth has now turned. First-half 2026 revenue was up 17.8% and operating income up 15.9%.[3] Over the 12 months to June 2026 we calculate revenue of $15,534.7 million, operating income of $3,843.1 million and free cash flow (FCF) of $3,760.4 million, which is 24.2% of revenue. Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capital spending, the cash left for owners. The company's non-GAAP results, which leave out stock-based pay and some other items, run higher: non-GAAP EPS was $14.29 in the second quarter against GAAP EPS of $12.23.[2]

The balance sheet is the quiet strength. At June 30, 2026 Regeneron held $17,822.9 million of cash and marketable securities against $1,986.6 million of long-term debt and $720.0 million of finance lease liabilities, a net cash position of $15,116.3 million.[2][3] The diluted share count fell from 115.1 million in 2024 to 108.6 million in 2025 and 106.8 million on average in the first half of 2026, because the company repurchased $3,438.6 million of stock in 2025 and $1,963.7 million in the first half of 2026.[4][3] Fewer shares raise profit per share even when profit is flat, which is worth remembering when reading the EPS estimates in Chapter 5.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

The stop follows the Core Alpha rule: the lower of the 20-day swing low ($722.02) and the 50-day average ($791.72), minus one ATR ($20.49).[1]
