1. What the company does

Gallagher is a global insurance broker, risk manager and consultant, run from Rolling Meadows, Illinois. It had about 72,000 employees at the end of 2025, 47 percent of them in the United States, and it serves clients in about 130 countries.[2] A broker is a middleman. A company that wants to insure its buildings, trucks, workers or health plan asks the broker to find the right insurer, negotiate price and terms, and help when a claim arrives. The broker does not take the insurance risk. The 2025 annual report says Gallagher does not assume net underwriting risk, which is why its results do not swing with hurricanes or car crashes the way an insurer's do.[3] The risk that remains is a business risk: how many policies it places, at what prices, and how well it keeps its people and clients.

The client pays in four main ways. Commissions were $8,024 million in 2025, about 58 percent of revenue before reimbursements, and they are a percentage of the premium the insurer collects. Fees were $4,195 million, about 30 percent, and are agreed amounts for advice, benefits consulting and similar work. Supplemental revenues ($466 million) and contingent revenues ($324 million) are extra payments from insurers for steering volume and for good results on the business placed. A fifth line of $769 million is mostly interest on client money held for short periods, plus premium-finance income.[1] That interest line fell to $98 million in the second quarter of 2026 from $233 million a year earlier, because in 2025 the company earned interest on the cash it had raised in advance to buy AssuredPartners.[8][9]

Gallagher says it grows in two ways. The first is organic growth: more revenue from the same businesses, which comes from higher insurance prices, more insured exposure at clients, and new clients won. Organic revenue excludes the first twelve months of any acquired business and removes currency swings, so it is the cleanest measure of whether the core is growing.[13] The second is buying other brokers. In 2025 the company closed 31 acquisitions with about $3.5 billion of estimated annual revenue, most of it from AssuredPartners, a US broker bought on August 18, 2025 for gross consideration of $13.8 billion. It funded the deal with $8.5 billion from a share sale and $5.0 billion of new bonds.[3] Small deals, which management calls tuck-ins, were priced at 9.4 times and 11.3 times their yearly profit in the first two quarters of 2026.[13]

The company reports in three segments. Brokerage was 87 percent of 2025 revenue and is made up of retail brokerage (75 percent of the segment), wholesale brokerage that serves other brokers on hard-to-place risks (13 percent), and reinsurance brokerage, called Gallagher Re, that helps insurers buy their own cover (12 percent).[2] Risk Management, branded Gallagher Bassett, handles claims for clients that self-insure and earned revenue before reimbursements of $1,585 million in 2025.[3] The Corporate segment holds the interest cost of the debt, acquisition costs and a small clean-energy legacy. No client is large: the largest was about 1 percent of segment revenue and the ten largest together about 3 percent.[2]

There are two ways to count profit, and the gap between them is the single biggest source of confusion about this company. Reported (GAAP) profit per share was $5.74 in 2025, after $916 million of amortization, which is the yearly write-down of the customer lists and other intangible assets bought in acquisitions. Management's adjusted profit per share, which removes that amortization and other one-off items, was $10.69.[1][3] We use both: the adjusted number is closer to what the operations earn, and the reported number is closer to what shareholders have paid for with their capital. Chapter 3 shows why that difference decides whether the business is a good use of capital.

What this means for an owner

For an owner, the point is that Gallagher is a fee-and-commission machine on top of other people's insurance spending. Its revenue rises when insurance prices rise, when clients insure more, and when it buys other brokers. It falls behind when prices fall, as they are doing for property cover in 2026, unless new business and acquisitions fill the gap. The next chapters ask how strong the position is and what the numbers say.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is profit before financing costs and accounting charges for past purchases. Gallagher uses a version called EBITDAC that also removes changes in estimated acquisition payments, and its adjusted EBITDAC strips out one-off costs as well.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share, which is profit divided by the number of shares. Adjusted EPS removes items that management calls one-off or non-cash.
  • ROIC stands for return on invested capital. It is after-tax operating profit divided by the money that shareholders and lenders have put into the business. A higher number means each invested dollar earns more.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after the business pays its running costs, interest and taxes and buys the equipment it needs. It is the cash an owner could take out.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates itself before borrowing, buying companies or paying dividends.
  • capital expenditure is spending on equipment, buildings and software that lasts for years.
  • SBC stands for stock-based compensation, the cost of paying employees partly in company shares.
  • net debt is borrowings minus cash. It shows what would still be owed if all the cash were used to repay lenders.
  • guidance is the company's own published forecast for a coming period.
  • consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for a number, such as next year's profit per share.
  • price-to-earnings is the share price divided by profit per share. It says how many dollars investors pay for one dollar of yearly profit.
  • buyback is when a company buys its own shares, which shrinks the share count.
  • dilution is the fall in each owner's slice of the company when new shares are issued.
  • working capital is the cash tied up in day-to-day operations, such as bills that clients have not yet paid.
  • RSI stands for relative strength index, a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been moving. Readings above 70 are called overbought and below 30 oversold.
  • ATR stands for average true range, the typical daily price swing in dollars.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing prices over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. They are slowly moving lines that prices tend to bounce off.
  • Stage 2 and Stage 4 come from the Weinstein stage scale: Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend.
  • base is a sideways stretch of trading that a price builds before a larger move; the pivot is the top of that base.
  • breakout is a move above a base or pivot on heavy trading, and a retest is the pullback that checks the new floor.
  • stop loss is the price at which we admit the idea is wrong and sell.
  • limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better and does not chase the market, and the entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop loss price: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).
  • measured move is the height of a base projected upward from its top as a rough target.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A small group of large global brokers competes for the same corporate clients: Marsh, Aon, Willis Towers Watson, Brown & Brown and Gallagher. Gallagher's case for strength rests on three things you can check. The first is a long record of profit growth. Adjusted EBITDAC rose from $3,584 million in 2024 to $4,491 million in 2025, about 25 percent, and management says it has grown by double digits for 25 quarters in a row.[3][15] The second is margin. The brokerage segment's adjusted EBITDAC margin rose from 35.1 percent to 36.5 percent between 2024 and 2025, and management says margins are up nearly eight percentage points since January 2020.[3][14] The third is breadth. No client is above about 1 percent of segment revenue. The business spans retail, wholesale, reinsurance, benefits and claims, and it works in about 130 countries.[2] A problem in one product or country is cushioned.

Strength is relative, and on growth the company is now level with its peers, not ahead of them. Organic revenue growth in the brokerage segment, which is 87 percent of revenue, was 10 percent in 2023, 7 percent in 2024 and 6 percent in 2025. For the two operating segments combined it was 6 percent in 2025, 5 percent in the first quarter of 2026 and 6 percent in the second.[13] In the third quarter Marsh reported 5 percent underlying growth and Aon 6 percent. Willis Towers Watson reported 5 percent in the second quarter, and Brown & Brown reported only 1.2 percent.[33][34][36][35] Gallagher guides to about 6 percent for the two segments in the third quarter, with about 5 percent from brokerage and about 10 percent from Risk Management. Its full-year 2026 brokerage outlook moved from about 5.5 percent to about 5 percent at the September 23 investor meeting.[14][13][32] So its edge today is steadier profit growth, built on margin gains and acquisitions, not faster core growth.

The pricing cycle explains most of the slowdown. Marsh's global index shows commercial insurance prices fell 5 percent in the third quarter of 2026, the ninth straight quarterly decline. Property fell 10 percent and casualty rose 1 percent.[41] Gallagher's own renewals say the same. In July and August its property renewals were down 6 percent and casualty up 5 percent, and everything except property was up about 3 percent. Management argued that clients use property savings to buy more cover, so premiums and broker revenue do not move one for one. It said price is only about one point of its organic growth.[14] Commissions are a percentage of premium, so falling prices are a headwind even when no client leaves. The quality of the business is not in doubt. The pace of its growth is.

The moat is thinner than the profit record suggests in four places. The first is capital. Goodwill and other acquired intangibles were $33.2 billion on June 30, 2026, against $23.7 billion of shareholders' equity, and the return on that capital is low (Chapter 3).[8] The second is governance. The president is the chief executive's brother, and other relatives work in the brokerage segment. The proxy discloses these ties and the board approved them, but they concentrate family influence.[21] The lead independent director died in July 2026, the board was cut to eight members, and the chief accounting officer was replaced in August. These are small but real continuity events.[23][24] The third is tax and legal risk. The 10-K says the micro-captive advisory business has been under IRS investigation since 2013, and management does not expect material harm.[3] The fourth is technology. On February 9, 2026 broker shares fell sharply on fears that artificial intelligence tools would cut into brokers' work. Gallagher fell 9.9 percent in a day, Willis Towers Watson 12 percent and Aon 9.3 percent.[42]

What the competition is doing

Aon reported 6 percent organic growth for the third quarter. Its release, dated October 28, 2026, one session after our ledger cutoff, says it expects to close its acquisition of USI in the fourth quarter of 2026, which we expect to make it a stronger rival in US retail brokerage, Gallagher's largest business.[34] Brown & Brown's 1.2 percent organic growth came with a fall in its adjusted EBITDAC margin from 36.6 percent to 34.6 percent, and adjusted profit per share rose only 1.9 percent. That is a warning about how soft pricing can squeeze brokers.[35] Gallagher's retail-heavy mix differs from Brown & Brown's, since retail is 75 percent of its brokerage segment. It is not immune to the same pressure.[2]

What this means for an owner

For an owner, the quality of the franchise is a reason to want the shares at the right price. It is not a reason to pay any price. The evidence supports a durable business with a record of margin gains, and the peers' results show that 5 to 6 percent organic growth is the industry norm at this point in the cycle. The next chapter scores the five operating measures that decide whether the record continues, and it checks whether management has done what it said it would do.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 of our underwriting asks which five measures actually decide whether this particular business keeps compounding, and then scores each one. We score a measure PASS (1.0 point) when it meets a standard we set in advance, WATCH (0.5) when it is close or heading the wrong way, and FAIL (0.0) when it is clearly short. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in the last chapter has a weakest assumption, and the honest way to test it is to attack it. This chapter does three things: it names the weakest assumption behind each number, it gives the red-team case, which is the strongest argument against our own conclusion, and it names the single claim on which the whole view hangs and the date on which we will learn if it holds.

Fatal flaw 1: the pricing cycle becomes a revenue recession

Commissions are a percentage of premium, so a long fall in insurance prices cuts broker revenue even when no client leaves. Marsh's index shows nine straight quarterly declines. Property is down 10 percent and only casualty is rising. Gallagher's own property renewals were down 6 percent in July and August.[41][14] Suppose casualty prices flatten while property keeps falling. Organic growth could then slide from 5 to 6 percent toward 3 to 4 percent. Brown & Brown's 1.2 percent suggests the weak end of the industry is already there.[35] Profit per share for 2027 would then be nearer $14.00 than $14.81. The premium multiple would disappear, and the price would approach $222.60, or go lower through the $216.15 stop. Management does not see this. It says price is only about one point of its organic growth and expects 2027 to feel like 2026.[14] The flaw is real if third-quarter brokerage organic growth comes in below 4.5 percent or if the next Marsh index shows casualty prices falling.

Fatal flaw 2: acquisition-led compounding stalls and AssuredPartners disappoints

Half of Gallagher's growth story is buying brokers. In the first half of 2026 it closed 14 deals with $107 million of annualized revenue, against 19 deals and $354 million a year earlier. By the September 23 call the year-to-date total was 21 deals and about $225 million, so about $118 million arrived in the third quarter, up from $58 million in the second. That is still about 43 percent below the roughly $394 million excluding AssuredPartners through September 2025. An analyst on the call said the company had done less than expected.[9][14][20][47] Insurance Journal's October 21 page carries a headline reporting that industry M&A fell 27 percent in the third quarter, which we have not read in full and treat only as a pointer.[41] Meanwhile the $13.8 billion AssuredPartners purchase has to deliver $160 million of run-rate savings by the end of 2026 and $325 million by early 2028. On the September call an analyst pointed out that the company's own table shows AssuredPartners fourth-quarter 2026 revenue below the same period of 2025. Management put this down to revenue items that were unusually high in 2025.[3][13][14] Earnouts, which are deferred purchase prices, are another place where optimism shows up later. Changes in earnout estimates cost $378 million in 2023. Earnout payments recorded in financing were $442 million in 2025 and $206 million in the first half of 2026.[5][1][8] The flaw is real if full-year 2026 acquired annualized revenue ends below about $300 million, which would mean the fourth quarter added less than $75 million after $225 million through September 23, or if synergy updates stop rising.

Fatal flaw 3: technology compresses what brokers earn

On February 9, 2026 Gallagher fell 9.9 percent in a single session, Willis Towers Watson 12 percent and Aon 9.3 percent. An AI insurance-shopping tool and new AI work tools from Anthropic had raised the question of whether brokers' work can be automated.[42] Compensation is the largest cost, $7,842 million in 2025 against $13,778 million of revenue before reimbursements, so any shift in the value of a broker's time moves the margin quickly.[1] Management's own estimate is that artificial intelligence can save 5 percent of sales effort, 10 to 15 percent of service effort and 20 to 30 percent of back-office effort over three to five years. That helps margins if it is real. It hurts revenue if clients and insurers can do the same work themselves.[14] The flaw is real if the underlying margin gain falls below 40 basis points or if personal and small-commercial revenue shows organic declines.
