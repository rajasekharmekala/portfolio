1. What the company does

AXIS Capital Holdings Limited is a holding company based in Pembroke, Bermuda, and its common shares trade on the New York Stock Exchange as AXS.[7][2] Its operating companies sell insurance to businesses and reinsurance to other insurers, and the company reports two segments, Insurance and Reinsurance.[7] It had 1,966 employees at December 31, 2025.[7] In the third quarter of 2026, Insurance growth came from marine and aviation lines (mainly marine war business), from property and professional lines written with third-party capital, and from accident and health lines.[1] Reinsurance grew in credit, surety and agriculture and shrank in liability lines, where it cut line sizes on purpose.[1]

An insurer collects premium first and pays claims later. The money it holds in between is called float, and the insurer invests it. AXIS held $16.4 billion of investments at September 30, 2026, plus $1.0 billion of cash, against $19.0 billion of reserves for unpaid claims.[1] Net investment income was $211 million in the third quarter, up 14 percent from a year earlier, and the book yield on fixed maturities was 4.9 percent against a market yield of 5.8 percent.[1] The gap means that money reinvested today earns more than the bonds already owned. Because most operating cash is premium waiting to be paid out, this report does not treat free cash flow (cash left after running the business and buying assets) as owner cash for AXIS.

The yardstick for underwriting is the combined ratio: claims plus expenses divided by premiums earned. The Group ratio was 94.0 percent in the third quarter, so claims and costs took 94.0 cents of each premium dollar and left 6.0 cents. It has four parts. Current-year losses without catastrophes were 57.0 points, catastrophe and war losses were 4.6 points, old-year reserve releases took away 0.9 points, the acquisition cost ratio (commissions paid to brokers) was 22.5 points and general and administrative costs were 10.8 points.[1] Those add to 94.0. For the nine months the ratio was 92.3 percent, against 89.5 percent a year earlier.[1]

The customers are businesses that buy specialty cover, and the brokers who place it. Short-tail lines, where claims are reported and paid quickly, were 56 percent of premiums in the third quarter, which is why the company is less exposed to slow-building casualty claims than a long-tail liability writer.[15] Insurance also grows through AXIS Capacity Solutions (ACS), a business that matches AXIS underwriting with third-party capital. ACS contributed 7 points of the 13 percent Insurance growth in the quarter, and expanded product classes added 5 points.[15] One ACS deal is Ryan Alternative Capital Re (RAC Re), a $400 million vehicle with an anticipated $900 million of multi-year premium capacity for Ryan Specialty's delegated underwriters, supported through AXIS's Lloyd's syndicate 1686.[32]

What the company does with its profit

AXIS returns profit mainly through share repurchases. A buyback is when a company buys its own shares, so each remaining share owns a bigger slice of the business. In the third quarter AXIS bought 1.067 million shares for $109 million at an average of $102.62, up from $89 million in the second quarter and $60 million in the first.[3][15] Diluted shares fell to 72.727 million from 78.796 million a year earlier, a drop of 7.7 percent (our arithmetic).[1] The common dividend is $0.44 a quarter, which cost $32 million.[1] At September 30, 2026 $154 million remained on the February 2026 program, about 1.4 quarters at the third-quarter pace, so the board must add authority again soon (our arithmetic).[3] In 2025 AXIS repurchased $888 million of shares and paid $139 million of dividends.[6]

What changed in 2025 and 2026

Four things changed. First, in April 2025 AXIS closed a loss portfolio transfer with Cavello Bay Reinsurance, a subsidiary of Enstar. It paid $2,039 million, and Enstar took a 75 percent quota share of about $2,060 million of net reserves, mostly casualty reinsurance from 2021 and earlier, with a policy limit of about $940 million.[7] Second, AXIS shrank its reinsurance book on purpose: reinsurance gross premium fell 7.0 percent over nine months and 25 percent in the second quarter, and management still expects a low double-digit decline for the full year.[1][16][15] Third, the war in the Middle East became a source of both premium and loss. About $100 million of third-quarter earned premium came from marine war and terrorism, and war losses were $44 million, or 2.7 points of the combined ratio.[15][1]

Fourth, management changed. Vince Tizzio has been chief executive since May 2023.[14] Matthew Kirk became chief financial officer on March 31, 2026, replacing Peter Vogt, and the Chief Administrative and Legal Officer, Conrad Brooks, leaves on December 31, 2026.[13] Management described the second quarter as its first full quarter under the new CFO, so the forecasts discussed on the July and October calls belong to the new finance team.[16]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio is claims plus expenses divided by premiums earned; below 100 percent is an underwriting profit.
  • Current accident year loss ratio, excluding catastrophes (called the attritional ratio) is the cost of this year's claims without storms, war events and old-year reserve changes; it shows the everyday margin.
  • Prior year reserve development is the change in the estimate of old claims. Favorable means the earlier estimate was too high and the difference adds to profit now.
  • Book value per diluted share is common shareholders' equity divided by diluted shares; it is the main scoreboard for an insurer.
  • Operating return on average common equity is operating income divided by average common equity; it leaves out investment price swings, currency swings and one-time costs.
  • AOCI (accumulated other comprehensive income) is the unrealized gain or loss on bonds that is booked in equity but not in profit; it shrinks as bonds move toward face value.
  • Loss portfolio transfer is a deal in which an insurer pays another company to take on old claims.
  • Float is premium collected now that will be paid out as claims later and invested in the meantime.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is profit earned on each dollar invested in the business; for an insurer we use operating return on equity instead.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before financing and accounting charges; insurers do not publish it.
  • FCF is short for free cash flow, which is the cash left after running the business and buying assets; for an insurer it is mostly float, so we mark it N/A.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying employees in shares.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast the price has moved; above 70 is hot and below 30 is cold.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in dollars.
  • A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the potential gain divided by the potential loss: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein scale and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend; Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase.
  • A base is a sideways stretch the price builds before a move, and its pivot is the top of that stretch.
  • Net debt is interest-bearing debt minus cash; for an insurer we use debt as a share of total capital instead.
  • Price-to-earnings is the price paid for each dollar of yearly profit, and operating margin is the share of revenue left after running costs.
  • A consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for a number.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

AXIS is a profitable specialty underwriter that is now earning less on each premium dollar. The strength shows in the scoreboard. Book value per diluted share excluding AOCI was $84.33 at September 30, 2026 against $73.69 a year earlier, up 14.4 percent, and the nine-month operating return on equity was 16.1 percent.[1] Over four years the combined ratio improved from 97.5 percent in 2021 to 89.8 percent in 2025.[9][7] The weakness shows in the last four quarters. The combined ratio was 90.4 percent in the fourth quarter of 2025 and 89.8 percent in the first quarter of 2026, then rose to 93.1 and 94.0 percent, and operating income fell 10 percent in the third quarter.[6][5][4][1] We read this as a business that is still good and is being priced down by a softening market.

Three facts frame the chapter. Growth is coming from places that carry a higher cost: the acquisition cost ratio was 22.5 percent in the quarter against 19.7 percent a year earlier.[1] Property prices are falling, with AXIS reporting rates down 10 percent in the third quarter and 17 percent in the second.[15][16] And the rating agencies are comfortable: AM Best affirmed an A (excellent) rating with a stable outlook in September 2026.[18][19] Each fact is explained below, with the evidence and the level that would change our view.

Where the competitive position is strong

Start with specialist depth. Management says AXIS is a leader in marine war and terrorism, often sets terms and conditions there, and has written the business for decades.[16][15] That claim is the company's own, but the numbers fit it: about $100 million of third-quarter earned premium came from war business at an acquisition ratio of 30 to 40 percent, and the Insurance segment still earned a 92.0 percent combined ratio.[15][1] The company also reports its 11th consecutive profitable quarter at AXIS Re, which management says it has repositioned as a boutique specialist.[1][15]

Capital and ratings are a second strength. Debt was 17.3 percent of total capital at September 30, 2026 (17.2 percent at year-end 2025), the investment portfolio had an average credit rating of AA-, and the operating companies are rated A+ by S&P and A by AM Best.[3][7][21] AM Best calls the balance sheet the strongest on its scale, supported by financial flexibility at the holding company.[18] S&P affirmed the holding company at A- with a stable outlook on July 15, 2026, though we saw only a headline for that action.[20] The third strength is the engine that lifts book value per share. Net investment income rose to $211 million in the quarter, and the share count is down 7.7 percent in a year (our arithmetic).[1]

Where the competitive position is thin

The underlying margin is slipping. In Insurance, the current accident year combined ratio without catastrophes was 87.2 percent in the third quarter against 83.3 percent a year earlier, and for the Group it was 90.3 percent against 87.7 percent.[1] The company explains the move as the market softening in property and getting more competitive in casualty, and management said in July and again in October that these pressures will continue.[16][15] Earlier in 2026 management had expected about one point of yearly deterioration in the Insurance underlying loss ratio. The second quarter, reported on July 29, came in 1.7 points worse and the third quarter 1.9 points worse.[16][1]

Growth is partly bought with margin. Insurance gross premium rose 12.8 percent in the quarter, but new property and professional business written through ACS carries higher commissions, and the acquisition ratio for Insurance rose 3.7 points.[1] Management told investors to use 22 percent as the fourth-quarter proxy for the acquisition ratio.[15] A fee offsets some of that cost in the G&A line (fee income was $23 million in the quarter), and management said the effect is broadly neutral to the expense ratio.[1][15] We read it as roughly neutral to profit, and also as a source of drift in the reported ratios that makes the underlying trend harder to read.

Reinsurance is smaller and still under pressure. Net premiums written fell 16.2 percent over nine months because AXIS bought more reinsurance of its own on liability, professional and motor lines, and the company reports pressure in the accident and health employer stop-loss business.[1][16] The company stays in the segment because it earned a 93.2 percent combined ratio over nine months, which is acceptable but no longer a source of outperformance.[1]

Reserves, investments and catastrophe load

Reserves are the money set aside for claims not yet paid. If they are too low, the shortfall hits a later quarter. AXIS has had one bad year: in 2023 it added a net $412 million to prior-year reserves, 8.1 points of the combined ratio, and AM Best says it strengthened casualty reserves for accident years 2017 to 2022 that year.[9][7][18] Since then development has been favorable every year: 0.5 points in 2024, 1.6 points in 2025 and $48 million over the first nine months of 2026.[7][1] The 2025 loss portfolio transfer removes the 2021-and-earlier tail, but accident years 2022 to 2025 are not covered by it.[7] Management says AXIS wants to see sustained positive signals before releasing reserves, and a third-party actuary reviewed reserves at year-end 2025.[17]

The investment portfolio carries more risk than it did. Management said in January that the share designated as higher-risk had reached 19 percent, the top of its 15 to 20 percent range.[17] Net unrealized losses on available-for-sale bonds were $450 million at September 30, 2026, against a $80 million gain at year-end, and accumulated other comprehensive loss was $384 million, about $5.28 a share (our arithmetic).[3][1] That loss is the gap between book value of $79.06 and $84.33 without it. It should shrink as the bonds approach face value, with an average duration of 3.2 years, unless rates rise further.[3][1]

Catastrophe exposure is modest for the capital. The company's own model puts the largest single-zone, one-in-100-year net loss (Southeast US hurricane) at about $225 million at January 1, 2026, which is 3.9 percent of year-end common equity of $5,806 million (our arithmetic), against an internal limit of 10 percent.[7] War and weather losses in the third quarter were $75 million, 4.6 points.[1] What this means for an owner: the downside from a single event is limited, and the larger risks are margin drift and reserve surprises, which the next chapters score.

A cycle turning down

AXIS operates in markets where prices are falling. The Guy Carpenter global property catastrophe rate-on-line index is down 16 percent for 2026 to date, the largest annual fall since the late 1990s, and 23 percent below its 2024 peak, although it is still 32 percent above the 2017 low.[29] Moody's says property catastrophe prices fell more than 20 percent in the 18 months since 2024 and expects January 2027 renewals to soften further, and Global RE reports outlooks of 10 to 15 percent lower prices on January 1, 2027.[30][31] In Reinsurance, catastrophe and weather losses were only $3 million over nine months, so the larger effect is on direct property: AXIS reported property rates down 10 percent in the third quarter, and down 15 percent in excess and surplus lines.[1][15] In liability, rates rose 8 percent, roughly in line with loss trends.[15] Casualty is therefore not following property down, which is the reason the company is reducing it rather than expanding.

3. The numbers, in plain words

The five-year table shows a company that was ordinary until 2023 and then turned sharply better. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter takes the weakest assumption behind each estimate, builds the strongest honest case against our conclusion, names the one claim the verdict depends on, and says when we will know.

The red-team case starts with the direction of the margin. The combined ratio has risen from 89.8 percent to 94.0 percent in two quarters, operating income fell 10 percent in a quarter in which premium rose 11 percent, and management says the pressure will continue.[5][1][15] A stock that earns 15.7 percent on equity but is heading toward 14 percent deserves a lower multiple than one whose returns are rising. If earnings land at $10.50 and the market pays 1.15 times a book of $90, the stock is worth about $104. In the bear case it is worth $86.60.

Flaw 1: the margin keeps slipping and the multiple follows

Our base case assumes a combined ratio of 94.5 percent in 2027, only 0.5 points above the third quarter. But the Insurance underlying loss ratio rose 1.7 points in the second quarter and 1.9 in the third (the combined-ratio version, which adds higher acquisition costs, rose 3.9 points in the third), property rates fell 17 percent in the second quarter and 10 percent in the third, and AXIS expects pressure to be ongoing.[1][16][15] The acquisition ratio is rising because AXIS is writing short-tail lines with higher commissions, and marine war at 30 to 40 percent commissions adds to it.[15] If the combined ratio reaches 97 percent, operating earnings per share would be about $10.50 and a multiple closer to 1.15 would follow. Evidence against us would be an Insurance underlying ratio above 88 percent for a full quarter, or a group combined ratio above 95.5 percent. We will know when the fourth-quarter results arrive (the next earnings date is estimated for January 27, 2027 and not confirmed) and when the January 1 renewals are reported.[35][31]

Flaw 2: casualty reserves and long-tail liability

Our base case assumes net development stays favorable. AXIS Re has reported 11 consecutive profitable quarters, reserve development has been favorable in every year since 2023, and the 2025 transfer covers 2021 and earlier.[1][7] But accident years 2022 to 2025 are not covered by it, liability rates rose 8 percent in the third quarter, which management said broadly keeps pace with loss trend, and in July AXIS said a 7 percent casualty rate change in the second quarter was behind trend.[16][15] AM Best notes that reserves developed favorably except in 2023, when AXIS strengthened casualty reserves for accident years 2017 to 2022 because of social and economic inflation.[18] A single adverse quarter above $50 million would turn the reserve KPI to WATCH, and two would be a FAIL. We will know in each quarterly report, and a third-party actuarial review is done at year-end.[17]

Flaw 3: war premium, third-party capital and the bond book

Growth leans on three things that may not last. About $100 million of third-quarter earned premium came from marine war at high commissions, and management could not size it for the fourth quarter because it depends on the conflict.[15] ACS, which includes the RAC Re sidecar, supplied 7 points of the 13 percent Insurance growth, and management said AXIS will curate the portfolio in year two, which could reduce volume.[15][32] Finally, the bond book matters: 19 percent of the portfolio is classified as higher risk, at the top of its 15 to 20 percent range, and unrealized losses on bonds are $450 million.[17][3] A conflict that ends would remove the premium and a wider credit spread would add to the loss. Evidence would be a Q4 acquisition ratio above 23 percent, a cut to ACS volumes, or book value per share below $79.06 at year-end.
