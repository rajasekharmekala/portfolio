1. What the company does

Williams is a natural gas infrastructure company. Think of it as the highway and toll system for gas: it does not drill wells or sell gas to homes, it owns the pipes, plants and storage caverns that sit between the two and charges fees to use them. In the trade this is called midstream, which means the pipes, plants and terminals between a gas well and the final customer. The best-known asset is Transco, the Transcontinental Gas Pipe Line, an interstate system of about 9,600 miles that runs from Texas and the Gulf of America through twelve southeast and Atlantic states to the New York City area, with a design capacity of about 20.6 million dekatherms a day.[1] A dekatherm is the energy in roughly one thousand cubic feet of gas. Northwest Pipeline adds about 3,900 miles in the Rocky Mountain and Pacific Northwest states, and MountainWest adds about 2,200 miles.[1]

The customers are local gas utilities, power plants, liquefied natural gas (LNG) export plants and gas producers. The 2025 annual report shows how concentrated they are in places: the three largest Transco customers produced about 22% of its operating revenue, and Dominion Energy alone paid Transco $216 million.[1] In gathering and processing, which means collecting raw gas from wells and cleaning it up, the top ten customers produced about 55% of fee revenue and liquids margins.[1] Williams also reports that about 93% of its natural gas liquids volumes were under fee-based contracts in 2025, so it is paid for handling the product, not for owning it.[1]

The company reports in dollars, and the most useful size measure is adjusted EBITDA, which is EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) with one-off items removed; it is a company measure, not an official accounting number. Adjusted EBITDA was $7,750 million in 2025 on total revenue of $11,950 million.[7][1] About 90% of that came from the three fee-based segments (our calculation: $7,750 million minus $193 million from marketing, $369 million from other activities including upstream gas, and $210 million of commodity margins inside the three segments).[5] Regulated interstate gas transportation and storage alone brought in $3,648 million of revenue, which is 43.7% of the $8,348 million of service revenue (our calculation).[5][1] Regulated here means the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) sets the rates so that the company earns an allowed return on the pipe it has built.[1]

Three new engines now sit on top of that foundation. First, Power Innovation is the company's name for gas-fired power plants built next to a customer, usually a data center,[6] that sell electricity under a long contract; the Socrates project in Ohio has 556 megawatts of capacity and a 10-year power purchase agreement that is primarily fixed-price, and its first phase entered service in late July 2026.[4] Williams sold 49% of five of these projects to an investor for $5.34 billion of committed capital in July 2026.[4][5] Second, it closed the $5.5 billion purchase of Momentum Midstream on September 3, 2026: about $3.5 billion of cash and debt and about $2 billion of Williams shares for 4,000 miles of Haynesville pipe, 6 billion cubic feet a day of gathering capacity and 4.05 billion cubic feet a day of take-or-pay pipelines.[13][5] Take-or-pay means the customer pays for the capacity even if it does not use it. Third, the $1.5 billion Delta Access pipeline expansion along the Transco route is due in the first quarter of 2029.[5]

How a pipeline fee works, and why it matters for a stock

A pipeline owner can charge in two ways. A reservation fee is paid whether or not the gas flows, much like paying monthly rent on a parking space. A usage fee is paid per unit moved, much like a toll. Williams' regulated pipelines earn mostly the first kind under long contracts, and FERC allows a return on the capital invested.[1] That is why the company can pay a dividend of $2.10 a year per share, now $0.525 a quarter, and show 52 straight years of payments.[4][7] The catch is that growth must be built first and paid for first. Williams plans growth capital spending of $7.3 billion to $7.9 billion in 2026, plus $850 million to $950 million to maintain existing assets, and the 10-Q says its Transco expansions are fully contracted with firm transportation agreements.[4][7] Chapter 3 shows what that spending does to cash left over.

Where Expand Energy and the Momentum deal connect

This idea came from our Expand Energy (EXE) work, where a gas producer holds a 35% interest in the NG3 pipeline joint venture with a partner named Momentum Sustainable Ventures LLC.[33] Williams' agreement names M6 Midstream LLC (called Momentum) as the company it bought.[4] The filings we read do not say whether these are the same legal entity or whether the NG3 interest sits inside the purchase, so we treat the link as unverified. What is verified is the strategic fit: Haynesville gas is the supply that Gulf Coast LNG plants will need, and Williams now owns more of the route to them. The company projects Gulf Coast LNG demand rising by about 20 billion cubic feet a day over ten years.[5]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Midstream - the pipes, plants and storage between a gas well and the end user.
  • Transmission - long-distance pipelines that move gas between regions; Transco is the largest example in this report.
  • Gathering and processing - collecting raw gas from wells, removing impurities and separating natural gas liquids.
  • NGLs (natural gas liquids) - ethane, propane and butane taken out of raw gas.
  • Adjusted EBITDA - Williams' own earnings measure before interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and items it calls unrepresentative; not an accounting standard.
  • AFFO (available funds from operations) - Williams' cash earnings measure: operating cash flow before working-capital swings, less preferred dividends and payments to minority partners; it is before any capital spending.
  • Take-or-pay - a contract in which the customer pays for capacity whether or not it uses it.
  • FERC - the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, which sets rates for interstate gas pipelines.
  • Power Innovation - Williams' behind-the-meter power plants, built beside a customer and paid for under a long power contract.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Williams is a strong business because its most valuable assets are hard to copy. A new interstate pipeline needs federal approval, state permits, landowner agreements and years of construction, and each step can delay a project. Transco's Northeast Supply Enhancement project shows the process: the FERC reissued its certificate in August 2025, water and related permits from Pennsylvania, New York and New Jersey were approved in October and November 2025, and customers subscribed to all of its capacity in August 2025.[4] Once a pipe exists and is full of contracted gas, a competitor cannot easily build beside it, so the owner earns steady fees on a long-lived asset. That is the moat, and it is a regulated one: it protects the return, but it also limits it.

The evidence is the five-year record in chapter 3 and the company's own long-run claim. Adjusted EBITDA grew from $5,635 million in 2021 to $7,750 million in 2025, which is 8.3% a year (our calculation), and adjusted earnings per share (EPS, the profit earned for each share) grew from $1.36 to $2.10, or 11.5% a year (our calculation).[10][7] Management says the five-year path to 2025 was 9% a year for EBITDA and 14% a year for earnings per share.[7] Credit rating agencies agree on the quality: Williams is rated BBB+ by S&P Global, Baa2 by Moody's and BBB by Fitch, with Moody's and Fitch both showing a positive outlook.[4]

Where the strength comes from

The first source is demand. Management projects Gulf Coast LNG demand to rise by about 20 billion cubic feet a day over ten years, and data-center power demand is adding a second pull on gas.[5] The second source is contracts. Transco's firm reserved capacity averaged 20.8 million dekatherms a day in 2025 against a design capacity of 20.6 million, which means customers have booked all of the pipe and more (the two figures are measured differently, so treat them as close to full, not exactly full).[5][1] The third source is the project list: the company completed 12 projects in 2025 and has 7.1 billion cubic feet a day of pipeline projects in execution as of February 2026.[7] The fourth source is the target. In February 2026 management set a goal of 10%-plus a year growth in EBITDA and earnings per share through 2030, and in August it raised the EBITDA part to 11%-plus after the Momentum deal.[20] Eleven percent a year from $7,750 million would mean about $13.1 billion in 2030 (our calculation).

Where the weakness is

The weakness is that growth is being bought with borrowed money and new shares. Cash from operations less capital spending fell from $3,422 million in 2023 to $1,005 million in 2025 and to a loss of $108 million over the twelve months to June 2026 (our calculation).[1][5] Debt rose from $23,675 million at the end of 2021 to $30,793 million at June 30, 2026, and the Momentum purchase and the September notes came after that date.[3][5][15] Net debt is borrowings minus cash, and it is 3.76 times adjusted EBITDA on our calculation, while the company's own ratio of 3.67 times subtracts $777 million of equipment that customers have agreed to reimburse.[5] The chief financial officer said on the second-quarter call that balance sheet tightness is mainly a 2026 and 2027 issue, before earnings growth he expects in 2028 and beyond.[20] A second weakness is price. The shares trade at 30.7 times the 2026 earnings analysts expect, against 21.9 times for Kinder Morgan and 16.1 times for ONEOK, so the market already pays for the growth.[21][29][30]

Customers, competitors, regulators and people

Williams' pipelines serve utilities that must keep homes heated, so the credit risk is low. The 10-K says its customers are generally rated investment grade, post prepayments or collateral, or depend on its pipes.[1] The newer Power Innovation business is different: management has said discussions are under way with multiple parties beyond the initial hyperscaler customer, which suggests that one large customer anchors the first projects.[20] Regulation is a live variable: Transco's most recent rate case settled, with FERC approval on December 30, 2025, effect from March 1, 2026, and a net $221 million refund in April 2026.[4] Competitors are growing at similar rates. Kinder Morgan reported third-quarter adjusted EBITDA up 9% and a $14.4 billion project backlog, ONEOK reported second-quarter adjusted EBITDA up 7%, and Enterprise Products Partners reported up 17%.[25][26][27]

Leadership has been stable and well supported. Chad Zamarin is president and chief executive officer, and he was re-elected to the board with 970,347,236 votes for and 15,789,776 against in April 2026.[17] The advisory vote on executive pay passed with 954,373,556 votes for and 29,628,316 against, which is 97.0% support (our calculation).[17] The board grew from ten to twelve directors on July 1, 2026, and one director, Michael Creel, will retire at the 2027 annual meeting without any stated disagreement.[18][19] Insiders own about 0.48% of the shares, so owner-operator alignment is limited.[22] What this means for an owner: the business is protected and well run, and the weakness is cash and price, not quality. 3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter shows five years of results from Williams' annual filings for 2021 to 2025, with a twelve-month column to June 30, 2026 that we built by adding the first half of 2026 to 2025 and subtracting the first half of 2025.[1][3][5] Every table cell in the last column is therefore our calculation from cited inputs. Two measures need a note. Williams reports adjusted EBITDA and AFFO, which are company measures, and both exclude items it calls unrepresentative.[7] And our scoring floors were written for ordinary companies. A pipeline company spends most of its operating cash on growth, so free cash flow (FCF), which is the cash left after paying for the pipes and plants the business needs, is a poor test on its own. We therefore score both the standard floor and a pipeline version, and we show the two side by side.

The pipeline version replaces three floors. Free cash flow margin is replaced by dividend cover from AFFO after maintenance spending and by whether AFFO pays for dividends plus all capital investments. Net debt divided by EBITDA is tested against 4.0 times, the company's own ceiling, if at least 85% of earnings are fee-based and interest cover is at least 3 times. Return on invested capital (ROIC, the profit earned on each dollar put into the business) is tested against the cost of capital rather than against 12%, because a regulated pipeline is allowed a return near that cost by design.[1] We say clearly where Williams fails the standard floor and where it passes only the adapted floor. Three smaller terms recur in the tables. The operating margin is operating income divided by revenue. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is the cost of paying staff in shares. Dilution is the increase in share count that shrinks each share's slice of profit.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter takes each estimate from chapter 5 and states its weakest assumption, what evidence would show it wrong, and when that evidence arrives. It also gives our strongest honest bear case, the argument a skeptical analyst would make against our own conclusion, and the single claim everything depends on. Our verdict is already a wait, so the three flaws below show how far the price could fall if we are early, and the case that waiting is too cautious is the bull scenario in chapter 5.

The red-team bear case in one paragraph: Williams is a pipeline company priced like a growth company. It trades at 30 times earnings while three of the four peers we compare trade at 12 to 22 times, it spends more than it earns after dividends, it has just issued shares and debt to buy a $5.5 billion business, and its biggest new growth line depends on a small number of data-center customers. The earnings promise, 11% a year to 2030, is back-loaded to 2028 and beyond, so for the next two years investors are paying today for results the company has not yet reported.

Fatal flaw 1: the growth plan outruns the balance sheet

Over the twelve months to June 2026, AFFO less dividends less capital investments was -$2,058 million, and the 2026 plan of $7.3 billion to $7.9 billion of growth spending is larger than that.[5] The company has funded the gap with a $2 billion share issue inside the Momentum price, $2.75 billion of new notes at coupons of 5.0% to 6.4%, and $5.34 billion of joint venture capital from an outside investor, which costs a capped 6.35% a year.[13][15][20] The company itself says leverage tightness is mainly a 2026 and 2027 issue and that its internal ceiling is 4 times.[20] If a project slips or the Momentum earnings arrive slowly, leverage could pass 4 times, ratings outlooks that are positive today could turn, and a further share issue would dilute the 2027 earnings we rely on. A fall to the bear value of $60.00 would be 8.1% below our $65.31 stop, so the stop would not hold the loss if the price gapped.

Fatal flaw 2: the market stops paying a growth multiple for a pipeline

At 30.7 times 2026 earnings WMB is priced above Targa at 26.9 times and well above Kinder Morgan at 21.9 times.[21][29][31] The premium rests on the Power Innovation story and an 11% growth target. If investors decide that a pipeline owner should trade like Kinder Morgan, with similar growth and a similar balance sheet, the price on 2026 earnings would be about $53 (our calculation), and on 2027 earnings of $2.62 at 24 times it would be $62.88. Nothing has to go wrong in the business for this to happen: a hyperscaler cutting its capital spending, a slower gas-demand forecast, or higher bond yields (the ledger shows the ten-year Treasury at 4.99% on October 27) could be enough.[34]

Fatal flaw 3: execution, customer and permitting risk on a long project list

The company has a lot in progress at once: Transco expansions, five Power Innovation projects, the Momentum integration and new Haynesville pipelines. Management said on the second-quarter call that the company has been adding talent and scaling up its capability to deliver larger projects.[20] Concentration is real: the second-quarter call transcript refers to an initial hyperscaler customer for Power Innovation, the Socrates power purchase agreement runs 10 years, and the 10-K says the top ten customers provided about 55% of gathering and processing fee revenue.[20][4][1] Transco has had a construction-contractor dispute that cost a settlement paid on April 14, 2026, of which $22 million was recovered from a co-owner, which is small but shows project risk.[4] A single large delay, for example in a project that needs a reissued federal certificate and permits from three states, as the Northeast Supply Enhancement did, would push the earnings step-up beyond 2028.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, and the rule behind it is the reward-to-risk floor. Reward-to-risk compares the gain from an entry price to our target with the loss from that price to our stop loss, which is the price at which we would admit the idea is wrong. A CORE_ALPHA name needs at least 2.0, measured at the planned entry price. With a central target of $73.85 and a stop of $65.31, Williams offers a negative figure at the current price of $74.27 (-0.05), 0.26 at the 50-day average of $72.10, and 2.01 at $68.15. The rule says buy only at or below $68.15, and the price is 9.0% above that level. The money floors from chapter 3 do not decide the verdict alone, because a pipeline-adapted version passes; the price decides it. WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT here is a conditional candidate, not a standing order: the zone is provisional until the November 2 report and the November 3 call have been reviewed and the work refreshed.

ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP needs the STANDARD band and the floor: the gate puts WMB inside the STANDARD band at 1.05 ATR above the 50-day average, but the ratio of 0.26 at that average fails the floor, so the band cannot be used.
