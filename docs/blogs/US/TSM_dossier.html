1. What the company does

TSMC is a contract chip maker, called a foundry. Think of a printing company that owns the world's most precise presses and never publishes a book of its own. Hundreds of chip designers send TSMC their designs, and TSMC prints them on silicon wafers. TSMC pioneered this pure-play model when it was founded in 1987, and in 2025 it ran 305 distinct process technologies and made 12,682 products for 534 customers.[1] Because it sells no chips of its own, in my view customers can trust it with their most secret designs.

The money comes from selling finished wafers, plus a growing slice from packaging, the step that joins several chips into one module. In the third quarter of 2026 revenue was NT$1,494.24 billion, which is US$46.72 billion at the rate TSMC reported, up 41.2% from a year earlier in US dollars.[1] Earnings per share (EPS) were NT$29.43, or US$4.60 per ADR.[1] Almost all sales are priced in US dollars, while the accounts are published in New Taiwan dollars, so every NT dollar figure in this book has a US dollar twin.[11] The 20-F annual report (TSMC files a 20-F and 6-K forms, not the 10-K and 10-Q that US companies file) says the same thing in its foreign-exchange note: the NT dollar's rise in 2025 cut reported NT dollar revenue.[12]

Two measures show where the revenue sits. By technology, 2-nanometer chips were 8% of wafer revenue in the third quarter, 3-nanometer 33%, 5-nanometer 28% and 7-nanometer 10%, so 79% of wafer revenue comes from the newest 7-nanometer-and-below nodes.[1] By end market, high-performance computing (HPC, the chips inside AI servers) was 68% of revenue, smartphones 21%, the internet of things (IoT) 4%, automotive 4% and consumer electronics 1%.[2] Concentration is high: the ten largest customers were 78% of 2025 revenue, the largest alone 19% and the second 17%.[15]

Most factory capacity is in Taiwan: the 20-F lists fabs in Hsinchu, Southern and Central Taiwan, plus Arizona (Fab 21), Kumamoto in Japan (Fab 23) and Nanjing in China (Fab 16).[16] Management says it has nearly 20 fabs in progress worldwide, 13 of them leading-edge or advanced-packaging fabs in Taiwan and five to six overseas.[2] That footprint is the source of both the strength of the business and its largest single risk, which Chapter 6 weighs.

How TSMC earns a margin that other manufacturers do not

A factory's profit depends on how full it is, how much it can charge, and how fast it cuts its own costs. TSMC's own list of profit drivers is leadership in technology, pricing, cost reduction, product mix, how busy the fabs are, and the exchange rate.[12] Because most fab costs are fixed, extra volume drops straight to profit. The 20-F says so directly: higher capacity utilisation and cost improvement lifted the 2025 gross margin from 56.1% to 59.9%.[12] In 2026 the margin rose again, to 66.2% in the first quarter, 67.7% in the second and 66.4% in the third.[5][3][1] Management uses the word dilution for the margin that a new node or an overseas fab takes away. It is a loss of margin points, not a rise in share count. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so 130 basis points is 1.3 points.

Management also sells a view of the future. In January 2026 it said a gross margin of 56% and higher is achievable through the cycle and a return on equity in the high 20s percent.[8] Actual return on equity in the third quarter was 45.5%, far above that target.[2] Read the gap as caution. The target is a through-the-cycle floor that management has historically beaten, not a forecast of the next quarter.

Cash returned to owners

TSMC pays a cash dividend every quarter. The board approved NT$7.0 per ordinary share for the second quarter of 2026, with the ADR going ex-dividend on 10 December 2026 and payment on 7 January 2027.[19] For 2026 as a whole management told investors to expect NT$24 per share, up 33% from NT$18 in 2025.[4] NT$24 per share is NT$120 per ADR, which is about $3.76 at NT$31.94 per dollar. That is a 0.91% yield at the 2026-10-16 price.[4][21] The aggregator shows a lower trailing-twelve-month ADR dividend of $3.21, a 0.78% yield, because it counts payouts that were smaller in 2025. Either way the dividend is a minor part of the return.[25]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The moat has four layers, and each one has evidence rather than slogans. The first is technology. TSMC's 2-nanometer process entered volume production in 2025, and by the third quarter of 2026 it was already 8% of wafer revenue.[12][1] Management says the 2-nanometer family's annual capacity will more than double in 2027, and its next node, A14, has risk production on track for 2027 and volume production in 2028.[2] A competitor that cannot match this ladder at volume cannot win the biggest AI designs.

The second layer is scale and customer planning. TSMC says it plans capacity with its customers and its customers' customers, the cloud companies, and that it already has a good view of multi-year product roadmaps.[2] The 20-F records an annual capacity above 17 million 12-inch equivalent wafers while shipments were about 15 million in 2025, up from 13 million in 2024.[16][12] That ratio means the fabs were running near full, an outcome that matches management's repeated remark that demand is higher than supply and that it is working hard to narrow the gap.[2] I did not find a company-published utilisation percentage, so the near-full reading is an inference from shipments and capacity, not a reported figure.

The third layer is packaging and the system view. TSMC says CoWoS is still the number one choice for customers. It says it is in real production at a 5x reticle size (a very large module), and the CEO does not believe anyone else can do that today. It is working on 9x and 14x.[2] Advanced packaging was about 8% of 2025 revenue and management expects slightly more than 10% in 2026, growing faster than the company overall.[8] Even TSMC says its advanced-packaging capacity is very tight.[6] It also has a deal with GlobalFoundries for silicon interposers to ease the back-end shortage.[2] Both statements point the same way: the constraint is capacity, not demand.

The fourth layer is pricing. A 66% gross margin on a manufacturing business is a price signal. Management describes its pricing as strategic and dependent on segment, location and technology, not opportunistic.[2] The two threats to that pricing power are rivals closing the technology gap and customers who build their own capacity. The CEO answered both on the call: the company treats every customer fairly, takes their in-house capacity into account when it plans, and expects A14 to be a bigger node than 2-nanometer.[2]

Where the moat is thinner than it looks

Customer concentration is the first soft spot. The largest customer fell from 25% of revenue in 2023 to 19% in 2025, yet the second largest rose from 11% to 17%, and the top ten reached 78%.[15] A buyer that large can ask for lower prices or move a design. The offset, in my judgment, is that moving a leading-edge design to another foundry means a long and costly redesign.

Capital intensity is the second soft spot. TSMC's 2026 capital budget is $60 billion to $64 billion, and management expects 2026 to 2028 spending to be significantly higher than the previous three years.[2] The company also says capital spending growth should stay below revenue growth, but that is a promise that must be checked each year.[2] Tool prices are rising too, and the CFO said tool price inflation is real.[2]

Geography is the third. The 20-F states that the principal production facilities and the majority of net revenue sit in the R.O.C. (Taiwan), and that markets have treated cross-strait developments as reasons to lower the price of Taiwanese securities, TSMC's included.[14] An independent monitor counted 255 aircraft sorties around the island in September 2026, 43% below the 444 of September 2025, so the near-term trend in military activity is calmer, which does not change the structural exposure.[33]

Competitors in plain terms

Samsung and Intel are the rivals analysts name on the calls.[6] On the Q3 call analysts asked whether an integrated chip maker customer would build more of its own capacity and whether Intel's packaging could substitute for CoWoS. The CEO said TSMC would act as a backup if a rival's packaging solution cannot deliver, and that the number one priority is customer products reaching the market smoothly.[2] The table in Chapter 3 compares TSMC's margins with five peers. None of the five earns TSMC's combination of margin and returns.[26][27][28][29][30]

The takeaway for an owner is that the moat is real and has widened in 2025 and 2026, but it is built on spending that never stops and on one geography. That is why the stock deserves a quality verdict of strong and a price verdict that depends on the chart and the reward-to-risk ratio, both examined later.

The number

ROIC (return on invested capital, the profit earned on each NT dollar tied up in the business) was 47.4% in 2025 and 37.5% in 2024.

How it was calculated

Divide NOPAT by invested capital. NOPAT (net operating profit after tax) is operating income times one minus the tax rate. Invested capital is equity plus debt plus leases minus excess cash.

The inputs

2025 operating income was NT$1,936.1 billion. Tax was NT$346.5 billion on pre-tax income of NT$2,041.7 billion, a 17.0% rate. NOPAT is therefore NT$1,607.5 billion.[11] Equity was NT$5,396.2 billion. Long-term debt was NT$1,033.0 billion, including NT$136.9 billion due within a year. Leases were NT$31.6 billion. Excess cash was NT$3,068.6 billion, which is cash plus current marketable securities.[11][12] Invested capital is NT$3,392.2 billion, so ROIC is 1,607.5 / 3,392.2 = 47.4%. For 2024 the same steps give NOPAT of NT$1,088.5 billion over invested capital of NT$2,904.3 billion, or 37.5%.[11]

The range

Treat all cash as operating capital and deduct none. Invested capital then becomes NT$6,460.8 billion and ROIC is 24.9%, still double the floor. On the third-quarter run rate of operating income, NT$876.4 billion a quarter, the ratio would be higher than 47%.[1]

If the number is off

A miss matters only below 12%, where the gate fails. That would need operating income to fall by about three-quarters at today's capital base. A smaller miss changes the margin of safety, not the pass.

5. What could happen next

This chapter answers a question that sounds simple: what does today's price already assume? Analysts answer it with a Reverse DCF (discounted cash flow, run backwards). Instead of forecasting cash flows and finding a value, it starts from the market value and solves for the growth rate that justifies it. I use the repository's standard settings: a 10% discount rate, a terminal value of 18 times the cash flow in year ten, and ten projection years.[36] The result is a plain-English check on how much good news the price already contains.

The ledger's own Reverse DCF is wrong for TSMC and I replaced it. It divided a free cash flow of 1,203 'billion' that is in New Taiwan dollars by a US dollar market cap, which produced a 56% cash flow yield and a growth rate of -1.6% a year. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could be wrong. For each estimate I name the weakest assumption. I say what evidence would show the error and when that evidence arrives. Then I write the red-team case, which is the strongest honest argument against this report's own conclusions. It has four failure scenarios.

Fatal flaw 1: AI customers slow their spending

The base case assumes that AI chip buyers keep funding capacity. HPC is 68% of revenue. The ten largest customers are 78% of it.[2][15] October reports say OpenAI's annualised revenue neared $50 billion, short of earlier $70 billion estimates. Some investors now call AI spending gambling.[31] TSMC answers that it has checked data-centre build-outs, power supply and customer funding. It says demand is still above supply.[2]

The damage would come from operating leverage in reverse. In 2023 revenue fell 4.5%. Gross margin fell 5.2 points, from 59.6% to 54.4%, because the fabs were less full.[18][11] Capex is now 1.85 times depreciation and amortisation (NT$1,272.4 billion against NT$688.1 billion in 2025). Depreciation will keep rising. A revenue pause would hit margin harder than in 2023.[11] Even so, a five-point margin drop on the 2027 run-rate would cut EPS only about 9%, from $20.6 to $18.8. A lower multiple would do more harm.

Fatal flaw 2: Taiwan Strait, tariffs and export controls

The 20-F says the main production sites and most revenue sit in the R.O.C. It adds that markets have used cross-strait news to lower the price of Taiwanese securities, TSMC's included.[14] No scenario in Chapter 5 includes a blockade or conflict. I cannot defend a probability for it. An owner must still accept that the tail exists. Arizona, Japan and Germany, plus the extra $100 billion Arizona plan, cut the exposure over years, not months.[4][2]

Trade policy is the second risk. A Section 232 order puts a 25% tariff on certain advanced computing chips unless they are imported for designated use in the US. Taiwan's January 2026 agreement caps reciprocal tariffs at 15%. It also gives preferential 232 treatment to firms that invest in US capacity. The details are unclear.[13] The US also opened Section 301 investigations in March 2026 into alleged overcapacity. The PRC fab faces export-license rules for tools.[13][14]

Fatal flaw 3: margin dilution stacks and the NT dollar rises

Three margin headwinds overlap. The steep 2-nanometer ramp dilutes gross margin by 3 to 4 points in the second half of 2026. Overseas fabs dilute it by about 2 points now and 3 to 4 points later. The NT dollar also cost margin in 2025.[4][2] The weighted-average rate moved from NT$32.13 per dollar in 2024 to NT$31.11 in 2025. The company's guidance rate has ranged from 30.6 to 32 in the past year, and it is 31.8 for Q4.[12][9][1] Almost all sales are in US dollars and most costs are in NT dollars. A stronger NT dollar therefore lowers the margin in dollars.[12]

Q3 gross margin already fell 130 basis points from the second quarter. The Q4 guide midpoint is another 40 basis points lower.[2] A five-point shortfall on the 2027 run-rate would lower EPS by about 9%. That alone would not break the thesis. Combined with a lower multiple, it would put the stock near the bear case.
