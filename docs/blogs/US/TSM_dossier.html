1. What the company does & day-to-day vertical operations

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company Limited (NYSE: TSM; TWSE: 2330) is a Taiwan-based contract chipmaker built around the dedicated-foundry model. Customers provide chip designs and rely on TSMC to manufacture semiconductors against those designs; TSMC is not underwriting demand for a consumer-facing chip brand of its own [1]. That separation lets a customer use TSMC's process and manufacturing scale without owning a fab network, while TSMC earns revenue by loading its capacity with customer wafers.

The 2025 Form 20-F shows where the economics sit: wafer fabrication represented about 86% of revenue; packaging and testing, mask making, design services and royalty income supplied most of the balance [1]. It is useful to treat those as stages in a customer program rather than standalone reporting segments. The audited platform table groups demand by the use of the finished silicon, not by a separate profit-and-loss statement for each customer vertical.

TSMC must coordinate many steps. It helps customers adapt designs to its processes. It prepares masks, books fab time, controls each step, then packages and tests chips. Its filing lists capacity, technology work, pricing, chip demand and exchange rates as sales drivers[1]. Public filings do not show the daily order queue or output at each fab. This report therefore tracks product mix, node mix, margins and spending.

The customer handoff continues beyond the wafer. The company reports packaging and testing, masks, design services and royalties as additional revenue streams, while its external learning pages describe advanced packaging as a system-level service. Those pages were not readable to the run fetcher, so this dossier does not infer a specific package design, wafer cycle time or capacity number from them [1]. The business point is that customers value the combined manufacturing relationship, and the company has to keep both front-end wafer output and downstream integration aligned with customer schedules.

Sales are classified by customer platform and headquarters geography. The annual filing says geography follows where customers are headquartered, which may differ from the actual ship-to or manufacturing location [1]. North America represented 75% of FY2025 net revenue, while HPC had become TSMC's largest end-market platform. Both figures show exposure to global AI and consumer supply chains; neither should be read as a map of where wafers are physically fabricated.

What changes a quarter from order intake to cash

A foundry quarter depends on customers placing orders early enough for TSMC to assign capacity to process technologies, then converting scheduled work into shippable output. TSMC reports the observable ends of that chain: revenue, product mix, margin, cash generation and capex. Its FY2025 operating cash flow of NT$2.275T exceeded capex of NT$1.272T, but that aggregate does not reveal the utilization, yield or customer profitability of each fab [1].

The next control point is node migration. In Q3 2026, 2nm contributed 8% of wafer revenue and all 7nm-and-below technologies contributed 79%; those are production-mix indicators rather than a yield report [4][5]. Management said N2 entered high-volume manufacturing in Q4 2025 with good yield, but a numeric yield rate was not found in the accessible primary material. The distinction matters: customers can buy more advanced-node capacity while unit costs and ramp losses still pressure margins.

Who pays and where customer dependence concentrates

TSMC sells to hundreds of customers, but demand is economically concentrated. Its ten largest customers represented 78% of FY2025 net revenue; the largest accounted for 19% and the second largest for 17% [1]. Customer identity is not disclosed in this filing, so no customer-level revenue attribution is supplied here. The concentration means that utilization and negotiating power can change quickly if a few large programs move, delay or redesign their products.

The annual filing also says sales are mostly denominated in US dollars while a large part of capital spending is in other currencies, including the New Taiwan dollar, euro and yen [1]. Currency therefore moves reported New Taiwan dollar revenue and margins even when wafer volumes do not change. This is a real operating exposure, not an accounting footnote.

2. Competitive moat, industry structure & external learning hub

TSMC's lead rests on process technology and its ability to make large volumes of chips for customers[1]. Customers build a design around a process, then need a steady supply of wafers. A new supplier must prove it can meet that need. A working design relationship can be hard to move.

That lead can narrow. Customers can test another supplier, and rivals can invest through a downturn. Track the time a new node needs to reach volume, its yield, customer demand, fab use and cash returns. Q3 demand and wafer mix were strong. But the CFO tied a 1.3-point fall in gross margin to 2nm ramp costs[5][8]. This shows new nodes can raise costs before higher volume offsets them.

The peer set contains unlike business models. Intel's 2025 Form 10-K says nearly all of Intel Foundry still supported internal Intel product manufacturing, even as the company pursues external customers [31]. GlobalFoundries describes a scaled pure-play foundry market that serves specialty and automotive or industrial demand [32]. UMC reports that 28nm-and-below technology contributed 36.8% of its FY2025 foundry revenue [33]. These are useful operating contrasts; this review does not compare their valuation multiples, because the accessible filings are not on a common current date or accounting base.

Capacity scale has to earn a return

TSMC generated US$40.895B of FY2025 capex, while management's current FY2026 budget is US$60B–US$64B [1][8]. That is a substantial step-up in capital intensity. The investment can widen the technology and geographic capacity envelope, but it also raises the amount of output needed before each new facility and process contributes acceptable returns. The company reported Q3 2026 gross margin at 66.4%, with the CFO attributing the sequential decline to the 2nm ramp; further growth in the chip mix therefore needs to translate into profitable volume.

3. Financial trajectory & valuation sanity check

Reported growth and capital spending are both accelerating. In New Taiwan dollars, annual revenue rose from NT$2.162T in 2023 to NT$3.809T in 2025; gross margin expanded from 54.4% to 59.9% and operating margin from 42.6% to 50.8% [1]. The Q3 2026 release shows a further step-up in revenue and margins, but the CFO linked the sequential gross-margin decline to the 2nm ramp [4][8].

The trailing business is cash generative, but the spending curve is changing. FY2025 operating cash flow translated to US$72.521B against US$40.895B of capex, while the current FY2026 budget is US$60B–US$64B [1][8]. The implied increase of 47%–56% from FY2025 capex is a simple comparison of company-reported dollar values, not a forecast of free cash flow.

6. Key risks, thesis breakers & bear case

The strongest bear case is not that advanced chips disappear. It is that TSMC spends ahead of realized demand, a smaller group of customers negotiates aggressively, process ramps dilute margin, and a geopolitical shock interrupts the production network. The Q3 results are strong, yet each of those exposures is visible in the company's own filings or current guidance [1][4][8].

Customer and platform concentration

The top ten customers contributed 78% of FY2025 revenue; the largest two contributed 19% and 17% [1]. Q3 2026 HPC had grown to 68% of revenue, an attractive mix during AI investment but also a single-platform concentration [8]. A delay by one or two large customers could leave expensive capacity underloaded before management can resize the capital base. TSMC does not disclose customer names in the cited concentration table, so customer-level scenario claims are omitted.

Capex, ramp dilution and yield disclosure

The FY2026 US$60B–US$64B budget is materially larger than FY2025 capex of US$40.895B [1][8]. The CFO attributed the latest sequential gross-margin decline to the initial N2 ramp, while N2 represented 8% of wafer revenue in Q3 2026 [5][8]. Management called the yield good but did not provide a percentage in the accessible material. If high investment fails to convert into stable output and customer revenue, depreciation, startup cost and facility overhead can outrun volume growth.

Geographic, currency and policy exposure

TSMC's main operating base is in Taiwan, while the FY2025 filing attributes 75% of net revenue to customers headquartered in North America [1]. Headquarters geography is not manufacturing geography; the revenue table cannot be used to imply that output is diversified. The filing discusses political and regulatory changes, foreign exchange and the cost of overseas capacity as material exposures [1]. Export controls, a Taiwan Strait disruption or a weaker US dollar against operating currencies could alter both customer access and reported profitability.

Valuation and price structure

The 52-week high was $487.47 and the October 30 close was 16.69% below that level; the stock ended below its 50-day and 30-week averages after a 1.99x-volume decline [34][35]. Its $400.12 swing-low area is only $6 below the $406.13 close, leaving little room for a fresh entrant to mistake nearby support for a confirmed base. If growth expectations slip, the forward-multiple case can compress before the operating thesis fully changes.

Demand is the bull case, but the hinge is profitable capacity: reported revenue and wafer mix must keep absorbing the capex without persistent margin erosion. The price screen has already weakened and the 50-day/Stage4 discrepancy needs a fresh, internally consistent read.

Hinge claim: TSMC can turn leading-edge demand into profitable, broadly supported capacity fast enough to earn returns on the enlarged investment program. If the 2nm ramp and HPC mix lift revenue but margins and cash returns deteriorate as capex compounds, the stock can de-rate despite a strategically important foundry franchise. If new nodes scale, customer concentration eases and margin holds through overseas and technology ramps, the current pullback may prove cyclical—but that evidence is not yet in the price structure.
