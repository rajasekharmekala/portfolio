1. What the company does

TSMC is a contract chip maker, which the industry calls a foundry. Other companies design chips and send TSMC the blueprints. TSMC then builds them on silicon wafers, which are thin round slices that carry hundreds of chips each. The company has done this since 1987 and describes itself as the world’s leading dedicated foundry[1]. In 2025 it ran 305 distinct process technologies and made 12,682 products for 534 customers[1]. The shares trade in Taipei as ordinary shares and in New York as American depositary shares (ADS, also called ADRs). Each ADS represents five ordinary shares[6], so an ADR price of $418.51 is the price of five Taiwan shares.

Customers pay for wafers and for services around the wafer. In 2025, wafer sales were NT$3,272.6 billion of NT$3,809.1 billion in net revenue (86%), and the remaining NT$536.5 billion came from other products and services[6]. The company reports in New Taiwan dollars (NT$) under IFRS, the international accounting standard, and the filing shows US dollar figures only as a convenience translation. This report quotes NT$ first and US$ where the company published it. Price per wafer rises with each new generation of chip technology, called a node and named in nanometers (nm). In the third quarter of 2026, the newest 2-nanometer node was 8% of wafer revenue, the 3-nanometer node 33%, the 5-nanometer node 28% and the 7-nanometer node 10%. Together, 7-nanometer and smaller chips were 79% of wafer revenue[1].

What the chips are used for has shifted. High-performance computing (HPC), which includes AI accelerators and server processors, was 68% of third-quarter revenue against 57% a year earlier, while smartphones fell from 30% to 21%[2]. By customer location, North America was 79% of revenue[2]. Management said AI accelerator revenue was a high-teens share of 2025 revenue and that it expects that line to grow at a mid-to-high 50s percentage rate a year from 2024 to 2029[13][9]. The company defines that figure narrowly, so it leaves out server processors for agentic AI, which management says are also rising[9].

Customers are few and large. The ten largest customers were 78% of 2025 revenue, the largest was 19% and the second largest was 17%[6]. The filing labels them Customer A and Customer B and does not name them. Press commentary often names the biggest buyers, but we searched the filing and the earnings call and found no verified names, so we do not name them. The practical meaning is simple: a decision by one or two buyers to move volume, delay a product or build their own plants would show up in TSMC’s revenue quickly.

Where the plants are

Most production is in Taiwan, and the filing says the majority of principal executive officers and principal production facilities are in the Republic of China (R.O.C.)[6]. Overseas sites are growing. The first Arizona fab reached high-volume production at the end of 2024 and the second fab is built, with volume production set for the third quarter of 2027, slightly ahead of the earlier schedule[6][9]. In Japan, the first Kumamoto plant started volume production in December 2024 and a second site began construction in October 2025, with 3-nanometer output planned for 2028[6][9]. In Germany, the Dresden subsidiary has an agreement for up to EUR5 billion of state aid[6]. In July 2026 TSMC raised its planned U.S. investment by $100 billion, to $265 billion and 12 facilities[22].

Management also said it is building 13 leading-edge and advanced packaging fabs in Taiwan and five to six more overseas[9]. It is studying Texas as a second U.S. state and talking to a customer about using its planned fab there, but said any such fab must be run by TSMC[11][9].

The packaging step

After wafers are made, advanced AI chips need a second step: joining logic chips and memory in one package. TSMC’s version is called CoWoS, short for chip-on-wafer-on-substrate. TSMC does not publish CoWoS capacity in wafers per month, so we searched its filing, call and investor site and found no company figure. A third-party estimate puts it at 72,500 wafers per month at the end of 2025, against 40,000 a year earlier, but that is an outside estimate, not a company number[24]. On the October call the CEO said CoWoS is still customers’ first choice, that TSMC is in production at five times the standard chip size, and that demand is higher than supply[9].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence of a moat is the profit left after the cost of making the chips. TSMC’s gross margin, which is revenue minus the direct cost of producing the wafers divided by revenue, rose from 54.4% in 2023 to 56.1% in 2024 and 59.9% in 2025, and reached 66.4% in the third quarter of 2026[6][1]. That happened while the 2-nanometer node ramped, which management said in July would cost about 3 to 4 points of third-quarter margin before offsets, and while overseas plants added a drag of about 2 points[12][9]. Four listed competitors reported gross margins between 25.3% and 40.4% in their latest quarter (table in chapter 3)[18][19][20][21]. A rival cannot charge enough to earn a margin that high unless it can make the same chip at a similar cost, and none of the four reports a margin near it.

The second piece of evidence is the technology lead. The 3-nanometer node produced NT$108.0 billion of revenue in 2023 and NT$794.3 billion in 2025[6]. The 2-nanometer node entered high-volume production in the fourth quarter of 2025 and management expects its annual capacity to more than double in 2027[9]. The next node, A14, is set for risk production in 2027 and volume production in 2028, and management said it has seen more customer activity on A14 than on 2-nanometer so far and expects A14 to be the bigger node[9]. Management also said it plans to use the newest chip-printing tool, called high-NA EUV (a machine that prints finer lines), only from 2030, because it wants the tool mature and its cost reasonable first, and an analyst noted that some competitors will use it earlier[9].

The third piece is scale and pricing. TSMC spent NT$1,272.4 billion on plants and equipment in 2025 (US$40.9 billion) and has budgeted $60 billion to $64 billion for 2026, of which $40.64 billion was spent through three quarters[6][13][9][2]. Few companies can fund that, and the CFO said customers plan their capacity with TSMC well in advance[9]. When asked about pricing, the CEO said TSMC’s pricing strategy depends on the market segment, location and technology, and that there is “no ceiling for TSMC’s profitability,” although a dramatic rise will not happen overnight[9]. He also said demand for leading-edge chips is higher than supply[9].

The business is not free of weakness. It is a cyclical business: in 2023, revenue fell 4.5% and net income attributable to shareholders fell 14.2%[6][7]. Customer concentration is high, with the top ten buyers at 78% of revenue[6]. Manufacturing is concentrated in one place, and the filing lists electricity and water supply in Taiwan as an ongoing concern[6]. Management itself said consumer and price-sensitive end markets are being challenged by rising component prices, that it is being prudent in its 2027 planning, and that leading-edge demand is still very strong[9].

Where competitors could close the gap

Analysts on the October call asked three direct competitive questions, and the answers are the best public view of the risk. One asked about a customer that also builds its own plants and might make more of its own chips. The CEO said TSMC treats every customer the same, counts their internal capacity when it plans, and expects A14 to be bigger than the 2-nanometer node[9]. A second asked whether Intel’s alternative packaging, called EMIB-T, could take work. The CEO said CoWoS is still the first choice and, when asked whether TSMC would serve as a backup if a rival route cannot deliver, answered “yes, we will be”[9]. A third asked about the secondary market in wafer capacity. He said TSMC does not allow customers to sublet or auction off capacity, and the amount involved is very small[9].

TSMC is also buying from smaller partners to ease its own shortages: GlobalFoundries agreed to supply silicon interposers, a layer used in advanced packaging, and the CEO said TSMC has “a little bit shortage on the back end”[9]. That tells you the packaging step, more than wafer making, is where the supply gap sits, and analysts asked about rival packaging routes for the same reason.

The base formula uses the 63-day base low of $372.72, minus one ATR, and gives $359.92. TSM is in Stage 3 and fails Test A, so it is not in a Stage 2 uptrend. We use the base formula. Here is how the Layer 3 sizing terms work. Portfolio heat is the share of your total money you would lose if the stop were hit. Risk-unit sizing sets the position so that heat stays inside a budget. The weight is the lower of the tier cap and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. We rate this idea TIER_3, which allows a 2% to 3.5% position. TIER_2 is too large for a wide stop and a valuation that depends on the multiple holding. The TIER_3 risk budget is 50 basis points, or 0.50% of net worth. The stop is 12.21% below $410. That would allow 4.1%. So the tier cap of 3.5% decides. Net worth in the positions file for October 23, 2026 is $82,376.82. A 3.5% position is about $2,883, or about 7 ADR units at $410. If the stop is hit in an orderly way, the loss is about $352, or 0.43% of net worth.

A gap is a jump past the stop, for example after an earnings report. An 8% gap below $359.92 would exit near $331.13. That loses 19.2% from $410. At 3.5% that is 67 basis points of net worth, which is above the 50 basis point budget. So we cap the position at 2.5% of net worth, about $2,059 or 5 ADR units, until the fourth-quarter report, expected around mid-January 2027 (an estimate). At 2.5% the same gap loses 48 basis points. After the report, and only if guidance holds, the position may grow to the 3.5% tier cap. We do not claim a separate gap budget.

A moving average is a line made from past closing prices. The simple moving average (SMA) weights all days equally. The exponential moving average (EMA) gives more weight to recent days. The let-winners-run rule works like this. If the stock rises, we do not sell just because the RSI goes above 70 or the weight passes 15%. We trail the stop up to the rising 21-day EMA or the 50-day SMA. The position can grow to as much as 22% of net worth while the trend and earnings stay intact. At a 3.5% start, that ceiling is a long way off.
