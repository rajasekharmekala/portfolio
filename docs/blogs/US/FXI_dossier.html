1. What the company does

The iShares China Large-Cap ETF, ticker FXI, is a fund that seeks to track the FTSE China 50 Index. That index holds 50 of the largest and most liquid Chinese companies listed and traded in Hong Kong.[2][6] An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a basket of shares that trades on a stock exchange like a single stock. FXI trades on the NYSE Arca exchange, started on 5 October 2004, and is run by BlackRock Fund Advisors.[2][3] A company has revenue, margins and managers to judge. A fund has none of those of its own, so this book judges the index it copies, the price trend, and the fund's structure: its cost, its trading, its concentration and its risks.

Who pays and for what? You pay an annual fee of 0.73% of the money you hold, which the prospectus shows as $75 in the first year on a $10,000 holding.[3] In return you receive the price changes of the 50 shares, plus their dividends. The fund pays those dividends out twice a year, in June and December. The 12-month trailing yield, which is the sum of payments over the past year divided by the price, is 1.99%, and the 30-day SEC yield, a standard yield figure after fees, is 2.20%.[1] Nothing in the summary prospectus describes borrowing or a leverage multiple. It says the fund may place up to 20% of assets in futures, options and swaps to help it track, which is a tool for matching the index.[3]

The index has three kinds of share, and the differences matter for risk. H shares are mainland companies that list in Hong Kong. Red chips are companies incorporated outside the mainland with at least 30% of their shares held by mainland state bodies. P chips are companies incorporated outside the mainland and controlled by mainland individuals. Red chips and P chips must also draw at least 50% of their revenue or assets from mainland China.[6] FTSE Russell lists the FTSE China 50 among its flagship China benchmarks, built to cover every Chinese share class.[8] The index caps each name at 9% and reviews its members every March, June, September and December. A company joins if it rises to 40th by size and leaves if it falls to 61st, so the list stays at 50 names.[3][7]

FXI is priced in US dollars but its holdings trade in Hong Kong dollars, a currency the Hong Kong Monetary Authority keeps within HK$7.75 to HK$7.85 per US dollar.[18] The currency leg is therefore small, and the earnings of mainland businesses, paid in renminbi, matter more. Timing also matters. Hong Kong trades from 9:30 a.m. to noon and from 1:00 p.m. to 4:00 p.m. local time, with a closing auction that ends at a random moment between 4:08 and 4:10 p.m.[22] NYSE Arca's core session runs from 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern time.[21] Hong Kong's 4:00 p.m. close is about 4:00 a.m. in New York while US daylight time lasts, so the two sessions never overlap and FXI often opens with a gap that reflects what happened after the Hong Kong close.

How the index chooses and reweights its 50 names

The FTSE China 50 Index weights each company by the market value of the shares an outside investor can actually buy, called the free float. It then applies the caps. No stock may exceed 9% of the index, and all stocks above 4.5% together may not exceed 38%.[3] The caps are applied once a quarter, so weights drift between resets. On 30 September the index's largest weight was 9.16%, and its top five weights, each above 4.5%, added up to 38.34%. Both are above the limits, and the fund also held a sixth name above 4.5% (Bank of China, 4.53%).[6][2] That drift is normal for a rule applied quarterly, and it shows how concentrated the index is even after capping. The next change takes effect after the close on the third Friday of December, which is 18 December 2026, and the list of changes is published on the Wednesday before the first Friday of the month.[7]

No leverage, so no decay

A leveraged fund multiplies each day's index move and resets daily, which wears the fund down in choppy markets. FXI does not do that. Its stated aim is to track the index itself, one for one, before fees and costs.[3] Decay is therefore not the risk here. The risks are the ones named in the prospectus: the China risk, the concentration in a few industries, the fact that shares trade at a small premium or discount to the fund's value, and the gap between market hours.[3]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Net asset value (NAV) is the value of everything the fund owns, divided by the number of fund shares. FXI's NAV was $32.42 on 23 October 2026.[1]
  • Premium or discount is the gap between the fund's market price and its NAV. On 23 October it was -0.35%, a small discount.[1]
  • Bid-ask spread is the gap between what buyers offer and sellers ask. FXI's 30-day median spread is 0.03% of the price.[1]
  • Tracking difference is how far the fund's return falls short of, or exceeds, its index over a period.
  • Representative sampling means the fund may hold a sample of the index instead of every name.[3]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

FXI's strengths are in how it is built and traded. It is large, with net assets of $3.72 billion on 23 October 2026, and it trades about 19.0 million shares a day on a 30-day average, which is roughly $615 million at today's price.[1] The median gap between bid and ask is 0.03% of the price, and the price sat 0.35% below net asset value on 23 October. A buyer or seller can move in and out of a normal-sized order with very little friction.[1] The fund has also copied its index closely: it held 50 names on 22 October, the same number as the index.[1][6]

Its weaknesses are cost and concentration. The 0.73% fee is the highest among the China funds compared in this book. The iShares MSCI China ETF charges 0.59%, the SPDR S&P China ETF 0.59%, the Xtrackers Harvest CSI 300 China A-Shares ETF 0.65%, and the KraneShares CSI China Internet ETF 0.69%.[10][12][13][14] The top ten holdings were 57.70% of the fund on 30 September, and the three largest, China Construction Bank, Tencent and Alibaba, were 27.23% together.[2] The prospectus also labels the fund non-diversified, which means it may hold a large share of its assets in a small number of issuers and so its value can swing more.[3]

The trend is the weakness that decides this verdict. The price of $32.31 is 21.65% below its 52-week high of $41.24 and sits close to the bottom of its 52-week range, whose low is $31.19. A confirmed downtrend, called Stage 4 on the Weinstein scale, means the price is below a falling 50-day average that is below a falling 200-day average. The session's price snapshot for 23 October shows exactly that, and Chapter 4 checks each part. Fund size has shrunk with the price: net assets were $6.63 billion at 31 January 2026, $4.34 billion at 31 July, and $3.72 billion on 23 October.[5][4][1] Part of that fall is lower prices, and part may be investors leaving. The sources do not split the two, so we treat the fall as a sign of fading demand, not a measured outflow.

How FXI compares with other China funds

The iShares MSCI China ETF holds 577 stocks and costs 0.59%, so it spreads risk across many more names. Its top ten holdings were 41.40% of the fund.[10][9] The SPDR S&P China ETF holds 1,549 stocks and has $421.75 million in assets.[11][12] The Xtrackers fund buys mainland A-shares, which are shares traded in Shanghai and Shenzhen, and its top ten are only 23.3% of the fund by our addition of the listed weights.[13] The KraneShares fund owns only internet-related companies, and it fell 28.05% from the start of 2026 to 30 September.[14] FXI sits in the middle: more focused than the broad funds, more diversified across industries than the internet fund, and pricier than both broad funds.

What the last fiscal year shows

For the year to 31 July 2026 the fund returned -0.85%, against -0.50% for its index and 20.13% for the FTSE Emerging All Cap Index.[4] The report says large Chinese banks helped returns, helped by capital inflows, higher dividend payouts and government support for the sector. Information technology stocks hurt most: a phone maker faced squeezed margins from memory chip costs and heavy spending on electric vehicles, a food delivery and hotel booking company lost money in a price war, and a travel service faced an anti-monopoly investigation.[4] The pattern is clear: the banks pay and defend, and the internet and consumer names carry the risk of swings. 3. The numbers, in plain words

A normal company dossier shows five years of revenue, profit and cash. FXI is a fund. It has no revenue and no cash flow statement of its own. Four records matter instead. They are the fund's return against its index, what it paid out, how big it was, and how the Chinese economy underneath it grew. The first four tables below cover those. The Layer 1 table then applies the underwriting tests in their fund form.

Several tests in the scoring rules ask about a company's finances. Return on invested capital (ROIC) is the profit earned on each dollar invested in the business. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit before financing and accounting charges, and net debt is borrowings minus cash. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is the cost of paying staff in shares, and earnings per share (EPS) is a company's profit divided across its shares. None of these exists for a fund as such. We replace them with measures drawn from the fund's 50 holdings in aggregate.[2]

The index is cheaper than the broad Chinese market. FXI's price-to-earnings ratio is 11.11. The broader MSCI China Index had 13.03 trailing and 10.15 forward on 30 September.[1][15] The ratio divides the price by the last 12 months of profit. The fund's own glossary notes that negative earnings are excluded and ratios above 60 are set to 60. So it reads a little better than a simple average.[2] Yearly returns have swung widely. Three years of losses from 2021 to 2023 were followed by gains of about 30% in each of 2024 and 2025. In 2026 the fund is down again.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one weakest assumption. The central price assumes 10% earnings growth with a steady ratio, and the weighted price assumes the probabilities are right. The stress assumes a gap from the $32.61 fill stays under about 14%. Below we state what would show each one wrong, and when we would know. The bear case is not a prediction. It is the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion.

Scenario 1: rates and stimulus keep the price falling

The bear case here is simple. Good economic news with no new stimulus is a bad mix for Hong Kong stocks. On 20 October the Hang Seng fell 1.32% after strong growth cut hopes for easing, and on 22 October it was down 2.87% by midday while the tech index hit a two-year low.[25][24] If US yields stay at a two-decade high, investors who can choose will keep selling Hong Kong shares, and the 63-day low of $32.16 would give way. The Fed's decision on 27 and 28 October is the first test.[23][27]

Scenario 2: US-China relations turn

The prospectus warns that the index may include companies facing trade or economic limits from the US government, and that Chinese companies listed in the US may be delisted if they do not meet US accounting and auditor oversight rules, which would cut their value.[3] The tariff pause runs only until 10 January, and the truce keeps US tariffs on Chinese goods at 30%. A breakdown would not need to reach the index's holdings directly to hit the price, because the whole market would reprice the risk.[19][20]

7. What we would do, and when

FXI is not held. The positions file for this session lists no FXI shares, and the options book holds no FXI contracts, so there is no holding audit here. The watchlist label read 'ETF tracking the 50 largest Chinese companies listed in Hong Kong offering deep value exposure'. We checked it against the prospectus and fact sheet: the index (FTSE China 50), the 50 holdings and the Hong Kong listing match, while 'deep value' is too strong, since the price-to-book of 1.35 is level with the broad market's 1.38.[3][1][15] The trigger that would change our answer is a cure of the trend, set out in the trigger paragraphs below.

The verdict is REJECTED, and the rule behind it is the one in the scoring file: only a Stage 4 breakdown, with the close below a falling 50-day average that is below a falling 200-day average and the recovery test failing, disqualifies a name. FXI meets both parts, so it is rejected even though its fund quality and valuation are acceptable. The planned position size is therefore 0% of net worth. A name that passes the rule is not bought at the market price because the support distance and the buy range also matter, but those only apply once the red line is lifted.

Several terms in the sizing table need plain words. The current market price (CMP) is the last close, $32.31. An exponential moving average (EMA) is an average that gives more weight to recent days, and a simple moving average (SMA) weighs all days equally. A basis point (bp) is one hundredth of a percent. Portfolio heat is the share of net worth we would lose if every open position hit its stop at once, and the scoring file caps it at 12%. A conviction tier sets the largest position size, and TIER_0 means no position. Risk-unit sizing sets the size from the stop distance: size equals the tier's risk budget in basis points divided by the stop distance, never above the tier ceiling. The stop is a structural invalidation level, which means a price that would show the idea is wrong, here below the $32.16 swing low and 63-day low.

If the trend cures, the posture would be a limit order, a buy order that fills only at or below a chosen price, in an entry zone of $32.16 to $32.61. The low end is the support price. The high end is that support plus 1.0 ATR, which sits inside the 'standard size' band of the support-distance ladder, under 1.2 ATR. Today's price of $32.31 is 0.33 ATR above support, inside the 'full size' band of under 0.6 ATR. The tactical stop rule is below the lower of the 20-day swing low ($32.16) and the 20-day average, minus 0.5 ATR, which is $32.16 minus $0.225, or $31.93.

The reward-to-risk ratio, expected gain divided by the loss at the stop, is the next check. At the current price, the central target of $35.50 gives (35.50 minus 32.31) divided by (32.31 minus 31.93), which is 3.19 divided by 0.38, or 8.39 to 1. At the top of the entry zone it gives 4.25 to 1. Both clear the 2.5 to 1 floor for the TACTICAL_MOMENTUM bucket. But these ratios are high only because the stop is close. It is 1.18% below the current price, which is less than one typical day's swing, and 2.09% below $32.61. A wider stop below the 52-week low of $31.19, minus 0.5 ATR, is $30.97. With that stop the ratios fall to 2.38 at the current price and 1.76 at $32.61, both below the floor. So the pass is fragile and depends on a tight stop that normal noise could hit.

A fully cured trend would allow a TIER_3 position, with a ceiling of 3.5% of net worth and a risk budget of 50 bps. At a stop distance of 2.09% the formula gives 50 divided by 2.09, about 24%, so the 3.5% tier ceiling is what limits size, not the risk budget. At the wider stop of 5.03% the formula gives about 9.9%, and the ceiling still binds. Measured from a $32.61 fill, a gap through the stop would cost about 21 bps on a 6% drop (to $30.65) and 35 bps on a 10% drop (to $29.35), inside the 50 bps budget. There is no earnings date to cap the weight for, because the fund holds 50 companies that report at different times. The let-winners-run rule says that for a Stage 2 winner with sound fundamentals, we would never trim only because the RSI passes 70 or the weight crosses 15%, and we would trail the stop under the rising 21-day EMA or 50-day SMA, up to a 22% weight. That rule does not apply today, because FXI is in Stage 4.
