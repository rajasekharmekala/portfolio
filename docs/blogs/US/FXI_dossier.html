1. What the company does

FXI is a basket that you can buy in one click. The fund, launched on October 5, 2004, holds shares of large Chinese companies that trade in Hong Kong, and its own price on the New York Stock Exchange's Arca market moves with the value of that basket.[1] It follows the FTSE China 50 Index, a list of the 50 largest and most heavily traded Chinese stocks that are open to foreign buyers; only Hong Kong-listed H shares, red chips and P chips qualify.[11][15] On October 16, 2026 the fund had $3,885,974,848 of net assets and 114,900,000 shares outstanding, and each share had a net asset value of $33.82.[1] You do not pay the companies directly. You pay BlackRock Fund Advisors a fee, and it buys and holds the shares for you.

Who pays, and how does the fund earn money? The prospectus quotes a fee of 0.73% a year, and BlackRock pays almost every other operating cost out of that fee.[13] The audited fiscal 2026 accounts show 0.74%, because the advisory fee schedule is 0.74% on the first $6 billion, and no source we read explains the gap of one hundredth of a point, so we use 0.73% for the quoted fee and 0.74% for what the fund actually charged.[8][7] The fund earns what the companies pay out. In the year to July 31, 2026 the fund received $160,971,730 of dividends, lost $12,817,090 to tax withheld abroad, and added $2,869,811 from lending shares to borrowers, which made total investment income $151,226,389.[5] After $45,297,624 of expenses, net investment income was $105,928,765, and the fund paid out $108,393,446 to owners as ordinary income.[5][6] So the revenue line of this product is the dividend stream of fifty companies, and the cost line is one fee.

The fund is not a full photocopy of the index. It uses what its prospectus calls representative sampling, which means it holds a selection of shares that behaves like the whole list, and it may put up to 20% of assets in futures, cash and shares outside the index.[11] Its annual report counts 53 holdings, its fact sheet counts 50, and a data vendor counts 59, so the exact count depends on who is counting.[3][1][21] Two rules cap concentration at each quarterly index review: no company may weigh more than 9%, and the companies above 4.5% may not add up to more than 38%.[16][11] The fund is also classed as non-diversified, which means the rules allow it to put a large share of its money in a few issuers.[11]

Two facts matter for an owner today. First, money is leaving: net assets fell 31.3% in the fiscal year to $4.34 billion, and owners redeemed a net $1.72 billion of shares, which is 27.2% of the opening net assets of $6.33 billion.[6][7] Second, you already own a large piece of the same basket. Your Tencent holding (TCEHY, 115 shares) is worth $6,066.20, or 7.34% of net worth, and Tencent is the third-largest line in FXI at 8.92%.[38][22] Buying FXI would therefore add to a position you already hold, which is a diversification question as much as a price question.

How owning the fund differs from owning the shares

Owning the fund is simpler and more expensive. You buy and sell during New York hours, the dividends arrive twice a year, and you never touch Hong Kong brokers, currency exchange or Hong Kong's settlement system. The fund paid $0.416015 a share in December 2025 and $0.263439 in June 2026, a total of $0.679454 over twelve months, which is 2.01% of the $33.84 price.[1] The cost is the 0.73% fee, the dividend tax withheld abroad (about 8% of gross dividends last year, from $12,817,090 on $160,971,730), and the price gap between what the fund holds and what it is worth at any minute.[5] The price gap is small here: the fund closed at $33.84 against a net asset value of $33.82, a premium of 0.06%.[1]

What changed in 2025 and 2026, and why it matters for an owner

The index is stable but not frozen. FTSE Russell reviews it every March, June, September and December, adds a company if it ranks 40th or better by full market value, and removes one that falls to 61st or lower.[15] The March 4, 2026 review added two companies, which under the constant-number rule means two others left, and the September 2, 2026 review added two more.[18][17] The fund's own turnover was 18% in fiscal 2026, against 26% a year earlier, so the changes were small.[7] The larger change is in the owners. Shares outstanding went from 142,500,000 on June 30 to 119,250,000 on July 31 and 114,900,000 on October 16, a fall of 19.4% in about three and a half months.[2][4][1] For an owner this means the trend of the fund is set by the Chinese stock market and by investors who are still selling, and the next chapters test whether the numbers and the chart support buying into that.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Relative strength index (RSI) is a momentum gauge from 0 to 100. Below 30 it is called oversold, and above 70 it is called overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing over 14 trading days. We use it to measure how far the price sits from support.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing prices over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. Each one is a slowly moving line that the price tends to bounce off.
  • Stage 2 and Stage 4 are the two ends of the Weinstein stage scale: Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend.
  • A base is a sideways stretch of trading before a move, a pivot is the top of that stretch, a breakout is a close above the pivot on heavy volume, and a retest is a pullback that holds the old pivot as support.
  • A measured move is the height of a base projected above its pivot to set a target.
  • A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold, the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned, and a limit order is an order that only fills at your price or better.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain from the price to the target divided by the loss from the price to the stop.
  • The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) is the price paid for each dollar of yearly profit. PEG (price-to-earnings-to-growth) is that ratio divided by the expected growth rate of profit.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is a company's profit divided by its share count. A consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for a number, and guidance is a company's own forecast.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before financing and accounting charges. Net debt is borrowings minus cash, and debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is the profit earned on the money invested in a business. FCF is free cash flow, the cash left after running the business and buying equipment, and cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before financing.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying staff in shares, and dilution is the growth in share count that makes each share a smaller slice. A buyback is a company buying its own shares, which shrinks the count.
  • Capital expenditure is spending on plants, equipment and computing capacity. Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. Gross margin, operating margin and net margin are the shares of revenue left after direct costs, all running costs and everything, in that order.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The fund's strongest feature is how easy it is to trade. The 30-day average volume is 18,058,530 shares, which at the $33.84 close is about $611 million of stock changing hands a day, the median gap between buying and selling prices is 0.03%, and the closing premium to net asset value is 0.06%.[1] Net assets of $3.89 billion put it third among the five China funds compared in chapter 3, behind MCHI at $5.82 billion and KWEB at $4.59 billion.[21][23][24] A buyer of $50,000 will not move the price, and a seller will not wait. That liquidity is documented in the daily figures cited here, and it is the main practical reason to hold a fund rather than the shares.

The weakest features are cost and shape. At 0.73% a year, the fee is 3.8 times the 0.19% charged by FLCH and well above the 0.59% charged by both MCHI and GXC.[1][26][23][25] The shape is heavy in banks and two internet platforms, and the largest three holdings (China Construction Bank 9.36%, Alibaba 9.06%, Tencent 8.92%) together weigh 27.34% of the fund.[22] Competitors that hold hundreds of companies spread that risk: MCHI holds 586 names with a top ten of 42.46%, while FXI's top ten is 58.52%.[23][21] In return, FXI holds more state-owned banks and fewer internet platforms than KWEB, whose top ten is 61.34% and whose total return was minus 34.70% over the past year.[24]

The evidence of how the position has paid is mixed. The fund returned minus 15.78% over the year to September 30, 2026, plus 11.33% a year over three years, minus 0.33% a year over five years and plus 1.45% a year over ten years, all on net asset value.[1] In the fiscal year to July 31, 2026 it returned minus 0.85% while the broad emerging-markets index it is compared with returned plus 20.13%, a gap of almost 21 percentage points.[3] The index provider's own review says the index is "deeply discounted" but that the discount is largely justified by weaker growth, lower profitability and subdued earnings momentum, with dividends as the main support.[19] A five-year maximum drawdown of minus 49.5% shows how deep the drops can be.[20]

Where the competitive position is weaker than it looks

Three structural points are easy to miss. The first is that the fund does not hold every share in its index: it samples, so its results can differ from the index by more than the fee, and calendar 2024 shows how: the fund returned 30.10% and the index 31.98%, a gap of 1.88 percentage points against a 0.73% fee.[11][1] The second is that the concentration caps are applied only at the quarterly review, so weights drift between reviews. On October 15 the seven holdings above 4.5% added up to 47.95%, against a 38% ceiling that applies on review dates, and two names (China Construction Bank and Alibaba) were above the 9% single-company cap.[22][16] The third is the set of China-specific risks the prospectus lists: government control over certain sectors, the use of variable interest entity structures by some listed companies, possible delisting of companies that do not meet U.S. auditor-oversight rules, and a currency that can move against the dollar.[12] On December 15, 2022 the PCAOB vacated its earlier finding that it could not inspect auditors in mainland China and Hong Kong, and the SEC says it will not identify an issuer under the delisting law while that stands, so the risk is dormant rather than gone.[36] For an owner this means the low spread is a trading advantage, not a protection against what the basket does, and the next chapter scores the fund on measures that fit an ETF.

3. The numbers, in plain words

A company report starts with sales, profit and cash. A fund has none of those. Its audited statements contain four parts: a balance sheet, an income statement for one year, a statement of changes in net assets for two years, and per-share financial highlights for five years. There is no statement of cash flows, so a free cash flow margin cannot be computed and is marked N/A with that reason.[10][7]

The four company-quality gates in the portfolio rules (ROIC, free cash flow margin, net debt to EBITDA and stock-based pay) therefore do not apply to FXI, and we say so in each row of the Layer 1 table instead of inventing a number.
