1. What the company does

Capital One is a bank holding company based in McLean, Virginia. At the end of September 2026 it held $672.9 billion of assets, $480.7 billion of customer deposits and $462.5 billion of loans.[2][1] It earns money the way any lender does. It pays savers a low rate, lends at a higher rate, and keeps the gap after paying for loans that go bad and for the cost of running the bank. In the third quarter it paid 2.94 percent on interest-bearing deposits and earned 13.56 percent on its loans.[1] The gap between what the bank earns on assets and what it pays for funding, measured against the assets that earn interest, is the net interest margin (NIM). Capital One's NIM was 8.41 percent in the third quarter, a high figure because credit card loans carry high rates.[1]

Three groups pay Capital One. Cardholders pay interest on balances they carry and fees, and that is the largest source of revenue. Stores pay too, through interchange, the fee a merchant hands to the card issuer each time a customer swipes a card. Borrowers pay interest on car loans and on loans to businesses. In the third quarter net interest income was $12.9 billion and non-interest income, mostly interchange and fees, was $3.5 billion, for total net revenue of $16.3 billion.[1] Net interchange income was $2.3 billion in the quarter, against $1.8 billion a year earlier, because Capital One debit cards now run on its own network.[1][8] Auto loans reached $92.9 billion and auto originations were $13.1 billion, up 22 percent from a year earlier.[1]

Two acquisitions changed the company. On 18 May 2025 Capital One closed its purchase of Discover Financial Services, a card issuer that also owns a payments network, at total purchase consideration of $51.8 billion.[17] Discover brought the Discover, PULSE and Diners Club networks, which Capital One reports together as the Global Payment Network.[17] In April 2026 it bought Brex, a company that sells business cards and spending software, for about $2.56 billion in cash and 10.6 million Capital One shares.[22] When the Discover deal was announced in February 2024 the companies promised $2.7 billion of pre-tax savings and extra revenue by 2027, with 15 percent or more added to adjusted earnings per share and a 16 percent return on the capital invested.[19] Management now quotes the savings goal as $2.5 billion, and says about $1.8 billion a year had been reached by the third quarter.[8][7]

Winning customers is expensive. Marketing cost $1.6 billion in the quarter, up 14 percent from a year earlier, and management plans to spend more in 2027.[1][7] About 73 percent of domestic card loans are to customers with credit scores above 660, a common dividing line between prime and weaker borrowers; the other 27 percent are at or below 660.[1] The bank also runs a national deposit franchise, with $431.1 billion of consumer deposits that fund most of the loans.[1]

How a dollar of assets becomes profit

A bank is easier to understand when you follow $100 of earning assets through the year. Using third-quarter results at an annual pace, $100 of average earning assets produced $10.67 of net revenue. Running the bank cost $5.78, of which marketing was a large part. Loan losses and reserves took another $1.90, which left $2.99 before tax. After tax at a 22.6 percent rate, the bank kept about $2.31.[1] Those figures are our arithmetic from the supplement, using total net revenue of $16,344 million, non-interest expense of $8,856 million, provision of $2,916 million and average interest-earning assets of $612,521 million.[1] The chain shows why small changes in credit matter. A one-point rise in the loss rate on a bank this size removes about $0.75 from the $2.99 of pre-tax profit per $100 of assets, which is a quarter of it. It also shows why the cost line matters, since it is about three times the credit line.

Two cautions apply. First, the third quarter is seasonally strong for margins and charge-offs, and management said so on the call.[7] Second, the quarter included a $225 million release of reserves, which lifted profit by about $0.29 a share after tax in our arithmetic (the release, taxed at the quarter's rate and divided by 609.5 million diluted shares).[1] For a reader, the practical point is that the headline return on tangible equity of 21.1 percent is a good quarter, not a steady state, and the next chapters test how much of it is repeatable.

Where management says growth will come from

Management names four growth areas. The first is card customers who spend heavily at the top of the market, served by premium cards and airport lounges. The second is a national bank without branches, where marketing does the work that branches do for other banks. The third is Discover Network volume, which already includes Capital One debit and is meant to grow through credit cards. The fourth is Brex, which Capital One wants to connect to its travel business and its small-business customers.[7] Management says these businesses cost more to build, because they depend on marketing, but they carry lower risk, lower capital needs and long-lasting revenue.[7] That claim is central to the case. If it is right, a rising marketing bill is an investment that pays back in later years. If it is wrong, it is a permanent cost that holds the efficiency ratio, which is costs as a share of revenue, near 54 percent.[1] The record of the past year, covered in chapter 2B, shows that management has delivered on cost savings faster than on network growth, which is why we treat growth as a watch item.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Net charge-off is a loan the bank has given up on, minus any money later recovered. The charge-off rate is those losses over a year as a share of average loans.
  • CET1 (common equity Tier 1) is the highest-quality capital a bank holds, shown as a share of its risk-weighted assets. Regulators set a minimum for each bank.
  • Return on tangible common equity (ROTCE) is profit to common shareholders divided by shareholders' equity after removing goodwill and other intangibles, so it shows the return on the hard capital.
  • Tangible book value is shareholders' equity minus goodwill and other intangibles. Dividing by shares gives tangible book value per share.
  • Efficiency ratio is non-interest expense divided by net revenue. Lower is better.
  • Allowance for credit losses is the reserve a bank sets aside for loans it expects to lose. A release lifts profit and a build cuts it.
  • Stress capital buffer is the extra capital the Federal Reserve requires on top of the regulatory minimum, set from its annual stress test.
  • Purchase volume is the dollar value of card purchases in a period, which drives interchange income.
  • Brownout is management's word for a temporary period in which Discover card loans shrink because credit limits and approvals were tightened. Chapter 2 now asks whether the business is strong or weak.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Capital One's competitive edge is a data and technology advantage in lending to consumers without collateral. The company says it is the only major US bank to have moved entirely onto the public cloud.[2] Its chief executive describes a technology rebuild, from the bottom of the technology stack up, that is now in its 14th year.[8] The reason this matters is that a card lender wins or loses on pricing risk correctly. A lender that sees more data, updates its models faster and monitors accounts in real time can lend to customers that others price too high or refuse, and can pull back sooner when trouble starts.

The best evidence that the edge pays is in credit results. Domestic card net charge-offs were 3.98 percent in the third quarter, down 65 basis points from a year earlier, and the 30-day delinquency rate was 3.74 percent, down 15 basis points.[1] Synchrony, a card lender with a different mix, reported net charge-offs of 4.79 percent, and Bread Financial reported a net loss rate of 6.40 percent.[27][30] JPMorgan's card charge-off rate was lower, at 2.91 percent.[29] We did not retrieve JPMorgan's credit-score mix, so we do not claim the two are like for like. What we can say is that Capital One earns a 17.66 percent yield on domestic card loans while keeping charge-offs under 4 percent, and that 27 percent of those loans go to customers with scores of 660 or below.[1]

The second strength is the network. A Capital One debit purchase now runs on the Discover Network instead of a rival's, so Capital One earns the network's fee as well as the issuer's. Global Payment Network volume was $190.9 billion in the quarter, up 25 percent from a year earlier, after the debit conversion finished.[1][7] The chief executive calls the network a rare and valuable asset, 'the crown jewel of the acquisition', and 'a very scale-sensitive business.'[7] Moving credit cards onto the network will be slower: in his words, 'This is not going to be a big bang thing like you saw on the debit side.'[7]

Financial strength supports the franchise. The common equity Tier 1 ratio was 13.8 percent against a regulatory requirement of 9.0 percent, and management calls 11 percent its long-term capital need.[1][13][8] S&P raised the parent company's credit rating to BBB+ in September 2026, citing its improved market position, profitability and integration progress, while Moody's rated it Baa1 and Fitch A- at the end of June.[42][13] Total liquidity reserves were $134 billion and the average liquidity coverage ratio was 163 percent.[7]

Where the edge looks real

Three pieces of evidence point to a real edge. First, credit is improving while the national pattern is flat to slightly worse: in the New York Fed's data the flow of card balances into serious delinquency was 6.97 percent in the second quarter, against 6.93 percent a year earlier.[33] Capital One's own domestic card delinquency fell 15 basis points over the same year and charge-offs fell 65, so its experience is better than the national pattern.[1] Second, the company has put money behind its view: it repurchased $2.6 billion of its stock in the third quarter and kept its capital ratio near 13.8 percent.[7] Third, the cost savings are showing. The adjusted efficiency ratio, which removes deal costs and purchase amortization, was 48.76 percent in the third quarter against 53.73 percent in the fourth quarter of 2025.[2][1] An owner can treat these as signs of an advantage in underwriting, technology and scale, though not yet as proof that the advantage lasts through a downturn.

Where the edge is unproven

Four things are not yet proven. The first is earnings stability. Return on tangible common equity was 28.4 percent in 2021, 13.0 percent in 2023 and 3.2 percent in 2025, when Discover purchase accounting included an initial allowance build of $8,767 million in the first nine months.[18][17][1] The second is Discover itself. Discover card loans shrank 2.9 percent from a year earlier, and management expects further contraction for several quarters before growth returns.[7] The third is regulation of the card business, covered in chapter 5, where a settlement on interchange and two bills in Congress could reduce revenue.[35][37][38] The fourth is inherited legal exposure. The company estimates reasonably possible losses above its reserves of up to $300 million, Discover entered a Federal Reserve consent order in April 2025 over the card misclassification matter, and a $425 million savings-account settlement is on appeal by an individual objector.[13] None of these breaks the case on its own. Together they explain why the stock trades at a low multiple, and chapter 2B shows how management has handled promises so far.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

A bank cannot be judged on free cash flow (FCF), the cash left after running the business and buying assets, or on net debt, which is borrowings minus cash. Lending and borrowing are a bank's products, not side effects. Those two checks are therefore marked not applicable, and the scorecard below takes their place. We picked five measures that decide whether a card-and-auto lender earns its keep. They are credit losses, capital, the Discover savings and cost ratio, loan and purchase growth, and net interest margin. Each is scored PASS for 1.0 point, WATCH for 0.5 or FAIL for 0.0. A name qualifies when the weighted score is at least 2.5 of 4 or 3.0 of 5, with no more than one FAIL.[43]

Red-team scenario 1: the credit cycle turns while reserves are thinner

The strongest bear argument is that card charge-offs of 3.98 percent are flattered by two temporary effects. Management says its recoveries are higher than normal because of an inventory of past charge-offs, and the third quarter included a $225 million reserve release.[7][1] The reserve against loans fell from 5.28 percent in the first quarter to 4.92 percent, which means less cushion if losses rise.[1] Meanwhile the Federal Reserve raised its target to 3.75 to 4.00 percent on 16 September and described inflation as elevated. The 10-year yield is 5.0 percent. Revolving consumer credit fell at a 4.2 percent annual rate in August, a sign that households are pulling back.[31][43][32] The New York Fed's flow of card balances into serious delinquency rose to 6.97 percent from 6.93 percent a year earlier. It described new delinquencies as elevated.[33] If charge-offs went back to the 5.10 percent of the first quarter, our rule says exit. At that rate, and with another reserve build, 2027 earnings could be 25 percent lower, which is our bear case of $149, or 1.45 times tangible book value. A deeper recession could take the price toward tangible book value of about $103. An analyst on the October call said the stock has historically fallen to tangible book value in downturns, and asked whether the Discover deal changes that.[7][1] We estimate the chance of the $149 outcome at 25 percent and of the $103 outcome at no more than 5 percent.

Red-team scenario 2: policy and court outcomes cut card revenue

A second bear case is that regulation changes the economics of rewards cards. A bill to cap card interest at 10 percent is in committee. The president demanded such a cap on 9 January 2026. Banks largely kept their rates, and experts said Congress would have to act.[37][39] The Credit Card Competition Act, which would require a second network on credit cards, was introduced in January 2026 and is also in committee.[38] Both lapse on 3 January 2027 and would have to be reintroduced. Among bills in committee this long only a small share ever advance. The November elections that the call transcript raises could change the mood.[37][38][7] The Visa and Mastercard settlement is the more concrete risk. It would cut average credit interchange by 10 basis points for five years. It would cap standard consumer rates at 1.25 percent. It would also let merchants refuse premium cards, the product Capital One is expanding.[35][36] Management says the loss roughly equals the savings still to come. By our arithmetic those savings are $0.7 billion ($2.5 billion goal less $1.8 billion reached). Management also says its estimate has 'a pretty wide error bar.'[7][8] Each extra $0.7 billion of lost interchange that savings do not offset would cost about $0.90 a share after tax, or about $9 a share at 10 times earnings.

Red-team scenario 3: the Discover brownout and marketing consume the synergies

The third bear case is an execution story. Discover card loans fell 2.9 percent in a year. Management expects contraction for several more quarters. The credit card move to the Discover Network has already slipped from a mid-2026 start to a gradual test.[7][10][8] At the same time marketing grew 14 percent in the quarter and will rise faster in 2027, as management says it must match competitors.[1][7] If volume stays flat and marketing rises 18 percent in 2027, the efficiency ratio would stop improving and the synergy gains would go into expenses and not into profit. Management said in 2025 that integration costs would be 'somewhat higher' than the $2.8 billion first announced, and no total has been stated.[17][1][11] Legal costs add a tail. There are up to $300 million of reasonably possible losses above reserves. There is a Federal Reserve consent order inherited from Discover. There is an SEC investigation of the card misclassification matter. A savings-account settlement is on appeal.[13] Suppose 2027 earnings stay at the 2026 level of $21.50 and the stock trades at 9 times. The value is about $194. That is 5 percent below today's price and 5 percent above our stop.
