1. What the company does

Capital One lends money mostly through credit cards, and it earns the gap between what it charges borrowers and what it pays depositors. It describes itself as a technology-based financial services company that runs three lines of business: Credit Card, Consumer Banking (which now includes its payment network), and Commercial Banking. It had $484.3 billion of deposits and $673.8 billion of total assets on June 30, 2026, and it operates in the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom.[2] It also says it is the only major U.S. bank to move entirely onto the public cloud.[2]

The size of the card business explains almost everything about the stock. Credit card loans were $275.4 billion of the $457.2 billion total loans on June 30, 2026, which is about 60 percent (our division of the two figures). Consumer Banking held $90.5 billion, mostly auto loans, and Commercial Banking held $91.3 billion.[2] In the second quarter of 2026, total net revenue was $15.85 billion, of which net interest income, the interest earned on loans minus the interest paid on deposits and debt, was $12.37 billion, or 78 percent.[1] That means the company lives or dies by two things: how much it can lend at what rate, and how many borrowers fail to repay.

Two acquisitions changed the company in the last eighteen months. The Discover purchase was announced on February 19, 2024 as an all-stock deal valued at $35.3 billion, and it closed on May 18, 2025.[6][9] Discover brought a second card portfolio and a payment network, meaning the rails that carry card payments from shoppers to merchants and earn a fee on each one. In the second quarter of 2026 the Global Payment Network processed $189.6 billion of volume, compared with $74.0 billion a year earlier, though the earlier figure covers only part of the quarter after the deal closed.[1] Capital One is moving its debit cards onto that network and testing the same for credit cards.[9]

The Brex deal is smaller. Capital One announced on January 22, 2026 that it would buy Brex, a corporate card and expense software company, in a stock and cash deal valued at $5.15 billion, and it completed the purchase on April 7, 2026.[7][8] The 10-Q (the quarterly report filed with the SEC) says the total consideration paid was about $4.5 billion, made up of $2.6 billion in cash and 10.6 million Capital One shares.[3] The chief executive said at announcement that Brex alone would reduce earnings at first, because it is a fast-growing business bought for its growth rate.[10] That matters for the report because both deals add costs now and promise savings and earnings later.

A last point about the business model: the loss on a card loan arrives after the interest has been earned. When a bank grows loans quickly it books the expected loss immediately, as a charge called the provision for credit losses, so profits dip in growth years and recover afterward. Capital One's provision was $2.99 billion in the second quarter of 2026 against $11.43 billion a year earlier, when it booked a one-time $8.77 billion reserve for the Discover loans it acquired.[1] Reading the profit history without that context makes 2025 look like a collapse and 2026 look like a miracle. Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPS stands for earnings per share, the company's profit divided across all its shares. It is the number analysts forecast most often.
  • NIM stands for net interest margin, the interest a bank earns on its loans and investments minus the interest it pays, divided by the assets that earn interest. Capital One's was 8.01 percent in the second quarter of 2026, unusually high because credit cards charge high rates.[1]
  • ROTCE means return on tangible common equity: yearly profit to common shareholders divided by their equity after subtracting goodwill and other acquisition intangibles. It is the main return measure for banks.
  • CET1 is the common equity Tier 1 ratio, the bank's highest-quality capital divided by its risk-weighted assets. Regulators require a minimum, and a bigger cushion means room to buy back shares or absorb losses.
  • Net charge-off rate is the share of loans written off as uncollectible, after recoveries, expressed as a yearly percentage of average loans.
  • Delinquency means a borrower is behind on payments. The 30+ day rate counts loans at least 30 days late and is the earliest warning of future charge-offs.
  • Allowance coverage is the reserve a bank holds against expected loan losses, divided by its loans. Capital One's was 5.02 percent on June 30, 2026.[1]
  • Efficiency ratio is operating costs divided by revenue. A lower figure means the bank spends fewer cents to earn each dollar.
  • Reverse DCF means working backward from today's price to find the growth in cash or earnings that the price already assumes.
  • Chart terms. The 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the average closing prices over the last 50 and 200 trading days, slow lines that prices tend to bounce off. The RSI, or relative strength index, is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been rising. The ATR, or average true range, is the typical daily price swing in dollars. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. A base is a sideways pattern a stock builds before a move, its pivot is the top of that pattern, and a breakout is a close through the pivot on strong volume.
  • Trade terms. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better and does not chase the market. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain from the price to the target divided by the loss from the price to the stop. At $201.74 with a target of $254.10 and a stop of $184.95, it is 3.12.
  • Valuation and gate terms. Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share. Net debt is interest-bearing debt minus cash, and EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit. ROIC means return on invested capital, the after-tax profit earned on each dollar put into the business. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment. SBC means stock-based compensation, the cost of paying employees in shares.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The evidence for a strong business starts with the margin on its loans. Net interest margin was 8.01 percent in the second quarter of 2026, up from 7.62 percent a year earlier. Management says the second half of 2025 is a fair guide to where it settles.[1][9] Return on tangible common equity was 18.04 percent in the quarter and 15.05 percent for the first six months. That clears a cost of equity that we assume is 10 percent, the same rate the scoring rules use for discounting.[1][30] Funding is a second strength. Deposits of $484.3 billion exceed loans of $457.2 billion, and the rate paid on interest-bearing deposits fell 9 basis points to 2.91 percent.[2] Liquidity reserves were about $144 billion. The average liquidity coverage ratio, a regulatory test of cash on hand against 30 days of stress, was 165 percent.[9]

Credit quality is the third strength, and it is improving. The domestic card charge-off rate was 4.71 percent in the second quarter, down 54 basis points from a year earlier. Its 30+ day delinquency rate was 3.39 percent, down 21 basis points.[1] The monthly report for August 2026 showed a domestic card charge-off rate of 4.16 percent and a delinquency rate of 3.57 percent. The improvement therefore continued after the quarter ended, with some seasonal drift in delinquencies.[11] Allowance coverage of 5.02 percent equals about 1.6 years of losses at the second quarter's annualized pace (22,966 divided by 14,568, our arithmetic).[1] The reserve was cut by $662 million in the quarter, which helped profit. Some of the reported gain is a smaller safety cushion and not new earnings.[2]

Scale and technology are the moat, which means the lasting advantage that keeps rivals from copying the model. Capital One says it is in the 14th year of rebuilding its systems from the bottom of the technology stack upward, and that it has moved to the cloud. It is also spending to win customers who spend heavily at the top of the card market.[2][9] Owning the Discover network adds a second source of profit. Management said the full quarterly run rate of debit revenue gains is already in second-quarter results.[9] These are claims by management. The filings measure the results and not the moat itself. Our reading is that a card lender with $259 billion of domestic card loans and its own network has more pricing and data advantages than a typical bank. None of that is proven to hold through a recession.

Now the weak side. Return on tangible common equity fell from 28.39 percent in 2021 to 3.16 percent in 2025. That decline includes a one-time Discover reserve and merger costs. It also reflects charge-offs that rose from 0.88 percent of loans to 3.30 percent.[4][5] Efficiency has worsened. The efficiency ratio was 57.05 percent in the second quarter against 55.96 percent a year earlier. Management said in January that its investments would put upward pressure on the ratio in the near term.[1][10] The Discover card portfolio is shrinking. Its loans fell 1.5 percent from a year earlier. Management calls this a brownout and expects it to bottom around the fourth quarter of 2026.[9] Management also declines to give a number for the efficiency ratio on the other side of the integration.[9]

Politics and rules add risk that is hard to size. President Trump asked Congress on January 21, 2026 to cap credit card interest at 10 percent for one year. Capital One's chief executive replied that a cap would make credit much less available for consumers.[22][10] The top Democrat on the Senate Banking Committee wrote regulators on April 28, 2026 that the January 20 deadline had passed with no action. The CFPB signalled in July that it might revisit credit card late fees.[23][24] A news report from October 15, 2026 also notes fresh scrutiny by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency over the bank's customer risk policies.[27] None of these has become binding, but each can move the stock on a headline. On January 21 the shares rose 1.9 percent and the KBW bank index rose 2.2 percent. Investors judged the legislative route to be the least threatening of the options, and analysts quoted by CNBC put the odds of passage low.[22]

What this means for an owner: the business earns high returns when credit behaves. It carries a real cost and political risk when credit does not. The strengths are visible in the second-quarter numbers. The weaknesses are in the five-year record and the integration timetable. Chapter 2B turns those observations into a scorecard with thresholds. It then checks whether management did what it said it would do.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of results show a lender whose profit was cut by a credit cycle and then by a merger, and which is now recovering. Total net revenue rose from $30.4 billion in 2021 to $53.4 billion in 2025, and $62.0 billion over the last four quarters, mostly because Discover was added in May 2025.[5][4][1] Net income went the other way: $12.4 billion in 2021, $2.5 billion in 2025 and $10.5 billion over the last four quarters, because the provision for credit losses rose from a $1.9 billion release in 2021 to $20.7 billion in 2025, including the $8.77 billion day-one reserve on Discover loans.[5][4][1]

Many of the usual quality tests in the scoring rules were written for industrial and software companies. Return on invested capital (ROIC), a test of profit on all capital employed, free cash flow (FCF) margin, which is cash left after buying equipment as a share of revenue, net debt compared with earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA), and stock-based compensation (SBC), the cost of paying staff in shares, do not carry the same meaning for a bank. A bank's raw material is money, so interest expense is its cost of goods and deposits are not debt in the industrial sense. We therefore state plainly which gates do not apply, show the arithmetic that proves it, and test the bank equivalents. No gate was passed silently.[30]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is weaker than the rest. For the Reverse DCF it is that the last four quarters of earnings are a fair base, when they include integration and acquisition costs that will fade and a reserve release that may not repeat. For the base case it is that a multiple built from three year-end observations applies in twelve months. For the bull case it is that the 2027 savings and the Discover loan recovery arrive together. For the bear case it is that tangible book value stays at $105.21 while the multiple falls. Each is visible at the next report or the one after.

The red-team view that follows is the strongest case against our own numbers. It is not a forecast. It lists what would have to happen for the REJECTED verdict to be too cautious, and for a purchase to lose money.

Fatal flaw 1: the credit cycle turns

Card losses are the largest swing in earnings. Loans held for investment are $457.2 billion, so each additional percentage point of charge-offs costs $4.57 billion before tax, or about $3.6 billion after a 20.9 percent tax rate, which is about $5.82 per share on 621.1 million shares and 28 percent of the FY2026 estimate of $20.56 (our arithmetic).[1][13] The allowance of $22.97 billion covers about 1.6 years of losses at the second quarter's annualized rate, which would absorb a moderate rise in losses but would not stop earnings from falling.[1]

What shows it: unemployment moving up from 4.2 percent, which has stayed in a 4.1 to 4.3 percent range since March, or domestic card charge-offs rising above the 4.71 percent of the second quarter.[21][1] The September 2026 payroll report showed a gain of only 29,000 jobs against an average of 45,000 a month over the prior 12 months, so hiring is slowing even though the unemployment rate has barely moved.[21] Peers' latest monthly data show no deterioration: American Express's U.S. consumer write-off rate was 1.5 percent in September against 1.7 percent in August, and Synchrony's 30+ day delinquency rate was 4.2 percent in each month from May to August.[18][19]

Fatal flaw 2: a rate cap or new rules cut card income

The domestic card portfolio earns an average yield of 17.16 percent on $254.6 billion of average loans. If a 10 percent cap applied to existing balances, annual interest income would fall by about $18.2 billion, which is more than the roughly $13.0 billion of annualized first-half pre-tax income (our arithmetic: 7.16 percent times $254.625 billion, against 6,517 times two). The arithmetic ignores any response by the bank in credit lines, rewards or fees, and it illustrates the size of the risk, not a forecast.[1]

The chance that this passes looks low today. Analysts quoted by CNBC said in January that the odds of implementation are low and that Republican leaders oppose the idea, and in April the Senate's top Democrat wrote regulators that the president's own deadline had passed without action.[22][23] The bank's chief executive has said a cap would make credit much less available, and that is the argument that the industry is using.[10] The CFPB's review of late fees in July is a separate and smaller risk.[24] The risk is a headline that moves the stock by several percent in a day, and our stop does not protect against a gap.

Fatal flaw 3: the integration slips and the savings fall short

The earnings case depends on $2.7 billion of pre-tax savings by 2027. Management quoted $2.5 billion on the July call, and we could not find an official explanation for the $0.2 billion gap.[6][9] Only about one-third of the expense savings are in the run rate at month 14 of 24, the Discover card portfolio is shrinking 1.5 percent a year, and the credit card migration to the Discover network, promised for mid-2026, is still in testing.[9][10]

Brex adds a second integration. Management said in January that Brex would reduce earnings at first, and that investments would put upward pressure on the efficiency ratio.[10] The efficiency ratio of 57.05 percent is already above last year's 55.96 percent. What shows it: a rising adjusted efficiency ratio in the October report, a delay to the October or January conversion waves, or a cut to the savings target.[1][9]

Fatal flaw 4: the chart breaks before the thesis proves out

The stock is below falling 50-day and 200-day averages in a market that is in a correction. A close below the base low of $189.78 would end the basing pattern and open a path to the 52-week low of $174.24, and a stock that falls into Stage 4 trips a hard red line in our rules.[28][30] The stock's drawdown from its high reached 31.73 percent on March 27, 2026.[25] It fell 4.2 percent in a day in January after a fourth-quarter profit miss and the Brex announcement, and about 5 percent on the day of the first-quarter report in April, when adjusted EPS of $4.42 came in 3.3 percent below the consensus.[26][25] Those episodes show that the stock reacts sharply to results, and the next result comes on 2026-10-20, four days after the data cutoff.
