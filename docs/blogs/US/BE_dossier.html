1. What the company does

Bloom Energy makes the Bloom Energy Server, a box of solid-oxide fuel cells that turns natural gas, biogas or hydrogen into electricity through a chemical reaction instead of burning the fuel.[2] Think of a battery that is refilled with gas instead of being recharged from a wall socket. There are no turbines or moving parts, which is why the company says its output is cleaner and more reliable than a gas engine. The company sells the units in modular blocks that arrive on a factory-built skid, so a site can add power in steps. Its second product, the Bloom Electrolyzer, makes hydrogen and is a small part of the story today.[2]

Customers pay in several ways. The company sells to financiers, strategic partners, distributors and directly to end users, and it treats every buyer as a customer.[10] Many projects are financed: a fund buys the servers and sells the power to the data center. The most important fund relationship is with Brookfield Asset Management, which agreed in August 2025 to a financing framework of up to $5.0 billion over five years for future Bloom projects.[10] American Electric Power signed in November 2024 to buy up to 1 GW of fuel cells, with an initial order of 100 MW.[10] On the third-quarter call management said the same utility will use 700 MW of Bloom servers with carbon capture to supply Google.[5] Oracle is the best-known data center customer: it received a warrant for 3,531,073 shares in the partnership, and Bloom says it delivered power to an Oracle site in Utah in under two months.[2][5]

Revenue comes from four lines. Product revenue, the sale of the fuel cell systems, was $1,073.6 million of the $1,235.8 million booked in the third quarter of 2026, or 86.9%.[1] Installation, which is the site work, was $84.9 million. Service, the maintenance contracts that last 5 to 20 years, was $67.6 million, and electricity sold from older company-owned sites was $9.8 million.[1][10] The mix has shifted hard toward product. Installation is also being handed off: the company now steers large sites to certified third-party installers and keeps only a consulting and commissioning role.[2] That reduces its exposure to field labor and cost overruns, and it explains why installation revenue is small and loses money.

How the money is made differs by line. In the third quarter product earned a 37.9% gross margin, service 17.8%, electricity 25.7%, and installation lost money at a negative 4.8% margin.[1] Gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of making and delivering what was sold. Because product is 87% of sales, the company’s overall 33.8% gross margin is mostly a statement about how cheaply it now builds fuel cell stacks. Management says that a stack line that once needed 600 workers for 100 MW a year now needs the same number for a full gigawatt, and it expects the same 600 workers to run a 5 GW line.[5]

The filings name few end customers. The quarterly report says two customers made up 43% and 26% of third-quarter revenue, and the second is a related party, meaning the Brookfield-backed funds in which Bloom holds a minority stake.[2] Related-party revenue was $321.6 million in the quarter.[2] The report also warns that a counterparty in these tables may be a project-finance affiliate and not the data center user.[2] The 43% customer is not named. Equinix, listed in the watchlist notes as a customer, is not named in the annual report, the three 2026 quarterly reports or the third-quarter call, so we cannot show a link between Bloom and Equinix and treat it as unverified.[10][2][5]

For readers, the practical meaning is simple. When two counterparties provide most of a company’s sales, the loss or delay of one order moves the whole year. Oracle’s New Mexico campus is the clearest example: it holds a 2.4 GW fuel cell contract with Bloom, and the project’s power plan is a 2,462 MW microgrid that was still waiting for a state construction permit in late September 2026.[19]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong where it matters most to a data center buyer: speed. Bloom says it delivered power to an Oracle site in Utah in under two months, and in New Jersey it reports a first block of power ready two months after planning approvals that took three months.[5] Grid connections for large loads can take years. That time gap is the product, and the company’s chief executive repeated an industry claim that every quarter of delay on a 1 GW data center costs $10 billion of missed revenue to the customer.[5] We treat that figure as the customer’s argument, not a proven number, but it explains why buyers accept a higher price per unit of power for a faster start.

The second strength is the pace of margin gain. Gross margin was 29.2% in the third quarter of 2025 and 33.8% in the third quarter of 2026.[1] Operating margin, the share of revenue left after all running costs, rose from 1.5% to 18.2%.[1] Operating costs grew 33% while revenue grew 138%.[1] The company guides full-year 2026 non-GAAP gross margin to about 34%, up from its original 32%.[1][8] Non-GAAP means the company’s own adjusted figures, which leave out stock pay and a few other items.

The third strength is the balance sheet. Cash was $2,486.7 million at September 30, 2026, against debt of $2,476.4 million, so the company holds about $10.3 million more cash than debt.[1][2] Most of the debt is $2.5 billion of zero-coupon convertible notes due November 2030 that convert into shares at $194.97, a price only 0.9% above the current $193.30.[2] The company also has an undrawn revolving credit line with $510.0 million available.[2] That gives it room to finish the capacity expansion without an immediate share sale.

The weaknesses are about quality of earnings and customers, not about whether the product works. Cash did not follow profit in the third quarter: operating cash flow was negative $27.1 million on $250.3 million of net profit, because working capital grew. Working capital is the cash tied up between making a unit and being paid for it, and here contract assets, receivables and inventory absorbed it, so free cash flow, the cash left after running the business and buying equipment, was negative $180.6 million.[1] Customer concentration is high, with 43% and 26% from two counterparties, and the older 3.0% green notes and the new zero-coupon notes both add possible dilution.[2] Installation loses money. The company also carries performance guarantees under which it could owe up to $466.1 million more if its units underperform.[2] Finally, the stock’s own history says to be careful: it has traded between $75.70 and $351.28 over 52 weeks, and the session ledger shows it 44.97% below that high today.

What protects the business

Three things protect the franchise. The first is manufacturing know-how: the stack line gets about ten times the output from the same 600 workers, and product gross margin rose to 37.9%.[5][1] The second is a time-to-power lead over conventional generators; GE Vernova, which sells gas turbines for the same customers, earned a 21.3% gross margin in the June 2026 quarter against Bloom’s 33.4%.[23][24][6] The third is regulatory fit: Virginia’s 2026 energy plan, quoted by the chief executive on the call, called non-combustion fuel cells lower in local emissions than gas-fired generation.[5] These are real advantages, but none is a legal barrier. A competitor with similar cost and speed would erode them.

What threatens it

The quarterly report lists the threats in plain terms. Opposition to data center building is growing, and New York State’s July 2026 order paused state environmental permits for new hyperscale sites for up to a year; similar bans have followed in other places.[2] Air permits and gas pipelines are slowing even fuel cell projects.[2] Financing for data centers has become harder, and customers seen as weak credits may push for lower prices.[2] A short-seller report published on July 8, 2026 alleged problems with the supply chain; the company rejected it, and it says it has enough raw materials for current demand.[2] Oracle’s New Mexico campus shows how these risks combine: Oracle sent a force majeure notice to its developer, a contract step that protects rights if a project slips, while saying the project remains on schedule.[19]

What this means for an owner: the product is proven and the margins are improving, so the question is timing and cash, not technology. The next chapter scores the operating measures one by one, and the chapter after it shows how the numbers move over five years.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years ago Bloom was a business that lost money on almost everything. Revenue was $972.2 million in 2021, gross margin was 20.3%, and the company lost $164.5 million.[12] Over the next three years revenue grew 15% a year, but net losses were about $300 million in both 2022 and 2023, and the share count climbed from 176 million to 225 million as the company sold stock to stay solvent.[12][11] The break came in 2024, when operating income turned positive at $22.9 million on revenue of $1,473.9 million.[10]

The step since then is extreme. Revenue was $2,024.0 million in 2025 and $3,052.3 million in the first nine months of 2026, a 50.8% gain on the whole of 2025.[10][1] Operating income for the same nine months was $479.9 million, against $72.8 million for all of 2025.[7][6][1][10] The figures in the table are GAAP, meaning they follow the standard accounting rules and include stock pay and other items the company leaves out of its adjusted numbers.

Cash tells a different story from profit. Free cash flow was negative from 2021 to 2023, near break-even in 2024 and 2025, and $41.6 million for the first nine months of 2026, even though net income for those nine months was $521.5 million.[10][2] The gap is working capital, the cash tied up between making a unit and being paid for it. The balance sheet shows where: contract assets, which are revenue booked before billing, rose from $241.2 million to $858.6 million in nine months, and receivables and inventory rose by another $547.3 million, while customer deposits and deferred revenue rose by $610.6 million to partly fund it.[1]

The financing history matters for what the shares are worth. Debt rose from $526.7 million in 2021 to $2,617.9 million in 2025 as the company issued convertible notes, loans that can be swapped for shares. In November 2025 it issued $2.5 billion of zero-coupon notes that convert at $194.97 a share, roughly today’s price.[12][10][2] Because it holds about as much cash as it owes, net debt is close to zero, but any rise in the share price above $194.97 would let those holders convert, adding up to 12.8 million shares.[2]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each of the estimates above rests on an assumption that could be wrong. The reverse DCF assumes the 12-month profit is a fair base. The target assumes the price returns to its pre-print level of $217.77, which is 41.6 times today’s estimate. The weighted value assumes our probabilities. The business quality score assumes cash will convert. We take them in turn, then give the bear case, the three ways this idea could fail outright, the one claim that matters most and the date we will know.

The red-team bear case is this. Bloom’s profit comes from selling very large systems to a small number of buyers who depend on outside financing and on regulators’ permits. If the financing market for AI building tightens, as the quarterly report itself warns, buyers can delay or reduce orders, and the stock’s 37 times multiple on 2027 profit would shrink toward 27 times. With profit 10% below consensus, the stock would trade near $127, a 34% fall.[2] The company’s defense is that deliveries are financed by third-party funds that are on the hook to take delivery, and that the 2026 guidance does not depend on any single project.[18]

Fatal flaw 1: cash never catches up with profit

In this scenario contract assets and receivables keep rising faster than customers pay. Contract assets rose from $241.2 million to $858.6 million in nine months, and the company’s explanation is that billing follows contract milestones and cash collection follows revenue.[1][5] The reassurance is that customers also paid $610.6 million more in deposits, which means they are funding part of the build. The fatal version is a quarter in which customers pause deposits at the same time as contract assets stay unbilled. The company would then use the $2.49 billion of cash, or raise money, and the zero-coupon notes convert at $194.97, so a higher price would lead to up to 12.8 million new shares.[2] Evidence that this is happening: fourth-quarter operating cash flow below that quarter’s capital spending (third-quarter spending was $153.5 million), which would leave free cash flow negative again, or any new equity or debt raise.

Fatal flaw 2: one large project slips or shrinks

Oracle’s New Mexico campus is tied to a 2.4 GW Bloom contract and a 2,462 MW microgrid that was awaiting a state construction permit.[19] Oracle sent a force majeure notice on September 24, the gas pipeline slipped to February 1, 2027, and on October 15 Oracle and the microgrid developer proposed a cap on the fuel cells’ operating output that would cut permitted emissions by about 35%.[18] Enverus, a data provider, estimates the contract is nearly half of its own estimate of Bloom’s product backlog; that is not a company number.[18] Bloom says Oracle remains committed and that 2026 guidance does not depend on any single project.[19][18] If the cap reduces installed capacity, or the permit is denied, the revenue timing and the backlog would shrink. We will know when the air permit decision and pipeline date arrive, expected by early 2027.

Fatal flaw 3: the AI building cycle turns

Bloom’s value depends on hyperscalers and their lenders continuing to build. The October 22 selloff began when Meta raised its spending forecast and investors questioned the return on that spending.[17] The quarterly report cites projections of AI-related debt issuance above $500 billion in 2026, financing that is harder to get for weak credits, state moratoriums on new hyperscale data centers and a short-seller report from July 8.[2] A turn in this cycle would hit orders, prices and the multiple together. The warning sign would be a customer asking to delay delivery or to renegotiate price, or a second state following New York’s moratorium into a major data center market.
