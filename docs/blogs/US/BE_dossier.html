1. What the company does

Bloom Energy makes the Bloom Energy Server, a stack of ceramic fuel cells that turns natural gas, biogas or hydrogen into electricity through a chemical reaction instead of burning the fuel.[18] Think of a battery that is refilled with gas rather than recharged from a wall socket. There is no flame, no turbine and no large moving part, which is why the company says its power is cleaner locally and more reliable than a gas engine. The units are built in a factory on a steel skid and arrive ready to connect, so a site can add power in steps. A second product, the Bloom Electrolyzer, makes hydrogen and is a small part of the business today.[18] The company employs more than 2,000 people and builds its systems in the United States.[2]

The buyer is rarely the end user. Customers can buy a system outright, or they can pay over time through a power purchase agreement (PPA), a contract to buy electricity at a set price, in which a financier owns the equipment and sells the power to the site. In the third quarter of 2026, 99% of revenue came from direct purchases, a group that includes third-party power purchase agreements, and 1% from the older managed-services model in which Bloom keeps the asset.[1] The most important financier is Brookfield Asset Management, whose framework to fund Bloom projects began at $5 billion in 2025 and, according to the chief executive, was expanded fivefold to $25 billion in June 2026.[10] The filings use the word customer for the contract counterparty, which may be a project-finance affiliate and not the data center that will use the power.[1]

Revenue comes from four lines. Product revenue, the sale of the fuel cell systems, was $1,073.6 million of the $1,235.8 million booked in the third quarter of 2026, or 86.9%. Installation, the site work, was $84.9 million. Service, the maintenance contracts that run 5 to 20 years, was $67.6 million. Electricity sold from older company-owned sites was $9.8 million.[2][18] The mix has moved hard toward product: a year earlier product was 74% of a $519.0 million quarter.[2] Bloom also now steers large sites to certified outside installers and keeps only a consulting and commissioning role, which removes field labor and cost overruns from its own books and explains why installation sales are small and unprofitable.[1]

Each line earns a different gross margin, the share of revenue left after the direct cost of making and delivering what was sold. In the third quarter product earned 37.9%, service 17.8%, electricity 25.7%, and installation lost money at a negative 4.8%.[2] Because product is 87% of sales, the company-wide 33.8% is mostly a statement about how cheaply Bloom now builds fuel cell stacks. The chief executive said a stack line that once needed 600 workers for 100 megawatts a year now needs the same 600 for a full gigawatt, and that the same crew would run a 5-gigawatt line.[5] That is the company’s explanation for rising margins, and it is a claim we can test each quarter in the product margin.

One feature of the revenue is easy to miss. In October 2025 Bloom agreed to give Oracle a warrant to buy up to 3,531,073 shares at $113.28; Oracle exercised it on May 1, 2026 and received 2,154,231 shares, including 248,798 extra shares as an inducement.[1][23] Accounting rules treat that share award as a discount to a customer’s customer, so Bloom books a $306.5 million asset, $91.0 million current and $215.5 million long-term, and writes it off against Oracle-related revenue as systems are delivered. Only $17.3 million had been written off by September 30.[1] Reported revenue from that program will therefore run below the contract price for years.

Who the customers are, and who they are not

The quarterly report says two customers made up 43% and 26% of third-quarter revenue, and the second is a related party, meaning a fund joint venture with Brookfield in which Bloom holds a minority stake.[1] Related-party revenue was $321.6 million in the quarter, or 26.0%, and $697.7 million in nine months, or 22.9%.[1] Earlier quarters were the same or more concentrated: 50% and 12% in the first quarter and 44% and 21% in the second.[12][8] The 43% counterparty is not named. Three counterparties also held 31%, 24% and 15% of unpaid customer bills at September 30, and 97% of third-quarter revenue came from the United States.[1]

The end customers are better known than the counterparties. Oracle signed for up to 2.8 gigawatts of capacity, and its Project Jupiter campus in New Mexico is planned around up to 2.45 gigawatts of Bloom fuel cells.[33][34] American Electric Power agreed in 2024 to buy up to 1 gigawatt, and on October 14, 2026 it announced a $4 billion, 702-megawatt project near Cheyenne, Wyoming to supply Google under a 20-year contract.[18][32] Nebius is bringing a 300-megawatt New Jersey site online block by block, and Equinix has agreed to deployments at 19 data centers above 100 megawatts in total.[5][33] The Equinix link comes from trade press, not a Bloom filing, so we treat it as reported and not verified in the company’s own documents.

When two counterparties supply most of a company’s sales, the loss or delay of one order moves the whole year. That is the practical meaning of the 43% and 26%. It is also why the next chapter spends time on cash, financing partners and a single New Mexico campus.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Solid-oxide fuel cell: a fuel cell that runs hot and uses a ceramic material, not a liquid, to move charge.
  • Financier: a fund or bank that buys the servers and then sells the electricity to the site owner.
  • Related party: a company Bloom has a financial interest in, so its sales are not fully at arm’s length.
  • Backlog: contracted sales that have not yet turned into revenue; Bloom’s marketed figure includes service contracts and expected tax credits and is wider than the audited figure.
  • Contract asset: revenue the company has booked but not yet billed to the customer.
  • Convertible note: a loan that the lender may swap for shares at a set price.
  • Warrant: a right to buy shares at a fixed price within a set time.
  • Time to power: the number of months between signing a contract and delivering working electricity.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong where a data center buyer feels the most pain, which is speed. Bloom says it delivered power to an Oracle site in Utah in under two months, and in New Jersey it reports a first block of power ready two months after planning approvals that took three months.[5] Grid connections for very large loads can take years, so the gap between months and years is the product. The chief executive repeated an industry claim that each quarter of delay on a 1-gigawatt data center costs $10 billion of missed revenue.[5] We treat that figure as the customer’s argument and not a proven number, but it explains why buyers accept a higher price per unit of power for a faster start. Turbine makers show the other side of the same coin: GE Vernova’s backlog reached $197 billion in the third quarter, and Bloom’s chief executive calls a four-year backlog a sign of constrained supply.[37][10]

The second strength is the pace of margin gain. Gross margin was 29.2% in the third quarter of 2025 and 33.8% in the third quarter of 2026, and operating margin, the share of revenue left after all running costs, went from 1.5% to 18.2%.[2] Operating costs grew 33.3% while revenue grew 138.1%, so most of each extra sales dollar fell through to profit.[1] One caution applies to the nine-month figures: they include a $37.2 million refund of import tariffs booked in the second quarter, which will not repeat. The tariff refund was booked in the second quarter, so the third quarter’s 33.8% is the cleaner reading.[1]

The third strength is the balance sheet. Cash was $2,486.7 million at September 30, 2026 against debt of $2,476.4 million, so Bloom holds $10.3 million more cash than debt, before $185.0 million of financing obligations.[2][1] Almost all of the debt is $2.5 billion of zero-coupon convertible notes, loans that holders may swap for shares, due November 2030 with a conversion price of $194.97, and the $218.50 close is 12.1% above that price.[1][21] At that price the notes would turn into about 12.8 million shares, or up to 19.6 million if a make-whole clause applies, but Bloom may settle in cash, shares or a mix, and it can call the notes only after November 2028 and only if the stock trades above 130% of the conversion price.[1][21] A $600 million bank line, with $510.0 million still available, adds liquidity, and it carries covenants that cap secured leverage at 3.25 times and require interest cover of at least 3.0 times.[22][1]

The weakness is cash, not profit. Operating cash flow was negative $27.1 million in the third quarter on $249.0 million of net income, and free cash flow (FCF), the cash left after running the business and buying equipment, was negative $180.6 million.[2] The cause is working capital, the cash tied up between making a unit and being paid for it. Customer bills not yet sent (contract assets) rose from $241.2 million to $858.6 million in nine months, unpaid customer bills from $371.8 million to $634.7 million, and inventory from $643.3 million to $927.7 million.[1] Long-term advances to suppliers jumped from $17.4 million to $327.0 million, and Bloom has signed purchase commitments of $5.8 billion that run past 12 months, $2.6 billion of it due in 2027.[1] Customers pay part of the bill up front: deposits rose from $78.2 million to $661.4 million.[1]

Those deposits matter more than they first appear. In nine months the company took in $610.6 million of customer deposits and $533.2 million of extra supplier and accrued credit, and it still reported only $272.9 million of operating cash flow.[1] Without the deposit inflow, nine-month operating cash flow would have been negative $337.7 million. Growth is therefore being financed by customers and suppliers, a float that grows while orders grow and drains if they slow. There is a mitigating pattern: fourth-quarter operating cash flow was $484.2 million in 2024 and $418.1 million in 2025, far above the other quarters, because billing milestones cluster at year end.[15] That pattern is the best argument that the third quarter was timing, and the fourth-quarter report is the test.

Two further weaknesses concern product quality and counterparties. Accrued warranty and product performance liabilities rose from $20.0 million to $70.3 million in nine months, and they include a specific warranty reserve that stood at $58.3 million in June and $42.5 million in September.[1][8] Bloom also guarantees how much electricity its older fleets will deliver, up to a cap of $847.2 million, with about $466.1 million of that cap still open, and it paid $21.3 million under those guarantees in nine months against $17.0 million a year earlier.[1] Service margin was 17.8%, less than half the product margin. On counterparties, a short-seller report published on July 8, 2026 alleged that Bloom depends on Chinese supplies of a key ceramic material and that revenue is largely financing arranged with Brookfield. Bloom rejects the claims and says it has visibility into supply for 25 gigawatts a year; a securities class action filed in July repeats the allegations, and a lead plaintiff was appointed on October 15.[36][24][1] We have not verified the allegations and treat them as a risk, not a finding.

What protects the business

Three things protect the franchise. The first is manufacturing know-how: the stack line gets about ten times the output from the same 600 workers, and product gross margin rose from 35.0% to 37.9% in a year.[5][2] The second is a permit advantage. Bloom says customers win air permits faster with its technology than with combustion, and the chief executive quoted Virginia’s 2026 energy plan, which called non-combustion fuel cells lower in local emissions than gas-fired generation.[10][5] The advantage is narrowing: New York’s July 2026 order paused state environmental permits for new hyperscale data centers for up to a year, and the quarterly report concedes that even fuel cell projects now face more scrutiny.[1] The third is a financing network that includes Brookfield and a group of lenders that has committed $2.6 billion.[10] None of the three is a legal barrier. A competitor with similar cost and speed would erode each of them.

What threatens it

The quarterly report lists the threats plainly. Opposition to data center building is growing, financing for data centers has become harder, air permits and gas pipelines are slowing even fuel cell projects, and new tariffs of 10% to 12.5% on imports from about 60 economies apply to parts and materials.[1] Oracle’s New Mexico campus shows how the risks combine. Its microgrid still needs a state air permit, for which filings cite a November 23 decision date, and a gas pipeline that the federal regulator has set a December 3 deadline to decide on.[34] Oracle and the developer proposed on October 15 to cut permitted emissions by about 35%.[35] A denial or a cut in the size of the plant would move revenue timing and the backlog.

What this means for an owner: the product is proven and the margins are improving, so the open questions are cash, concentration and a few dated events. The next chapter scores the operating measures one by one, and the chapter after that shows how the numbers moved over five years.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has a strong record on the numbers it controls and an open question on the cash it has talked about. In February 2025 it gave guidance — management's official forecast —, the company’s own published forecast for the coming period, of $1.65 to $1.85 billion of 2025 revenue, a non-GAAP gross margin of about 29% and non-GAAP operating income of $135 to $165 million. It delivered $2.02 billion, 30.3% and $221.0 million, above the top of every range.[17][15] Non-GAAP figures leave out stock pay and a few other items.

For 2026 the first forecast, in February, was $3.1 to $3.3 billion of revenue and $425 to $475 million of non-GAAP operating income, with non-GAAP earnings per share (EPS) of $1.33 to $1.48. The company raised it in April, July and October, and the current range is $4.4 to $4.6 billion and $960 to $1,030 million.[15][13][9][2] Nine-month revenue of $3,052.3 million is 66% to 69% of the new range, so the fourth quarter has to bring in $1.35 to $1.55 billion; the chief financial officer said the timing of a small number of deliveries around year end decides where in the range the year lands.[1][5] After three raises a flat fourth quarter would disappoint.

The cash promises are less settled. The February slide showed an operating cash flow outlook of $200 million for 2026, the April slide showed more than $200 million, the July slides dropped it, and an analyst noted that the cash guide had been pulled.[16][14][11][10] The chief financial officer then said investors should think of $375 million or more as a new baseline, while stressing it was not formal guidance.[10] Nine-month operating cash flow is $272.9 million, so the fourth quarter would need at least $102.1 million to reach it.[1] The third-quarter call did not repeat the baseline, and management said only that it views Bloom as a self-funding business that will be free cash flow positive.[5] We read the cash promise as open, not broken.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years ago Bloom was a business that lost money on almost everything. Revenue was $972.2 million in 2021, gross margin was 20.3%, and the operating loss was $114.5 million.[20] Over the next two years revenue grew 11% to 23% a year, but operating losses were $261.0 million in 2022 and $208.9 million in 2023, and the share count climbed from 176.5 million to 224.7 million as the company sold stock to stay funded.[20][19][18] The break came in 2024, when operating income turned positive at $22.9 million on revenue of $1,473.9 million.[18] The figures in the first table are GAAP, meaning they follow the standard accounting rules and include stock pay and other items that the company leaves out of its adjusted numbers.

The step since then is extreme. Revenue was $2,024.0 million in 2025 and $3,052.3 million in the first nine months of 2026, which is already 50.8% above the whole of 2025.[18][1] Operating income for the same nine months was $479.9 million against $72.8 million for all of 2025, and net income to common holders over the latest 12 months was $518.3 million after five years of losses.[1][18] The 12-month column in each table is built as the 2025 annual figure, plus nine months of 2026, minus nine months of 2025, so it covers October 2025 to September 2026.

Cash tells a different story from profit, and the quarterly table shows why. Free cash flow was negative from 2021 to 2023, near break-even in 2024 and 2025, and $436.7 million over the latest 12 months, but $395.1 million of that came in the fourth quarter of 2025 alone, and nine-month 2026 free cash flow is $41.6 million against $521.5 million of net income.[19][18][1] When the fourth quarter of 2025 drops out of the 12-month window in February, the 12-month figure will fall unless the fourth quarter of 2026 is just as strong.

The financing history matters for what a share is worth. Debt rose from $526.7 million in 2021 to $2,617.9 million in 2025 as the company issued convertible notes, and in November 2025 it issued $2.5 billion of zero-coupon notes that convert at $194.97 a share.[20][18][21] Because Bloom holds about as much cash as it owes, net debt, which is debt minus cash, is close to zero. The cost is dilution, the rise in share count that shrinks each share’s slice: shares outstanding were 295.5 million at September 30, 2026, up 25.0% from 236.4 million a year earlier, after note conversions, an Oracle warrant and routine stock awards.[1]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate above rests on an assumption that could be wrong. The reverse DCF assumes that 12-month free cash flow of $436.7 million is a fair starting point. The central target assumes the market keeps paying 41 times 2027 profit per share. The weighted value assumes our probabilities. The Layer 2 score assumes that cash will convert. We take them in turn, after the bear case.

The red-team bear case is this. Bloom’s profit comes from selling very large systems to a small number of buyers who depend on outside financing and on permits. Two counterparties supplied 69% of third-quarter revenue, one of them a Brookfield fund joint venture, and the company’s own report warns that financing for data centers has become harder.[1] If lenders pull back, buyers can delay or shrink orders, the customer deposits that fund the float stop growing, and $5.8 billion of purchase commitments stay on Bloom’s books.[1] Profit 10% below consensus at 27 times 2027 earnings is $129, a 41% fall. Bloom’s defense is that customers pay deposits ($661.4 million so far), that Brookfield’s framework is $25 billion and that fourth quarters have always produced cash.[1][10][15]

Fatal flaw 1: cash never catches up with profit

In this scenario contract assets and unpaid bills keep rising faster than customers pay. Contract assets rose from $241.2 million to $858.6 million in nine months, and the company’s explanation is that billing follows contract milestones.[1][5] The reassurance is that customers also paid $583.1 million more in deposits. The fatal version is a quarter in which deposits stop growing while contract assets stay unbilled. Bloom would then use some of its $2.49 billion of cash, and its $327.0 million of supplier advances and $5.8 billion of commitments would be harder to carry. The evidence would be fourth-quarter operating cash flow below that quarter’s capital spending, which was $153.5 million in the third quarter, or any new debt or equity raise.[1][2]

Fatal flaw 2: one large project slips or shrinks

Oracle’s New Mexico campus is planned around up to 2.45 gigawatts of Bloom fuel cells. Its air permit has a decision date of November 23 and the gas pipeline a federal deadline of December 3, and Oracle and the developer proposed on October 15 to cut permitted emissions by about 35%.[34][35] The short-seller report argues that the campus could slip for years and that AEP’s project has moved to a later date; those are allegations, not findings.[36] AEP’s own release says the Wyoming plant should be fully operational by the end of 2028.[32] If a permit is denied or the plant is cut in size, revenue timing and the backlog would shrink, and the $306.5 million Oracle customer-consideration asset would be written off against less revenue than planned.[1] The first dated test is November 23.

Fatal flaw 3: the AI building cycle turns

Bloom’s value depends on hyperscalers and their lenders continuing to build. The quarterly report cites projected AI-related debt issuance above $500 billion in 2026, financing that is harder to get for weaker credits, state moratoriums on new hyperscale data centers and the July short-seller report.[1] News reports tied the October 23 fall to cash-flow concerns, and a separate note linked it to worries about AI spending.[29][31] A turn in this cycle would hit orders, prices and the multiple together. The warning sign would be a customer asking to delay delivery or reopen price, or a second state following New York’s moratorium into a major data center market.

Fatal flaw 4: costs that sit outside the headline margin

Warranty and performance liabilities tripled to $70.3 million in nine months, the guarantee cap is $847.2 million, and a class action alleging misleading statements about the supply chain has a lead plaintiff and lead counsel.[1] The converts are in the money, so a stock price above $194.97 means up to 12.8 million new shares unless Bloom settles in cash. None of these items has to hurt, but each is a cost that a 41 times multiple on adjusted profit does not show. Evidence would be a larger warranty reserve, a ruling in the class action or a cash settlement of notes.
