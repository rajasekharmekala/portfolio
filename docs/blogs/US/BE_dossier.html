1. What the company does

Bloom Energy makes the Energy Server, a stack of solid oxide fuel cells. A fuel cell makes electricity by a chemical reaction instead of by burning fuel. Natural gas goes in, electricity comes out, and nothing is burned, so the system gives off almost no smog-forming gases and uses almost no water.[5] The machine makes direct current, the form of power that computer chips use, and the company says every shipment is now ready for the 800-volt direct current designs that new AI data centers plan to use.[8][5] Units are modular, and the 10-K says they can be arranged from kilowatts up to hundreds of megawatts at one site.[5]

Who pays is the more important question. For two decades the buyers were hospitals, factories, telephone carriers, campuses and shops that wanted reliable power on their own premises.[14] Since October 2025 the buyers that matter most are AI data centers. They cannot wait years for a grid connection, so they pay for power that sits next to the building. The chief executive told investors that all of the major US hyperscalers (the largest cloud companies) and more than a dozen smaller AI cloud and colocation operators have approved Bloom for their data centers, and that Bloom's share of fuel-cell power in data centers is in the 'very high 90s' percent.[14] The market share figure is management's own claim, and no filing or outside source confirms it.

The money comes from four revenue lines. Product revenue is the sale of the Energy Servers. Installation is the work of putting them in. Service is a maintenance contract that runs for five to twenty years, and electricity is the older model in which Bloom owned the equipment and sold the power.[5][8] In the June 2026 quarter product was 87.8% of revenue, against 75.7% for all of 2025, so the company has become mostly an equipment seller. That raises sales quickly, but it also means each quarter depends on how many large projects reach their delivery date.

Who is the customer: the end user or the financier

A Bloom deal starts with a contract with the end user, such as a data center operator. The user either buys the equipment outright or pays for power over time without owning the equipment. Most users choose to pay over time, so Bloom brings in a financier that buys the equipment, owns it and delivers the power under the contract Bloom helped win.[14] In the filings the financier is the 'customer', because it is the party that signs with Bloom and takes delivery. The 10-Q says plainly that a customer may be 'a project-finance affiliate rather than the ultimate end user'.[3]

Two financiers carry most of the weight. Brookfield, a large infrastructure investor, raised its financing framework for Bloom projects from $5 billion to $25 billion on June 30, 2026.[15] A second group led by Industrial Development Funding, with Oaktree, MUFG Bank and Morgan Stanley, has committed $2.6 billion in total, and the CFO named the AI cloud company Nebius as the end user behind one of the IDF deals.[14] This structure lets a customer without a large equipment budget order power, and it explains why one financier can account for most of a quarter's revenue.

What changed in 2025 and 2026, and why it matters for an owner

Revenue was $1.47 billion in 2024 and $2.02 billion in 2025.[5] Management now guides to $3.9 to $4.2 billion for 2026, which is about 100% growth at the midpoint, and it said the June quarter was the first with more than $1 billion of sales.[1] Product backlog, the revenue tied to signed commitments to buy Energy Servers, was about $6 billion at the end of 2025, up about 2.5 times in a year, and the company counted about $20 billion of total backlog including service.[8] At the July 28 call the chief executive said backlog was growing faster than revenue, but the company gave no new dollar figure.[14]

For an owner this means the company is trying to scale a factory as fast as orders arrive, and the main risks are timing and concentration, not demand alone. Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Energy Server: Bloom's fuel cell power system, sold as modular units that sit beside the building they power.
  • Solid oxide fuel cell: a fuel cell that works at very high temperature and makes electricity from natural gas, biogas or hydrogen without burning it.
  • Onsite power: electricity made on the customer's property, so it does not wait for a utility connection.
  • Time to power: the days or months between signing an order and having electricity available, which is the main reason AI data centers buy from Bloom.
  • Backlog: the company's measure of signed commitments that have not yet turned into revenue. It differs from the accounting measure called unsatisfied performance obligations, which is far smaller.
  • Non-GAAP: company-adjusted profit that leaves out items such as stock-based pay. We label every use.
  • Convertible note: a loan that the lender can swap for shares at a set price. Bloom's $2.5 billion of 0% notes can be swapped at $194.97 a share.[3]
  • Financier: the investor that buys Bloom equipment and rents the power to the end user.
  • Weinstein stage: a four-phase label for a stock chart. Stage 2 is a confirmed rising trend, with the price above a rising 50-day and 200-day average. Stage 3 is a top or a deep pullback, and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend.
  • Base, pivot, breakout and retest: a base is a flat stretch of trading before a move. The pivot is the top of that stretch, a breakout is a close above it on heavy volume, and a retest is a pullback that holds the old pivot as support.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a momentum gauge from 0 to 100. Below 30 it is called oversold, and above 70 it is called overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing, measured over 14 trading days. We use it to measure how far the price sits from support.
  • Stop loss, entry zone and limit order: the stop loss is the price at which we would sell because the thesis is wrong, the entry zone is the range where we would buy, and a limit order is an order to buy at or below a chosen price.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain from the price to the target divided by the loss from the price to the stop.
  • Gross margin and operating margin: gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of making the product, and operating margin is the share left after all running costs.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a rough measure of operating profit before interest, taxes and the write-off of equipment cost. EPS (earnings per share) is the profit for each share. ROIC (return on invested capital) is the profit earned on the money invested in the business.
  • FCF is free cash flow, which is operating cash flow minus spending on equipment. SBC (stock-based compensation) is the value of shares and share awards paid to staff, and dilution is the growth in share count that makes each share a smaller slice.
  • Net debt is borrowings minus cash. Working capital is cash tied up in customer bills, inventory and supplier bills.
  • Guidance is management's own forecast. A consensus estimate is the average forecast of Wall Street analysts. The price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E, is the share price divided by earnings per share.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Bloom's strength is speed. The company delivered power to its first direct hyperscaler customer, Oracle, in 55 days, and it says it can deliver in months what utilities quote in years.[14] The 10-K describes skid-mounted modular units that can be installed in weeks and months, and says onsite generation can supply power in about 90 days in some project scopes.[5] That matters because a data center that waits for power earns nothing. The chief executive put it as time to power being time to revenue, and used a $12 to $24 billion a year revenue range per gigawatt of AI data center to argue that a month's delay costs the customer $1 to $2 billion.[14] That figure is the company's own illustration, not an audited number.

The second strength is the market it sells into. Large turbine makers have long order books. GE Vernova counts 116 gigawatts of gas turbine backlog and slot reservations and expects to reach at least 125 gigawatts by the end of 2026.[26] Bloom's 10-K says the major turbine makers are 'currently supply constrained' and that this gives alternative onsite technologies a window.[5] The chief executive says legacy suppliers have backlogs stretching to 2029 and beyond, so a buyer who cannot get a turbine for years may accept a fuel cell now.[14] Bloom also benefits from community acceptance: the system has no combustion, almost no water use and low noise, which management says helps customers win air permits faster.[14] If turbine supply loosens, that window narrows.

The third strength is financing. A $25 billion Brookfield framework and $2.6 billion from IDF and its partners mean a customer can order power without owning the equipment.[15][14] Management says 80% of 2025 orders were repeat orders from existing customers.[14] Repeat orders are a good sign of customer satisfaction, but the number is a management statement and the 10-K does not audit it.

Where the competitive position is weaker than it looks

The first weakness is concentration. The 10-Q/A states that one customer, which is not a related party, supplied about 73% of revenue in the three months to June 30, 2026, and that two customers supplied 44% and 21% of revenue for the six months, the second being a related party.[4] Three customers held 36%, 34% and 17% of accounts receivable at June 30.[3] The CFO calls this 'delivery timing, not the composition of our backlog' and says customers rotate as projects reach their delivery windows.[14] That may be true, but a reader cannot check it, because the filings do not name the customers or the projects.

The second weakness is that the biggest projects depend on permits, pipelines and other people's schedules. On September 24, 2026 Oracle sent a force majeure notice (a contract clause that excuses delay from events outside a party's control) to the developer of Project Jupiter, the New Mexico campus that Bloom is to power with more than 2.4 gigawatts of fuel cells.[17] Oracle said the project remains on schedule and the company reaffirmed its commitment the next day, but the gas pipeline serving the site slipped to February 1, 2027 and an air-quality permit is still pending.[18][19] Bloom's CFO says the units can be redeployed to other projects, which limits the damage from a delay at one site but does not remove it.[14]

The third weakness is trust. A short seller, Hunterbrook Media, which said it may profit from a fall in the stock, published a report on July 8, 2026 that questioned Bloom's results, its accounting and its supply of scandium oxide, a material used in its cells. Bloom 'categorically rejects' the claims on its results and accounting and says its scandium supply is not dependent on China.[13] A second report followed on July 30, and a securities class action over the scandium disclosures has been reported, with a lead-plaintiff deadline of September 28.[20][19] These are allegations, not findings: the company has not restated anything, its auditor gave a clean opinion on the 2025 statements and on internal controls in the 10-K,[5] and no filing shows a going-concern warning. We therefore pass the governance red line but treat it as a live risk.

The fourth weakness is cost control on a rapidly scaling product. Service gross margin was 18.7% in the June quarter on a GAAP basis, and the 10-Q says higher product performance guarantee costs 'reflecting the effects of fleet degradation'.[1][3] Warranty accruals rose from $20.0 million at December 31 to $77.8 million at June 30.[1] Tariffs are a further swing factor. The June quarter included a $37.4 million recovery of previously paid import tariffs, the 10-Q notes the Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling that certain tariffs were unlawful, and a new round of 10% to 12.5% tariffs began in late July.[3] What this means for an owner: the position is strong on speed and financing and weak on concentration and trust. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has a clear pattern of guiding low and delivering higher. For 2025 it guided revenue of $1.65 to $1.85 billion in February 2025 and reaffirmed that range in April and July, and the year came in at $2.024 billion, about 9% above the top of the range.[10][11][8] Non-GAAP operating income was $221.0 million against a guide of $135 to $165 million.[8] For 2026 the company guided revenue of $3.1 to $3.3 billion and EPS (earnings per share, profit divided by the number of shares) of $1.33 to $1.48 in February, then raised both twice, to $3.9 to $4.2 billion and $2.55 to $2.85 by July.[8][9][1] The June-quarter non-GAAP EPS of $0.78 compared with a consensus of $0.41, and the $37.4 million tariff refund, worth about $0.12 a share, helped.[22][3]

Capital allocation is where the record is more mixed. In November 2025 Bloom issued $2.5 billion of 0% convertible notes due 2030, and it ended June with $2,666.9 million of cash against $2,475.4 million of recourse debt, with no revolving credit drawn.[3] The price of that comfort is dilution: shares outstanding rose from 280.0 million to 293.4 million in the first half, and the notes would add 12.8 million more shares if swapped at $194.97, a price below today's $221.31.[1][3] Management did not buy back shares, and the first-half cash flow statement shows $0.9 million of dividends paid. Insiders adopted selling plans for up to 200,000 shares by the chief executive and 60,000 by a director, and a secondary source reports about $21.8 million of insider sales in the last quarter, some of it to cover tax on vested awards.[3][21]

Management also made statements that the filings do not fully support. The chief executive said Bloom is 'not dependent on one customer or one project', while the 10-Q/A shows one customer at about 73% of June-quarter revenue.[14][4] The CFO explained that revenue is lumpy and customers rotate, which is plausible, but no document shows the rotation.
