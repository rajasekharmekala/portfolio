1. What the company does

Talos Energy is an independent oil and gas producer. It owns shares of oil fields, drills wells, and sells what comes out at market prices. It does not refine or retail anything. Its centre of gravity is the deepwater Gulf of America, which the filings use as the name for the Gulf of Mexico. In the second quarter of 2026 revenue was $664.8 million. Oil brought in $620.8 million of that, or 93%. Natural gas brought in $31.0 million and natural gas liquids (NGLs, the heavier hydrocarbons separated from gas) $13.0 million.[2][3] Output was 93.7 thousand barrels of oil equivalent a day (MBoe/d). A barrel of oil equivalent counts six thousand cubic feet of gas as one barrel.[2]

The price Talos gets follows world oil. In the second quarter it realized $99.47 a barrel of oil, while WTI (West Texas Intermediate, the U.S. benchmark) averaged $92.79. Gas sold for $3.17 per thousand cubic feet and NGLs for $19.85 a barrel, which is $77.95 per Boe in all.[3] Lease operating expense (LOE, the cost of running wells and platforms) was $18.25 per Boe, including about $1.75 of one-time well work.[3] Three buyers took 70% of 2025 revenue: Shell Trading (US) Company 35%, Exxon Mobil 23% and Chevron 12%.[1]

Hedges are contracts that fix or bound a price. As of July 31, 2026 the company had about 22,978 barrels a day under collars for October to December 2026. A collar sets a floor of $61.52 and a ceiling of $73.81. Another 4,000 barrels a day were under swaps at $62.50.[3] At $100 oil those contracts give money away. In the second quarter they cost $74.1 million in cash and cut the realized price from $77.95 to $69.25 per Boe.[2]

The portfolio has four layers. The first is operated deepwater hubs. Katmai is operated, 50% owned, and tied to the Tarantula platform, which can now process about 38 thousand Boe a day. The Pompano area, with the Cardona well, is 65% owned.[1] The second layer is non-operated stakes, which made up 19% of 2025 output.[1] Monument is one, with Talos holding 29.7%. Its operator, Beacon Offshore Energy, found about 250 feet of net pay in the first well. First oil is due by late 2026 through a tie-back to the Shenandoah facility, at 20 to 30 thousand Boe a day gross.[3][2]

The third layer is exploration and appraisal. Daenerys is 27% owned, and its appraisal well was spud on July 1, 2026. Talos also farmed into Block 29 offshore Mexico and agreed to take 80% of an offshore Honduras block, though part of the Honduras deal still needs approval.[1][2] The fourth layer is Zama, a Mexican discovery. Talos owns 20.0% of the entity that holds a 17.35% interest in the unitized field. Harbour Energy became operator in December 2025, and no final investment decision (FID) has been taken.[1][2]

Growth came from buying companies. QuarterNorth Energy was bought on March 4, 2024, not in 2025. Talos paid $1,247.4 million in cash plus 24.3 million shares worth $322.6 million.[1] The cash came partly from a January 2024 sale of 34.5 million shares, partly from bank borrowings and partly from senior notes. The cash-flow statement shows $1,250,000 thousand of notes issued in 2024.[1]

The latest deal closed on September 22, 2026. With an affiliate of Ridgewood Energy, Talos bought Shell Offshore's 50% operated stake in the Coulomb field. It also bought a 25% stake in the BP-operated Na Kika platform and four fields. Talos paid $420 million of net cash, including a $42.5 million deposit.[11] The assets add about 23 million Boe of proved reserves, 10 million of probable reserves and about 16 thousand Boe a day of first-quarter output, 77% of it oil.[10] Talos paid with cash, a new $800.0 million 8.000% note due 2034, and a credit line raised from $700 million to $850 million. The new note also retired $625.0 million of 9.000% notes at 104.500%. Talos posted about $195.5 million of surety bonds for the decommissioning it took on.[2][12]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Working interest is a share of a field's costs and output. A 50% working interest pays half the bills and gets half the barrels.
  • Tie-back is a new well connected by pipe to an existing platform, which is usually cheaper and faster than a new platform.
  • Workover is repair work on an existing well to restore its flow.
  • Plugging and abandonment (P&A) is the permanent sealing of old wells and removal of platforms. The matching liability is the asset retirement obligation (ARO).
  • Borrowing base is the most a bank group will lend against an oil company's reserves, reset twice a year.
  • Ceiling test is the quarterly check that writes oil properties down when their value falls below their recorded cost.
  • Proved reserves are the oil and gas the company can recover with reasonable certainty at current prices. PV-10 is their value after discounting the future cash at 10%.
  • Realized price is the price actually received per unit before hedge gains or losses.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Talos is a good operator in a business where nobody sets the price. Its strengths are cost, scale in one region and skill at complex offshore projects. Its weaknesses are the oil price, the weather, the regulator and a short reserve life. In 2025 adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, with the company's adjustments) was $1,197.6 million on revenue of $1,780.1 million. That is a 67% margin. LOE per Boe fell from $16.70 in 2024 to $15.83 in 2025.[1]

Production has held at 92.6 to 94.6 MBoe/d in 2024 and 2025, after the QuarterNorth deal. It was 59.5 in 2022.[1][8] Debottlenecking lifted Tarantula's capacity from 35 to about 38 thousand Boe a day. The company says the Cardona and CPN wells were drilled under budget and ahead of schedule.[1][5]

The weaknesses show up in the reserve numbers. Proved reserves fell from 194.2 to 174.7 million Boe during 2025. That is 5.1 years of 2025 output (174,693 thousand Boe over 34,534 thousand Boe).[1] By our calculation 2025 additions were 15.0 million Boe against 34.5 million produced, a replacement of about 43%. A short reserve life forces a company to buy or drill just to stand still, and that is what the 2024 and 2026 deals did.

The asset retirement obligation is the bill for sealing wells and removing platforms. It stood at $1,394.1 million on June 30, 2026, and Talos had $1.5 billion of performance bonds outstanding.[2] Surety agreements signed in late 2025 require $251.7 million of collateral through 2031. They also require at least $90.0 million a year of abandonment spending from 2026 to 2028.[1] The company has never paid a dividend.[1]

Costs and debt show where Talos stands against rivals. Talos reported LOE of $18.25 per Boe in the second quarter. Kosmos Energy, with West Africa in its mix, reported production expense of $25.61 per Boe. W&T Offshore comes to about $22.68 by our calculation ($71.6 million over 34.7 MBoe/d for 91 days).[3][29][28] The definitions differ, so this is a guide and not a ranking. On debt Talos is the strongest of the three. Net debt was 0.5 times trailing adjusted EBITDA at June 30, 2026, against 1.2 times at W&T and about 3.2 times at Kosmos on our arithmetic.[3][28][29] Chapter 3 sets these side by side.

Regulation, weather and counterparties

Offshore producers carry regulatory risk that onshore firms do not. The 10-K says Talos is particularly vulnerable to hurricanes. Hurricane Isaias, the first of the 2026 season, hit land on October 9 as a Category 2 storm. At the peak, operators had shut in 71.51% of Gulf oil output. By October 12 the shut-in had fallen to 29.63%, and BP was restarting Na Kika, where Talos now owns a quarter.[1][19][20] We found no statement from Talos on storm effects in its filings through October 23. The cost to fourth-quarter output stays unknown until the November 3 release.

A federal proposal of March 9, 2026 would change the credit-rating test for lessees from BBB- to BB-. On March 31, 2026 the Endangered Species Committee exempted Gulf oil and gas work from Section 7 consultation. Lawsuits challenge that exemption, so its final effect is uncertain.[2] The Mexico Zama project is the other open item. The 10-K lists unitization on March 22, 2022, with gross interests of 17.35% for Talos, 32.22% for Harbour affiliates and 50.43% for PEMEX, and operator approvals in December 2025. The filings we read describe no active dispute and no FID date. We treat Zama as upside that is not in any number we use.[1]

Every share price carries a hidden forecast. A reverse discounted-cash-flow (reverse DCF) model starts from today's price and solves for the growth in free cash flow that a standard model needs to give that price. We use our house settings: a 10% annual discount rate, an 18 times multiple of cash flow in year ten, and a ten-year path. The market-data snapshot reports an implied growth of negative 8.6% a year for TALO. It used an enterprise value of $3.84 billion and free cash flow of $0.551 billion.

We replaced that figure with our own, for the reasons in Chapter 3. The snapshot's free cash flow cannot be reproduced from the filings, which give $0.331 billion. A price that is an equity value should also be paired with cash flow after interest. We used equity value of $3.070 billion, which is the $18.39 close times 166.96 million shares at June 30, 2026. We paired it with trailing free cash flow of $0.331 billion. That fits a producer with 5.1 years of proved reserves.

The result is sensitive to the terminal multiple. At 8 times instead of 18 times, the implied growth is about 0.1% a year. At 5 times it is 2.3% (our calculation). So the figure measures how much staying power the market gives the cash flow. It does not show whether the stock is cheap.

The nearest support the ledger finds is the 20-day average of $17.17. The current market price (CMP) of $18.39 is 1.77 ATR above it, so CMP is in the no-buy band.
