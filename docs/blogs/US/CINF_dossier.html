1. What the company does

Cincinnati Financial is an Ohio holding company whose lead business, The Cincinnati Insurance Company, was founded in 1950. It sells property casualty insurance, which means cover for buildings, vehicles, liability claims and workers, mainly to small and mid-size businesses and to well-off households, through independent insurance agencies in 46 states [9][19]. An independent agency is a local firm that offers policies from several insurers and chooses whom to recommend, so Cincinnati competes for each agent's attention with service, local decision-making and claims handling. At the end of 2025 it had 2,292 agency relationships with 3,702 reporting locations, supported by 2,195 field associates who live in the communities they serve [9].

The customer is the policyholder, who pays a premium for cover, and the agent earns a commission for placing the policy. The company is paid up front and pays claims later. Net written premiums, the premiums kept after paying for reinsurance (insurance the insurer buys for itself), were $10,082 million in 2025, and earned premiums, the part of those premiums that relates to cover already provided, were $9,983 million for the whole company [7]. About half of the premium is commercial insurance ($4,998 million). Personal lines, mostly home and car cover for well-off households, were $3,430 million. Excess and surplus lines, which cover risks that standard insurers decline, were $729 million, and the rest, $925 million, came from reinsurance and the Lloyd's business (Cincinnati Re and Cincinnati Global) [7]. A life insurance subsidiary earned $106 million in 2025 [7].

The company earns money in two ways, and the second is what sets it apart. The first is underwriting: premiums collected less claims and running costs. The measure of that is the combined ratio, which is claims plus expenses divided by earned premium. Below 100 percent means an underwriting profit. In the first nine months of 2026 it was 95.8 percent, so each $100 of premium left about $4.20 of underwriting profit before tax, or $337 million in all [2]. The second is investing. Because premiums arrive before claims are paid, Cincinnati holds a large pool of other people's money, called float, and invests it. Cash and investments were $34.378 billion at September 30, 2026, and investment income before tax was $962 million for nine months, up 12 percent [2].

What makes Cincinnati unusual is how it invests. A bond portfolio of $18.9 billion, rated A2/A on average, earned an average yield before tax of 5.04 percent in the third quarter. Alongside it sits an equity portfolio of $13.145 billion, 39.7 percent of all investments, with $8.872 billion of price gains that have not been sold [2]. Its largest holding is Apple at $1.156 billion, 3.6 percent of total investments, and the five largest holdings (Apple, Microsoft, Broadcom, AbbVie and Lam Research) together are worth $3.661 billion [4]. Accounting rules count changes in the market value of those stocks in reported net income. That is why third-quarter net income fell from $1.122 billion in 2025 to $459 million in 2026 while operating income, which leaves out investment gains and losses, moved only from $449 million to $443 million [2].

What this means for an owner: you are buying two things at one price. One is a careful, agent-driven insurer whose underwriting has been profitable for 14 years in a row. The other is a large basket of dividend-paying stocks, mostly in technology, whose value moves with the market and shows up in book value per share, which was $105.42 at September 30, 2026 [2]. Management measures itself on a value creation ratio, which adds the growth in book value per share to the dividend, and targets 10 to 13 percent a year over five years [9]. The next chapters ask how strong the insurance position is and how much of the return depends on the stock market.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • earnings per share (EPS) — the company's profit divided by the number of shares. We use operating EPS, which leaves out gains and losses on investments, and say so each time.
  • return on invested capital (ROIC) — profit earned on each dollar put into the business. For an insurer we use return on equity instead, which is profit divided by shareholders' money, because debt is tiny and equity is what funds the risk.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) — a profit measure used to judge debt loads. It does not fit an insurer, so we mark it not applicable and use debt as a share of total capital.
  • FCF (free cash flow) — cash left after running the business and buying equipment. For an insurer it is misleading, because cash flow from operations, the cash the business itself generates before financing, rises when premiums are collected before claims are paid. Cincinnati's operating cash flow was $3,112 million in 2025 against capital spending of $20 million, so we mark FCF margin not applicable [12].
  • net debt — debt minus cash and the like. At Cincinnati debt is $791 million of long-term debt against $6.044 billion of parent company cash and marketable securities, so net debt is negative [2].
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) — the cost of paying staff in shares. At Cincinnati it was $46 million in 2025, about 0.4 percent of revenue [9].
  • consensus estimate — the average of what analysts expect for profit per share. Only two analysts feed the figure we found, so it is thin.
  • guidance — a forecast the company publishes for the coming year. Cincinnati does not give a profit guidance; it sets long-term targets, which we test in Chapter 2B.
  • buyback — the company buys its own shares, which cuts the share count and lifts profit per share. The board added 15 million shares to the authorization on August 21, 2026 [16].
  • price-to-earnings — the share price divided by profit per share; it says how many years of current profit you pay for.
  • RSI (relative strength index) — a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been rising; above 70 is called overbought, below 30 oversold.
  • ATR (average true range) — the typical daily price swing in dollars, used to measure distance from support in units of normal movement.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average — the average closing price over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days. Each is a slow line the price tends to bounce off.
  • Stage 2 — the confirmed uptrend in the Weinstein stage scale; Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend and is the only chart condition that rejects a stock outright.
  • base — a sideways price shelf a stock builds before a move; pivot is the top of that shelf and a breakout is a move above it on heavy volume. A retest is a pullback to that level from above.
  • stop loss — the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold; a limit order only fills at your price or better, so it never chases.
  • entry zone — the band of prices where buying is planned; asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The central strength is distribution. Cincinnati sells only through independent agencies, and it deliberately stays small in each one: its share of an agency's business is 7.4 percent in agencies that have represented it for more than 10 years, 3.2 percent at six to ten years, 1.5 percent at two to five years and 0.4 percent in the first year [9]. That ladder is the moat in numbers. New agencies start small and grow with trust, so each new appointment (420 in 2025 and 300 in the first nine months of 2026) is a source of future premium that costs little to keep [7][2]. The business is also spread out: the largest single agency is about 0.5 percent of earned premium, no ownership group exceeds 8 percent, and the ten largest states, led by Ohio at 12.8 percent, are 50.2 percent of premium [9]. On the shareholder side, Executive Chairman Steven Johnston owns 893,652 shares (0.57 percent) and Vanguard owns 12.32 percent, as of March 4, 2026 [14].

The second strength is a record of underwriting profit through bad years. The combined ratio averaged 93.9 percent over 2021 to 2025, inside the 92 to 98 percent target, and the company has earned an underwriting profit for 14 consecutive years [9][7]. Reserves, the money set aside for unpaid claims, are kept in the upper half of the range the actuaries consider reasonable, and management says the approach has been consistent for 30 years, and in 2021 it reported favorable development for the 33rd consecutive year; in the first nine months of 2026 favorable development cut the combined ratio by 2.9 points [19][11][2]. Financial strength is high: the main insurance subsidiaries are rated A+ by A.M. Best and S&P, AA- by Fitch and A1 by Moody's, written premium is only 1.0 times statutory surplus, and debt is 4.8 percent of total capital [3][9][2].

The weaknesses are growth and volatility. Premium growth has slowed from 9 percent in 2025 to 7, 3 and 2 percent in the first three quarters of 2026, and new business written through agencies fell 12 percent in both the quarter and the nine months; in personal lines the quarterly fall was 34 percent [7][3][2]. Management calls this a normalization after exceptionally strong growth, and prices still rise: commercial renewals averaged low single-digit increases in the third quarter, homeowners high single-digit and personal auto mid single-digit [19]. But the CEO also said the most pressure is on larger commercial accounts, and that workers' compensation and general liability prices are under pressure relative to claim costs [19]. When prices stop outrunning claim inflation, the combined ratio has little room to improve.

Volatility comes from catastrophes. Catastrophe losses added 12.0 points to the nine-month 2026 combined ratio and 10.7 points in the third quarter, against 3.7 points a year earlier [4]. The second quarter ran at 100.8 percent because storms added 14.5 points; in January 2025 the wildfires pushed the first quarter to 113.3 percent [5][3]. The company ceded only $309 million of its $7,981 million of property casualty premiums to reinsurers in nine months (about 3.9 percent, our arithmetic), so most weather losses stay on its own books [3]. What this means for an owner: the franchise is strong and slow-moving, but quarterly earnings can swing sharply on weather alone: operating EPS was $2.10, $1.43 and $2.86 in the first three quarters of 2026 [6][5][2]. The next chapter's numbers must be read with that in mind.

How the investment portfolio helps and hurts

The portfolio is the second engine and the second risk. Bond interest income grew 11 percent in the third quarter because new money is going in at a pretax yield of 5.93 percent, well above the portfolio's 5.04 percent average, and $2.0 billion of bonds were bought net over nine months [19][2]. That shift is real earning power. The equity side works differently from a bond fund: the holdings paid $214 million of dividends in nine months, about $285 million a year at that pace (our arithmetic), and dividends were down 4 percent in the quarter. The holdings also carry the price risk [4][2].

The equity portfolio of $13.145 billion is 81 percent of shareholders' equity of $16.182 billion, a ratio we compute from the balance sheet. Technology is 35.6 percent of the stock portfolio [2][4]. The company's own sensitivity table shows what that means: a 30 percent fall in stock prices would reduce the portfolio by about $3.9 billion before tax, which is roughly $20 per share of book value after tax at the 21 percent rate we assume. Offsetting that, the bond portfolio carries a net unrealized loss of $1.489 billion because rates rose, and that loss will shrink as bonds approach maturity if rates do not rise further [4]. Management has sold $784 million of stocks this year to buy bonds, a modest step toward less equity risk [19].

Commercial casualty had adverse prior-year development of 3.5 points in the second quarter of 2026 and 6.0 points in the third quarter of 2025 [3]. The next tests are the January renewal season, which shows whether price increases still outrun claim costs (third-quarter commercial renewals rose by low single digits), and the fourth-quarter report and 2026 10-K, which include the annual reserve review [19].

Failure 3: a catastrophe-heavy fourth quarter or a repeat of 2025

Cincinnati ceded only about 3.9 percent of its property casualty premium in nine months. Most storm losses therefore stay on its books [3]. Calendar-year catastrophe losses were $919 million in nine months, against $952 million a year earlier. The January 2025 California wildfires alone cost $448 million, and the April 12 to 16 storms cost $130 million in the second quarter of 2026 [4][18]. A repeat of that event would cost roughly $2.30 a share after tax (our arithmetic: $448M x 79 percent / 155.1M shares). The 2026 hurricane season runs to November 30. So far it has had 10 named storms and one major hurricane, Isaias [30]. Isaias came ashore near Destin, Florida late on October 9 and was expected to cause more than $2 billion of insured losses in the United States [31]. Florida and Alabama are not among Cincinnati's ten largest states, but Georgia, in the Southeast, is its eighth largest at 3.7 percent of premium [9]. Its exposure to Isaias is unknown until it reports.

The evidence is any catastrophe disclosure the company chooses to make before the report. It is also the fourth-quarter catastrophe loss in the February 2027 release. The last two fourth quarters carried 1.0 and 4.0 points of catastrophe loss [8]. A fourth-quarter load of 8 points instead of the 2.5-point average would cut fourth-quarter operating EPS by about $0.74. That is our arithmetic: 5.5 points of $2,650M of earned premium, after 21 percent tax, over 155.5M shares.

7. What we would do, and when

Our decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. Do not buy Cincinnati at the $179.39 close, and do not treat the zone below as a standing order. The rule is the support ladder in our scoring file. The close is 3.2 ATR above the 20-day average, beyond the 1.6 ATR limit for a starter position. Reward-to-risk is 0.41 to 1 at the close, against a 2.0 to 1 floor for a compounder [32][33]. The decision would change only if the price falls into the $160.50 to $164.40 zone and the checks in Trigger 1 pass, including a fresh valuation. It would also change if the facts move as listed in Triggers 3 and 4.

The zone is built from three levels. Its top, $164.40, is about 1.6 ATR above the 20-day swing low of $159.34. The 63-day base low is $159.20. That is the limit of the buyable band. It is also where the $189 base target pays 2.99 to 1. Its bottom, $160.50, is about 0.4 ATR above those lows, where the payoff is 6.57 to 1. The stop loss is $156.16. That is the lower of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average, less one ATR of $3.18. It is how our rule places stops for core holdings in a Stage 2 advance [32][33]. We did not use the watchlist seed stop of $167.21, which ignored the swing low. On the seed's own levels, a zone of $169.25 to $173.00 and a target of $194.81, reward-to-risk at $173.00 would be 3.77 to 1. With the correct stop and our target it is 0.95 to 1.

The size follows the risk-unit rule. TIER_4 is our smallest position size class. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so 35 basis points is 0.35 percent of net worth. The risk budget is the most we accept to lose if the stop is hit. TIER_4 carries a risk budget of 35 basis points of net worth. At the zone top the stop is 5.01 percent away, so the formula allows 6.98 percent of net worth. The tier ceiling of 2.0 percent binds. So the starting position is 2.0 percent of net worth, half bought at $164.40 and half at $160.50, once the checks in Trigger 1 pass. If the stop is hit after a full fill at the top of the zone, the loss is about 10 basis points of net worth. We chose the smallest tier because the Layer 2 score is 3.5 of 5.0 with three WATCH marks, and the zone is far from the market. This is not a current holding, so there is no position to audit. After a fill, the let-winners-run rule applies. Do not sell merely because RSI passes 70 or the weight passes 15 percent. Trail the stop under the rising 21-day average or 50-day average. Let the weight grow to the 22 percent ceiling.
