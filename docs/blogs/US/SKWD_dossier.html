1. What the company does

Skyward Specialty Insurance Group is a Houston company that sells insurance for risks that standard insurers handle badly or avoid. A restaurant group with a poor claims history, a dairy farm worried about falling milk prices and an employer that pays its own staff medical bills all fit that description. At the end of 2025 it had about 611 employees.[5] On January 1, 2026 it closed the purchase of Apollo, a Lloyd's of London managing agency, and since then it reports two parts: the Skyward Specialty segment in the United States and the Apollo segment.[15][3]

Customers do not buy from the company directly. Policies reach them through retail agents, wholesale brokers, program administrators and captive managers, which are firms that run group insurance arrangements for their clients. In 2025 about 41% of premium was written on an admitted basis, meaning the policy follows state rate and form rules, and 59% was non-admitted, meaning the insurer prices each risk freely.[5] Property cover was 34% of premium, so about two-thirds of the book is liability, surety, medical and other lines.[5]

Skyward Specialty earns money in three ways. The first is underwriting profit: premium collected minus claims paid and costs. It was $106.4 million in the first half of 2026.[1] The second is investment income. Premium arrives before claims are paid, so the company invests the cash in between, and net investment income was $57.8 million in the half.[1] The third is new with Apollo: fees for managing Lloyd's syndicates that other investors fund, which were $22.7 million in the half.[1] Fee income needs little capital, so it can lift returns without adding much risk.

The main score for an insurer is the combined ratio, which is claims plus costs divided by premium. Below 100% means an underwriting profit. In the second quarter of 2026 the group ratio was 89.5%, with the Skyward Specialty segment at 86.9% and Apollo at 97.6%, which includes 5.4 points of catastrophe losses linked to the conflict in the Middle East.[1] The group expense ratio was 27.2%, so claims took the remaining 62.3 cents of each premium dollar.[1] Chapter 2 asks whether that margin is durable.

How the Apollo purchase changed the company

Apollo is a managing agency at Lloyd's, the London insurance market where investors back syndicates run by specialist underwriters. The company paid about $559.1 million: 3,679,332 new Skyward shares valued at $51.11 each, plus $371.1 million in cash.[2] It raised debt to fund the cash part. Notes payable rose from $100.4 million at the end of 2025 to $417.6 million at June 30, 2026.[1]

Management sells Apollo as a second engine. Managed premium, which is what the group underwrites itself plus what third-party syndicates write under its management, was $1,058.7 million in the second quarter, up 17.5%.[1] Fee-earning premium rose 28.8% to $318.1 million.[1] The cost is a thinner balance sheet: goodwill and intangible assets grew from $88.0 million to $471.2 million.[1] Chapter 2B and Chapter 3 put numbers on that trade.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • E&S (excess and surplus lines) is the market for risks that standard insurers decline. These insurers can price each risk on its own merits.
  • Gross written premium is the total premium charged to customers in a period. Net written premium is what remains after the part paid to reinsurers, who take on part of the risk.
  • Combined ratio is claims plus expenses divided by premium. Below 100% means an underwriting profit, and each point below 100% is one cent of profit per premium dollar.
  • Reserves are money set aside for claims that have happened but are not yet paid. If reserves turn out too low, the insurer must charge the shortfall to profit later.
  • Return on equity (ROE) is a year of profit divided by average shareholders' equity. For an insurer it plays the role that return on invested capital plays for other companies.
  • Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding. It is the accounting value of each share, and for an insurer it is the usual yardstick for price.
  • Catastrophe losses are claims from large single events such as storms, floods and wars. They are reported separately because they swing so much from year to year.
  • earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. Operating earnings leave out swings in investment values and one-off items, so they show the insurance business more cleanly.
  • stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given in shares or options. It is a real cost to owners because it creates new shares.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit before borrowing costs, taxes and non-cash charges. We use it only to test borrowing, as the scoring rules require.
  • return on invested capital (ROIC) is profit divided by the money invested in a business. For an insurer we use return on equity instead, because the equity is the capital.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment. For an insurer it is mostly premium waiting to be paid out as claims, so it says little and we mark it not applicable.
  • net debt is borrowing minus cash. It matters when debt is large, so we also measure debt against total capital.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash that comes in from running the business. For an insurer it rises with premium and falls as claims are paid.
  • buyback means the company buys its own shares in the open market, which shrinks the share count and raises each remaining owner's slice.
  • dilution is the rise in share count when a company issues new shares, for example to pay staff or to buy another company. It makes each existing share worth a smaller slice.
  • guidance is a company's own forecast or target. We check later whether the company met it.
  • consensus estimate is the average forecast from the analysts who follow a stock. We use it to see what the market expects.
  • price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share. It tells you how many years of current profit you pay for.
  • gross margin, operating margin and net margin are profit measures as a share of revenue. They matter less for an insurer, where the combined ratio does the same job.
  • debt-to-equity is borrowing divided by shareholders' equity. For insurers we use debt as a share of total capital, which is easier to read.
  • working capital and capital expenditure are short-term operating funds and spending on equipment. Neither is a main driver for an insurer.
  • Stage 2 is the rising part of a stock's life cycle, when the price is above a rising 50-day average and a rising 200-day average. Stage 4 is the falling part, when the price is below both and both are falling. A stock in the third stage, as this one is, is either topping or pulling back.
  • A 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 sessions. The 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the same measure over 50 and 200 sessions, and they act as moving floors in an uptrend.
  • A base is a stretch of weeks or months when a stock moves sideways after a decline. A pivot is the price at the top of that sideways range, and a breakout is a rise through it that signals a new advance. A retest is a return to a level the price recently bounced from, to see whether it holds.
  • relative strength index (RSI) is a 0 to 100 reading of how fast a price has moved. Under 30 is called oversold and over 70 is called overbought.
  • average true range (ATR) is the typical size of one day's price swing over 14 days. It is the ruler we use to measure distances on a chart.
  • A stop loss is a price at which you sell to limit a loss. A limit order is an order to buy at a chosen price or lower, so you never pay more than you decided.
  • The entry zone is the band of prices at which we would start buying. Asymmetry and reward-to-risk mean the same thing here: the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop.
  • A measured move is a target taken from the height of a chart pattern. The terms take rate and book-to-bill do not apply to an insurer, so this report does not use them. The next chapter asks whether the business itself is strong.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Skyward Specialty's first strength is spread. Nine divisions make up the continuing book and none is above 16.0% of premium.[5] Management says nearly half of its business sits in lines that do not follow the usual property and casualty price cycle.[4] That matters now, because prices are falling in property and some professional lines. In the second quarter Global Property premium fell 15.3%, Energy Solutions fell 16.2% and Professional Lines fell 6.9%, while Accident & Health grew 57.8%, Specialty Programs 29.7% and Credit & Surety 15.6%.[1] The company is leaning toward the lines that are not softening.

The second strength is a steady improvement in underwriting. The annual combined ratio went from 97.8% in 2021 to 94.0% in 2022, 90.7% in 2023, 92.3% in 2024 and 89.3% in 2025.[8][7][5] The 2024 figure was hurt by a 1.1 point charge from ending a loss portfolio transfer, a reinsurance deal that had covered old claims, so the underlying path is smoother than the headline.[4][26] The first half of 2026 held at 89.5% even with Apollo added, and the Skyward Specialty segment ran at 86.9% in the second quarter.[1]

Costs are the third strength. The Skyward Specialty expense ratio was 24.3% in the second quarter, 2.7 points better than a year earlier, and management credits scale, a shift in business mix and what it calls AI-driven efficiency, with submissions reaching underwriters 40% faster.[1][17] Some of the gain is mix: Accident & Health and agriculture pay lower commissions. So part of it will not repeat if the mix moves back. Pay also points the right way. The proxy ties executive pay to premium growth, combined ratio, total shareholder return and book value per share growth, and shareholders backed it with 99.1% support.[15]

The cases against the business are the next five paragraphs: a short record, growth that leans on one line, reserves with no safety net, a soft market in the old core lines, and a balance sheet stretched by the Apollo deal.

What is weakening: the track record is short

The company listed on the stock market on January 18, 2023.[8] Before that it was a private company, and its numbers then were weaker: a 97.8% combined ratio and a 9.4% return on equity in 2021.[8] The good numbers cover only three full years. That is long enough to show the strategy works in a firm market, which is the market of 2023 to 2025, but too short to show it holds up when prices fall for several years. The first test is happening now.

There is a second reason for care. In 2024 the auditors gave an adverse opinion on the company's internal controls over financial reporting because of a weakness in controls over information technology, and the company filed two corrected audit reports in 2025 to fix typing errors.[6][28] The 2025 annual report says the weakness was remediated by December 31, 2025.[5] We treat that as fixed, but it is a reminder that the finance function was building scale at the same time as the business.

What is weakening: growth leans on one line and on true-ups

Gross written premium grew 21.7% in 2022, 27.6% in 2023, 19.4% in 2024 and 24.3% in 2025.[8][7][5] The growth quality is the issue. Agriculture and Credit went from $30.6 million to $346.2 million in two years, and it supplied 54% of the 2025 increase.[5] In the second quarter of 2026 Global Agriculture grew 95.8%, and the chief executive said most of that growth came from premium true-ups, mainly in the U.S. dairy livestock program.[1][17] A true-up is an adjustment of premium after the period ends, so it adds revenue without a new customer.

Mix also costs margin. The non-catastrophe loss ratio of the Skyward Specialty segment rose 1.4 points in the second quarter because Accident & Health and agriculture run higher loss ratios, and the CFO said so on the call. Management expects the full-year accident-year loss ratio to be about one point or a little more higher, driven entirely by mix, with a matching offset in acquisition costs.[17] Higher claims are offset by lower commissions and costs, so the combined ratio held, but it shows that the growth is not free. Premium grew 51.6% in the third quarter of 2025 and 13.2% in the fourth, then 9.9% in the first quarter of 2026 and 13.3% in the second, both on a pro forma basis, so the growth rate is already slowing from 24.3% for the full year 2025.[27][4][3][1]

What is weakening: reserves with no safety net

Reserves are the insurer's estimate of claims it has not yet paid, and for casualty lines the claims can take years to settle. Gross reserves were $3,070.6 million at June 30, 2026, against equity of $1,267.5 million.[1] In January 2025 the company ended its loss portfolio transfer, a deal that had passed the risk on claims from accident years 2018 and earlier to another insurer, so that risk is now its own.[26]

The record is mixed. In 2024 prior years added $25.7 million of adverse development, and in 2025 the company released a net $7.5 million: favorable development of $24.6 million and $5.3 million in two groups of lines, partly offset by $22.4 million of adverse development in exited lines, mainly commercial auto and excess over auto.[5] In the first half of 2026 no development was recognized, which means prior-year reserves were neither raised nor released.[2][17] Chapter 2B scores this measure as a watch item for that reason.

What is weakening: the old core lines are in a soft market

Insurance prices move in cycles. When profits are high, new capital arrives and prices fall. Management says conditions are tougher in property, both global and E&S, and in miscellaneous professional lines, and the chief executive agreed with a rival's chief executive who had called the marketplace "idiotic".[17] Pure rate, meaning the price change for the same risk, was in the high single digits excluding Global Property and in the low single digits including it.[17] Apollo's risk-adjusted rate moved to a low single-digit decline.[17]

The company's defense is to shrink where prices are poor and grow where they are not. That is why premium in Global Property, Energy Solutions and Professional Lines fell in the quarter.[1] It protects the margin, but it also means growth must come from lines that are newer to the company and have shorter track records. Loss costs are the other worry: the chief executive asked why anyone would grow into a market when the loss cost inflation cannot be known with confidence.[17]

What is weakening: a balance sheet stretched by Apollo

Before Apollo, debt was 10.6% of total capital at the end of 2025. After it, debt was $437.2 million against $1,267.5 million of equity, which is 25.6% of capital.[1][5] Management calls this financial leverage of 26% and says it is moving toward a target in the low 20s, after repaying $50 million of a $150 million term loan in June.[17][2] Goodwill and intangible assets are now $471.2 million, which is 37% of equity, so tangible equity is $861.9 million.[1]

Reinsurance recoverables, the amounts reinsurers owe the company on claims, were $1,411.6 million, or 111% of equity, and the group kept 65.2% of its gross premium in the first half.[1][2] Reinsurers are rated and the recoverables are normal for an insurer that cedes a third of its business. Even so, the figure is large against equity, and it ties the company's results to the health of its reinsurers.

People matter too. Chief Financial Officer Mark Haushill told the company on August 17, 2026 that he will retire effective March 31, 2027, and the company named Taryn McHarg, who has been Apollo's chief financial officer since 2022, as his successor.[14] The handover is planned, but it puts a new signature on the numbers in the middle of an integration.

What this means for an owner

Skyward Specialty is a good business with a short record, and it trades at a lower price than its better-known peers. Its margin and return are strong, its costs are falling, and its mix is moving to lines that do not follow the price cycle. What it has not shown is a full soft market. Its growth also leans more on one line, and on a leveraged acquisition, than the headline suggests. For an owner, that means the low price has a reason, and the reason is the risk that the margin fades before the record is long enough to trust. We solve for the growth rate g that makes ten years of operating income, each discounted at 10%, plus 18 times the tenth year's income, also discounted, equal to today's equity value. Value = sum of E0 x (1+g)^t / 1.10^t for t = 1 to 10, plus 18 x E0 x (1+g)^10 / 1.10^10. The answer is found by trial: at g = -2.2% the sum is $2,573 million.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report has a weakest assumption. This chapter states the strongest honest argument against buying, three ways the case could fail outright, and the single claim everything depends on. It also says when we will know. Chapter 7 then turns the view into a plan.

The red-team bear case: what the strongest honest argument against buying says

The bear says the stock is cheap because it should be. Skyward Specialty has shown a combined ratio below 91% in only two of its last three full years, 90.7% in 2023 and 89.3% in 2025 against 92.3% in 2024, and those years were a firm market in which most specialty insurers did well.[7][5] The 89.5% in the first half of 2026 has no reserve release helping it and 1.9 points of catastrophe loss, and management itself expects the claims ratio to rise.[2][17] Return on equity of 20.4% is an operating figure, and the GAAP figure after Apollo's amortization is 17.3%.[1]

The balance sheet is also weaker than the return suggests. Debt is 25.6% of capital, intangible assets are 37% of equity and reinsurance recoverables are 111% of equity.[1] The acquisition was paid for with 3.68 million new shares valued at $51.11 and $371.1 million of cash, and it was done in a firm market.[2] If the combined ratio returned to 94% (the 2022 level), EPS would be about $4.30, and at 10 times that is $43.

Our reply is that the bear has a fair point on duration and not on price. At 11.5 times 2026 EPS, a company with a 20% return does not need the 90% combined ratio to last forever. It needs it to hold through 2027, and the Skyward Specialty segment is at 86.9%.[1] That is why we assign 30% to the bear case and refuse to buy at the market.

Fatal flaw 1: old claims turn out to cost more than reserved

Reserves are the estimate of claims not yet paid, and for liability lines they are estimated years before they settle. The company took that risk back when it commuted its loss portfolio transfer in January 2025.[26] In 2025 it released $29.9 million from short-tail lines and had to add $22.4 million in exited lines, mostly commercial auto and excess over auto.[5] If the same pattern appeared in lines that are still growing, such as general liability sold through programs and captives, an adverse charge of $60 million would cost about $1.04 a share after tax, which is about 19% of a year's earnings.

The first sign would be a reserve charge in a quarterly report, shown as prior-year development in the combined ratio. The company has recognized none in the first half of 2026.[17] Management says it was early to warn about loss cost trends in occurrence liability and in claims for bodily injury.[17] A second, slower sign would be reserves growing faster than earned premium. We will watch both in the third-quarter report on November 5.[16]

Fatal flaw 2: Apollo disappoints while the debt stays

Apollo's second quarter combined ratio was 97.6%, with 5.4 points from catastrophe losses tied to the conflict in the Middle East, and its first-half ratio was 91.3%.[1] Apollo carried 5.4 points of catastrophe loss in the quarter against 1.3 points in the Skyward Specialty segment, so a Lloyd's syndicate takes more of this risk.[1] If a large event in 2027 added 8 to 10 points of catastrophe loss on Apollo's annual net earned premium of about $264 million, which is four times its $66.1 million second quarter, group EPS would fall by about 35 to 45 cents a share. A market-wide Lloyd's event could be larger.

The harder risk is financial. The term loan carries covenants on minimum net worth, maximum debt to capital, minimum A.M. Best rating and minimum liquidity, and the company reported compliance at June 30, 2026.[2] A hit to equity from a reserve charge would raise debt-to-capital above the 25.6% it reports now and make the covenant test closer. It would also delay buybacks, which management has linked to lower leverage.[17] We would see the first signal in a rise in debt-to-capital above 27% or a cut to the buyback pace.

Fatal flaw 3: the soft market reaches the growth lines

The company is shifting toward Accident & Health, agriculture, surety and programs because prices in property and professional lines are falling. These are not immune. Accident & Health is medical stop-loss, where claims follow medical cost inflation, and in the second quarter premium grew 57.8%.[1] Agriculture grew 95.8% mostly through premium true-ups, and its dairy and livestock cover is hedged with options and futures on milk, cattle and hog prices, which means a sharp commodity move could change its results.[1][17][5]

If these lines soften as the older ones did, growth would fall from about 13% to the low single digits, and the margin benefit from lower commissions would fade. The first sign would be premium growth under 8% and a rising claims share in Accident & Health and agriculture. Management's own cap on how large any division can get, about 17% by year end and under 20% as a rule, shows it is aware of the concentration.[17]
