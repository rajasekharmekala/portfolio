Before you read

Meta Platforms owns Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Messenger, and it sells ads to businesses that want to reach 3.64 billion people a day.[1] This report says REJECTED for now. Ads are growing 26 percent, but free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after paying for equipment and data centers, and it fell from 21.7 percent of sales in 2025 to 10.4 percent, below our 12 percent floor.[1][2][5] The stock closed at $642.62 on 2026-10-23 after earnings and a $25 billion New Mexico penalty.[25][14] We would be wrong if 2027 spending falls below operating cash flow, or if the penalty is cut on appeal[2][33].

1. What the company does

Meta runs the social apps that billions of people open every day: Facebook, Instagram, Messenger and WhatsApp. In September 2026 the average number of people using at least one of them on a given day was 3.64 billion, up 3 percent from a year earlier.[1] Nobody pays to use these apps. Meta earns its money from businesses that pay to show ads to those people, which is why advertising was $63.06 billion of the $64.60 billion in third-quarter revenue.[2]

An ad sale has two parts: how many ads Meta shows, and how much each ad sells for. In the third quarter the number of ads shown rose 10 percent and the average price per ad rose 14 percent, which together explain revenue growth of 26 percent.[1][2] Rising volume and rising price at the same time usually means advertisers are getting better results per dollar, because they would not pay more otherwise. Meta says its artificial intelligence (AI) investments are bringing in extra ad revenue through better recommendations and more efficient ad sales.[1]

A second, much smaller business is Reality Labs, which makes smart glasses and virtual reality headsets. It had $443 million of revenue and lost $4.13 billion in the quarter, and it has lost between $16 billion and $19 billion every year since 2023.[2][5] Meta also launched an AI personal assistant called Muse in September 2026, and the chief executive said more than 10 million people in the US and Canada had set one up within about six weeks.[16] The documents we read do not report Muse revenue separately.

The third thing to know is how the company is owned. Founder Mark Zuckerberg controls a majority of the votes through the dual-class share structure.[2] On 2026-10-21 Meta announced a new non-voting Class C share that every holder will receive one-for-one in January 2027, which works like a two-for-one split, and the filing says the company expects to use Class C shares for future equity offerings.[3][4][2] That matters for outside owners because it makes selling new shares easier.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPS stands for earnings per share: net profit divided by the number of shares.
  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of cash profit from operations.
  • ROIC stands for return on invested capital: after-tax operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business.
  • SBC stands for stock-based compensation: pay given to employees in shares, which is a real cost to owners.
  • RSI stands for relative strength index, a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has moved (below 30 is called oversold, above 70 overbought).
  • ATR stands for average true range: the typical size of one day's price move, used to place stops.
  • Gross margin is the share of sales left after the direct cost of delivering the service; operating margin is the share left after all running costs.
  • Net debt is borrowings minus cash and securities; a negative number means the company holds more cash than debt.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates before paying for equipment; capital expenditure is the spending on equipment and data centers.
  • Dilution is the shrinking of each owner's slice when a company issues new shares; a buyback is the opposite, when it buys shares back.
  • Guidance is the forecast management gives for the next quarter or year; a consensus estimate is the average forecast of Wall Street analysts.
  • Price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share, a measure of how many years of profit the price equals.
  • Stage 2 is the rising-markup phase of a price trend, and Stage 4 is the falling-markdown phase.
  • Entry zone is the price range where we would buy; a limit order is an order to buy only at or below a set price; a stop loss is the price where we sell to cap the loss.
  • Asymmetry and reward-to-risk both mean the potential gain divided by the potential loss; a base is a flat price shelf where a stock rests before its next move.
  • A breakout is a close above that shelf on heavy trading, and the pivot is the exact price a stock must clear for the breakout to count.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of Meta is reach plus results. About 3.64 billion people use its apps each day, and 97.6 percent of third-quarter revenue ($63.06 billion of $64.60 billion) came from ads.[2] Advertisers follow audiences, and the two ad-funded rivals in our peer table are far smaller (Pinterest has $4.56 billion of trailing revenue and Snap $6.35 billion), so a marketer that wants to reach most of the online world still has to buy from Meta. The pricing evidence is the best sign of that power: the average price per ad rose 12 percent in the first quarter, 12 percent in the second and 14 percent in the third, while the number of ads shown also grew.[9][10][1]

The same data also shows what is weakening. Growth in ads shown slowed from 19 percent in the first quarter to 14 percent in the second and 10 percent in the third, so price is now doing more of the work.[9][10][1] People using the apps daily grew 7 percent in December 2025, then 4 percent, 3 percent and 3 percent in the next three quarters, and the third-quarter figure of 3.64 billion was slightly below the 3.65 billion analysts expected.[8][9][10][1][16] A business that is close to everyone has to grow by selling each person more, and that depends on advertisers continuing to accept higher prices.

Cost is the second weakness. Total costs rose 68 percent in the quarter against revenue growth of 26 percent. Even after removing the $9.89 billion legal charge, operating income rose only 11 percent to $22.88 billion, so the underlying operating margin was 35.4 percent against 40.1 percent a year earlier.[1][2] The company points to legal costs, data centers, cloud rental and third-party AI processing.[2][17] This is the price of keeping the ad engine ahead, but it means the moat is being defended with more and more capital each year.

The third weakness is the legal and regulatory overhang, which is now large enough to move the share price on its own. The 10-Q records a $25 billion New Mexico penalty, a 52-attorney-general agreement worth up to $18.59 billion, a European fine and several open trials.[2][1] None of these has so far stopped the ad business from growing, but they take cash and management attention, and they raise the chance that regulators force changes to how ads are targeted.

Why the stock fell this month

The catalyst was the 2026-10-21 earnings report together with a court ruling, not a collapse in the ad business. September 2026 was Meta's best month in the stock market since 2022 after the Muse launch, and the 52-week high is $779.82.[16][26] On 2026-10-21 a New Mexico judge ordered the $25 billion penalty.[14][13] After the close that day Meta reported revenue of $64.60 billion, above the $63.34 billion analysts expected.[1][21]

Investors then read the cash-flow statement. Capital spending of $34.91 billion exceeded operating cash flow of $32.79 billion, so free cash flow was negative $2.12 billion, which a secondary source calls the first negative quarter since the 2012 stock-market listing.[1][17] Full-year 2026 capital spending guidance was narrowed to $135-145 billion by lifting the low end, the filing showed about $810 billion of leases not yet started ($355.73 billion) and non-cancelable purchase commitments ($454.52 billion). A further $14.72 billion of contingent cloud purchases lifts the total some outlets quote to about $825 billion. The company also created a non-voting share class that it says it may use to raise money.[1][2] On 2026-10-22 the stock fell 7 percent to $648.85 from $697.52, and on 2026-10-23 it closed at $642.62, down another 0.96 percent.[19][17][25] A related headline on the Investor's Business Daily article page reads "Nasdaq Suffers Worst Session Since June As AI, War Jitters Ramp Up; Meta Plummets", but the page does not show its date, so we treat it as background only.[18]

What this means for you: the sell-off is about cash and funding, not about demand for Meta ads, and that is why the next chapters spend more time on the cash-flow statement than on the revenue line. The bridge to the next chapter is the set of operating measures that show whether the ad engine is really as strong as the revenue line says.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter follows revenue, profit, cash and debt over five fiscal years and the latest twelve months. It then tests them against our fixed quality floors, which we call Layer 1. Meta does not report a gross profit line, so gross profit here is revenue minus cost of revenue, our own arithmetic.[5][6] The resulting gross margin has stayed between 78 and 82 percent. That means serving the apps costs little, and the real spending is in research and data centers.

Revenue grew from $116.61 billion in 2022 to $200.97 billion in 2025, a three-year growth rate of 19.9 percent a year. The latest twelve months reached $241.61 billion.[6][5][26] Operating income peaked at $83.28 billion in 2025, a 41.4 percent operating margin. Over the latest twelve months it fell to $79.38 billion, or 32.9 percent. Three things caused that: the legal charges, $1.18 billion of severance from the May 2026 job cuts, and the surge in data center costs.[5][2] Net profit over the same period was $75.25 billion. An $8.03 billion tax benefit in the first quarter of 2026 helped it, and the Q3 legal charge hurt it.[9][1][2]

Cash tells a different story from profit. Cash from operations has risen every year since 2022, to $115.80 billion in 2025 and $133.09 billion over the latest twelve months. Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

5. What could happen next

A reverse discounted cash flow (reverse DCF) works backward from the price. It asks what yearly growth in free cash flow, over ten years, the market must expect for today's price to be fair. The portfolio settings are a 10 percent required return and a terminal value of 18 times free cash flow in year ten.[2] We computed it ourselves because the data vendor's valuation feed was unavailable on this date. The inputs are an enterprise value of $1,643.9 billion and trailing free cash flow of $25.13 billion. Enterprise value is market value of $1,642.7 billion plus debt of $83.7 billion minus cash and securities of $82.4 billion.[2][24]

The answer is 21.25 percent a year. That means free cash flow would have to rise from $25.1 billion to about $173 billion by 2036, nearly seven times, for a buyer at $642.62 to earn 10 percent. Analysts are more optimistic than that sounds. They forecast 2026 revenue of $254.19 billion, up 26.5 percent, and 2027 revenue of $306.91 billion, up 20.7 percent. They forecast earnings per share of $30.37 in 2026 and $34.63 in 2027, which is 14.0 percent growth.[23]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above rests on one assumption that could be wrong, and the red-team job is to name it, state what would show it wrong, and give a date. We also list the three events that would damage the thesis most, and then the single claim that matters more than the rest.

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF

The weakest assumption is that trailing free cash flow of $25.1 billion is the right starting point. If 2026 ends negative, as our estimate suggests, the starting figure is lower than the one we used and the implied growth rate is higher. Evidence that it is wrong would be a fourth quarter with operating cash flow far above $32.8 billion; we will know when the fourth-quarter report arrives, expected in late January 2027 but not yet confirmed by the company.[1][33]

Weakest assumption in the base target

The base target assumes consensus 2027 earnings of $34.63 are close to right. That requires revenue of $306.91 billion (up 20.7 percent) while 2026 depreciation and cloud costs from $135-145 billion of spending flow into the income statement.[23] Ad impressions growth has already slowed from 19 to 10 percent in three quarters, so price per ad must keep rising by double digits. The test is each quarterly report: if impressions growth falls to the low single digits and price growth falls below 10 percent, revenue growth of 20 percent is out of reach.[9][10][1]

Red-team bear case

The strongest bear case is about commitments, not demand. The 10-Q lists $454.52 billion of cancel-proof purchase commitments, of which $121.41 billion is due in 2027, equal to 91 percent of the $133.09 billion of operating cash flow generated over the last twelve months, plus $355.73 billion of leases that have not yet started.[2] If advertisers slow their spending, Meta cannot easily cut these costs, so profit would fall faster than revenue. The company has also built $19.88 billion of legal reserves on its balance sheet and faces a New Mexico penalty with 15 percent annual interest and a possible bond requirement during appeal.[2]

The bear case also notes the debt market. A secondary source reports that the five-year credit default swap on Meta rose to as high as 119.3 basis points, the highest intraday level since active trading began, and Oppenheimer was quoted projecting about $135 billion of free cash flow losses tied to AI through 2028.[17] That second figure is an analyst's estimate we cannot verify, but it shows how the Street frames the funding question.

Fatal-flaw scenario 1: the funding plan dilutes owners

If free cash flow stays negative into 2027, the company will fund the gap with notes and with Class C shares. The filing says Class C shares may trade below Class A shares, so more of them might be needed per dollar raised, and that the issuance could be dilutive to all stockholders.[2] A 5 percent share issue at today's price would raise about $82 billion and cut each owner's slice by 5 percent. This scenario is realistic because the company has already issued $25 billion of notes in May 2026 and stopped buybacks.[2]

Fatal-flaw scenario 2: the legal tail turns into cash

The New Mexico order is $25 billion plus 15 percent a year, the District of Columbia is seeking up to $1.7 billion, a federal court hearing on Meta's challenge to the Federal Trade Commission's effort to change the 2020 privacy order is set for 2026-11-12, and the 10-Q warns that youth-related trials are scheduled in the coming months.[2][1] The company has said it will appeal the New Mexico order.[13][15] The antitrust case brought by the Commission was decided in Meta's favor on 2025-11-18, but the Commission has appealed.[2] If appeals fail and several large cases settle at similar sizes, legal cash cost could reach tens of billions over two to three years.

Fatal-flaw scenario 3: ad price growth stalls while costs are fixed

Price per ad has risen 12, 12 and 14 percent in three quarters while the number of ads shown slowed from 19 percent to 10 percent.[9][10][1] If advertisers resist further price increases, growth could fall into the low teens, and with $454.52 billion of committed purchases, operating margin might fall from 35 percent toward the high 20s. That is a different business from the one the stock price assumes.

Next earnings date. Meta has not announced its fourth-quarter 2026 report date. Definitions and derived figures. Gross profit is revenue minus cost of revenue, because Meta does not report it. Free cash flow is our calculation: cash from operations minus equipment purchases minus finance-lease principal. It matches the company's reported $43.59 billion for 2025 and negative $2.12 billion for Q3 2026. ROIC uses an assumed 17 percent tax rate. Three secondary sources (TOPONE Markets, Investor's Business Daily and Openbook) are used only for price reaction and market comment. The commitments total is stated two ways. The body uses about $810 billion: $454.52 billion of non-cancelable commitments plus $355.73 billion of leases not yet started. Adding $14.72 billion of contingent cloud purchases gives $824.97 billion, the roughly $825 billion some outlets quote. The New Mexico loss accrual amount is not published in the 10-Q.
