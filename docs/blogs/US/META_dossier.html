1. What the company does

Meta runs four large social and messaging apps: Facebook, Instagram, Messenger and WhatsApp. The company groups them as the Family of Apps. A second unit, Reality Labs, sells virtual reality headsets and smart glasses. In the second quarter of 2026 the Family of Apps brought in $60.37 billion of the $60.80 billion total revenue, and advertising alone was $59.36 billion, or 97.6% of everything Meta earned.[1] Our arithmetic gives the 97.6% as 59,363 divided by 60,801.

Who pays? Businesses of every size, from a local shop to a global brand, pay Meta to put their ads in front of people. Meta's AI systems decide which ad to show to which person. Revenue is therefore the number of ads shown times the average price of each ad. In the second quarter, ad impressions (the count of ads delivered) grew 14% over a year earlier and the average price per ad grew 12%.[1] Those two growth rates combine to roughly the 27% rise in advertising revenue that the company reported.[1]

The audience is the raw material. Meta counts daily active people (DAP), meaning individuals who used at least one of its apps on a given day. DAP was 3.60 billion on average for June 2026, up 3% from a year earlier.[1] Average revenue per person (ARPP) is Family of Apps revenue divided by the average of DAP at the start and end of the quarter. It was $16.86 in the second quarter, up 24% year over year.[2] Growth now comes mostly from earning more per person, not from adding people.

Reality Labs is the long-term bet on new devices. It brought in $431 million of revenue in the second quarter and lost $4.62 billion at the operating line.[1] The loss for all of 2025 was $19.19 billion, and management says the 2026 loss will stay similar.[2]

The newest part of the story is spending. Meta is building data centers to train its own AI models, to run the ad-ranking systems, and to offer new AI products. Capital expenditure, meaning cash spent on buildings, servers and equipment, including finance leases, was $72.22 billion in 2025 and is guided to $130 billion to $145 billion in 2026.[4][1] On the July 2026 call, the CEO said, "We're getting a lot of offers for compute at a significant premium over what we paid for it," which hints at a possible new line of business.[24] Founder Mark Zuckerberg holds a majority of the voting power, so outside owners cannot overrule his spending choices.[9]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Family of Apps — Meta's advertising business: Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, WhatsApp and related services.
  • Daily active people (DAP) — the estimated number of distinct individuals who use at least one Meta app on a given day.
  • Average revenue per person (ARPP) — Family of Apps revenue divided by average DAP in the quarter; it shows how well Meta earns from each user.
  • Ad impressions and price per ad — the number of ads shown, and the average revenue earned from each one; together they give advertising revenue.
  • Capital expenditure — cash spent on long-lived assets such as data centers and servers; Meta includes principal paid on finance leases.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) — cash from operations minus capital expenditure; it is what is left for owners, debt holders and new ideas.
  • Share-based compensation (SBC (stock-based compensation)) — pay to employees in company shares; it is a real cost because it adds shares and shrinks each owner's slice.
  • Return on invested capital (ROIC) — after-tax operating profit divided by the money owners and lenders have put in.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A strong business earns more than it costs to run, keeps customers, and is hard to copy. Meta passes the first two tests easily and the third mostly. The question this chapter answers is whether the strengths are growing faster than the weaknesses. The evidence says the strengths are intact, but the weaknesses are growing quickly.

Where Meta is strong

Meta's first strength is scale that feeds itself. Advertisers go where the people are, and people stay where their friends are. With 3.60 billion daily users, the audience advertisers can reach is very large.[1] That scale produces data, and the data trains the AI that ranks ads. Better ranking raises the price advertisers will pay, which is what the 12% rise in price per ad in the second quarter suggests.[1]

The second strength is profit per sale. The Family of Apps made $102.47 billion of operating income in 2025 on $198.76 billion of revenue, a 51.6% margin.[4] Our arithmetic from the same release gives a company-wide gross margin, the share of revenue left after the direct cost of serving users, of 81.7% over the last twelve months. A business with margins like these can pay for a lot of mistakes.

The third strength is growth that is still accelerating. Revenue grew 22% in 2025 and 28% in the June 2026 quarter, with every region growing: in the second quarter, 32% in the United States and Canada, 24% in Europe, 19% in Asia-Pacific and 36% in the rest of the world.[4][1][2]

Where Meta is weak

The first weakness is cost. Total costs rose 55% in the second quarter against revenue growth of 28%. Research and development rose from $12.94 billion to $21.66 billion.[1] The operating margin, the share of revenue left after all running costs, fell from 43% to 31%. Two one-off items explain part of the drop: $2.40 billion of legal charges and $1.18 billion of severance.[1] Adding both back gives operating income of $22.36 billion, a 36.8% margin, which is still well below last year's 43%.

The second weakness is legal and regulatory risk, and it grew in the past six months. A New Mexico jury ordered $375 million in penalties in March 2026 over child safety claims.[21] In August a judge added a $567 million abatement fund and five years of required product changes.[23] On September 25, 2026 a second New Mexico jury found that Meta willfully violated the state's consumer protection law 43,899,720 times; at the legal maximum of $5,000 per violation the exposure is about $219.5 billion. That is a theoretical ceiling, not an expected payment, and a judge will now set the actual amount.[22] Meta itself warns that youth-related trials this year may result in a material loss.[1]

The third weakness is dependence. Substantially all revenue comes from ads on Facebook and Instagram.[2] The founder controls a majority of the votes, so a change of course depends on one person.[9] The fourth is financing. Long-term debt was zero at the end of 2021 and is $83.66 billion now, after bond sales of about $30 billion in November 2025 and $25 billion in May 2026, our sums of the tranches listed in the two filings.[12][1][14][13] Interest expense in the second quarter was $783 million, 225% above a year earlier.[2]

The fifth weakness sits partly off the balance sheet. Meta's maximum exposure to loss on its Louisiana data center venture, in which it holds a 20% membership interest, is $46.03 billion, it signed another $68 billion of data center leases that start in 2027 and 2028, and non-cancelable contractual commitments total $349.31 billion.[2] These are promises to pay whatever happens to ad revenue.

How Meta compares with its rivals

Alphabet grew revenue 24% in the same quarter, and its Google Cloud revenue rose 82%.[26] Microsoft grew 18%, with Azure and other cloud services up 43%.[27] Amazon grew 20%, with AWS up 37%.[28] Each of the three therefore earns fast-growing cloud revenue next to its other income. CNBC's report on Meta's results says that, unlike them, Meta doesn't have a thriving cloud-computing business.[24] Meta is growing faster than any of them, but its capital spending has no cloud revenue line beside it yet. Pinterest, a smaller ad platform, grew 18% and generated cash, but it is far smaller.[29]

What this means for an owner: Meta has the fastest growth in this group, an 81.7% gross margin and a 51.6% Family of Apps operating margin. Its company-wide operating margin of 30.9% in the quarter is below Alphabet's 34.0% and Microsoft's 45.1%. Per CNBC, it is also the only one of the three large spenders without a thriving cloud business. The next chapter turns that observation into five measured indicators.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means comparing what management said it would do with what it did. Guidance is the company's own published forecast. We checked each outlook given in the January 2025 and January 2026 earnings releases against later releases. The pattern is clear. The direction is reliable. The size keeps growing.

Take 2025 capital spending. In January 2025 the company guided to $60 billion to $65 billion.[8] It raised the range to $64-72 billion in April, $66-72 billion in July and $70-72 billion in October, and the final figure was $72.22 billion, 11% above the original top end.[7][6][5][4] Total expenses for 2025 started at $114-119 billion and landed at $117.69 billion, inside the range.[8][4] Management controlled the costs it could control. It under-forecast capital spending.

The same thing is happening in 2026. The capex range started at $115-135 billion in January, rose to $125-145 billion in April because of higher component prices, and was narrowed to $130-145 billion in July.[4][3][1] The midpoint moved from $125 billion to $137.5 billion, up 10% in six months. In October 2025 the company had also said that 2026 capital spending dollar growth would be notably larger than 2025, and that promise has been kept in full.[5]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks whether the company clears the hard floors in our rules file. It also asks which of three types it is. A COMPOUNDER has high quality, steady growth and a fair price. A TURNAROUND misses a standard today, but is cheap and improving. The third type is a stock in a confirmed uptrend with accelerating results. A breakout is a move of the price above a ceiling it failed to clear before, and the rules call this third type TACTICAL_BREAKOUT. Stage 2 means the confirmed uptrend phase on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 means the confirmed downtrend phase. A base is a flat trading range where a stock rests before its next move.[31] Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating profit divided by invested money. Free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operations minus capital spending. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is employee pay in shares. EBITDA, which stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, is profit before those costs. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop loss, which is the price at which the idea is wrong. A name fails if any hard floor fails.

4. What the price is doing

A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of trading days. The 20-day moving average, the 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are slow lines, and a rising stock tends to stay above them. The relative strength index (RSI) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been moving. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing. A base is a flat trading range where a stock rests before its next move. The pivot is the top of that base. A breakout must clear it on heavy volume. A retest is a pullback that checks the broken level from above.

Momentum and turnaround inflection gate

Our momentum gate has three tests, and passing any one of them lets a stock be allocated at market. Each test asks a different question about the trend.

Test A asks whether the stock is already in an established uptrend. The price must sit above a rising 50-day average, which itself sits above the 200-day average, and the 200-day line must not be falling. The stock must also have beaten the S&P 500 over 63 days, so that it is a leader and not a passenger. Finally it must be within 1.2 swings of support, which keeps buyers from chasing an extended price.

Test B asks whether a stock is only now leaving a quiet base. Here the 50-day average must be holding, relative strength must be improving and selling must have dried up. The price must be within 1.0 swing of the 63-day base low, which shows it is still hugging the base. Volume must be at least average, which shows buyers arriving.

Test C looks for a recovery floor in a beaten-down stock. It wants higher lows, a price at or above the 20-day average, support within 1.8 swings and an RSI of at least 35, so that selling has stopped.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in the last chapter rests on assumptions, and the weakest assumption in each is the one to watch. This chapter names each, says what would prove it wrong, then gives the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, which here is a case for owning the stock, not avoiding it.

Weakest assumption in the Reverse DCF

The calculation starts from twelve-month free cash flow of $37.87 billion. That figure still contains the lower-spending quarters of 2025. Meta's own guide implies spending runs near $40 billion to $47 billion a quarter in the second half, while cash from operations ran at $31.86 billion in the June quarter.[1] If the starting cash flow is lower, the implied growth is higher, and at or below zero the solver returns no answer. Evidence that this assumption is wrong: free cash flow below $5 billion in the September quarter. We will know on October 21, 2026.[15]

Weakest assumption in the price target

The $803.90 target uses 2027 consensus EPS of $33.92 and a 23.70 multiple taken from only three year-end snapshots in a secondary source.[16][18] Two things can break it. EPS estimates are falling, and the Yahoo series is labelled normalized and mixes GAAP quarters that include tax items. Also, the possible loss from the New Mexico case is not in any consensus figure that we could verify. At $500 per violation, 43,899,720 violations would be $21.95 billion, about 25% of twelve-month pre-tax income of $87.52 billion; at the $5,000 maximum it would be $219.5 billion, 12.1% of today's market value.[22] The judge has not yet set the amount, and Meta will appeal.

The red-team case: why this rejection could be wrong

The strongest case for owning Meta is this. Revenue is growing 28% with 31% operating margins even after $3.58 billion of one-off charges. Ad price and volume are both rising, and ARPP is up 24%.[1][2] The AI spending may be the best investment the company has made. The CEO said Meta is getting "a lot of offers for compute at a significant premium over what we paid for it."[24] Capacity bought at a price others will pay a premium for is not wasted. Cash from operations is $130.30 billion a year, and net cash is still positive. Heavy capital spending can also sit beside strong cash: Microsoft spent 39.8% of revenue on equipment and still kept a 21.8% free cash flow margin.[27] On pay in shares, the bull view concedes that Meta is an outlier. Its SBC is 12.6% of revenue in the quarter, against 6.6% at Alphabet, 3.5% at Microsoft and 3.0% at Amazon.[1][26][27][28] The argument is that the 5% rule is too blunt for a company with a 38% operating margin. On this view, rejecting a 28% grower at 23.9 times adjusted earnings (working in Chapter 5) costs the portfolio a leader.

Our answer is that the rule is deliberate and the cash evidence is current. Meta's pay in shares is about twice the highest of the three other large spenders (Alphabet, 6.6%). Only the much smaller Pinterest, at 27.5%, pays more. The price also offers no margin of safety against three risks that sit together: more capital spending, a large legal penalty, and a market in correction. The $219.5 billion figure is a ceiling set by the number of violations, not a forecast, and our 30% bear probability is a judgment. The case would change on evidence, not on argument.
