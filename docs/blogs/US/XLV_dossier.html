The State Street Health Care Select Sector SPDR ETF (XLV) launched on December 16, 1998, and listed on NYSE Arca on December 22, 1998 [1][4]. It was one of nine original sector funds carved out of the S&P 500 under The Select Sector SPDR Trust [1][4]. Before 1998, investors seeking U.S. healthcare exposure faced high-fee active mutual funds (0.75%–1.25% fees plus capital-gains payouts) or risky single-stock bets. State Street Global Advisors (SSGA Funds Management, Inc.) and Standard & Poor's solved this by slicing the S&P 500 along GICS sector lines [1][6].

Unlike all-cap healthcare funds, XLV tracks the Health Care Select Sector Index, drawn solely from S&P 500 healthcare stocks [1][4]. It spans six GICS industries: Pharmaceuticals, Biotechnology, Health Care Providers & Services, Equipment & Supplies, Life Sciences Tools, and Health Care Technology [1][4]. To meet IRS Subchapter M RIC rules, S&P Dow Jones Indices applies float-adjusted market-cap weighting with quarterly 25/50 capping [6]. At each quarterly rebalance (March, June, September, December), no single stock can exceed 25% (buffered at 23%) [6]. Also, the sum of all stocks above 5% cannot exceed 50% (buffered around 45%–48%) [6].

Over nearly 28 years, XLV has scaled into the top liquidity vehicle for U.S. healthcare equities. It reached $46.57 billion in AUM ($46,568.92M) across 262.87 million shares outstanding on November 20, 2026, supported by +$1.02 billion in trailing 1-year net inflows [1][10]. That scale let SSGA cut XLV's gross expense ratio from 0.28% down through 0.14% (2017), 0.13%, 0.12%, 0.10%, 0.09%, and now 0.08% [1][5][10]. That matches the lowest fee among broad U.S. healthcare ETFs [10]. Annual turnover stays just 2.00% because changes occur mainly on S&P 500 additions, removals, or quarterly RIC trims [6][8].

Why S&P 500 Membership Is XLV's Hidden Filter

XLV holds only healthcare companies that pass the S&P 500 Committee's profitability, liquidity, and market-cap gates. This rule excludes pre-revenue, cash-burning clinical-stage biotechs. As a result, XLV compounds far more steadily than equal-weighted biotech funds.

To see why XLV differs from equal-weighted or active funds, examine S&P Dow Jones Indices' quarterly rebalance rules [6]. Each constituent must first pass the S&P 500 Committee's eligibility gates: U.S. domicile, minimum market cap, public float, and four straight quarters of positive GAAP earnings [6]. Once admitted to the S&P 500, any GICS Health Care company enters XLV's index automatically.

Index weights use float-adjusted market cap, excluding closely held insider or government stakes [6]. On the second Friday of March, June, September, and December, S&P DJI checks the 25/50 RIC caps using Wednesday reference prices [6]. If a leader like Eli Lilly nears the 23% cap or the >5% group nears 48%, excess weight shifts proportionally to smaller stocks. Because rebalances are quarterly and index churn is low, XLV's annual turnover stays at 2.00%, avoiding market-impact drag [1][8].

Institutional desks treat XLV as the standard U.S. healthcare benchmark [1][6]. Its 61-stock S&P 500 basket captures ~85% of investable U.S. healthcare market cap while skipping illiquid micro-caps [1][6]. Over 10 years ended October 31, 2026, XLV compounded NAV at 12.48% annualized (vs. 12.62% index) with a 5-Star Morningstar rating [1][8]. During the 2022 rate shock (-2.1% vs. -5.6% for VHT) and March 2020 trough (~-29%), XLV's quality screen and penny-wide spreads let institutions rebalance cleanly [1][11].

This structural design also explains XLV's durability across shifting rate and policy cycles. When interest rates rose sharply in 2022, speculative biotech firms struggled to raise equity capital. By contrast, XLV's 61 profitable holdings funded clinical trials and plant expansion out of internal operating cash flow. Over nearly three decades, the index has rotated smoothly from legacy small-molecule drugs toward biologics, medical devices, life sciences tools, and GLP-1 therapies. Investors gain exposure to the winning healthcare franchises of each decade without paying active manager fees or guessing which sub-industry will lead next.

Why Sub-Industry Balance Matters

When hospital surgical volumes surge, Providers (UNH) face higher MCR claims costs, but Equipment makers (ISRG, ABT, SYK) and Pharma capture higher procedure and prescription volumes. XLV's multi-vertical structure creates natural internal hedging across the healthcare dollar.

While XLV holds exclusively U.S.-listed S&P 500 companies, its look-through revenue base is deeply global. Mega-cap pharmaceutical and MedTech constituents such as Eli Lilly, Johnson & Johnson, Merck, and Intuitive Surgical generate roughly 35% to 48% of their revenue outside the United States across Europe, Japan, and emerging markets [16][20][22][24]. By contrast, Health Care Providers & Services (16.12% of XLV) are overwhelmingly domestic, tied to U.S. commercial employer plans, Medicare Advantage, and Medicaid reimbursement [1][19]. This split provides natural currency and regulatory diversification: U.S. domestic managed-care margin pressure does not impair international medical-device or specialty-pharma demand.

The smallest GICS slice inside XLV—Health Care Technology at 0.61%—comprises healthcare IT, clinical workflow software, and electronic health record infrastructure [1]. Though minor in weight today, software-enabled revenue-cycle management and AI-assisted clinical documentation increasingly interface with the Providers and Life Sciences verticals, improving prior-authorization turnaround times and clinical-trial patient recruitment across XLV's larger constituents [19][23].

A critical nuance inside XLV's constituent revenue engines is the divergence between list prices and realized net prices. In Pharmaceuticals (37.15% weight) and Biotechnology (20.21% weight), manufacturers negotiate confidential rebates with pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) such as OptumRx, CVS Caremark, and Express Scripts [1][17][19]. Eli Lilly's FY2025 results illustrate the trade-off: realized U.S. prices declined 6% (and 9% in Q2 2026), yet 50%–60% volume growth drove revenue up 43% to $65.18B and lifted non-GAAP gross margins to 86.3% [16][17]. Conversely, legacy franchises facing biosimilar entry—such as Johnson & Johnson's Stelara—experienced -3.1% net pricing headwinds that trimmed consolidated gross margin by 120 bps to 67.9% [22].

Downstream Wholesaler Concentration (Blind-Spot Audit): Beyond PBM rebates, XLV's pharmaceutical and biotech constituents (57.36% of fund assets) rely heavily on an oligopoly of three U.S. wholesale distributors—McKesson (1.94% of XLV), Cencora, and Cardinal Health. Eli Lilly's FY2025 Form 10-K confirms that each of these three distributors accounted for a significant percentage of consolidated revenue across 2023–2025 [16]. Because XLV holds both the drugmakers and the wholesalers inside the same sector basket, wholesaler fee-for-service renegotiations, inventory destocking, or distributor credit/regulatory disruptions transmit directly across multiple sub-industries.

340B Drug Discount Drag (Blind-Spot Audit): Drug makers in XLV (57.36% weight) also face growing discounts under the federal 340B program. HRSA data from July 2026 show discounted 340B hospital drug purchases jumped 23% YoY to $100.0 billion in CY2025, up from $81.4 billion in 2024 and $53.7 billion in 2022 [40]. At list prices, those drugs topped $180 billion, creating about $79.5 billion in steep discounts [40]. Twenty-one states now bar drug makers from capping contract-pharmacy sales, and HRSA set a new 340B rebate pilot to start January 1, 2027 [40]. This steady cash-flow leak sits right next to commercial PBM rebates and trims real pharma margins.
