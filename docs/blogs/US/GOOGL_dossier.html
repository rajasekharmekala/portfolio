1. What the company does

Alphabet is the holding company for Google. Almost all of its $402.8 billion of 2025 revenue came from three kinds of customers: advertisers, businesses that rent computing power, and people who pay for subscriptions, apps and devices.[1] Advertisers paid $224.5 billion for ads beside search results, $40.4 billion on YouTube and $29.8 billion on partner websites through the Google Network.[1] Businesses paid Google Cloud $58.7 billion, and consumers and device buyers added $48.0 billion through subscriptions, platforms and devices.[1]

In the second quarter of 2026 revenue was $119.8 billion, up 24 percent from a year earlier.[4] Google Services, the advertising and subscription engine, earned $39.5 billion of operating income on $94.5 billion of revenue, a margin of 41.8 percent.[4][6] Google Cloud earned $8.8 billion on $24.8 billion of revenue, which was 82 percent more than a year earlier.[4][6] Search sells ads against questions typed or spoken into Google, and it pays partners, such as phone makers, for sending those questions its way. Those payments, called traffic acquisition costs, were $16.2 billion in the quarter.[6]

Google Cloud earns usage fees and subscriptions for computing power, data tools, workplace software and AI services. Since the second quarter of 2026 it also books revenue from selling TPU systems, which are Alphabet's own AI chips, delivered to customer data centers.[6] Two other pieces are small in revenue and large in cost. Other Bets, which includes the Waymo driverless ride service, had revenue of $382 million and an operating loss of $1.8 billion in the quarter. Shared AI research that no segment pays for cost another $5.8 billion.[4][6]

AI is now both the product and the cost. The Gemini app has 950 million monthly users, AI Mode in Search has passed one billion monthly users, and the company's model interfaces process about 22 billion tokens a minute.[6] The cost is capital expenditure, which means spending on data centers, chips and equipment. It was $44.9 billion in the second quarter alone.[4]

Alphabet has three classes of shares with identical economic rights and different votes. Class B carries ten votes per share, Class A one vote and Class C none.[1] Larry Page and Sergey Brin held about 89.3 percent of the Class B shares at the end of 2025, which was about 52.7 percent of all votes.[1] This book prices Class A, ticker GOOGL, and compares it with Class C, ticker GOOG, which you already own.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Search is still the center of the business, and the numbers do not show the damage that AI answers were feared to cause. Search and other revenue grew 17 percent in the second quarter to $63.3 billion, and the company says AI Mode, the AI-answer part of Search, has passed one billion monthly users.[6] Google's share of worldwide search was 89.94 percent in September 2026, against 5.29 percent for Bing.[22] The share figure is a count of page views by one tracking firm, so it says nothing about revenue, and it cannot show how a rival's next AI product will change habits. YouTube advertising grew 13 percent to $11.1 billion, and Google Network revenue fell 1 percent to $7.3 billion. Growth is therefore concentrated in Cloud, which added $11.1 billion of quarterly revenue, and Search, which added $9.1 billion; subscriptions added $1.7 billion (our calculation).[6][4]

Google Cloud is the second engine, and its figures changed the story this year. Cloud revenue rose 82 percent in the second quarter and its operating margin, the share of revenue left after operating costs, rose from 20.7 percent to 35.6 percent.[6] The signed backlog, meaning customer contracts not yet turned into revenue, reached $519.5 billion on 30 June 2026, of which $513.9 billion is Google Cloud, against $242.8 billion of total backlog at the end of 2025.[4][1] The company expects to record just over half of it as revenue within 24 months.[4] Backlog is a promise to buy, not cash received, and the company says it is short of capacity: it plans to rent third-party capacity in the third quarter, which it expects to cause modest margin pressure.[6] It also expects to recognize only a relatively small part of its TPU system sales this year, with most of that revenue in 2027.[6]

The weakness is the cost of that growth. Capital spending was $44.9 billion in the second quarter. The company now forecasts $195 billion to $205 billion for 2026, up from $180 billion to $190 billion three months earlier. It expects 2027 spending to increase significantly.[4][6] It also holds $707.0 billion of future purchase commitments, the significant majority of them long-term supply agreements that secure production capacity for its infrastructure.[4] Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capital spending. It was negative $5.9 billion in the quarter and $53.3 billion over the trailing twelve months, and management says it will stay under pressure.[6][5] To pay for the build-out Alphabet raised $56.2 billion of debt, $30.5 billion of common stock and $19.1 billion of mandatory convertible preferred stock, all net of costs, in the first half of 2026. Long-term debt rose from $46.5 billion to $98.2 billion.[4][1] The shares fell 7.1 percent on 23 July, the day after the report.[19]

AI competition is real, and Alphabet says so itself. Its annual report calls AI technology and services highly competitive, rapidly evolving and dependent on large investment.[1] CNBC quoted one analyst saying that continued delays to the Gemini 3.5 Pro model and a lack of standout product releases raised doubts about whether the spending was yet producing a clear lead.[19] Another CNBC report said Alphabet was no longer getting the benefit of the doubt on spending.[20] The owner's question is therefore not whether AI is a threat, since the Search and Cloud numbers say demand is strong, but whether Alphabet earns enough on each dollar it spends to keep up with rivals that are spending too.

Regulation is the third pressure. A US court entered a final judgment in the Search case in December 2025. It restricts how Google distributes its services and requires it to share some search data with rivals. Google appealed in January 2026, and the Justice Department appealed in February.[4] In the advertising technology case, the court's remedies opinion declined to order a sale of the AdX exchange. It was unsealed on 16 September 2026, and it ordered behavioral changes instead.[21] In Europe the Android fine became final in July 2026, and Alphabet paid $5.2 billion. A separate EU advertising technology fine of 3.0 billion euros is under appeal. A Swedish court awarded about $2.1 billion in damages to PriceRunner, a Klarna subsidiary.[4] None of these has broken the business. They add costs and can limit how Search is distributed.

Class A versus Class C: what the extra vote is worth

The two share classes are claims on the same business. The charter says the rights of each class are identical except for voting. It bars the board from paying unequal dividends per share, and the second-quarter 10-Q reports a $0.22 dividend on each class.[1][4] Full-year 2025 diluted earnings per share were $10.82 for Class A and $10.80 for Class C, two cents apart. The company says immaterial differences can arise from how earnings are allocated across the classes.[1][4] A Class A share carries one vote, and a Class C share has none. Class B shares have ten votes each. Class A and Class B vote together as a single class, and Class C holders have no vote except where the law requires one.[13][15] On the 15 July 2026 share counts, Class A holders as a group had 5,868 million votes against 8,350 million for Class B. That makes Class A about 41 percent of the vote (our calculation). A 24-share position would carry about 24 of roughly 14.2 billion votes.[4]

The price difference is small and moves around. GOOGL closed at $320.21 and GOOG at $317.28 on 23 October 2026, a gap of $2.93, or 0.92 percent (our calculation).[16] In the public offering of 4 June 2026 Class A sold at $355.1982 and Class C at $351.8018, a gap of 0.97 percent, and Berkshire Hathaway's affiliate paid about $351.81 for Class A and $348.20 for Class C in the private placement, a gap of 1.04 percent (our calculation).[4][14] No filing we read explains why Class A trades higher. The only difference the filings state is the vote, so the vote is the only thing a buyer of Class A receives for the extra $2.93; our reading is that the market charges about one percent for it, but that is an inference and not a cited fact. Management treats the gap as a real variable: the annual report says Alphabet repurchases both classes while weighing their relative trading prices and volumes.[1]

For your portfolio the two classes are a duplicate, not a substitute. A substitute would bring different risk, such as a different business or sector. These shares bring none of that. Over the last month GOOGL fell 6.47 percent and GOOG fell 6.41 percent. Over the last day GOOGL fell 1.24 percent and GOOG 1.08 percent. A GOOGL purchase would add the same Alphabet risk on top of the 24.8076 GOOG shares you hold. The fund GGLL, which you also hold, tracks the Class A shares, so some of your Class A price exposure already exists through the fund.[23] If you ever wanted Alphabet in Class A form, GOOG is about 0.9 percent cheaper for the same earnings and dividends. The vote is too small to matter at this size. That is why we see no reason to buy GOOGL as an addition or a swap.

Alphabet is a high-quality franchise that has moved from a cash-rich business to a heavy investor. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk asks whether management did what it said it would do. On capital spending it said less than it did. In February 2025 it planned about $75 billion for the year, in October 2025 it raised that to $91 billion to $93 billion from $85 billion, and actual 2025 spending was $91.4 billion, which is 22 percent above the first plan (our calculation).[10][9][1] For 2026 the plan went from $175 billion to $185 billion in February, to $180 billion to $190 billion in April, where part of the rise paid for the Intersect acquisition, and to $195 billion to $205 billion in July.[8][7][6] With $80.6 billion spent in the first half, the July range implies $114 billion to $124 billion in the second half (our calculation).[4]

On capital returns, a buyback is a program in which a company repurchases its own shares, and the board authorized a $70 billion buyback in April 2025; Alphabet bought back $45.7 billion of stock in 2025, and it bought nothing in the first half of 2026 while $69.5 billion of the authorization remained.[1][4] In the same half-year it raised $49.6 billion of new equity in June, including $10 billion from an affiliate of Berkshire Hathaway.[4][14] The dividend was raised 5 percent to $0.22 a quarter in April 2026 and declared again at that level in July.[4] Management therefore kept the dividend, kept the buyback authorization, and paused the buyback itself.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter sets out five years of audited figures and then tests them against our Layer 1 rules. Revenue grew from $257.6 billion in 2021 to $402.8 billion in 2025, a compound rate of 11.8 percent a year, and growth has varied. It slowed to 9.8 percent in 2022 and 8.7 percent in 2023, then accelerated to 13.9 percent in 2024 and 15.1 percent in 2025 (our calculation).[3][2][1] In the second quarter of 2026 it reached 24 percent.[4] Operating income, the profit from running the business before investment gains and taxes, rose from $78.7 billion to $129.0 billion over the same years, and its margin moved from 30.6 percent to 32.0 percent, with a dip to 26.5 percent in 2022. Gross margin, which is the share of revenue left after direct costs, rose from 56.9 percent to 59.7 percent. Net margin, which is the share of revenue left as net profit, was 32.8 percent in 2025.[3][1]

The figure that needs care is net income. Net income for the twelve months to 30 June 2026 was $244.2 billion on $147.6 billion of operating income, because the first half of 2026 included $135.8 billion of gains on investments, mainly a $99.0 billion second-quarter gain on equity stakes that the company ties to SpaceX and one private company.[4][1] Those gains are real accounting income but not cash from customers, and they will not repeat on a schedule. The reported earnings per share of $19.93 and the price-to-earnings ratio, which is the share price divided by those earnings, of 16.07 that data vendors show both include them, so we use operating income to judge the business and our own core earnings per share, built in the estimate below, to judge the price.[16]

The second figure that needs care is free cash flow. The market-data snapshot behind this report lists trailing free cash flow of $22.7 billion, but Alphabet's own reconciliation gives $53.3 billion: operating cash flow of $185.7 billion less capital spending of $132.4 billion.[5] We use the company's figure, which gives a margin of 11.95 percent of revenue (our calculation). Capital spending was $24.6 billion in 2021 and is running at $132.4 billion a year now, which is 5.2 times the $25.2 billion depreciation charge, so the cash margin has fallen from 26.0 percent to 11.95 percent while the profit margin held up.[3][4][1] Depreciation, the yearly accounting charge for equipment as it ages, will rise as the new equipment goes into use, and management says so.[6]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is weaker than the rest. The bear case, as strongly as it can be made, is this. Alphabet has turned from a company that returned cash into one that borrows and sells shares to build data centers. It sold $30.5 billion of common stock in June at $355.1982 for Class A and $351.8018 for Class C. An affiliate of Berkshire Hathaway paid about $351.81 and $348.20. In that world the price is valued on a lower multiple of lower earnings. The bear case of $254 is then not the floor.

Fatal flaw 1: spending outruns returns

The first way the thesis breaks is that capital spending keeps rising faster than operating cash flow. Free cash flow then stays negative into 2027 and 2028, while depreciation squeezes the operating margin. The company forecasts $195 billion to $205 billion for 2026 and significantly more for 2027. It plans to rent outside capacity at a modest cost to margin.[6] Three signs would show this happening: a Cloud margin below 25 percent, which is our WATCH line; a further raise of the 2026 range; or a 2027 plan far above $205 billion. Fatal flaw 2: a legal remedy changes how Search is distributed

The second way is a court or regulator that limits how Google reaches users. The Search judgment already restricts distribution agreements and requires data sharing. The third way is that AI answers from rivals pull queries and advertisers away faster than Google's own AI Mode adds them. Google's share of worldwide search was 89.94 percent in September 2026, and Search revenue grew 17 percent. There is no sign of this yet.[22][6] The warning signs in the news are delays to the Gemini 3.5 Pro model and doubts about whether the spending is producing a clear lead.[19] The evidence would be Search revenue growth below 10 percent, which is our WATCH line, or a clear drop in Google's share of search. Layer 3 is the third of our underwriting layers. It sets the entry timing, the size and the exit rules. The current market price (CMP) is the last close, $320.21. A moving average is the average of recent closing prices. A simple moving average (SMA) weights every day equally. An exponential moving average (EMA) weights recent days more. A structural invalidation level is the price at which the reason for owning the shares no longer holds. It is placed just under a support level. A stop loss is the order that sells there. Portfolio heat is the total amount of net worth you would lose if every stop were hit together. The risk-unit rule limits it by sizing each position so that hitting its stop costs a fixed number of basis points. One basis point (bps) is one hundredth of one percent of net worth.

The tiers set both the ceiling and the risk budget. TIER_1 allows 7 to 10 percent of net worth and a loss at the stop of 100 bps. TIER_2 allows 4 to 6.5 percent and 75 bps. TIER_3 allows 2 to 3.5 percent and 50 bps. TIER_4 allows 1 to 2 percent and 35 bps. TIER_0 allows nothing.
