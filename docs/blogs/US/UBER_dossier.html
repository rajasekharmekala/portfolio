The business passes our quality tests, but the price is in a confirmed Stage 4 downtrend and the recovery test fails. 1. What the company does

Uber is a matching service. A rider opens the app and a nearby driver who owns a car takes the trip. A hungry customer orders from a restaurant, grocer or store and a courier delivers it. A shipper posts a load and a trucking carrier hauls it.[1] The people who pay are the riders, eaters and shippers, and the total they spend is called Gross Bookings. In 2025 that was $193,454 million across 13,567 million trips, with 202 million monthly active platform consumers (MAPCs) in the fourth quarter.[1] Gross Bookings is the first number to watch, because it measures how much business flows through the marketplace before Uber takes its share.

Uber keeps only a part of each dollar. Revenue as a share of Gross Bookings is the take rate, and it stayed near 27% from 2022 to 2025 (27.6%, 27.0%, 27.0% and 26.9%, our calculation from cited revenue and Gross Bookings).[3][1] In the second quarter of 2026 it fell to 24.5%, but the cause is accounting and not price. Mobility business-model changes in the United Kingdom cut reported revenue by $1.1 billion and total revenue growth by 8 percentage points, while Gross Bookings grew 24%.[6][4] When Uber books a trip as an agent, only its fee counts as revenue; when it acts as the principal, the whole fare counts. A falling take rate from a change like this does not mean Uber is earning less per trip, and the right yardstick is profit as a share of Gross Bookings, which rose to 4.9% in that quarter.

Three other levers sit on top of the core matching business. Advertising sells sponsored listings to merchants and brands, and the Uber One membership had 46 million members at the end of 2025.[1] The company also says cross-platform users, who use both rides and delivery, generate roughly 3 times the Gross Bookings and profits of single-product users.[14] The last lever is insurance. Uber carries large insurance reserves for accidents on its platform. It keeps $8,874 million in restricted investments held in trust accounts under contracts with insurance providers, which are not available for day-to-day use. At the end of 2025 those reserves were $12,463 million ($3,387 million short-term plus $9,076 million long-term).[1] The yearly build in those reserves is counted as operating cash flow, which matters for how much of the headline cash flow an owner can really spend.

Uber's strategy is now to add more places and more services to the same app. On 2026-07-16 it agreed to offer €41.50 per share in cash for all of Delivery Hero, an equity value of $14.8 billion ($13.7 billion after counting stakes Uber already bought), with completion expected in the second half of 2027.[14][13] In February 2026 it also agreed to buy Getir's food delivery business in Turkiye for $335 million in cash.[12] It says it is partnering with more than 25 companies on autonomous vehicles across rides, delivery and freight.[18] Both moves widen the business, and both carry risks that Chapters 2, 5 and 6 weigh.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Gross Bookings — the total dollar value of rides, delivery orders and freight loads on the platform, including taxes, tolls and fees, before discounts and driver or merchant earnings are taken out.
  • Monthly active platform consumers (MAPCs) — the number of unique people who took a ride or received a delivery in a month, averaged over the quarter.
  • Adjusted EBITDA — profit before interest, tax, depreciation, stock pay and certain legal and restructuring items; in the second quarter of 2026 the company said it is no longer a key measure it uses.
  • Non-GAAP operating income — the company's newer headline profit measure: operating income without acquired-intangible amortization, certain legal items and restructuring.
  • Insurance reserves — money Uber sets aside for accident claims on its platform; building the reserve adds to operating cash flow before the claims are paid.
  • Reverse DCF — a method that starts from today's price and solves for the cash-flow growth rate the price assumes over ten years.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Uber's strength is that both sides of the market come to it. More riders attract more drivers, and more drivers shorten the wait for riders, so each new user makes the service better for the next. The company reports record consumer numbers: MAPCs grew 16% to 208 million in the second quarter of 2026, and Trips grew 18% to 3,867 million.[6] Scale also shows in profit. Segment adjusted EBITDA in Mobility rose 22% to $7,899 million in 2025 and in Delivery rose 45% to $3,572 million.[1] A smaller rival has to pay for drivers and customers in each city, and Uber can spread those costs over far more trips.

The second strength is the cross-platform habit. The company says cross-platform users generate roughly 3 times the Gross Bookings and profits of single-product users, and that the Delivery Hero deal would lift the number of markets with both rides and delivery from 34 to 58.[14] This is a company claim and we have not tested it, but it fits the pattern in the numbers: Gross Bookings grew 19.5%, 18.1% and 18.8% in 2023, 2024 and 2025 (our calculation), and free cash flow rose from $390 million in 2022 to $9,763 million in 2025.[3][1] The proxy statement also shows pay tied to Adjusted EBITDA less stock-based compensation, which means management is rewarded for profit after the cost of stock pay.[18]

The weak points are easy to name, and each has a source. First, autonomous vehicles. Uber names Waymo (Alphabet), Zoox (Amazon) and Tesla as companies building autonomous cars that may compete with it, and it admits that partners could expire or pull their cars from the platform.[1] Second, driver status. More than 150,000 U.S. drivers with arbitration agreements have filed or said they will file claims that they should be treated as employees; the company has settled most of them individually.[1] Third, insurance. The company says inflation has raised and is expected to keep raising its insurance costs, and its cost of insurance is higher in the United States and Canada than elsewhere.[1] Fourth, concentration: 15% of 2025 Mobility Gross Bookings came from airport trips, which are exposed to airport rules and travel swings.[1]

Competition is real in each segment. The filing names Bolt, Didi, Lyft and Ola in rides, and DoorDash, Instacart, Gopuff and Delivery Hero among others in delivery.[1] The peer numbers in Chapter 3 show that DoorDash grew revenue faster in 2025 (28% against Uber's 18%) but earned a smaller operating margin, and Lyft is a fraction of Uber's size. The moat is wide but not permanent, because the same network effects that protect Uber could be rebuilt around robotaxi fleets owned by someone else. What this means for an owner: the business is strong today and the cash proves it, but the main long-run risk is a change in who owns the car, and that risk cannot be priced from filings alone. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management gave three-year targets at its investor update on 2024-02-06 and a $7 billion buyback plan the same day.[11][10] We compare each promise with what the filings show. The record is good: the company has met or beaten the targets in most years, and it has beaten its own quarterly ranges on Gross Bookings in both quarters we checked. The two caveats are that the profit-growth rate in 2026 has slowed to 32.9% in the first half, below the high-30s target band, and that management dropped Adjusted EBITDA as a key measure in the second quarter of 2026 while the targets were set in that measure.

The buyback promise is the one that took longest. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Uber went from heavy losses to large profits in four years. Revenue rose from $17,455 million in 2021 to $52,017 million in 2025, a three-year compound growth rate of 17.7% from 2022 (our calculation).[3][1] GAAP operating income, which is profit after all running costs under U.S. accounting rules, moved from a loss of $3,834 million to a profit of $5,565 million, and free cash flow, which is operating cash flow less capital spending, went from an outflow of $743 million to $9,763 million.[3][1] The table below shows each year, and one caution applies to the net income line: it is not a clean measure of earnings.

Net income was $9,856 million in 2024 and $10,053 million in 2025, but those years include one-time tax gains. Stock pay of $1,826 million is the largest piece, and the rest is depreciation and the legal, restructuring and acquisition items the company adds back. The decision is REJECTED, and the rule that triggers it is the technical routing rule in our scoring file: a close below a falling 50-day average that sits below a falling 200-day average, with the turnaround test failing. In either case we rerun the full underwriting, because a buy needs a new analysis and not a trigger alone.

A few terms set the size. Portfolio heat is the share of net worth that would be lost if every position hit its stop at once, and our ceiling is 12.0%. Risk-unit sizing sets a position's weight from the loss we accept: weight = risk budget in basis points divided by the distance to the stop, capped by the tier. A basis point (bp) is one hundredth of a percent, so a 50 bp budget means we accept losing 0.50% of net worth if the stop is hit. Tiers rank conviction: TIER_1 allows 7% to 10% of net worth, TIER_2 4% to 6.5%, TIER_3 2% to 3.5%, TIER_4 1% to 2%, and TIER_0 is zero size. Uber is TIER_0 today. If it ever qualified, TIER_3 would be the yardstick because an event risk (the bid) is open, with a 50 bp budget. CMP is the current market price, $66.70.

A moving average smooths the price. A simple moving average (SMA) weights every day equally, and an exponential moving average (EMA) weights recent days more; we use the 50-day SMA and the 21-day EMA to trail a stop under a winner. Structural invalidation is the price at which the reason to own the stock no longer holds. Our rule for a core holding sets the stop one average true range below the 63-day base low: $66.43 minus $1.87 gives $64.56. The let-winners-run rule means we never sell a rising stock with intact fundamentals just because its relative strength index is above 70 or its weight passed 15%; we allow up to 22% while the stop trails the 21-day EMA or the 50-day SMA. It does not apply here, because we hold no shares.

Layer 3 starts with the support level. We use the 63-day base low of $66.43, which is also the 20-day swing low, and the ledger puts the close 0.14 average true ranges above it. On the support-distance ladder in our scoring file that is the FULL_SIZE band (within 0.6 average true ranges), which would normally mean an AT_SUPPORT posture and full risk-adjusted tier size. The stop uses the same level, one average true range lower, so it does not rely on a different support. The Stage 4 red line overrides the ladder here.

The entry zone is where we would place a limit order if the chart blocked nothing. Its top is $67.09, which is 1% above the 63-day low and is the level at which the higher-low part of Test C turns to pass. Its bottom is $66.43, the 63-day low and the nearest support. Reward-to-risk, or asymmetry, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop.
