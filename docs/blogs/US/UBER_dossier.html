1. History & Corporate Evolution

Uber was founded in March 2009 by Travis Kalanick and Garrett Camp as UberCab in San Francisco, launching its first smartphone-dispatched black luxury sedan service in May 2010[8]. In 2012, Uber introduced UberX, enabling peer-to-peer ridesharing with non-professional drivers operating their personal vehicles, initiating hyper-aggressive global expansion backed by venture capital from Benchmark, Google Ventures, and SoftBank[9].

In 2014, the company launched Uber Eats in Santa Monica, expanding its network matching algorithms from passenger transportation into restaurant food delivery. In August 2017, following corporate governance crises, Dara Khosrowshahi was appointed Chief Executive Officer, instituting financial discipline, selling off dilutive international operations (Uber China to Didi, Southeast Asia to Grab, Russia to Yandex) in exchange for equity stakes, and steering the company through its May 2019 initial public offering on the NYSE[10].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Khosrowshahi divested capital-intensive non-core initiatives—selling the Advanced Technologies Group (ATG) autonomous driving unit to Aurora Innovation and the Elevate flying taxi division to Joby Aviation—while acquiring Postmates ($2.65B) and Drizly ($1.1B) to dominate US food and alcohol delivery[11]. This structural rationalization enabled Uber's delivery segment to offset mobility declines, culminating in historic full-year GAAP operating profitability in 2023, inclusion in the S&P 500 Index in December 2023, and initiation of a $7.0 billion share repurchase authorization in February 2024[12].

2.1 The Multi-Product Membership Flywheel (Uber One)

The core engine of customer lifetime value (LTV) expansion is the Uber One subscription membership ($9.99/month or $96/year). Uber One surpassed 50 million active subscribers in 2025. Members spend 3.4x more on the platform than non-members and demonstrate a 50% lower churn rate. Crucially, multi-product consumers (using both Mobility and Delivery) generate 3x the gross bookings of single-product users, while platform acquisition costs (CAC) for cross-sold products are near zero[14].

3. Sector-Specific Dynamics & Autonomous Vehicle Thesis

The rise of autonomous vehicle (AV) commercialization represents both the greatest perceived threat and the single largest financial catalyst for Uber. While market commentators initially feared that OEM robotaxis (such as Tesla Cybercab or Alphabet Waymo) would disintermediate third-party rideshare networks, industry unit economics demonstrate the opposite: fleet utilization is paramount[15].

An autonomous vehicle costs $80,000–$150,000 in sensor hardware and depreciation. Operating a standalone consumer dispatch app requires spending billions on customer acquisition, multi-city customer support, payment handling, and local safety compliance. By integrating with Uber, AV operators achieve instant access to 175 million active riders, maximizing daily vehicle utilization rates from 30% to over 80%. Uber's landmark partnerships with Waymo (in Phoenix, Austin, and Atlanta) and Cruise prove that AV developers view Uber as their essential distribution network[16].
