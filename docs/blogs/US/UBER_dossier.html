Before you read

Uber runs the world's largest app for rides and food delivery. It booked $58.0 billion in the second quarter of 2026 and produced more than $10 billion of free cash flow over the last twelve months[1]. We reject the stock for new money at $70.35, because the chart is still building a floor and the possible gain is only 2.08 times the possible loss, against a required 3.0[32][34]. We hold 20 shares and five long-dated call options, so the advice is to keep the shares, add nothing, and sell the shares on a close below $65.34[33]. This verdict would prove too cautious if robotaxi owners fail to win riders away from Uber and the $14.8 billion Delivery Hero deal is financed without strain[11], and we learn most on November 4, when third-quarter results arrive[20].

1. What the company does

Uber runs an app that matches people who want something moved with people who will move it. A rider asks for a car, an eater asks for dinner, a shipper asks for a truck, and Uber finds a driver, courier or carrier nearby. It does this in more than 15,000 cities[5]. Monthly active platform consumers (MAPCs) are the unique people who ride or order at least once in a month, averaged over the quarter. There were 208 million of them in the second quarter of 2026, up 16%, and they took 3.9 billion trips, which are rides plus delivery orders[1].

Uber reports three segments. Mobility is rides, Delivery is food, groceries and other local orders, and Freight connects shippers with trucking carriers. The size of the platform is measured by Gross Bookings, the total dollars customers pay for rides, orders and freight, including taxes and fees, before drivers and merchants are paid. Gross Bookings were $58.0 billion in the second quarter, up 24% as reported and 22% after removing currency swings[1]. Uber keeps only part of that money. Revenue was $14.2 billion, so the take rate, which is the share of each booking that Uber keeps as revenue, was 24.5% on our arithmetic, against 27.1% a year earlier. Most of that fall is an accounting change, not lost business. From January 2, 2026, Uber stopped being the responsible party for rides in certain UK markets, so payments to drivers there now reduce revenue instead of appearing as a cost. That cut reported revenue by $1.1 billion in the quarter[2].

The people who pay Uber are not only riders. Riders and eaters pay the fare. Drivers and couriers hand back a service fee out of that fare. Restaurants and stores pay a commission, and many also buy advertising, which added $182 million of delivery revenue year on year in the second quarter[2]. Uber One is a paid membership that covers rides and delivery. It had 46 million members at the end of 2025, passed 50 million in the first quarter of 2026, and members now drive about half of Gross Bookings[5][3]. Customers who use both rides and delivery generate more than three times the bookings of customers who use one[5]. That cross-use is the reason Uber wants to buy more delivery businesses.

Profit comes mostly from rides. In the second quarter Mobility earned $2,215 million of segment operating income, Delivery earned $1,055 million and Freight lost $24 million. Corporate costs and platform research cost $1,103 million, which leaves non-GAAP operating income of $2,143 million, or 3.7% of Gross Bookings[1]. Two things changed in 2026. First, Uber started spending on deals: about $4.0 billion in the second quarter, mostly on Delivery Hero shares[26], then an agreed $14.8 billion offer for all of Delivery Hero[11], a $2.3 billion agreement for the US catering platform ezCater[21], and smaller purchases of Getir ($465 million) and Blacklane ($550 million)[2]. Second, management said it expects to commit more than $10 billion over several years to autonomous vehicles[22]. The rest of this book asks whether the business can pay for both and still earn a good return for a shareholder at $70.35.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Gross Bookings: total dollars customers pay on the platform before payouts to drivers and merchants. It is a size measure, not revenue.
  • Take rate: revenue divided by Gross Bookings, the share of each dollar of bookings that Uber keeps.
  • Constant currency: growth recomputed at last year's exchange rates, so a weaker or stronger dollar does not distort the trend.
  • Non-GAAP: profit measures that leave out items the company calls unusual. Since 2026 Uber's measures include stock pay and depreciation, which makes them closer to GAAP[16].
  • Autonomous vehicle (AV): a car that drives itself. A robotaxi is an AV that gives paid rides.
  • LEAPS: long-dated call options that give the right to buy 100 shares each at a fixed strike price until a date a year or more away. We hold five contracts that expire on December 17, 2027[33].
  • Reverse DCF: a model that starts from today's price and solves for the growth rate the price already assumes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of the business is its network. A rider opens the app where waits are short, because many drivers are nearby, and drivers stay where trips are plentiful. That loop has now run for 15 years in more than 15,000 cities[5]. In the second quarter Uber had 208 million monthly users, Gross Bookings grew 22% in constant currency for the fourth quarter in a row above 20%, and management said it added more first-time users in the last twelve months than in any period of the past five years[1][26]. Only about one in five eligible consumers is active monthly in both rides and delivery, so the cross-selling opportunity is large[5].

The profit evidence is strong and recent. Income from operations went from a loss of $3,834 million in 2021 to $5,565 million in 2025[6][5], and $6,700 million over the twelve months to June 2026 (our sum of the filed figures). In the second quarter Mobility segment profit grew 28% and Delivery grew 38%[1]. Operating profit measured the way Uber now reports it, with stock pay and depreciation included, grew 40% to $2,143 million[1]. This is profit earned on a larger number of trips, not a one-off.

The weak points are competition, rules and price. Barriers to entry are low, and the 10-K names Lyft, DiDi and Bolt in rides and DoorDash, Instacart and Delivery Hero in delivery[5]. On the second-quarter call the CEO said Brazil has become far more competitive in food, with DiDi and Meituan chasing the same two-wheeler couriers that also carry riders[26]. The classification of drivers and couriers as contractors is still contested in courts and legislatures, and a ruling that they are employees would raise costs[2]. Insurance is both a risk and a lever: insurance cost rose $851 million in 2025[5], and in 2026 management says falling insurance cost is a tailwind that it is reinvesting in lower prices[26]. Lower prices help growth and cut the take rate.

The robotaxi question

The main fear behind the share price is that self-driving cars will let someone else own the rider. The 10-K lists Alphabet's Waymo, Amazon's Zoox and Tesla as competitors that already offer, or may offer, driverless rides[5]. On August 5 the stock closed 5.3% lower after a soft third-quarter profit forecast, and CNBC noted that Waymo appears to be pulling away, because the two companies will end an exclusive agreement in Atlanta and Austin by early 2028[22]. Management's reply is that AVs are under 0.5% of roughly 300 million weekly trips, that Uber is live in seven AV cities and aims for 15 by the end of 2026, and that it has secured access to more than 120,000 AV-ready vehicles[22][23][24]. It also said trips in Phoenix, Los Angeles and San Francisco, where robotaxis already compete, accelerated in the second quarter[22].

We cannot settle this from filings, because Uber does not publish AV bookings or profit. What we can do is size the exposure. The 20 biggest US cities are 30% of US Gross Bookings and 25% of US profit[26]. A robotaxi operator that kept those riders in its own app would hurt, but it would not remove most of the business, and Uber's other bets, including 40% growth in Uber for Business and rising use in sparse US markets, sit outside those cities[26]. Chapter 6 treats this as the hinge of the whole case.

How good is reported profit?

GAAP net income is a poor guide here. Net income was $10,053 million in 2025, but $4,346 million of that was a tax benefit, and the third quarter of 2025 alone included a $4.9 billion tax valuation release[5][8]. In the second quarter of 2026 net income of $2,394 million included a $1.6 billion pre-tax gain on equity stakes, led by a $1.1 billion gain on Delivery Hero and $899 million on Aurora, partly offset by a $437 million loss on Didi[2]. We therefore judge Uber on operating income, non-GAAP earnings per share and cash flow, and treat net income as context.

Cash flow needs one more caution. Uber runs its own insurer, and as trips grow it sets aside reserves for claims it will pay later. That reserve growth added $2,660 million to 2025 operating cash flow and $830 million in the first half of 2026[5][1]. The cash is real, but it is held against future claims and is typically restricted[10], so we strip it out in the stricter free cash flow measure in Chapter 3.

Capital allocation is changing

A buyback is a company purchase of its own shares, which shrinks the share count. Until 2026 Uber was a story of rising cash and large buybacks: $6,523 million of shares bought in 2025 against $9,763 million of free cash flow[5]. In 2026 the same cash is being split between buybacks ($3,529 million in the first half), the Delivery Hero stake and takeover, ezCater, and AV partnerships[1][11][21]. The deal is financed with new debt: a €14.2 billion bridge facility, cut to €10.2 billion by a €4.0 billion term loan, and €4.5 billion of euro bonds sold on September 15[15][14]. Management says gross leverage will stay below 2 times and the credit rating will remain investment grade[11]. That is plausible, and Chapter 3 tests it with our own numbers.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue grew from $17,455 million in 2021 to $52,017 million in 2025, a compound rate of 31.4% a year, and reached $55,227 million over the twelve months to June 2026 (our sum of filed figures)[6][5][1]. Gross Bookings grew from $90.4 billion to $193.5 billion over the same four years[7][5]. The better news is below the revenue line. Income from operations rose from a loss of $3,834 million to a profit of $6,700 million over the twelve months to June 2026, a margin of 12.1%. GAAP net income, by contrast, is distorted by a $4.3 billion tax benefit in 2025 and by swings in the value of Uber's stakes in other companies, so the numbers in this chapter lean on operating income and cash.

Several terms come before the tables. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. EBITDA, short for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, is a rough measure of operating cash profit. We compute a GAAP version as operating income plus depreciation and amortization, and we also show Uber's own Adjusted EBITDA, which also adds back stock pay. Net debt is borrowings minus cash and short-term investments. Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money invested in the business. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay in shares, a real cost to owners. Free cash flow, also called FCF, is cash from operations minus the cost of buying equipment and software.

Terms about the chart and the trade also need plain definitions before Chapters 4 and 7. The Weinstein stage scale names the phase of a stock: Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend, and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend. A base is a flat stretch of trading before a move, the pivot is the top of that stretch, and a breakout is a move above the pivot on heavy volume. The relative strength index (RSI) is a gauge from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has moved, where under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing in dollars, and we use it as a ruler for distance. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut. The entry zone is the band where we would buy.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate rests on an assumption that could fail. For ROIC, the weakest assumption is the 24% tax rate and the treatment of Delivery Hero capital, which will arrive before its profit. For free cash flow, it is that insurance reserves keep growing: if claims paid rise faster than premiums reserved, the cash that helped the filed figure reverses. For leverage, it is that the committed financing is fully drawn at the exchange rate we used. For the reverse DCF, it is that last year's cash flow is a stable starting point for ten years while Uber is about to spend more than $10 billion on AVs and finance a $14.8 billion takeover. For the EPS estimate, it is that growth stays near 31% as price cuts and AV costs build. For the target, it is the 19 times multiple, because 15 times would put the price below today's close. For the weighted value, it is the probabilities themselves.

The evidence that would show us wrong arrives on dated events. Third-quarter results come on November 4, 2026[20]. The Delivery Hero acceptance period is reported to end on November 5[24]. Fourth-quarter results and 2027 guidance come in February 2027. The Delivery Hero deal is expected to close in the second half of 2027[11]. The LEAPS expire on December 17, 2027[33].

Fatal flaw 1: a robotaxi operator takes the rider

Waymo, Zoox and Tesla are named competitors[5]. Uber and Waymo plan to end their exclusive arrangement in Atlanta and Austin by early 2028[22]. If a robotaxi operator wins riders through its own app in the 20 largest US cities, which are 30% of US Gross Bookings and 25% of US profit[26], Uber would lose its most profitable trips first. We cannot compute the dollar loss because Uber does not publish US profit. Our bear EPS of $3.50 therefore rests on judgment: it assumes price cuts and Delivery Hero costs as well as lost share, and is not derived from a share-loss calculation.

The early evidence is mixed. Uber says trip growth accelerated in Phoenix, Los Angeles and San Francisco, where robotaxis compete[22]. But AVs are still under 0.5% of weekly trips, and partners need years to build fleets. The test is whether growth in those cities stays above the national rate through 2027.

Fatal flaw 2: the Delivery Hero deal is financed with strain or goes wrong

Uber will pay €41.50 a share, an equity value of $14.8 billion, and has committed financing of €14.2 billion[11][15]. The price is about 8 times 2027 Adjusted EBITDA including more than $1.2 billion of synergies[12], so the deal works only if synergies arrive. Regulators must approve it. The sale of 14 overlapping markets to SSW Partners is meant to ease approval, but whether it does is untested, and a delay past 2027 is possible[11]. A term loan commitment fee starts on November 13, 2026[13].

If the deal closes and integration is slow, net debt divided by EBITDA could stay near 2.8x on our strict measure for several quarters, and Uber would likely slow its buybacks again. The company already deployed about $4.0 billion in the second quarter and bought back only $518 million[26][1]. We would treat a second quarter of low buybacks without a restart as a negative signal.

Fatal flaw 3: price war, rule change or legal loss in rides

Uber is cutting prices with the savings from lower insurance cost[26], and the underlying Mobility take rate is down about 1.5 points. If competitors match with lower prices, margins would give back the gain. A ruling that drivers are employees would raise costs materially[2]. Uber has also paid $1.8 billion of UK VAT assessments that it carries as a receivable and expects to recover; a loss on appeal would mean that money does not come back[2]. General and administrative expense rose $266 million in the quarter, including a $138 million rise in legal accruals[2].
