Chapter 1

Garrett Camp and Travis Kalanick founded UberCab in San Francisco in March 2009 [1]. Their goal was simple: hail a car with one phone tap instead of waving on a street corner. From 2010 to 2017, Uber built a vast two-sided mobile market. It spent venture funds at high speed to subsidize fares and driver bonuses in over 60 countries. That rapid push made 'Uber' a global verb and drove huge top-line growth. Yet it also bred weak controls, fierce local fights, and heavy cash burn. Uber fought costly battles in China, Southeast Asia, Russia, self-driving cars, and air taxis at the same time [10], [11].

The big turn began in August 2017 when the Board named Dara Khosrowshahi as CEO [1]. He came from Expedia Group to clean up the firm before its May 2019 IPO. Uber ended super-voting founder shares and moved to a one-share, one-vote setup. It split the Chair and CEO jobs and built formal safety and audit teams. Next, Khosrowshahi cut losing regional wars instead of burning more cash. Following the 2016 China sale to DiDi, Uber merged its Russia unit with Yandex in 2018. In March 2018, it sold Southeast Asia to Grab for a 27.5% equity stake [10]. In December 2020, Uber sold its self-driving unit ATG to Aurora Innovation and put $400 million in for a 26% stake [10]. Days later, it sold its Elevate air-taxi unit to Joby Aviation alongside a $75 million investment [11]. Uber traded heavy R&D cash burn for partner stakes and app rights.

Uber also changed how it spent money inside the company. From 2019 to 2021, corporate overhead grew fast to feed moonshot labs and extra offices. After layoffs and a hiring freeze in 2022–2023, fixed costs grew much slower than Gross Bookings [1], [6]. Uber also changed how it worked with cities and labor boards. In the early years, Uber fought city taxi rules in court and through public campaigns. Under Khosrowshahi, Uber worked with lawmakers to write clear, stable rules. In California, it backed Proposition 22 [1], [14]. Turning open legal battles into steady per-trip costs let Uber plan for long-term profit growth.

By late 2026, Uber looked nothing like the cash-burning startup of 2017, yet its capital-allocation playbook had entered a new phase. While Uber stayed asset-light on autonomous hardware—turning 2020 moonshots into partner stakes—management resumed bold balance-sheet M&A in 2026 to lock in global delivery scale and corporate catering rather than funneling 100% of Free Cash Flow into buybacks [1], [3]. Operating Cycle Resilience: From Cash Burn to Capital Return

During the 2020 pandemic lockdowns, Mobility trips collapsed while Delivery surged, proving the strategic value of operating two distinct demand graphs on one shared driver-courier network [1].

Between 2021 and 2025, Mobility rebounded past pre-pandemic peaks while Delivery retained its pandemic scale and turned highly profitable, producing five consecutive years of 20%+ Gross Bookings growth [2].

In February 2026, CFO Prashanth Mahendra-Rajah stepped down to join the U.S. Department of Commerce and was succeeded by internal veteran Balaji Krishnamurthy, preserving continuity in capital allocation and margin discipline [17].

The mid-2026 M&A pivot marks a major test of capital discipline: Uber is committing ~$18 billion across Delivery Hero, ezCater, and tuck-ins while guiding gross leverage below 2.0x and seeking over $1.2 billion in Delivery Hero run-rate synergies by 2028 [3].

Concrete End-to-End Walkthroughs: Consumer, Merchant, Driver & Shipper Economics

Mobility Walkthrough (Chicago O'Hare Airport Trip): A consultant lands at O'Hare at 6:15 PM and opens Uber. Holding Uber One, she earns 6% Uber Cash back and priority matching. Upfront pricing quotes $54.00 for a 19-mile Uber Comfort ride downtown; Uber's dispatch engine matches a driver 4 minutes away who just dropped off a departing passenger (minimizing unpaid deadhead miles). Out of the $54.00 gross fare, ~$36.50 goes to the driver, ~$4.50 covers state/airport fees and commercial auto insurance reserves, and ~$13.00 is recognized as Uber net Mobility revenue.

Delivery + Merchant Economics Walkthrough (Friday Dinner + Ad Monetization): A family in Austin opens Uber Eats at 7:30 PM. At the top of the search feed for 'Thai food', a local restaurant pays $1.80 per click for a Sponsored Listing placement—pure ~90%+ margin ad revenue for Uber [19]. The family orders $62.00 of food, pays $0 delivery fee via Uber One, and tips $8.00. On the merchant side, the restaurant pays a tiered marketplace commission (~15%–25% depending on marketing tier) on the $62.00 basket—worth paying because incremental off-premise orders utilize idle kitchen capacity without adding rent. A courier finishing a nearby drop-off bundles the pickup. Uber collects the merchant commission, consumer service fee, and ad click, netting ~$11.50 in revenue while paying the courier base fare plus 100% of the tip.

Freight Walkthrough (Midwest Pallet Load): A packaged-goods manufacturer needs 42,000 lbs of beverage pallets moved from Columbus, Ohio to Indianapolis on 24 hours' notice. Using Uber Freight's enterprise portal, the shipper posts the load at a dynamic market rate of $950. Uber Freight's algorithm matches an independent owner-operator whose truck is unloading 15 miles away, eliminating an empty backhaul. Uber Freight books $950 in Gross Bookings (and revenue, as principal broker), pays the carrier ~$825, and retains ~$125 gross margin to cover brokerage operations.

Chapter 3

Uber's distribution edge comes from local two-sided market liquidity [8]. Unlike national software firms, rides and food delivery are strictly local. Supply and demand must clear within five miles in under five minutes. Uber solves this with live map pricing, multi-order batching, and cross-dispatching [8]. Earners can carry commuters during morning rush hour and deliver lunch at noon on the same app. Higher hourly earner pay keeps drivers online without forcing higher fares for riders.

Unit economics change sharply with city density. On a 15-minute ride in Midtown Manhattan, dense demand lets Uber match the driver's next trip within seconds of drop-off. Unpaid empty miles stay low, and engaged time often tops 80% of the hour. On a 15-minute ride in a quiet outer suburb, unpaid drive time to pickup eats into driver pay. Uber uses upfront trip pricing and food-order batching to protect suburban margins. Across all cities, 4.1 billion quarterly trips spread fixed tech and payment costs very thin [3], [8].
