1. What the company does

CNX Resources is an independent natural gas producer centred in the Appalachian Basin, the shale region under Pennsylvania, West Virginia and Ohio. In 2025 it sold 629.0 billion cubic feet of gas equivalent, which is 1,723 million cubic feet a day. Of that, 92% was natural gas and 8% liquids, and 94% came from shale rather than coalbed methane[4]. Gas equivalent means oil and liquids are counted as gas at six thousand cubic feet per barrel, so one number covers everything the company sells. At 2025-12-31 the company held 9.7 trillion cubic feet equivalent of proved reserves, of which 72.2% was developed and 99.1% was in wells it operates itself[3].

Who pays, and for what? CNX sells gas into pipelines to marketers, utilities and power buyers, and the price follows the New York futures price for gas delivered at Henry Hub in Louisiana, minus a regional discount. That discount is called basis, and CNX's weighted basis for 2026 is a loss of $0.59 per million British thermal units[8]. In the second quarter of 2026 one buyer, NRG Business Marketing, took $57.5 million of $417.2 million of segment sales, or 13.8%[14]. The sales are spread across seven hubs, from Eastern Gas-South and TETCO M2 at 27% each to the Michigan hub at 7%[8]. So the revenue is a commodity price times a volume, and neither is set by CNX.

Two smaller lines sit beside the gas. The first is midstream, meaning the gathering pipelines and compressors that move gas from the wellhead to the big interstate lines. CNX owns or operates about 2,600 miles of gathering pipe and owns substantially all of its shale gathering in Pennsylvania and West Virginia[4]. Midstream revenue was only $15.3 million in the second quarter[14], so the pipes matter mainly because they cut CNX's own cost. The second is environmental attributes. CNX captures methane from abandoned coal mines, sells Pennsylvania alternative energy credits ($13 million in the quarter) and sold federal 45Z clean-fuel tax credits for about $30 million in July. Management expects the two together to run near $90 million a year from 2027[12][28].

CNX also calls itself a technology company, but it does not report a technology segment or its revenue, so we cannot size it. The reported segments are Shale, Coalbed Methane and Other[14]. Earnings are hard to read for one more reason. CNX sells most of its gas ahead with hedges, which are contracts that fix a price. Gains and losses on open hedges are booked as revenue before the gas is delivered. That is why second-quarter revenue of $618.5 million contains a $176.3 million derivative gain, and why the first quarter showed a $222 million realised hedge loss next to a $226 million paper gain[7][9]. We therefore use the company's adjusted figures, which strip out the paper gains, and we say which one we use each time.

How a well turns into cash, and why spending never stops

A shale well produces most of its gas early and then declines quickly. To hold output flat, a producer must keep drilling. CNX calls this a maintenance programme: about 1.5 rigs and a part-year crew to complete wells, 34 new wells brought online in 2026, and baseline capital spending of $540 million to $570 million[12]. A Utica well costs about $1,700 per foot of horizontal pipe, and the company's best drilling day in the Utica reached 4,416 feet[28][12]. Capital spending is cash spent on wells and equipment that lasts for years. It is deducted before anything is left for shareholders.

The cost that follows the gas is depreciation, depletion and amortisation, which spreads the well cost over the gas it will produce. It was $0.86 per thousand cubic feet equivalent in the second quarter[8]. What is left after cash costs and spending is free cash flow, and the company reported $138 million of it in the second quarter, its 26th quarter in a row[12]. Note the company's definition: it adds proceeds from asset sales, which were $108 million in 2025 and are guided to $45 million in 2026[11][10]. We show our own figure, without asset sales, in Chapter 3.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Mcfe, Bcfe and Tcfe are thousand, billion and trillion cubic feet of gas equivalent. Henry Hub is the Louisiana delivery point whose futures price, called NYMEX, is the benchmark for US gas. Basis is the gap between that benchmark and the price CNX actually gets.
  • A hedge is a contract that fixes a future price. A realised hedge gain or loss is cash settled in the period. An unrealised one is a paper mark on contracts still open. Adjusted figures leave the paper marks out.
  • EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating cash profit. EBITDAX also adds back exploration cost. CNX reports adjusted EBITDAX, which also adds back stock pay and some one-off items.
  • Free cash flow, short form FCF, is cash from operations minus capital spending (CNX also adds asset-sale proceeds). EPS is earnings per share. SBC is stock-based compensation, the cost of paying staff in shares. ROIC is return on invested capital, the after-tax profit earned on each dollar tied up in the business.
  • Net debt is borrowings minus cash. A share repurchase, or buyback, retires shares the company buys in the market, so each remaining share owns a bigger slice. Guidance is the company's own published forecast.
  • RSI is the relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has moved, where above 70 is usually called hot. ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price swing, which here is $0.98. The ledger is the dated data file for this session. Reverse DCF is a valuation run backwards: it finds the cash-flow growth that today's price already assumes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong where it matters for a gas producer: it owns a deep, mostly developed resource and a clean balance sheet, and it fixes most of its prices in advance. Proved reserves rose 13.2% in 2025, from 8.54 to 9.66 trillion cubic feet equivalent, while the company produced 0.63 trillion[3]. Drilling added 867 billion cubic feet and revisions 69 billion, so reserves from the drill bit replaced about 149% of output, or 176% once higher prices are counted. A further 668 billion came from buying Apex Energy (our arithmetic from the roll-forward). At the 2025 rate of output, the reserves last 15.4 years, and 72% are already developed, so most of that gas needs no new drilling to reach the market[3].

The second strength is price protection. At 2026-07-15 CNX had hedged 460.5 billion cubic feet for 2026, 400.4 for 2027 and 167.6 for 2028[9]. The 2027 volumes are sold at an average of $3.31 per thousand cubic feet after basis, against $2.73 in 2026. That is a 21% step-up in the locked-in price for two thirds of next year's gas (our arithmetic). Here the hedge does what it was bought for. In the first quarter, with futures at $5.04, it cost $1.59 per thousand cubic feet. In the second, with futures at $2.90, it paid $0.33[8]. The result is cash that arrives in every quarter, which is why the company has reported positive free cash flow for 26 quarters[12].

The third strength is how management treats the share count. Since the third quarter of 2020 it has bought back about 106 million shares for $2.2 billion, an average of $20.43, and says the share count is down 34% even after issuing shares for convertible notes and staff pay[12]. Those shares cost an average of 54% of today's price. In the second quarter it bought 5.6 million at $35.28, and after quarter-end a further 1.2 million at $33.03[12]. The authorisation is large: $2.17 billion remained at 2026-06-30, which is 39% of the current market value (our arithmetic)[13].

The weaknesses are equally plain. First, CNX is a price taker in a market that is currently soft. The Henry Hub spot price was $2.76 on 2026-10-27, and the Energy Information Administration expects 2027 to average $3.16, which is 9% below 2026[41][40]. The chief executive himself said the move from 2026 into 2027 is 'setting up to be a little bit soft'[28]. Second, volume is falling by design. Second-quarter output was 151.5 billion cubic feet equivalent, 9.6% below 167.6 a year earlier, and 2026 guidance of 605 to 620 is below 2025's 629.0[8][16]. Per-share growth must therefore come from buybacks and price, not from more gas.

Where the moat is thin: basis, costs and scale

A moat is something that protects profits from competitors. In a commodity business it can only be low cost or good position, and CNX has some of both. Its cash production cost was $0.87 per thousand cubic feet equivalent in the second quarter, and fully loaded cash cost including overhead and interest was $1.19, against a cash price of $2.87 including hedge settlements. That leaves a margin of $1.68[8]. The trend is the problem. A year earlier the fully loaded cost was $1.05 and the transport, gathering and compression line was $0.58. It is now $0.68, up 17%[8]. Owning the pipes has not stopped that line from rising.

Basis is the second thin spot. The $0.59 discount to Henry Hub is about 17% of a $3.55 futures price, and CNX cannot change it. Its gas goes to many hubs, so one pipeline problem hurts less than it would a single-hub producer, but the average discount is still wide[8]. Third, scale: EQT, the largest, is worth more than six times CNX, and its merger with Range will make that gap larger when it closes at the start of next year[43][34]. Pure scale cannot be bought by CNX, so its edge must come from per-share discipline.

What the market is also paying for: group consolidation

EQT agreed on 2026-10-26 to acquire Range Resources in an all-stock transaction. EQT holders would own about 73% of the combined company and Range holders 27%, and the companies expect to close at the beginning of next year[43]. Range Resources shares are up 49.6% in 52 weeks and its relative strength index is 84.7, while CNX is up 16.8% in a year and 69.0 on the same gauge[37][33]. Part of CNX's 21.9% rise in the last month[45] may reflect hope that it is the next target. We have no evidence of an approach, and we do not underwrite a takeover. It matters because the hope can fade as fast as it came.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Guidance is the company's own forecast, and the first test of management is whether it hits it. In January 2025 CNX guided, including the Apex acquisition, to free cash flow of about $575 million, adjusted EBITDAX of $1,225 million to $1,275 million, volume of 605 to 620 billion cubic feet equivalent and capital spending of $450 million to $500 million[18]. It delivered free cash flow of $646 million, adjusted EBITDAX of $1,247 million, volume of 629.0 and capital spending of $495 million[16][11]. In October 2025 it had lowered EBITDAX guidance to $1,200 million to $1,225 million because gas fell, and then beat that range[17]. On volume, free cash flow and spending the company met or beat what it said.

The second test is 2026, where guidance has been cut on price but not on execution. Initial 2026 guidance in January assumed NYMEX gas at $4.07, EBITDAX of $1,310 million to $1,360 million and free cash flow of about $550 million[16]. By July the gas assumption was $3.55, EBITDAX was $1,265 million to $1,315 million and free cash flow about $525 million[10]. Volume and capital spending held steady, the planned well count rose from 30 to 34, and free cash flow per share was restored to about $3.55 after buybacks[15][10]. A company that cuts a forecast for the commodity price it cannot control, and keeps what it can control, is doing what it said.

The third test is capital allocation. The company said it would buy back stock when the margin of safety is wide, and it did: 16.9 million shares for $528 million at $31.00 in 2025, and 7.18 million shares in the first half of 2026[4][13]. It also said it would manage its debt, and it did that too. It issued $500 million of 5.875% notes due 2034 and repaid the $500 million of 6.00% notes due 2029, and it settled the $209 million of convertible notes in shares[13][12]. The one change we would flag is people: the finance chief appointed in January left in September[23][22].

3. The numbers, in plain words

Reported profit at CNX is distorted by hedge accounting, so the first job is to choose the right yardstick. Derivative gains and losses swing the headline numbers wildly. Revenue under US accounting rules, called GAAP, was $1,261 million in 2022 after a $2,664 million derivative loss. It was $3,435 million in 2023 after a $1,929 million gain. The gas sold changed little[6][1]. Net income swung from a $142 million loss to a $1,721 million profit between those two years. We therefore lean on three measures that remove the paper marks: sales including cash settlements, adjusted net income, and adjusted EBITDAX. All three are the company's own figures. We cross-check them to cash flow, which cannot be marked.

Over five years the cash engine has been steady rather than growing fast. Sales including cash settlements were $1,645 million in 2021 and $1,733 million in 2025, and were $1,777 million over the twelve months to 2026-06-30. Adjusted EBITDAX moved from $1,195 million to $1,247 million, and adjusted net income from $348 million to $402 million[21][16][11]. The dip in 2023 and 2024, when gas fell, is the cyclical part. The recovery in 2025 came mostly from higher gas prices and from the Apex acquisition, which closed on 2025-01-27 for about $518 million[1][4].

Cash is where the story is clearest. Cash from operations less capital spending was $460 million in 2021, a peak of $669 million in 2022, a low of $135 million in 2023 when spending ran at $679 million, and $534 million in 2025. Over the last twelve months it was $526 million (our arithmetic)[6][1][7]. The company's own free cash flow adds asset-sale proceeds and so reads higher, at $646 million for 2025 and $635 million for the last twelve months[11]. We prefer our figure because asset sales cannot be repeated for ever. The difference is $109 million over the last twelve months.

The balance sheet tells the capital allocation story. Net debt stayed between $2.1 billion and $2.4 billion for five years. Over the same time the share count fell from about 204 million to 143 million. The company spent about $1.84 billion on repurchases from 2021 to 2025 and $537 million over the last twelve months[6][1][7]. Shares outstanding rose to 147.9 million by 2026-07-15 only because 12.6 million shares were issued to settle convertible notes, which also removed a debt[13][12]. Equity grew from $3.7 billion to $4.8 billion, which means the company is retaining profit, not just returning it.

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could fail. We name it, say what evidence would show it wrong and say when we would know. Then we argue the case against our own view as hard as we can, with three scenarios in which the idea fails.

Fatal flaw 1: the hedge lift is a one-year peak

The central value rests on 2027 profit of $4.18, and two thirds of that volume is fixed at $3.31. But 2028 has only 167.6 billion cubic feet fixed at $3.23, and no consensus EPS for 2028 was visible on the page we read[9][32]. If gas stays at the EIA's 2027 level of $3.16 and the discount to Henry Hub stays near $0.59, the company will realise about $2.57 on unhedged gas (our arithmetic: $3.16 less $0.59). That is below the $3.31 it has locked for 2027. Investors buy the year after next, so the market could price 2028 and treat 2027 as the peak. In that case a multiple of 8 on a falling EPS is a fair outcome, and the bear value of $26.50 is the answer, not the exception.

Fatal flaw 2: the per-share engine stalls

The whole per-share story relies on buying back stock. The authorisation is $2.17 billion, 39% of the market value, and the chief executive said CNX could spend more than free cash flow on repurchases if the discount is wide[13][28]. If it borrows to do so while gas is low, net debt over adjusted EBITDAX could climb back toward the 2.29 times of 2023. The stock is also up 24% from its low, so each repurchase now costs more than in the second quarter[11][45]. A new finance chief, the third since late 2025 (Mr Shepard, then Mr Good, now Mr Srivastava), makes the risk of a change in policy real. S&P rates the company BB with a stable outlook, so there is headroom, but it is not investment grade[31][22].

Fatal flaw 3: credits, rules and a bigger neighbour

About $90 million a year of credit income is expected from 2027, which is 17% of the $525 million free cash flow guide[28][10]. The federal 45Z rule is not final, the Pennsylvania credit price is described by the company as volatile, and the company argues against proposed wider buffer zones around wells[12][28]. Any of these could cut that income. Separately, EQT and Range will form a producer worth more than $50 billion in enterprise value, with more power in pipeline deals and drilling services[43]. A smaller neighbour gets little of that benefit.
