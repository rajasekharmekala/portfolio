1. What the company does

Agilysys writes software for one industry: hospitality. Its customers are hotels, multi-amenity resorts, casinos, cruise lines, corporate and campus dining operators, stadiums and healthcare facilities.[3] The software covers three jobs. A property management system (PMS in the trade) runs rooms, reservations and check-in. A point-of-sale system (POS) runs the till in restaurants, bars, spas and golf shops. Inventory and procurement software tracks the food and drink behind them.[3] Think of it as the operating system of a resort: the same guest profile follows a person from the front desk to the spa to the steakhouse. The 10-K lists Marriott International, Intercontinental Hotel Group, Royal Caribbean, Carnival UK, Venetian, Cosmopolitan and Compass Group among its customers.[3]

Revenue comes in three kinds, and they behave differently. In the September 2026 quarter, subscription and maintenance fees were $62.2 million, or 66.6 percent of the $93.4 million total.[1] Subscription fees alone grew 32.6 percent from a year earlier and are now 71.2 percent of those recurring fees; the remainder is annual maintenance on older, on-premises installations.[1] Professional services (implementation, training and integration work) brought in $19.2 million, and products (hardware and perpetual software licences) brought in $12.0 million.[2] Subscriptions are the prize. They repeat, and subscription and maintenance fees carry a gross margin near 80 percent (revenue minus the direct cost of delivering it, as a share of revenue), and each new module sold to an existing customer should cost less to win than a new customer.

The company's own strategy is to raise the share of revenue that comes from subscriptions, cloud applications and support.[3] It has worked. Recurring revenue was 61.7 percent of sales in fiscal 2025 and 66.6 percent in the latest quarter.[4][1] Fiscal years end on March 31, so fiscal 2027 runs from April 2026 to March 2027 and the quarter just reported is the second fiscal quarter, July to September 2026.[1] Management said on the call that it has now posted 20 straight quarters of subscription growth of at least 23 percent and 19 straight quarters of record total revenue.[21]

Two projects explain much of the recent speed. In August 2024 the company bought Book4Time, a spa and wellness booking product, for $145.8 million in cash, funded partly with a $49.6 million loan that it has since repaid.[3] And it is installing its property management system across Marriott's select-service and full-service hotels. On the October 26 call management said well over 2,000 properties are live and that the project has not reached the halfway mark by properties, rooms or revenue.[21] The company counts the Marriott revenue in its reported results but leaves it out of its sales, backlog and recurring-revenue-implemented statistics, so those statistics describe the rest of the business.[21]

How a customer buys, and why modules matter

Agilysys sells an ecosystem, not a single product. In the quarter it added 14 new customers (24 counting Book4Time), and the 14 bought an average of 5.6 products each; where a property management system was part of the deal the average was 9.3 products.[21] It also added 83 new sites at companies that were already customers and sold add-on products into existing properties 119 times, the fourth straight quarter above 100. Those 119 deals involved 331 products, or 2.8 per deal, the highest on record.[21] Add-on modules now make up 34 percent of subscription revenue.[21] The reason this matters is simple. Winning the first property is expensive. Selling a second, third and fourth module to the same property costs far less, so more modules per customer lifts profit.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • PMS: property management system, the software that runs rooms, rates and check-in at a hotel or resort.
  • POS: point-of-sale system, the software and terminals that take orders and payments in restaurants, bars and shops.
  • Recurring revenue: subscription fees plus maintenance fees; money the customer pays again each year.
  • Backlog: signed orders not yet delivered or turned into revenue.
  • Adjusted EBITDA: the company's measure of operating profit, which starts from earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization and also removes share-based pay and one-off items; it is not an accounting-standard measure.
  • Free cash flow: cash from operations minus capital spending; what is left to build cash or repay debt.
  • Rule of 40: revenue growth plus profit margin; a software company at or above 40 is considered healthy.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business rests on focus, integration and the cost of switching. The company serves only hospitality, and its 10-K says it competes with larger full-service providers, naming Oracle, Shiji and Infor, plus smaller specialists such as Maestro and the in-house systems some hotel chains build for themselves.[3] Its answer is a connected set of products on one data model: property management, point of sale, inventory, payments and guest-facing modules that share a single guest profile and a single set of interfaces.[3][1] That is hard to copy quickly, because a rival has to rebuild every module and then persuade a hotel to rip out the system that runs its front desk and kitchens. In our view, switching is slow, risky and disruptive, and that is the main reason customers stay.

The evidence is in the sales numbers management gave on the October 26 call. Domestic sales to hotels, resorts and cruise ships rose 33 percent from a year earlier, food-service sales rose 42 percent and Asia-Pacific sales rose 47 percent.[21] The quarter included a seven-figure replacement of a competitor at a multi-property resort, with property management, inventory and as many as 15 more modules.[21] Subscription revenue linked to property management grew 53.8 percent while point-of-sale subscriptions grew 18.4 percent.[21] These are management's figures, and the company gives no dollar totals for orders, so we treat them as direction, not proof.

The financial proof is in the margins. Gross margin was 62.6 percent in fiscal 2026 and 64.6 percent in the latest quarter, and management expects it to reach the mid to high 60s over the next several years as subscriptions grow faster than services.[3][1][21] Adjusted EBITDA margin (the company's operating profit measure, described in the glossary above) rose from 15.6 percent in fiscal 2024 to 21.2 percent in fiscal 2026 and was 25.9 percent in the latest quarter.[14][4][1] About 70 percent of the 2,413 employees are in India, where the company runs a software development centre.[3] That likely lowers cost, but it ties profit to wage inflation there and to the company's ability to hire and keep engineers.

Where the business is weaker

The business is small next to its rivals. Revenue was $319.3 million in fiscal 2026, against $19.3 billion in a single quarter at Oracle, which also sells hotel software.[3][31] The 10-K says competition exists on pricing, customer relationships and support, and that some rivals are much larger than Agilysys.[3] Hospitality is also cyclical. The 10-K warns that downturns in the industry could cause customers to delay, cancel or reduce spending, and the latest 10-Q says that global conditions are negatively influencing customer spending.[3][2] A slowdown would show first in delayed or cancelled installations.

Three more weaknesses matter for the price. First, the Marriott project is large and unfinished, and management itself said that big technology projects cannot be expected to go completely smoothly every step of the way.[21] The filings do not name a customer above 10 percent of revenue, but they also give no figure for Marriott's share, so the concentration is unknown. Second, services are the slowest line: professional services revenue grew only 5.3 percent, and its gross margin of 30.9 percent is less than half that of subscriptions.[2] Third, the company does not publish net revenue retention (NRR), the share of last year's customer revenue still paid this year, so we cannot measure how fast existing customers expand or leave. Management says only that churn is low.[21]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A management team earns trust by doing what it said. Agilysys guides in public at the start of each fiscal year and updates the guidance every quarter, so the record can be checked. For fiscal 2026 it began with $308 million to $312 million of revenue, 25 percent subscription growth and a 20 percent adjusted EBITDA margin.[10] It raised subscription growth guidance to 27 percent in July and 29 percent in October 2025. It raised the revenue range to $315 million to $318 million in October and to $318 million in January. It then delivered $319.3 million, 30.2 percent subscription growth and a 21.2 percent margin.[9][8][7][4]

Fiscal 2025 was the exception. The company began with $275 million to $280 million, raised to $280 million to $285 million in October 2024 and then cut to about $273 million in January 2025 because point-of-sale orders were slow in the first half and large development projects were winding down.[13][12][11] It finished at $275.6 million: below the raised range, inside the original one. The profit and subscription promises held, with a 39.5 percent subscription growth against at least 38 percent guided and adjusted EBITDA of $53.8 million, or 19.5 percent, against 18 percent guided.[10] So the miss came from one-time revenue, meaning products and development work, and not from subscriptions.

Revenue guidance has gone from $365 million to $370 million, to $368 million to $373 million, to $374 million to $378 million, and subscription growth from at least 30 to 32 to 34 percent.[4][5][1] The pattern of raising each quarter has been honest so far, but it also means guidance starts low, so a raise is less informative than it looks. How it was calculated

For ROIC we took twelve-month operating income of $49.3 million, removed $2.2 million of net one-off gains and added back $0.04 million of legal costs to get $47.2 million, taxed it at 25 percent to get $35.4 million, and divided by average invested capital of $228.8 million (shareholders' equity plus debt minus cash, averaged over September 2025 and September 2026).
