Devon Energy is a large American producer of oil and gas, with about 1.36 million barrels of oil equivalent a day after its May 2026 merger with Coterra[2][9]. The shares closed at $51.39 on 2026-10-23, up 4.49 percent, on a day when oil settled at $103.27 and four large peers also rose[18][19]. We reject the stock for now at zero size: our base case is worth $41.82, so reward-to-risk (the possible gain divided by the possible loss) is -1.45 to 1 against a 2.5 to 1 floor. Our three cases weigh to $45.13, which is 12 percent below the price. 1. What the company does

Devon finds oil, natural gas and natural gas liquids (NGLs, the propane-like liquids that come out of the ground with gas) in U.S. shale rock and sells them to refiners, pipelines and gas buyers. It has no overseas production: the 10-K says its exploration and production is solely focused in the U.S., and one reporting segment covers the whole company[3]. Customers are many and small. In 2025 no customer took more than 10 percent of sales, so losing one buyer would not hurt much[3]. What matters is the price the company gets. A barrel of oil sold at about the West Texas Intermediate (WTI) benchmark price is the main earner, while gas is a minor earner at today's prices.

The company changed shape on May 7, 2026, when it closed an all-stock merger with Coterra Energy. Each Coterra share became 0.70 of a Devon share, and Devon was the accounting buyer[9][2]. Devon issued 531.6 million new shares, valued at $24.8 billion using its $46.60 close on May 6. With $174 million of replacement awards, the total purchase price was $24.9 billion. Devon also took on $3.3 billion of long-term debt and $0.2 billion of short-term debt from Coterra (the balance-sheet figures at the closing date)[2]. At announcement the deal was described as $58 billion of combined enterprise value (the value of all shares plus net debt), with Devon holders owning about 54 percent and Coterra holders about 46 percent[8]. The merger is finished, so it is not a pending deal. Shareholders of both companies voted yes on May 4, 2026, and the closing came 94 days after announcement[9][1]. What is still pending is a smaller deal: on October 8, 2026 Devon agreed to sell its Eagle Ford assets in South Texas to Crescent Energy for $4.2 billion in cash[6].

Oil is still the biggest value driver even though gas volumes jumped. In the second quarter Devon produced 503,000 barrels of oil a day, 314,000 barrels of NGLs and 3,252 million cubic feet of gas a day[2]. Gas is converted at six thousand cubic feet to one barrel of oil equivalent (Boe, a common unit that lets oil, NGLs and gas be added together), which makes gas 542 thousand Boe a day, or 40 percent of the total. Yet gas earned little: the realized price was $1.05 per thousand cubic feet with hedges, only 36 percent of the $2.90 Henry Hub benchmark, because pipeline limits in the Permian pushed local Waha prices below zero in the quarter[2]. Oil, by contrast, sold at $88.09 a barrel after hedging, against a WTI average of $92.47[2].

The plan for the next year has three moving parts. First, Devon is running 31 rigs and 10 completion crews and plans about $4.9 billion of capital spending in 2026[10]. Second, it says it can find $1.0 billion of yearly pre-tax savings (synergies, the cost savings from combining two companies) by the end of 2027, with about $600 million of that captured during 2027[1]. Third, it is reviewing every asset. The Eagle Ford sale is the first result: the company says those assets are about 4 percent of its production and that the sale price is above Devon's own internal value[6]. The basin table shows Eagle Ford at 6 percent of Q2 output because only about seven of the thirteen weeks include Coterra; the 4 percent is the share of combined production[6]. The rest of this book asks whether the shares already price in all of that, and the answer in later chapters is that at $51.39 they do.

How Devon earns money, in numbers

Revenue is not only oil and gas sales. In 2025 Devon booked $17,188 million of revenue, of which $11,223 million was oil, gas and NGL sales, $402 million was gains on hedges, and $5,563 million came from marketing and midstream (buying third-party product and moving it, at roughly zero profit)[3]. That is why revenue is a poor yardstick here and why the book leans on cash flow and per-barrel margins. Oil alone was $8,906 million of the sales, gas $842 million and NGLs $1,475 million[3].

Costs are mostly set by geology and by taxes. In the second quarter, production costs including taxes were $11.27 per Boe, of which lease operating expense, the cost to run the wells, was $5.06 per Boe[1]. Depreciation, depletion and amortization (DD&A, the yearly write-off of drilling cost) was $11.19 per Boe, so oil at $90 earns far more than oil at $60 while most costs stay the same[2]. That fixed-cost shape is what makes profit swing so hard with the oil price, a point we turn into numbers in chapter 5.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Boe (barrel of oil equivalent) is a unit that adds oil, NGLs and gas on one scale, with six thousand cubic feet of gas counted as one barrel.
  • WTI and Brent are the two main crude oil price benchmarks, WTI for U.S. inland oil and Brent for seaborne oil from the North Sea.
  • Henry Hub is the U.S. natural gas benchmark price, in dollars per million British thermal units.
  • Hedge means a contract that fixes or limits the price of future production, which protects against falls and gives up some of the gains.
  • Synergies are the cost savings a merger is supposed to produce, here $1.0 billion a year.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Devon's strength is acreage. After the merger the Permian supplies 748 thousand of the 1,359 thousand Boe a day, and the company says its Delaware Basin holding sits in the economic core of the play[2][8]. At the merger announcement it put pro forma Delaware output at 863 thousand Boe a day across nearly 750,000 net acres, with more than 10 years of top-tier inventory. Those are the company's own words and its own count, not an independent audit[8]. In May 2026 it added 16,300 net acres and about 400 locations at a New Mexico federal lease sale for $2.6 billion, paid in cash[1]. Spending $2.6 billion at a time when WTI averaged $92.47 in the quarter is a choice to buy inventory at a high price, and it is the main reason trailing free cash flow looks weak[2].

Cost position is good and improving. LOE fell from $6.48 per Boe in the first quarter to $5.06 in the second, below the $5.00 to $5.20 guided range, which the company attributes to a different asset and product mix after the merger; gathering and transport cost rose from $2.54 to $3.16 per Boe for the same reason[2][10]. Management also reports that its stand-alone business optimization plan, a $1 billion yearly cash-flow improvement target, was on track to be fully achieved ahead of schedule[12]. Reserves are adequate: at the end of 2025 stand-alone Devon had 2.4 billion Boe of proved reserves, of which 24 percent were undeveloped, and it replaced 193 percent of 2025 output at a finding and development cost of $6.14 per Boe[11]. Those reserve figures pre-date the merger.

The weaknesses are exposure and quality of earnings. Devon has no pricing power: it takes the oil, gas and NGL prices the market gives. Gas takeaway is a real problem in the Permian, where the second-quarter realized gas price was $0.35 per thousand cubic feet before hedges, 12 percent of Henry Hub, and Devon expects basis (the local price gap) to improve as new pipes open in late 2026 and early 2027[2]. Hedges also cost money in a rising market: oil hedge settlements were a $321 million loss in the second quarter, against $60 million in the first[2]. About 30 percent of remaining 2026 oil and 25 percent of gas is hedged, falling to 15 and 10 percent for 2027[2].

Leadership and governance deserve a note. Two executive vice presidents who ran the Permian and the other basins left on September 1, 2026, and the company named internal replacements[14]. The board raised the chief executive's salary to an annual $1,500,000 with effect from the merger close, and granted him a restricted stock award worth $2,700,000[15]. Neither item is a red flag by itself. They do show that the organization is still being rebuilt, which matters when the promised $1.0 billion of savings depends on execution. There is also a tax dispute: the Canada Revenue Agency has proposed material adjustments to earlier years for Devon's former Canadian business, and a contest may require material cash deposits[2].

How the competitive position compares with peers

Peers matter because Devon cannot differentiate its product. On the vendor figures in chapter 3, Devon trades at a higher trailing enterprise-value-to-EBITDA multiple (enterprise value, meaning shares plus net debt, divided by earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) than EOG, Occidental, Diamondback and than ConocoPhillips' 7.35x, and its return on invested capital of 10.06 percent sits below EOG's 19.75 percent[23][24][25][27][26]. Part of that gap is accounting: the merger put Coterra assets on Devon's balance sheet at a $24.9 billion purchase price, which enlarges the capital base while only about two months of Coterra earnings are in the trailing numbers[2]. The fair summary is that Devon owns very good rock, runs it at competitive cost, and has a clean balance sheet, but it is not cheaper or higher-returning than its best peers today, and its first full-quarter numbers arrive on November 5[6].

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means comparing what management said it would do with what it did. We looked at promises made from November 2025 to June 2026 and the outcomes reported through August 2026. The record is strong on delivery and mixed on timing of benefits, because the largest promise, $1.0 billion of savings, is not yet measurable.

On the operating side, Devon beat or met its own targets four quarters in a row. Third-quarter 2025 production of 853,000 Boe a day and fourth-quarter 851,000 Boe a day both exceeded the top of guidance; first-quarter 2026 oil of 387,000 barrels a day and second-quarter 503,000 were at the top end[13][11][12][1]. Capital came in 5, 4, 6 and 2 percent below guidance in those quarters[13][11][12][1].

On capital allocation, management promised in February a dividend of $0.315 and a buyback of more than $5 billion after the merger. It delivered $0.32 and an $8.0 billion authorization. It said it would retire $1.25 billion of debt in 2026, and it had done $500 million by June and the final $750 million of term loan in July[11][1][10]. The caution is pace: only $197 million of shares were repurchased in the seven weeks after close, against $7.8 billion of remaining capacity[1]. Pace should rise if the Eagle Ford proceeds arrive, as the company says they will fund buybacks and debt reduction[6]. The dividend is now a fixed payment of $0.32 a share plus buybacks. Devon paid $377 million of variable dividends in 2024 on top of the fixed one, and the 2025 dividend table shows only the fixed $0.24 each quarter[3].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on one weak assumption. For the earnings estimate it is the oil deck: we used $78 for 2027, but the market can move $10 in a week. For the multiple estimate it is the 9.31x P/E, which comes from a high-oil year (2026) and may be too low or too high for 2027. For the probability-weighted value it is the 30/50/20 split, which is judgment. For the reverse DCF it is the trailing cash flow, which holds only seven weeks of Coterra.

The bear case against our own call is that we are too cautious: analysts see a $61.50 mean target, oil is above $100, the Eagle Ford sale is accretive, a $7.8 billion buyback is open and the stock is in a clean Stage 2. If oil stays high, buying at $51.39 would have worked, and we would have missed a good tactical trade. We accept that risk because a position taken with a negative expected value is a bet on one variable, the Middle East, and not an underwriting edge. The three fatal-flaw scenarios below are the ways our rejection could be wrong or the company could hurt a buyer.

Fatal flaw 1: WTI holds near $96 or more into 2027 and we are wrong to reject

If WTI averages $92.47 in 2027, our model gives EPS of $6.69 and a value of $62.30, and a buyer at $51.39 gains 21 percent. The signs are present: Brent is at a six-month high, the U.S. is expected to resume strikes, Houthi attacks continue against Saudi Arabia, and the State Department ordered non-essential personnel to leave that country[18]. The EIA's own text notes that the forecast does not account for market events after October 1, 2026[21]. At $92.47 the ratio is still only 1.66 to 1 at the close, below the 2.5 floor. The rejection fails only if sustainable earnings reach $7.29 a share, about $96 WTI in our model. Two different markers apply. The re-underwrite trigger in chapter 7 is a WTI deck of $90 or more, which is when we would rerun the full work, not when the rejection fails. The evidence that we are wrong is a fourth-quarter 2026 realized oil price near $95 and 2027 guidance built on $95 or more. We would know on 2026-11-05 and in February 2027.

Fatal flaw 2: integration and portfolio review disappoint

The base case includes $600 million of 2027 synergies. If synergies slip, each $100 million is worth about $0.07 a share after tax on 1.15 billion shares, our calculation. Evidence of this flaw would be a third-quarter gas realization below 36 percent of Henry Hub or a lower synergy number on November 5.

Fatal flaw 3: capital allocation at the top of the cycle

Devon spent $2.6 billion on federal acreage at a quarterly average WTI of $92.47 and issued 531.6 million shares at $46.60 for Coterra[2].
