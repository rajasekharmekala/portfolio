Before you read

Intuitive Surgical (NASDAQ: ISRG) makes the da Vinci robots that surgeons use for soft-tissue operations, and it earns most of its money each time a hospital uses one.[1][2] The shares closed at $452.29 on 2026-10-23, and our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, because our central value a year out is $462, which leaves almost no gain.[27][28] We would buy only on a pullback to between $392.00 and $404.00, after a fresh check that the trend is intact. Neither account holds the stock, so this is a watchlist decision and not a portfolio action.[29] We would be wrong if the planned price cuts of 3% to 5% per procedure prove deeper, or if US procedure growth, now 10%, keeps falling.

1. What the company does

Intuitive Surgical builds and sells the da Vinci surgical systems that surgeons use for operations on soft tissue, meaning organs and muscle between the pelvis and the neck.[7] A da Vinci system has a surgeon console, a cart at the patient's side, and a vision system. The company also sells Ion, a flexible robotic catheter used to take lung biopsies.[7] A hospital buys or leases the machine, pays for instruments and accessories each time it is used, and pays for service under an annual contract.[2]

Three revenue lines pay the bills. The first is instruments and accessories, the tools that expire or wear out, and the company generally earns between $900 and $3,700 of this revenue for each procedure.[2] In the third quarter of 2026 it was $1,797.4 million, 59% of the $3,048.7 million total (our calculation).[1] The second is systems, $769.1 million, from selling or leasing the robots.[1] The third is service, $482.2 million, from annual contracts that run between $95,000 and $225,000 per system.[1][2]

Instruments, service and operating-lease revenue together are what the company calls recurring revenue, because they come back each time a robot is used or a contract renews. It was 84% of revenue in the third quarter and 85% over the first nine months.[2] It was 84% in 2025 and 83% in 2023.[7] The watchlist seed said more than 75%, and the filings show a higher figure.

Customers are hospitals and, as a newer focus, outpatient surgery centers: 18 of the systems placed in the US in the third quarter went to such centers. On the third-quarter call management said almost 7,000 customers in more than 70 countries used da Vinci, at over 10,000 procedures a day.[25] The United States produced $6,815.8 million of the $10,064.7 million of 2025 revenue, 68% (our calculation), and the rest came from outside the US.[7] More than half of new robots are leased and not sold. Of the 546 systems placed in the third quarter, 291 were under operating leases, and 160 of those were usage-based, which means the customer pays per procedure.[1][2] A lease moves revenue from the day of placement to the years of use, so system revenue is lumpier than procedure revenue.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of what was sold, as a share of revenue. Operating margin is revenue minus all running costs, as a share of revenue.
  • EPS is earnings per share (earnings per share is the company's profit divided across all its shares). GAAP means generally accepted accounting principles, the standard US accounting rules, and non-GAAP means the company's own adjusted version that leaves out items such as stock pay and one-time charges.
  • Free cash flow is cash from operations minus capital spending, and its short form is FCF (free cash flow). SBC is stock-based compensation, the cost of paying employees in shares.
  • ROIC is return on invested capital, the profit earned on each dollar the business has tied up. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating cash profit.
  • RSI is the relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has risen or fallen, where above 70 is usually called hot. ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price swing in dollars, which here is $13.22.
  • Placement means a system delivered to a customer, by sale or lease. A trade-in is an older robot returned when a customer takes a newer one. Utilization is procedures per system per year. The installed fleet is the number of systems in customers' hands.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The company's strength starts with the number of robots already in hospitals. It had 12,094 da Vinci systems installed at 2026-09-30, up 12% in a year, and its Ion fleet grew 21% to 1,152.[1] Surgeons train on the system, hospitals build operating-room routines around it, and each procedure uses Intuitive's instruments, although third-party repair and remanufacture is a live dispute, as the legal section below explains. Management cited more than 48,000 peer-reviewed publications on da Vinci results.[25] Those facts fit with recurring revenue of 84% of sales and with da Vinci procedures of about 3,153,000 in 2025, up 18%.[7]

Demand looks healthy but is slowing at the edges. da Vinci procedures grew 14% in the third quarter, to about 906,000.[2] In the US they grew 10%, against 16% a year earlier, and outside the US 20%.[2] The company says the end of enhanced ACA premium subsidies had a modest negative effect on US growth, and it reports a low double-digit fall in US bariatric procedures, which are about 2% of volume.[2] Use of each system rose 2% in the quarter and 3% in 2025.[2][7] Management also said da Vinci 5 accounted for more than a quarter of US procedures, up from 13% a year earlier.[25]

The da Vinci 5 launch is working as a replacement cycle. The company placed 546 systems in the third quarter against 427 a year earlier, and 315 were da Vinci 5, 58% of the total (our calculation), against 240 a year earlier.[1] Of all third-quarter placements, 150 involved a trade-in of an older system.[2] Trade-ins were 437 of 1,721 placements in 2025, up from 150 of 1,526 in 2024, and the 10-K says that fewer third-generation systems remain to trade in.[7] That point matters, because a trade-in cycle runs out and the company must then rely on new hospitals and on extra systems per hospital.

For the first time the moat is being tested in the US on a wide front. Medtronic's Hugo system was cleared for urologic surgery on 2025-12-03, and the company filed for general and gynecologic surgery on 2026-06-03.[19][20] Johnson & Johnson received De Novo authorization for Ottava on 2026-07-22 for several general surgery procedures, including gallbladder removal, appendectomy and gastric bypass, and said it will launch with select customers first.[18] CMR Surgical's Versius Plus was cleared for gallbladder removal in December 2025, and on 2026-10-12 Stryker announced a $400 million investment, the exclusive US commercial agency and an option to buy CMR.[21][30][23] The 10-K names fourteen companies as entrants or declared entrants, including these three.[7] None of the pages we read gives a competitor's revenue, procedure count or price, so we cannot say how much share they have taken. We can say that a US hospital now has cleared choices it lacked a year ago, and that Intuitive's planned price cuts are its answer.

Where the moat is under pressure: price, China and the courts

In the third quarter management put a number on its own price cuts. From the first half of 2027 it will extend the life of some core instruments on fourth- and fifth-generation systems and add volume-based pricing, and from the fourth quarter of 2026 it will cut instrument prices in China. Together these lower instrument revenue per procedure by about 3% to 5% once fully in place, with product, procedure and country mix held constant.[2][25] Instrument revenue per procedure was about $1,860 in the quarter against $1,800 a year earlier.[25] Instruments were $6,018.9 million of 2025 revenue, 60% (our calculation), so a 1% price change moves revenue by roughly 0.6%.[7] Management expects only a modest gross margin effect, which it plans to offset with cost programs during 2027.[25]

China is the weak spot. The 10-K says fewer systems were placed in China in 2025 than expected, and the 10-Q says system demand there has been hurt by domestic competitors and a government campaign on healthcare.[7][2] In the second quarter of 2026 China's National Healthcare Security Administration set a national price framework for robotic surgery. Intuitive says the limits applied so far had no material effect, but it expects instrument price cuts in China from the fourth quarter.[2] The company does not publish China revenue, so we mark it not published. Outside the US was 32% of 2025 revenue (our calculation from $3,248.9 million of $10,064.7 million), and China is only part of that.[7]

Two legal matters bear on the instrument franchise. In October 2026 the company agreed to pay $450 million, subject to court approval, to settle the class action over third-party repair of instruments, and it agreed to injunctive relief whose terms we did not find in the pages we read.[1][2] Separately, on 2026-08-13 the Ninth Circuit reversed a January 2025 trial win against a repair company and sent the case back, and Intuitive asked for a rehearing on 2026-09-29.[2] The 10-Q adds a risk factor on third parties that remanufacture instruments or service systems without authorization.[2] These matters are why the moat score in this chapter is strong but not clean.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's guidance, which is the company's own published forecast for the year, is the first test of whether its words can be trusted. In 2025 it raised its range for procedure growth three times, from 13% to 16% in January to 17% to 17.5% in October, and finished at about 18%. It ended 2025 with a non-GAAP gross margin of 67.6%, above its final range, even though its tariff estimate swung from 1.7% of revenue in April to 0.7% in October.[11][12][14][5][7] In 2026 the range has narrowed from 13% to 15% to 14% to 15%, and the first nine months came in at 15%.[5][1][2]

The second test is execution and capital allocation. In January 2025 the company said it would buy its Italian, Spanish and Portuguese distributors and expected to finish in the first half of 2026, and it finished on 2026-03-02.[7][15] It has repurchased shares heavily, $2.30 billion in 2025 and $4.42 billion in cash in the first nine months of 2026, and the share count fell as a result.[7][2] The weakest record is on lawsuits. The 10-K filed in February 2026 said the company could not estimate a loss on the class action, and about eight months later it agreed to pay $450 million.[7][2] A company cannot estimate what it does not know, so we do not call this a broken promise, but it was a large cost that nothing in the earlier guidance pointed to.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue rose from $5,710.1 million in 2021 to $10,064.7 million in 2025, a four-year growth rate of 15.2% a year, and was $11,578.0 million over the twelve months to 2026-09-30 (our calculations from the filings).[9][7][1] Growth is speeding up, not slowing: +9.0% in 2022, +14.5% in 2023, +17.2% in 2024, +20.5% in 2025 and +21.0% in the first nine months of 2026 (our calculations).[7][1] Per-share figures are split-adjusted, because a three-for-one stock split was distributed on 2021-10-04 and the filings restate earlier years.[9] The ledger's 501 daily bars begin after that date, so its prices need no adjustment, and its prices sit on the same scale as the $566.36 year-end 2025 close in the 10-K.[28][7]

The gross margin, which is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of sales, slid from 69.3% in 2021 to 66.0% in 2025.[7][9] For 2025 the 10-K blames new tariffs, depreciation on expanded factories and higher da Vinci 5 costs; we did not source the causes of the earlier decline.[7] It has recovered to 67.4% over nine months of 2026, helped by $63.7 million of tariff refunds.[1][2] The operating margin was 31.9% in 2021, fell to 24.8% in 2023 and was 29.3% in 2025. For the twelve months to September it is 28.4% after the $450 million charge, and 32.3% without it (our calculations).[9][7][1]

Free cash flow dipped to $749.6 million in 2023, when capital spending peaked at $1,064.2 million, and recovered to $2,490.7 million in 2025, 24.7% of revenue, as spending fell to $539.8 million.[7] Over the last twelve months it is $3,598.0 million, 31.1% of revenue, but that includes $450 million of litigation cost that is accrued and not yet paid, so the cleaner figure is $3,148.0 million, 27.2% (our calculations).[2] The ledger shows $3.435 billion for the same year from a data vendor; we use the filed figures.[28]

Stock-based compensation was 7.8% of revenue in 2025 and 7.2% over the last twelve months on the cash flow statement; in 2022 to 2024 it ran 8.1% to 8.3%, so SBC has stayed near the 8% floor: 7.9% in 2021, above it in 2022 to 2024 and under it again in 2025 (our calculations).[7][9][2] The 10-K's own expense measure for 2025 is $802.8 million, which is 7.98% of revenue, a hair under the floor.[7] Repurchases more than offset the shares issued to staff. Dilution is the rise in share count from issuing new shares, and here the count fell: average diluted shares were 361.8 million in the third quarter of 2025 and 351.6 million a year later, down 2.8% (our calculation).[1]

Cash and investments were $6,800.4 million at 2026-09-30, down from $9,034.1 million at year-end after $4,419.2 million of repurchases and a $518.6 million acquisition in nine months.[1][2] The balance sheet shows no borrowings, and total liabilities are $3,231.3 million, so net debt, which is borrowings minus cash, is negative: the company holds net cash.[1] About $450 million of that cash will go to the class action settlement once the court approves it.[2]

The price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E, is the share price divided by earnings per share, which says how many years of current profit the price pays for. At $452.29 the shares trade at 54.7 times the $8.27 of GAAP EPS earned over the last twelve months, or 48.8 times if the $1.00 per share litigation charge is added back (our calculations).[1][7] The company publishes no historical P/E series, so we built a proxy from filed numbers: the year-end closing price divided by that year's diluted EPS, which gives 67.1 times for 2023, 81.3 times for 2024 and 72.0 times for 2025, a median of 72.0 times.[9][8][7] The proxy has three data points and is not a median of daily readings, so we call it our proxy and use it for context only. On it, today's 54.7 times is 24% lower (our calculation).

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 compares static levels with the direction of travel. Hard red lines are separate: net debt of 4.0 times EBITDA or more with weak interest cover, a Stage 4 breakdown, which is a close below a falling 50-day average that sits below a falling 200-day average, and any sign of fraud or a going-concern warning. Stage 2 is the opposite state, a confirmed uptrend, and a breakout is a move above the top of a trading range on heavy volume. The asymmetry test, also called reward-to-risk, divides the gain to our target by the loss to our stop, and a core name needs at least 2.0 to 1.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above rests on an assumption that could be wrong, and the honest bear case, which is the strongest argument against buying, is stated here in full. Fatal flaw 1: the price cut is only the first one

The company says its actions lower instrument revenue per procedure by 3% to 5% at constant mix.[2][25] The cuts are aimed at benign procedures, where hospitals are more sensitive to cost.[2][25] Those are also the procedures that Medtronic's filings for hernia repair and gynecology, Johnson & Johnson's gallbladder and appendix indications and CMR's gallbladder clearance point at.[20][18][21] If rivals force a cut of 8% and not 4%, the extra 4% on instruments, which are 60% of revenue, is 2.4% of revenue, or about $320 million on the Street's 2027 revenue of $13.33 billion (our calculation).[24] After tax and over about 352 million shares that is $0.71 a share, 6.8% of our $10.50 (our calculation). A cut that deep would also tell us the platform has less pricing power than its history suggests.

Fatal flaw 2: US procedure growth keeps falling

US da Vinci procedures grew 10% in the third quarter, down from 16%, and they are about 62% of volume (559 of 906 thousand).[2] Management names lower ACA enrollment, changes to Medicaid eligibility and possible changes to the 340B drug-pricing program as risks it is watching.[25] If US growth were 6% and outside-US growth stayed at 20%, worldwide growth would be about 11% (our calculation using the US share of last year's procedures, 64%), which is under the bottom of the 14% to 15% guide and under our 12% scoring line. Procedure growth is the base of instrument revenue, so this flaw would hit revenue and the multiple together.

Fatal flaw 3: the courts narrow the instrument franchise

The $450 million settlement is subject to preliminary and final court approval, and the agreed injunctive relief limits what the company may do about third-party repair of instruments; we did not find its terms in the pages we read.[1][2] The Ninth Circuit reversal of the repair-company judgment, the rehearing petition and the new risk factor on remanufactured instruments all point at the same revenue line.[2] The company says it cannot estimate a loss or range for the repair-company case, so we do not put a number on it and show it as not sized.[2]

Several terms need plain definitions. CMP means current market price. A basis point, or bps, is one hundredth of a percent, so a 50 bps risk budget means we accept losing 0.5% of net worth if the exit price is hit. Portfolio heat is the total of those accepted losses across all holdings, and our cap is 12%. Risk-unit sizing sets the position so that hitting the exit price costs no more than the risk budget, subject to a ceiling for the tier. Tiers run from TIER_1 (7% to 10% of net worth, 100 bps) to TIER_4 (1% to 2%, 35 bps), and TIER_0 means zero size. Our yardstick is TIER_3 (2% to 3.5%, 50 bps): a high-quality business, a price that leaves little cushion and two unresolved legal and pricing questions.

A stop loss is the price at which we exit because the reason for buying has failed. The price chart defines that reason through structural invalidation: the level below which the pattern that justified the purchase no longer holds. An SMA, or simple moving average, gives equal weight to every day in its window, and an EMA, or exponential moving average, gives more weight to recent days.
