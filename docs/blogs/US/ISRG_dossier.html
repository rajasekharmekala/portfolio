1. What the company does

Intuitive Surgical builds and sells soft-tissue surgical robots. A surgeon sits at a console a few feet from the patient and moves hand controls; the robot translates those movements into tiny, steady motions of instruments inserted through small cuts. The aim is less pain, shorter hospital stays and more precise surgery than open operations allow. The company's own filing describes the da Vinci system as its flagship, with the Ion system used for lung biopsies as a second platform.[1]

How it earns money

There are three revenue streams, and the mix matters more than the total. In 2025, instruments and accessories brought in $6,018.9 million, systems (the robots themselves) $2,473.7 million, and service contracts $1,572.1 million.[1] Instruments are single-use or limited-use parts that wear out with each operation, so they behave like razor blades. Together with service, they made up 75.4% of 2025 revenue, which we call the recurring share.[1] A hospital that buys or leases a robot keeps paying every time it operates.

The company is shifting how it sells robots. In the second quarter of 2026 it placed 468 da Vinci systems, of which 254 were under operating leases and 131 of those were usage-based, meaning the hospital pays per procedure instead of an upfront price.[4] That lowers the barrier to entry for hospitals, and it pushes more of the revenue into the recurring bucket over time. It also makes system revenue less lumpy, but it defers cash.

Where it sells

About 68% of 2025 revenue came from the United States and 32% from the rest of the world.[1] In March 2026 the company bought three distributors (ab medica, Abex and Excelencia Robótica) for about $533.1 million to take direct control of sales in several markets.[3] The company had roughly 17,021 employees at the end of 2025.[1]

The size of the installed base

At the end of the second quarter of 2026 there were 11,710 da Vinci systems in hospitals (up 12% in a year) and 1,096 Ion systems (up 21%).[4] Worldwide procedures grew 16% in the quarter, with da Vinci procedures up 15% and Ion procedures up 36%.[4] Each installed robot is a small toll booth, and the count of robots times the use per robot is the engine of the whole company.

Glossary

These terms recur in the rest of the book.

  • ATR: average true range, the typical daily price swing; we use it as a ruler for distances on the chart.
  • Free cash flow: cash from operations minus spending on equipment and buildings.
  • Asymmetry: expected gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop. Higher is better.
  • 200-day average: the average closing price of the last 200 sessions, a common line between long-term strength and weakness.
  • Stage 1: a stock that has stopped falling and is moving sideways, not yet rising.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation): share-based compensation, the cost of paying staff in stock.

What this means for an owner

You own a toll-booth business with a very large installed base and a high recurring share, and the revenue it earns tends to grow with surgical volume rather than with hospital budgets alone. That is the quality part of the story.

Next we ask whether the quality is durable, and who is trying to take it away.

2. Why the business is strong, and where it is exposed

A business is strong when customers keep coming back and rivals cannot easily copy it. Intuitive has two such advantages. The first is the installed base described above: surgeons train on da Vinci, hospitals build operating rooms around it, and switching means retraining every team. The second is evidence: the company has two decades of clinical data and a procedure growth rate that has held at or above the low-to-mid teens.[1]

The competition is real for the first time

The 10-K names Johnson & Johnson and Medtronic as competitors.[1] Medtronic's Hugo system received FDA clearance on December 3, 2025 for urologic procedures (prostatectomy, nephrectomy, cystectomy), backed by a 137-patient trial with a 98.5% surgical success rate.[7] The first US commercial case followed in February 2026 at Cleveland Clinic.[8][9] Johnson & Johnson's Ottava system reportedly received FDA De Novo authorization in July 2026 for general-surgery use; we read that from trade press summaries only and could not open the company release.[10][11] Neither rival has shown up in Intuitive's procedure growth yet. But markets price the possibility early, and that is a plausible part of why the stock fell from $603.88 to $328.57.[13]

Legal exposure

A jury sided with Intuitive in an antitrust case brought by Surgical Instrument Service Co. in January 2025, and the matter is on appeal to the Ninth Circuit.[1] We treat an adverse appeal outcome as a risk to third-party instrument pricing, not to the installed base.

Dilution versus buybacks

Share-based compensation was $788.2 million in 2025, or 7.83% of revenue.[1] Our Layer 1 floor asks for under 5%, so read literally it fails. We flag it honestly but also note that the share count is flat: diluted shares were 362.0 million in 2024 and 362.7 million in 2025, because buybacks of $2,295.3 million offset the issuance.[1] The cost is real, but it is paid in cash through buybacks, not hidden.

What this means for an owner

The franchise is strong and the operating KPIs are healthy. The one thing that is changing is that competitors now have FDA-cleared products. That does not break the thesis today, but it removes the assumption of an unchallenged monopoly from the valuation.

The momentum gate in plain words

The gate has two tests and the stock must pass at least one to be bought. Test A asks for a proper uptrend structure with the price near its pivot; ISRG fails because the 200-day line is falling and the price is 2.04 ATR above the nearest 20-day-average support; the 8.35 ATR figure is the distance to the 63-day base pivot, far beyond the 1.0 ATR the test allows. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

An honest estimate states what would make it fail. We built the strongest case against our own conclusion, which is a rejection, and then asked which single claim it rests on.

Case against our own rejection

The case for buying is that the business is compounding at close to 20% growth, margins are rising, there is no debt and the 50-day average has turned up. If the third-quarter report on October 20 shows procedure growth at or above 15% and gross margin at the top of guidance, the stock may break above the 200-day line immediately and never revisit $400.65. Waiting for a pullback could mean missing the move. That risk is real, and we accept it as the price of buying with a margin of safety.

Case against our upside

Competition is the largest threat. Hugo has FDA clearance for urology and Ottava for general surgery, and hospitals now have a credible second robot to bargain with.[7][10] If discounting appears in instruments and systems pricing, margin guidance of 68% to 69% would be at risk. Two other threats are tariffs, where the replacement 10% tariff runs only 150 days from February 24, 2026 and what follows is N/A, and the Ninth Circuit appeal of the antitrust case.[4][1]

The hinge claim

Everything depends on one claim: that the 200-day average at $443.41 is the right breakout line for the measured move. The average falls each day, so the line itself will drop by about $0.70 per session at the recent rate ($487.79 to $443.41 over 63 sessions), which would lower the target slightly and raise asymmetry. We therefore recompute the target at each review rather than fix it.

Data limits

  • Analyst EPS is on a non-GAAP basis and our history is GAAP, so we did not multiply one by the other.[12]
  • The median P/E uses 680 sessions, not a full three years.
  • The Ottava details come from trade-press summaries; the J&J release was not accessible to us.[10][11]
  • The earnings date of October 20, 2026 (call at 1:30pm Pacific) was taken from a calendar aggregator, and the company's own investor page was not accessible.[26][27]
  • Probabilities are our judgment, not statistics.

What this means for an owner

If you already own the stock, none of this says sell: the business case holds. It says that adding at $424 gives you worse odds than waiting, and that a sharp rise on the earnings date could change the picture quickly.
