1. What the company does

Intuitive Surgical builds and sells the da Vinci surgical systems that surgeons use for operations on soft tissue, meaning organs and muscle between the pelvis and the neck. A da Vinci system has a surgeon console, a cart at the patient's side and a vision system. The company also sells Ion, a thin robotic catheter that takes lung biopsies[2][7]. A hospital buys or leases the machine, pays for instruments and accessories every time it is used, and pays for service under an annual contract.

Three revenue lines pay the bills. Instruments and accessories, the tools that wear out or expire, were $1,797.4 million in the third quarter of 2026, which is 59% of the $3,048.7 million total (our calculation)[1]. Systems, the robots themselves, were $769.1 million. Service, the annual contracts on robots already installed, was $482.2 million. Management said that a da Vinci instrument and accessory set earned about $1,860 per procedure in the quarter, against $1,800 a year earlier[3].

Instruments, service and operating-lease revenue together are what the company calls recurring revenue, because they return each time a robot is used or a contract renews. It was 84% of third-quarter revenue and 85% over nine months, and the same measure was 83% in 2023 and 84% in 2025[2][7]. The company also reports $3.8 billion of remaining performance obligations, which are signed contracts for work not yet delivered, and about half will be recognized within 12 months[2]. In the third quarter more than half of the new robots were leased and not sold: 291 of the 546 systems placed were operating leases, and 160 of those were usage-based, which means the customer pays per procedure[1]. A lease moves revenue from the day of placement to the years of use.

Customers are hospitals and, as a newer focus, outpatient surgery centers. The company installed 18 systems in US surgery centers in the third quarter, 15 of them the lower-cost XiR model[3]. Almost 7,000 customers in more than 70 countries used da Vinci in the quarter, at over 10,000 procedures a day[3]. The United States produced $6,815.8 million of the $10,064.7 million of 2025 revenue, 68% (our calculation), and the other 32% came from outside the US[7].

How a robot placement turns into revenue for years

A placement is a delivered system, by sale or lease. The company placed 546 da Vinci systems in the third quarter against 427 a year earlier, and 315 were the fifth-generation da Vinci 5, up from 240[1]. The installed base, which is the count of systems in customers' hands, reached 12,094 da Vinci systems, up 12%, and 1,152 Ion systems, up 21%[1]. Every installed system is a stream of future instrument orders and a service contract.

Utilization, the procedures done per system, matters as much as the count. The chief financial officer said average utilization rose 2% in the quarter[3]. When utilization is flat and placements rise, revenue growth runs ahead of procedure growth for a while, which is exactly what happened: revenue grew 22% while worldwide procedures grew 15%[1]. That gap is where this report looks hardest, because placement-led growth can fade faster than procedure-led growth.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of what was sold, as a share of revenue. Operating margin is revenue minus all running costs, as a share of revenue.
  • EPS is earnings per share (the company's profit divided across all its shares). GAAP means generally accepted accounting principles, the standard US accounting rules, and non-GAAP is the company's own adjusted version, which leaves out items such as stock pay and one-time charges.
  • Free cash flow, short form FCF, is cash from operations minus capital spending. SBC is stock-based compensation, the cost of paying employees in shares.
  • ROIC is return on invested capital, the profit earned on each dollar tied up in the business. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating cash profit.
  • RSI is the relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has moved, where above 70 is usually called hot. ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price swing in dollars, which here is $12.69.
  • A placement is a system delivered to a customer, by sale or lease. A trade-in is an older robot returned when a customer takes a newer one. The installed base is the number of systems in customers' hands.
  • Ledger is the dated data file for this session: prices, moving averages, momentum tests and vendor estimates for each stock, built from daily bars. Reverse DCF is a calculation run backwards: it finds the growth rate of cash flow that today's price already assumes. Consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who cover the stock.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The company's strength starts with the number of robots already in hospitals. It had 12,094 da Vinci systems installed at 2026-09-30, up 12% in a year, and the Ion fleet grew 21% to 1,152[1]. Surgeons train on the system, hospitals build operating-room routines around it, and each procedure uses Intuitive's own instruments. Management cites more than 48,000 peer-reviewed publications on da Vinci results[3]. Those facts fit with recurring revenue of 84% of sales and with about 3,153,000 da Vinci procedures in 2025, up 18%[7].

Demand is healthy but slowing at the edges. Da Vinci procedures grew 14% in the third quarter, to about 906,000[2]. In the US they grew 10%, against 16% a year earlier, and outside the US 20%[2][3]. Management called the US figure consistent with what it described in July and pointed to a hard comparison, while it named lower ACA enrollment, changes to Medicaid eligibility and possible changes to the 340B drug-pricing program as things it is watching[3]. Worldwide growth has slipped one point each quarter: 17%, 16%, 15% and 14%[6][5][4][1].

The da Vinci 5 launch is working as a replacement cycle. Of the 546 systems placed in the third quarter, 315 were da Vinci 5, 58% (our calculation), and the installed base of da Vinci 5 was 2,023 systems[1][2]. In 2025, 437 of 1,721 placements involved a trade-in, up from 150 of 1,526 in 2024[7]. A trade-in cycle runs out, and the company must then rely on new hospitals and on extra systems per hospital. US placements rose 27% to 335 in the quarter, driven by customers buying several systems at once[3].

For the first time the moat is being tested in the US on a wide front. Medtronic's Hugo system was cleared for urologic surgery on 2025-12-03 and the company filed on 2026-06-03 for general and gynecologic surgery[19][20]. Johnson & Johnson received De Novo authorization for Ottava on 2026-07-22 for several general surgery procedures and said it will launch with select customers first[18]. CMR Surgical's Versius Plus was cleared for gallbladder removal and has a gynecology filing pending[21][22]. On 2026-10-12 Stryker announced about $400 million of investment in CMR, the exclusive US sales agency and an option to buy the company[23]. The 10-K names fourteen companies as entrants or declared entrants[7].

None of the pages we read gives a competitor's revenue, procedure count or price, so we cannot say how much share any rival has taken. We can say that a US hospital now has cleared choices it lacked a year ago, and that the company's planned price cuts are its answer.

Where the moat is under pressure: price, China and the courts

On the third-quarter call management put a number on its own price cuts. A China price reduction starts in November 2026, and from the first half of 2027 the company will extend the life of some core instruments and add volume-based pricing. Together these lower da Vinci instrument revenue per procedure by about 3% to 5% once fully in place, holding procedure, product and country mix constant[3]. Management said the remaining effect builds over 2027 and is not a step, and that the 3% to 5% is below the 7% it estimated when it launched its first Extended Use Program in 2020[3]. Instruments were $6,018.9 million of 2025 revenue, 60% (our calculation)[7], so a 1% price change moves revenue by roughly 0.6%.

China is the weak spot. The 10-Q says that system demand there has been hurt by domestic competitors and a government campaign on healthcare, and that fewer systems were placed in China in the third quarter than the company anticipated[2]. The company does not publish China revenue, so we mark it not published; the filings split revenue only into US and outside-US[7].

Two legal matters touch the instrument franchise. In October 2026 the company agreed to pay $450 million, subject to court approval, to settle the class action over third-party repair, and it also agreed to injunctive relief whose terms we did not find[1][2]. On 2026-08-13 the Ninth Circuit reversed the trial judgment in a separate repair-company case, and the company asked for a rehearing on 2026-09-29[2]. The company says it cannot estimate a loss for that case. These matters are why the moat score is strong but not clean.

The second test is execution and capital allocation. The 10-K for 2024 said the company expected to complete its purchase of the Italian, Spanish and Portuguese distributors in the first half of 2026, and it finished on 2026-03-02[8][15]. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue rose from $5,710.1 million in 2021 to $10,064.7 million in 2025, a four-year growth rate of 15.2% a year, and was $11,578.0 million over the twelve months to 2026-09-30 (our calculations from the filings)[9][7][1]. Growth is speeding up, not slowing: +9.0% in 2022, +14.5% in 2023, +17.2% in 2024, +20.5% in 2025 and +21.0% in the first nine months of 2026 (our calculations). Per-share figures are split-adjusted, because a three-for-one stock split was distributed on 2021-10-04 and the filings restate earlier years[9].

The gross margin, the share of revenue left after the direct cost of sales, slid from 69.3% in 2021 to 66.0% in 2025 and has recovered to 67.4% over nine months of 2026, helped by $63.7 million of tariff refunds[7][1]. The operating margin was 31.9% in 2021, fell to 24.8% in 2023 and was 29.3% in 2025. For the twelve months to September it is 28.4% after the $450 million charge and 32.3% without it (our calculations).

Free cash flow dipped to $749.6 million in 2023, when capital spending was $1,064.2 million, and capital spending peaked at $1,111.2 million in 2024. Free cash flow then recovered to $2,490.7 million in 2025, 24.7% of revenue, as spending fell to $539.8 million[7]. Over the last twelve months it is $3,598.0 million, 31.1% of revenue (our calculation from cash from operations of $4,089.5 million less capital spending of $491.5 million)[2][7]. A data vendor shows $3.435 billion for the same period in the session ledger[37]; the filed figure is the one we use, and an independent S&P-based page shows $3.60 billion[27].

Stock-based compensation was 7.8% of revenue in 2025 and 7.2% over the last twelve months, against 8.1% to 8.3% in 2022 to 2024, so it has stayed near the 8% floor[7][2]. Repurchases more than offset the shares issued to staff, and dilution, the rise in share count from new issuance, is negative here: average diluted shares were 361.8 million in the third quarter of 2025 and 351.6 million a year later[1]. Cash and investments were $6,800.4 million at 2026-09-30, down from $9,034.1 million at year-end after $4,419.2 million of repurchases and a $518.6 million acquisition in nine months[1][2]. The balance sheet shows no borrowings, so net debt, which is borrowings minus cash, is negative: the company holds net cash. About $450 million of that cash will go to the class action settlement once the court approves it.

The price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E, is the share price divided by earnings per share. Fatal flaw 1: the price cut is only the first one

The company says its actions lower instrument revenue per procedure by 3% to 5% at constant mix. The cuts are aimed at benign, high-volume procedures such as gallbladder removal and hernia repair, where hospitals are more sensitive to cost[3]. Those are also the procedures that Medtronic's hernia and gynecology filings, Johnson & Johnson's gallbladder and appendix indications and CMR's gallbladder clearance point at[20][18][21]. If rivals force a cut of 8% and not 4%, the extra 4% on instruments, which are 60% of revenue, is 2.4% of revenue, or about $320 million on the Street's 2027 revenue of $13.34 billion (our calculation)[25]. Taxed at 22%, which is the company's guided rate for the fourth quarter[3], and spread over about 352 million shares, that is $0.71 a share, 6.7% of our $10.52 (our calculation). A cut that deep would also tell us the platform has less pricing power than its history suggests.

Fatal flaw 2: US procedure growth keeps falling

US da Vinci procedures grew 10% in the third quarter, down from 16%, and the US is about 64% of volume (2,012 of 3,153 thousand procedures in 2025, our calculation)[2][7]. Management names lower ACA enrollment, Medicaid eligibility changes and possible 340B changes as risks it is watching[3]. If US growth were 6% and growth outside the US stayed at 20%, worldwide growth would be about 11% (our calculation using last year's mix), under the bottom of the 14% to 15% guide and under our 12% scoring line. Procedure growth is the base of instrument revenue, so this flaw would hit revenue and the multiple together.

The $450 million settlement is subject to preliminary and final court approval, and the agreed injunctive relief limits what the company may do about third-party repair; we did not find its terms in the pages we read[1][2]. The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. That means we do not buy at today's price, and we leave a standing buy order lower down. The nearest support, the 20-day average at $426.00, is 1.43 ATR below the close[37]. Our rule allows a purchase only within 1.6 ATR of support, and then only as a limit order, which is a buy order that fills at a set price or lower and never above it. On 2026-10-23 the price was 2.40 ATR above support, where our rule allowed no buy at all. Support is closer now, but the reward-to-risk ratio is the problem. Reward-to-risk is the gain we expect divided by the loss we would take if the exit price is hit. It is 0.28 to 1 at the $444.18 close, and a core name needs at least 2.0 to 1. A buy at the close would make a gain of $18.82 on our central value against a loss of $67.76 at the stop. The price already reflects our central case, so our rules send the idea to a wait.

Several terms need plain definitions. CMP means current market price. A basis point, or bps, is one hundredth of a percent, so a 50 bps risk budget means we accept losing 0.5% of net worth if the exit price is hit. Portfolio heat is the total of those accepted losses across all holdings, and our cap is 12%. Risk-unit sizing sets the position so that hitting the exit price costs no more than the risk budget, subject to a ceiling for the tier. Tiers run from TIER_1 (7% to 10% of net worth, 100 bps) to TIER_4 (1% to 2%, 35 bps), and our yardstick is TIER_3 (2% to 3.5%, 50 bps): a high-quality business, a price that leaves little cushion and two unresolved legal and pricing questions.

A stop loss is the price at which we exit because the reason for buying has failed. Our stop is $376.42, one ATR ($12.69) under the 20-day swing low of $389.11[37]. That is 6.83% below $404.00, the top of the buy band, 3.97% below $392.00, the bottom, and 15.3% below the close.

The rule for a core name in an uptrend is the lower of the swing low and the 50-day average, minus one ATR. The 50-day average ($399.37) is higher than the swing low, so the swing low governs. ISRG is not yet in Stage 2, so the rule for stocks that are basing is also plausible: the 63-day base low of $345.25 minus one ATR, which is $332.56. We use the nearer stop because the price is above a rising 50-day average and the base low is 22% below the close. On the wider stop the ratio at $404.00 is 0.83 to 1, which fails the floor. The highest price that would give 2.0 to 1 on the wider stop is $376.04, under our swing low. So the plan works only on the nearer stop.

Two different exit rules apply, depending on whether we own the stock. Before any fill, a close below $389.11, the 20-day swing low, cancels the buy order and triggers a rerun of this underwriting, because the floor under the buy band has failed. After a fill, the exit is the $376.42 stop. The gap between the two is deliberate. The $389.11 rule keeps us from buying into a broken floor, and the $376.42 stop gives a position we already own one ATR of room below that floor.
