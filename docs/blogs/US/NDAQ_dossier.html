Before you read

Nasdaq runs stock exchanges, but it earns most of its money from market data, stock indexes and software sold to banks.[1] The business is healthy: free cash flow is 39 percent of net revenue, and net debt is about 2.5 times yearly operating profit before depreciation.[1][2] We rate it WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT because the stock closed at $104.72, far above its 50-day average after a 12 percent jump in one week, so the reward-to-risk ratio is only 1.14 to 1.[27] A limit order between $94.50 and $96.20 would clear our 2.5 to 1 floor at 3.50 to 1 or better, and the floor still holds up to $98.39 on today's inputs. 1. What the company does

Nasdaq is best known for its stock exchange, but the exchange is now the smaller part of the company. In the third quarter of 2026 the company reported $1,481 million of net revenue, which is total revenue less the rebates and fees it passes on to traders and clearing partners.[1] Of that, $1,172 million, about 79 percent, came from what Nasdaq calls Solutions: data, listings, indexes and software.[1] Only $309 million, about 21 percent, came from running markets and clearing trades.[1] Think of Nasdaq as a toll road that also owns a software company. The toll road still matters, because trading volume swings its results, but the software and data business is where most of the growth and most of the stability now sit.

The first division, Capital Access Platforms, earned $641 million in the quarter, up 20 percent.[1] It holds three businesses. Data and Listing Services ($224 million) charges companies to list their shares and sells market data. Index ($286 million, up 39 percent) licenses the Nasdaq-100 and other indexes, and earns fees on the money that exchange-traded funds hold against those indexes. Workflow and Insights ($131 million) sells investor-relations, board and analytics software, including the eVestment database.[1] The listing franchise won 79 percent of eligible new listings in the quarter and 8 of the 10 largest operating-company IPOs, which raised $32 billion; the funds tracking Nasdaq indexes held a record $1.160 trillion at quarter end.[1]

The second division, Financial Technology, sells mission-critical software to banks, brokers, regulators and other exchanges. It earned $529 million, up 16 percent.[1] It has three parts: Financial Crime Management Technology, which is the Verafin anti-money-laundering product ($103 million); Regulatory Technology, which covers the Surveillance product and the AxiomSL reporting software ($123 million); and Capital Markets Technology, which covers the Calypso trading and risk platform, the exchange technology Nasdaq sells to other markets, and hosting services ($303 million).[1] Much of this came from the Adenza purchase, which closed in 2023 for $5,750 million in cash plus 85,608,414 Nasdaq shares valued at $48.71 each, and which left $5,933 million of goodwill and $5,050 million of acquired intangible assets on the balance sheet.[4] Software customers sign multi-year contracts: subscription agreements are generally three years long, paid in advance.[3]

The third division, Market Services, runs the Nasdaq equity and options exchanges in the United States and the Nordic cash and derivatives markets. Its gross revenue was $1,221 million in the quarter, but Nasdaq paid out $623 million in rebates to traders who add liquidity and $289 million in brokerage, clearance and exchange fees, leaving $309 million of net revenue, up 2 percent.[1] Volumes matter here. Industry options volume averaged 63.8 million contracts a day against 55.8 million a year earlier, while industry cash-equity volume was 16.9 billion shares a day against 17.6 billion.[1] On October 15 Nasdaq closed the purchase of LeveL Markets, the third-largest alternative trading system in the United States by volume, which management expects to add $7 million to $8 million of revenue in the fourth quarter.[1][14]

Who pays Nasdaq, and why they keep paying, explains the rest of this report. Listed companies pay annual fees because leaving an exchange is costly and rare. Asset managers pay index fees that rise with the money in funds tracking the index. Banks pay software subscriptions because the software is wired into their risk, reporting and fraud systems. Traders pay per trade. The first three streams repeat, which is why Nasdaq reports annual recurring revenue (ARR) of $3,365 million, up 12 percent as reported and 14 percent on an organic basis.[1]

How the pieces fit together

Nasdaq describes its plan as One Nasdaq: sell the same banks and exchanges more than one product. FinTech signed 5 cross-sells in the quarter, where a cross-sell means an existing client of one product buys a second product, and cross-sells made up over 15 percent of the FinTech sales pipeline.[1] The company also keeps trimming its portfolio. It sold Nasdaq Fund Secondaries and Nasdaq Metrio in the quarter and is winding down a sustainability advisory business, which management says would have added about 0.5 point to Workflow and Insights growth had those items been excluded from the prior-year period.[14] That matters because it means the reported growth rates are slightly understated, but it also means management is willing to prune businesses that do not grow.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Net revenue: total revenue minus transaction-based expenses (rebates and pass-through fees). It is the number Nasdaq uses to measure growth.
  • Annual recurring revenue (ARR): the yearly value of contracts that renew, such as software subscriptions and data licenses.
  • Organic growth: growth with acquisitions, sales of businesses and currency moves removed.
  • ETP assets under management: money held in exchange-traded funds and similar products that track a Nasdaq index.
  • Free cash flow: cash from operations minus the cash spent on equipment and capitalized software.
  • ASR (accelerated share repurchase): a deal in which the company pays a bank up front for shares that the bank delivers over the following weeks.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Nasdaq's strength comes from three positions that are hard to copy. Its listing franchise lets companies keep a ticker for decades, and the company reported a 74 percent win rate on new listings so far this year, with Hilton announcing a transfer of its listing in October.[1][14] Its index business owns the Nasdaq-100 brand, which sits under funds that held $1.160 trillion at quarter end, up from $829 billion a year earlier, and it signed new partnerships with BlackRock and State Street that already exceed $1 billion in combined assets.[1][14] Its software business sells products that banks cannot easily replace because they sit inside daily compliance, risk and fraud routines; Nasdaq ranked second on the Chartis RiskTech100 this year, up from fifth.[1]

The evidence for strength shows up in margins and cash. Operating profit was 49 percent of net revenue on the GAAP measure and 57 percent on the company's adjusted measure, each up from the prior year.[1] Divisional margins were 64 percent in Capital Access Platforms, 46 percent in Financial Technology and 61 percent in Market Services.[1][2] Over the last twelve months the company generated about $2.3 billion of free cash flow, which management says is 98 percent conversion, and our own arithmetic from the filings gives $2,258 million.[14][2][3]

How much of the growth is the company and how much is the market

Management itself splits growth into "alpha," which is growth the company earns through clients, products and pricing, and "beta," which is growth that comes from rising market values and volumes. In the third quarter, net revenue grew 14 percent, made up of 10.5 points of alpha and 3.5 points of beta.[14] In Index, revenue grew 39 percent, but only 15 points of that came from alpha; the remaining 24 points came from a record asset level and higher derivatives volume.[14] This is the main reason we do not simply extrapolate the quarter. Index revenue is a growing and valuable stream, but its fastest growth came while the market was rising, and it will not look like that in a falling market.

The same logic applies to Market Services. It set a record for a third quarter, yet net revenue grew only 2 percent and its operating margin fell 5 points to 61 percent. Management said the capture rate on U.S. options, which is the net revenue kept per contract, was a strong $0.09 but rounded down because more order flow came from consolidators, and that European equity capture fell after a pricing change.[14] Market Services is also where a quieter market costs the company most, since lower volume cuts both volume-based fees and the market data it sells.

What weakens the case

Three things weaken the case. First, the balance sheet is full of purchase accounting: goodwill is $14,247 million and acquired intangible assets are $6,106 million, together $20,353 million against total equity of $12,003 million, so tangible equity is negative by about $8.35 billion.[1] That is normal after a large software acquisition, but it means static return measures look weaker than the cash the business produces, and it means a failed integration would produce a large write-down.

Second, the company says it faces intense price competition in all areas of its business, and its listing, index and trading products all compete with other exchanges and data firms.[3] Third, the IPO market, which supplied record listing proceeds this year, showed what management called a modest softening in recent weeks.[14] Listings revenue is a small piece of the whole, but a new-listing slowdown also reduces future data and workflow revenue, so it is a leading signal we will watch.

Put together, the business is strong where it is recurring and weaker where it depends on markets staying high and active. That conclusion shapes everything after this chapter: it supports owning the stock, but it is the reason we want a lower entry price and a stop that respects a market-driven pullback.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A management team earns trust by doing what it said. The Adenza acquisition closed on November 1, 2023, and the company set three hard promises at that point: $80 million of annual net expense synergies by the end of the second year, $100 million of revenue synergies over the long term, and leverage of 4.0 times within 18 months and about 3.3 times within 36 months.[19] On cost, the company later raised its goal to $140 million by the end of 2025, and then reported more than $160 million of net expense synergies actioned.[5][6][2] On leverage, it reported reaching its gross leverage milestone ahead of schedule in the third quarter of 2025, S&P raised the debt rating from BBB to BBB+, and gross leverage stood at 2.5 times at September 30, 2026.[9][14]

The revenue promise is the one we cannot verify. Management repeated that it expects more than $100 million of run-rate revenue from cross-sells by the end of 2027, and it counts the number of cross-sells: 42 by the end of 2025, then 1, 7 and 5 in the first three quarters of 2026, which we add to 55.[6][7][8][1] It does not publish the dollars, so we cannot say whether the $100 million is on track, only that the sales activity is steady.

Capital allocation shifted as the debt came down. In 2025 the company repaid $826 million of debt and bought back $616 million of stock. In the first nine months of 2026 it bought back $1,226 million of stock and paid $500 million of dividends, while total debt moved from $9,004 million to $8,740 million.[6][3][2] A $315 million to $385 million accelerated share repurchase began in October.[14] Spending on expenses has crept up with revenue: 2026 non-GAAP operating expense guidance went from $2.455 billion to $2.535 billion in January, to $2.485 billion to $2.545 billion in April, to $2.530 billion to $2.570 billion in July, and to $2.550 billion to $2.580 billion in October.[6][7][8][1] The raises are tied to higher revenue and two acquisitions, which is normal, but they show costs are not falling as fast as promised.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 shows what Nasdaq earns, how much cash it keeps, and how much it owes, first as a five-year record and then against the entry standards in our scoring file.[28] Net revenue grew from $3,420 million in 2021 to $5,249 million in 2025.[4][3] Over the three years from 2022 to 2025 that is a compound rate of 13.6 percent a year, and about 12 percent in 2025 on an organic basis.[6] Part of the jump came from the Adenza acquisition in late 2023, so the reported figures overstate what the company would have grown without a deal.

Profit tells a messier story. GAAP earnings per share (EPS) fell from $2.35 in 2021 to $1.93 in 2024 because the Adenza deal added interest, deal costs and the amortization of acquired intangible assets, then recovered to $3.09 in 2025.[4][3] The company's own adjusted measure, which removes acquisition amortization and one-time items, rose from $2.52 to $3.48 over the same period and reached $4.03 for the twelve months to September 2026.[4][3][1] We show both and lean on cash flow, which is harder to adjust.

Cash flow is the best part. Operating cash flow was $1,083 million in 2021 and $2,255 million in 2025, and capital spending, which includes capitalized software because that cost sits in property and equipment, was only $266 million in 2025.[4][3] Free cash flow (FCF), which is operating cash flow minus that spending, reached $2,258 million for the twelve months to September 2026. That is 39.1 percent of net revenue, or 24.7 percent of the larger gross revenue figure, so the 12 percent test passes either way.[2][3][1] Stock-based compensation (SBC), the cost of paying employees in shares, was $165 million in 2025, or 3.1 percent of net revenue.[3]

The balance sheet shows the effect of Adenza and the work to repair it. Total debt rose from $5,399 million at the end of 2022 to $10,454 million at the end of 2023, then fell to $8,740 million by September 30, 2026.[4][3][2] We measure the burden as net debt divided by earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA), using GAAP operating income plus depreciation and amortization as the earnings figure. On that basis the ratio peaked at 5.26 times at the end of 2023 and is 2.47 times now, below the 3.0 times line. The 4.0 times red line, which only bites when interest cover is also below 2.0 times, was not tripped in any year because cover never fell below 4.3 times.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter argues against our own verdict. The strongest honest bear case does not say the business is bad. It says that the business is good, that everyone knows it, that the price has just risen 12 percent in a week to a 52-week high, and that a good company bought at a full price after a great quarter often returns little for a year. About a quarter of last quarter's growth was market-driven, the stock is at 26 times trailing adjusted earnings, and our own central case of $123.17 assumes the multiple holds.[14][1][21]

The estimates in chapters 3 and 5 depend on a short list of assumptions. Each one has a weak point, a sign that would show it has failed, and a date when we will know. The three scenarios that follow are the ways the verdict could fail badly, not the ways it could fail mildly.

Fatal flaw 1: the market tailwind reverses

Index and trading revenue are tied to market levels and activity. Index revenue was $1,010 million over the last twelve months, which is 17.5 percent of net revenue, and management said only about 15 of its 39 points of third-quarter growth was alpha.[1][14] If average assets under management in index funds fell 20 percent and fees per dollar held steady, index revenue would fall by roughly $200 million, which is about $0.28 a share after tax at the trailing tax rate, or about 6.8 percent of 2026 adjusted earnings. We treat that as a rough ceiling on the effect from index alone, since part of index revenue is subscription-based.

In the same scenario, volumes and IPOs would also fall. Market Services margin has already dropped 5 points on a record quarter. A drawdown would likely cut 2027 earnings below the lowest street figure of $4.55 and compress the multiple at the same time, which is the $90.45 outcome. Nasdaq would still generate cash, so this is not a solvency risk, but it could cost 14 percent from today's price and 6 percent from the order zone before the stop is reached.

Fatal flaw 2: FinTech growth stalls

Financial Technology ARR growth of 18 percent is a record, and records are hard to extend. It rests on the Adenza products, on pricing increases in trade-management services, and on a surge in anti-crime enterprise signings.[14] Management says sales cycles and contract terms are unchanged. A slowdown would show first in ARR growth, then in revenue growth three to four quarters later.

If FinTech organic ARR growth fell to 10 percent, total organic ARR growth would drop to about 9 percent and the stock would likely lose its premium to peers. A stall of that kind would also call the revenue-synergy promise into doubt, because cross-sell dollars are not reported and cannot be checked. This is the risk most tied to the Adenza purchase, and it is why we kept the integration KPI at WATCH on verifiability even though costs and leverage beat the promises.

Fatal flaw 3: leverage returns through acquisitions or buybacks

Net debt is 2.47 times EBITDA after a long repair. The company has begun to return nearly all free cash flow, drew $269 million of commercial paper this year, signed a new $1.5 billion revolving facility with a 3.75 times covenant and, in October, closed the LeveL purchase.[2][12][1] A larger deal could take leverage back toward 3.3 times, the level management had promised to reach only by November 2026, and would repeat the pattern that produced a 5.26 times ratio in 2023.[19][4]

This risk is not a likely failure; management has shown discipline for two years. It is, however, the failure that would be hardest to see coming, because it would arrive as an announcement. The tell would be a new financing 8-K, a pause in buybacks or an upward revision to acquisition-related expense guidance.
