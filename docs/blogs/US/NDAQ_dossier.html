1. What the company does

Nasdaq is best known for the stock exchange, but that is now the smallest part of how it earns money. In the third quarter of 2026 it reported net revenue of $1,481 million, and $1,172 million of that, or 79%, came from what it calls Solutions: data, listings, index licences and software.[1][21] Net revenue means revenue after subtracting the rebates and fees that Nasdaq pays out on each trade. In the 2025 fiscal year gross revenues were $8,262 million and net revenue was $5,249 million.[3] The gap is mostly money that Nasdaq collects from trading firms and hands straight back to other trading firms, so net revenue is the fair measure of what the company keeps.

Think of Nasdaq as three shops under one roof. The first shop, Capital Access Platforms, charges companies to list their shares and charges investors for data. Its biggest line is Index: Nasdaq builds indexes such as the Nasdaq-100, and fund companies pay a licence fee so that their exchange-traded funds can track them. By the end of September 2026 there were 487 licensed products holding $1.160 trillion, up from $829 billion a year earlier, and the fee grows as that pile of assets grows.[1] The second shop, Financial Technology, sells subscription software to banks. The third shop, Market Services, runs the trading venues, and its revenue rises and falls with how much the market trades.

The software shop came mostly from one deal. Nasdaq bought Adenza in November 2023, paying $5,766 million in cash plus shares, and so added the AxiomSL and Calypso products to the Verafin business it already owned.[5] Financial Technology revenue was $772 million in 2021 and $1,850 million in 2025, which is why the company now looks more like a software supplier than an exchange.[5][3] Verafin alone checks bank transactions for money laundering and fraud, and its network includes clients holding more than $10 trillion in assets.[10]

Who pays is the easiest way to see the risk. Listed companies and fund managers pay on contracts that renew every year, so that money is steady. Trading firms pay each time they trade, so that money moves with the market. Management splits third-quarter growth of 14% into 10.5 points from its own selling and product work, which it calls alpha, and 3.5 points from market conditions such as higher index levels and volumes, which it calls beta.[16] A good half of the story in this report is whether the alpha part continues when the beta part fades.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ARR (annual recurring revenue): the yearly value of subscription contracts that renew, as Nasdaq defines it; it excludes one-off and volume-based fees.
  • NRR (net revenue retention): the share of last year's customer revenue still arriving this year after cancellations and upgrades; above 100% means existing customers are spending more.
  • EPS (earnings per share): the company's profit divided across all its shares.
  • FCF (free cash flow) means the cash left after running the business and buying equipment and software; it is what pays dividends, buys back shares and repays debt.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation): the cost of paying staff in shares instead of cash.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital): yearly after-tax operating profit divided by the money shareholders and lenders have put into the business.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization): operating profit with the accounting write-downs of equipment and acquired assets added back; it is the usual yardstick for debt.
  • RSI (relative strength index): a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been rising; above 70 is called overbought.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale, where the price sits above a rising 200-day average; Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days; the 50-day and 200-day versions work the same way over longer spans, and the price tends to bounce off these slow lines.
  • stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong and the position is sold.
  • limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better and never chases the market.
  • entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the potential gain divided by the potential loss from an entry price: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).
  • base is a sideways stretch in the price that a stock builds before a move, and a pivot is the top of that base; a breakout is a move above the pivot on heavy volume, and a retest is the pullback that checks the pivot from above.
  • guidance is the company's own published forecast for the coming period; Nasdaq gives it for non-GAAP operating expenses and for its tax rate.
  • dilution is the rise in share count from issuing new shares, which shrinks each holder's slice of profit; the opposite is a buyback, which is the company buying its own shares.
  • net debt is interest-bearing debt minus the cash held, and price-to-earnings is the price paid for each dollar of yearly earnings.
  • operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue, and cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before financing.
  • consensus estimate is the average of the forecasts that professional analysts publish for a number such as next year's earnings per share.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Nasdaq's strength is that several of its businesses are hard for a rival to copy, and each one has a number that proves it. The listings business is a good example. Through the first nine months of 2026 it won 74% of the new operating-company listings it competed for, and in the third quarter it landed 8 of the 10 largest IPOs.[1] Hilton's announced move from the New York Stock Exchange to Nasdaq, with trading on Nasdaq starting on November 5, 2026, shows that listings can be won from the largest rival.[31] There were 4,726 companies listed on The Nasdaq Stock Market at the end of September 2026.[1]

The index business is a second source of strength because it grows by itself while funds attract money. Net inflows into products tracking Nasdaq indexes were $141 billion over the last twelve months, a record, and 35% of that came from products launched in the last five years, so the franchise is renewing, not just riding the Nasdaq-100.[1][16] The fee depends on assets, so a rising market helps twice: assets rise from performance and from new money. Of the 39% growth in Index revenue in the third quarter, management credited 15 points to its own work and the rest mainly to higher asset levels and record derivatives volumes.[16]

The software business adds stickiness. Verafin had net revenue retention (NRR) of 111%, meaning the same bank customers spent 11% more than a year ago, and 85% of new Financial Technology bookings were cloud-based.[16][1] Nasdaq ranked second in the Chartis RiskTech100, up from fifth a year earlier, which is an independent sign that clients see the products as top tier.[1] Banks do not swap anti-money-laundering or regulatory reporting systems casually, because a failed swap can bring a regulator's fine. That is the switching cost that protects revenue.

The balance sheet supports all of this. Standard & Poor's raised its rating to BBB+ in August 2025, saying the move recognized the company's deleveraging progress, and Moody's raised its rating to Baa1 in March 2025.[9][10] At September 30, 2026 total debt was $8,740 million, down from $9,004 million at the end of 2025, and gross leverage was 2.5 times.[2][16] A new $1.5 billion revolving credit line signed on June 30, 2026 carries a leverage limit of 3.75 times, so the lenders see room above today's level.[13]

Where the business is weak, and what that costs

The first weakness is that part of the revenue depends on markets Nasdaq cannot control. Market Services net revenue grew 2% in the third quarter and its operating margin fell 5 points to 61%, which management put down to revenue performance and a $5 million expense benefit in the year-earlier quarter. Options capture (the amount kept per contract) also slipped as order flow shifted toward large consolidators.[16][1] Nasdaq matched 28.1% of US options volume, against 28.5% a year earlier.[1] Industry options volume itself is strong, at 70.6 million contracts a day through September 2026, up 20.6% from a year earlier, so even a record industry did not lift this division much.[33] If volumes fall, this division has little to offset the drop.

The second weakness is the new-listing cycle. Operating-company IPOs on Nasdaq were 15 in the third quarter against 46 a year earlier, even though SpaceX raised $86 billion in the second quarter.[1][8] The chief executive said on October 22, 2026 that there has been a modest softening in the IPO environment in recent weeks.[16] Listing fees are a small part of revenue, but a closed IPO window also tells you something about the mood that supports the higher index levels and trading volumes.

The third weakness is regulation and technology change. The Securities and Exchange Commission approved Nasdaq's request to trade 23 hours a day, five days a week on April 10, 2026, and trading under it starts on December 6, 2026.[32][1] At the same time the Commission has allowed an innovation exemption for tokenized stocks that trade outside the normal tape. Management said the exemption limits such trading to 25 basis points of volume for stocks in the Russell 1000 and 250 basis points for smaller companies, so the immediate effect is small, but Nasdaq opposes the design.[16] The risk is slow rather than sudden: a new venue type could take trading share over several years.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management made specific promises when it bought Adenza, and most can be checked. At closing in November 2023 the company said it would deliver $80 million of yearly net expense synergies by the end of the second year, reach gross leverage of 4.0 times within 18 months and about 3.3 times within 36 months, and earn $100 million of revenue synergies over the long term.[12][5] It later widened the cost target to $140 million by the end of 2025, and the 2025 annual report says over $160 million of net expense savings had been put into action.[4][3] That is twice the original promise.

The debt promise was also kept early. The company said in October 2025 that it had reached its gross leverage milestone ahead of schedule, and by September 2026 gross leverage was 2.5 times against the 3.3 times it had promised for November 2026.[9][16] S&P and Moody's both raised the company's rating back to its pre-deal level.[9][10] Total debt fell from $10,454 million at the end of 2023 to $8,740 million at September 30, 2026.[5][3][2][37]

The revenue promise is the one we cannot confirm. Management now says it is on track to surpass $100 million of run-rate revenue from cross-sells by the end of 2027, and it counted 42 cross-sells since the deal at the end of 2025.[9][6] Nasdaq does not publish the cross-sell revenue itself, so we mark this as unverified rather than delivered. A second caution is cost guidance: the 2026 range for non-GAAP operating expenses was raised in April, July and October, from a first range of $2.455 billion to $2.535 billion to $2.550 billion to $2.580 billion.[6][7][8][1] The raises were tied to stronger revenue and two acquisitions, and the non-GAAP operating margin still rose to 57%, but a reader should not call this tight cost control.[16][1]

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter puts five years of results beside the standards in our scoring file. Two ideas run through it. The first is the difference between a static level, such as a return on capital measured today, and a trajectory, which is the direction those numbers are moving. A company can miss a static standard and still be improving fast, and a company can pass the standard while getting worse. The deal shows up in three places. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in the last chapter rests on an assumption, and the honest question is which one is weakest. This chapter lists the weak points, builds the three most damaging stories against the idea (a red-team bear case), and picks the single claim that most decides the outcome. Each item says what would show the claim wrong and when we will know.

Scenario 1: buyers pay less than the 25 times we assume

Every multiple in this report names its basis. A forward multiple divides the price by estimated future earnings per share. A trailing multiple divides it by the last four quarters of reported earnings. At $104.36 the stock trades at 25.2 times 2026 consensus earnings of $4.14, at 22.0 times 2027 consensus earnings of $4.75, and at 25.9 times trailing non-GAAP earnings of $4.03.[37][17] The base price of $118.75 assumes that a year from now, when 2027 is the trailing year, buyers pay 25.0 times $4.75. Scenario 2: market beta reverses

Management says 3.5 points of the 14% third-quarter growth came from market conditions, but Index and trading revenue carry more than that, because Index revenue grew 39% with only 15 points credited to Nasdaq's own work.[16][1] Index fees follow assets in funds, and assets rose from $829 billion to $1.160 trillion in a year, of which $190 billion was market appreciation and $141 billion was inflows.[1] As an illustration only, a 20% fall in average index assets with unchanged fee rates would cut about $229 million a year of revenue, 4% of net revenue, and about $0.32 of earnings per share if it all reached profit. That is most of the $0.40 gap between our base and bear earnings per share. We would know within a quarter, since the assets are reported each quarter.

Scenario 3: technology and regulation cut into the franchise

Financial Technology is 35% of net revenue on 2025 figures, and a software business with AI-driven change risk can lose its multiple even while growing.[3][37] Nasdaq itself is the one selling agentic AI tools to banks, but the same change could let new competitors build rival tools quickly. Separately, tokenized stocks that trade outside the exchange tape could draw volume away over time. Management opposes the Securities and Exchange Commission's innovation exemption and says the volume limits make the near-term effect small.[16] The European Commission opened a formal antitrust investigation in November 2025 into whether Nasdaq and Deutsche Boerse coordinated on derivatives, and Nasdaq says it cannot estimate the outcome or any fine.[2] None of these is likely to hit 2027 earnings, but each could lower the multiple.

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, and no order rests in the market today. The rule behind it is our support-distance ladder. A stock more than 1.6 daily swings above support is in the NO_BUY band. The plan is a limit order, which fills only at our price or lower, in an entry zone of $92.00 to $93.40. We first drafted the zone at the 50-day average of $95.17, but a valuation test moved it down. The zone bottom is 0.99 swings above that low.[34][37] On the base price of $118.75 the reward-to-risk is 4.18 to 1 at the top and 5.73 to 1 at the bottom.

The stop loss is $87.33. It is the lower of the swing low and the 50-day average ($89.67), minus one daily swing of $2.34, the rule for a core holding, and it is judged on the daily finish and not on intraday prints.[35][34] A fill in the zone would probably come with the stock under both the 20-day and 50-day averages but above the 200-day average of $90.51, which is a pullback and not a breakdown. The fresh check must confirm that the 200-day average is flat or rising and that no Stage 4 breakdown exists.[34] The sequence is fixed. The stock trades into the zone. That evening we refresh the ledger, the stop, the consensus and the trailing multiple. If the valuation test below passes, we place the limit order for the next session, and a fill makes the status ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP. We do not wait for two sessions above the 50-day average, because the zone sits below it.

The valuation test replaces any fixed multiple. The required target is the entry price plus 2.5 times the distance from the entry to the refreshed stop. At $93.40 with a $87.33 stop that is $108.58, which is 22.9 times $4.75.[37] The justified multiple is the lower of 25.0 times and the trailing multiple at the order price, which is the order price divided by the last four quarters of non-GAAP earnings per share then published. The order is armed only if the justified multiple times the then-current 2027 consensus earnings is at least the required target. If the market de-rates the stock, or consensus falls, the justified target falls and the order is cancelled. At 22 times the target is $104.50 and the ratio is 1.83 to 1, so there is no order.

Size is set by risk, not by enthusiasm. We assign TIER_4, the smallest tier, for three reasons. The adjusted return on capital is under 12% at the top of guided tax. Much of the recent gain was multiple and not earnings. The market is narrow. Risk-unit sizing divides a 35 basis-point risk budget by the 6.50% distance from $93.40 to the stop. That gives 5.39%, and the tier cap of 2.0% binds, so the initial position is 2.0% of the portfolio.[35][37] The planned loss at the stop is about 0.13% of the portfolio. That figure assumes a sale at exactly $87.33 and leaves out gaps, slippage and fees. If the stock gapped and sold 3% below the stop, at $84.71, the loss would be about 0.19%.[37] If the stock is bought and then rises, we do not trim because RSI is above 70 or because the weight grows. We trail the stop to the rising 21-day average or 50-day average. The weight can drift up to 22% while trend and estimates hold.
