CHAPTER I

From Oilfield Seismography & Jack Kilby's Integrated Circuit to the 300mm Analog Fortress

"What we did in the summer of 1958 was show that you did not have to wire separate components together by hand. That an entire circuit could live on a single monolithic piece of silicon."
— Jack S. Kilby, Texas Instruments Engineer & 2000 Nobel Laureate in Physics

To understand why Texas Instruments commands a quarter-trillion-dollar valuation and a 64 percent gross margin in late 2026, you and I have to travel back ninety-six years to the oilfields of Texas. Great corporate moats do not appear by accident. They are forged through decades of institutional memory and a willingness to walk away from glamorous, low-return businesses in favor of enduring manufacturing advantages. In December 1930, right in the depths of the Great Depression, J. Clarence 'Doc' Karcher and Eugene McDermott founded Geophysical Service Incorporated, or GSI, in Dallas, Texas. Their breakthrough was reflection seismography: detonating small charges in the earth and using sensitive analog acoustic instruments to measure how sound waves bounced off deep underground rock layers to locate oil traps.

When World War Two broke out, the United States Navy and Army Signal Corps realized that the exact same analog signal-processing expertise used to listen for underground oil could be used to detect German U-boats beneath the Atlantic and track aircraft on radar. Led by four legendary builders. Eugene McDermott, Cecil Green, J. Erik Jonsson, and Patrick Haggerty. The company expanded rapidly into precision military electronics. Recognizing after the war that solid-state electronics would eclipse oilfield contracting, the partners reorganized the company, renamed it Texas Instruments Incorporated in 1951, and listed its shares on the New York Stock Exchange on October 1, 1953.

Within five years of that NYSE listing, engineers inside Texas Instruments' Dallas laboratories achieved two breakthroughs that gave birth to the modern information age. First, in May 1954, TI research director Gordon Teal grew the world's first commercial silicon transistor. Until that day, early transistors were made of germanium. This stopped working whenever a radio or aircraft cockpit grew warm. Teal's silicon transistor proved that semiconductors could operate reliably across extreme temperatures, powering both the famous Regency TR-1 pocket radio and United States missile guidance systems.

Then came the hot Texas summer of 1958. A newly hired engineer named Jack Kilby had not yet accrued enough vacation time to leave Dallas during the company's annual July shutdown. Working alone in a quiet laboratory, Kilby confronted what engineers called the 'tyranny of numbers'. The fact that complex computers and rockets required tens of thousands of discrete transistors, resistors, and capacitors soldered together by hand, where a single faulty solder joint doomed the entire machine. On September 12, 1958, standing before TI's executive leadership, Kilby connected a sliver of semiconductor material to an oscilloscope, pressed a switch, and produced a continuous sine wave. He had invented the monolithic integrated circuit. This proves that every component of an electronic circuit could be fabricated on a single piece of semiconductor material. A feat that earned Kilby the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics.

The Templeton–Ilan Pivot: Exiting Smartphones to Build the 300mm Analog Fortress

For four decades after Jack Kilby's invention, Texas Instruments competed in almost every corner of electronics: defense radar, minicomputers, scientific calculators, DRAM memory chips, digital signal processors, and eventually 2G and 3G mobile phone baseband and OMAP application processors. In the early 2000s, if you opened a Nokia or Motorola cellphone, Texas Instruments was the undisputed king inside. Yet by the middle of that decade, Chief Executive Officer Rich Templeton, who led TI as CEO from 2004 to April 2023 and served as Chairman through 2025, spotted a fatal flaw in the economics of digital smartphone chips.

In digital wireless processors, you had to spend billions of dollars every twenty-four months shrinking transistor geometries down the Moore's Law curve at external foundries, only to depend on three or four giant handset customers. Worse still, Templeton saw that handset makers like Apple and Samsung would eventually design their own internal processors or pit suppliers against each other until margins collapsed. Analog and embedded processing chips, by contrast, obeyed completely different economic laws. They ran on mature process nodes between 45 nanometers and 180 nanometers, served more than one hundred thousand industrial and automotive customers, and once designed into an assembly line or car platform, stayed in production for ten to thirty years with virtually zero maintenance R&D.

Working alongside a rising engineering executive named Haviv Ilan. Who had joined Texas Instruments in 1999 when TI acquired wireless startup Butterfly Communications, rose to Chief Operating Officer in 2020, became CEO in April 2023, and assumed the combined role of Chairman and CEO in 2026. Templeton executed one of the boldest capital reallocations in corporate history. Having already sold defense electronics to Raytheon in 1997, exited commodity DRAM, and divested sensors and controls in 2006, TI systematically wound down and exited its multi-billion-dollar wireless baseband and OMAP smartphone processor business between 2008 and 2012.

While Wall Street fretted over the lost handset revenue, Templeton and Ilan deployed that capital counter-cyclically into analog manufacturing during the depth of the 2008–2009 global financial crisis. When German memory chipmaker Qimonda went bankrupt in 2009, Texas Instruments stepped into the liquidation court and bought complete 300-millimeter wafer fabrication toolsets for pennies on the dollar to open RFAB1 in Richardson, Texas. Two years later, in September 2011, TI acquired National Semiconductor for 6.5 billion dollars, adding over 12,000 high-margin power management and precision analog parts. Rich Templeton spent forty-three years at Texas Instruments instilling the creed that free cash flow per share is the true north of ownership, and when Haviv Ilan became CEO in 2023 and Chairman in 2026 alongside 25-year TI veteran CFO Julie Knecht in August 2026, that cultural continuity remained unbroken.

Inside the 80,000-Part Catalog — How Analog & Embedded Chips Power AI Racks, Robots & EVs

"The excitement I have about industrial is growing very fast. Systems that are being designed by our customers four years later than the COVID peak are much more rich with content. And on the robotic side, per humanoid is very high. Somewhere between $500 to $1,000 of content for TI."
— Haviv Ilan, Chairman & CEO of Texas Instruments (Q3 2026 Earnings Call, 21 October 2026)

Every digital computation in the modern global economy begins and ends in the physical universe. Think about that for a moment. A thirty-thousand-dollar graphics processing unit inside an artificial intelligence training cluster, a central computer inside an electric vehicle, or a programmable controller on an automated assembly line understands nothing except binary ones and zeros. Yet the physical world in which you and I live does not operate in binary. It consists of continuous analog waves: electrical voltage, amperage, heat, pressure, light, sound, fluid flow, speed, and mechanical torque.

Analog semiconductors are the indispensable translators and power regulators that bridge the physical universe and the digital processor. Before a digital chip can execute a single line of code, analog power management chips must step down high-voltage electricity from the wall or battery, isolate dangerous voltage spikes, and feed a rock-steady sub-one-volt current to the processor core within millivolt tolerances. At the same time, analog signal-chain chips, amplifiers, data converters, sensors, and transceivers, measure real-world temperature, current, and motion, translate those continuous physical signals into digital data streams, and then convert digital commands back into physical motor torque or optical pulses. Paired with Embedded Processing microcontrollers. This act as localized microsecond brains executing real-time control loops, Texas Instruments sells a catalog of more than 80,000 distinct products to over 100,000 customers around the globe.
