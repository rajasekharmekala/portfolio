Path Dependence & The Split Illusion

Reverse and forward splits do not alter a shareholder's proportional ownership of fund net assets [3]. Even so, corporate splits serve as an essential forensic barometer for leveraged ETFs [3]. Frequent reverse splits indicate prolonged periods where realized volatility and downward drift eroded fund capital [3][5]. In contrast, a forward split signals an unusually sustained, unidirectional market advance [3].

To evaluate KORU's past cycles, an analyst must distinguish index-level return from currency-adjusted return [1][4]. The MSCI Korea 25/50 Index tracked by KORU is denominated in U.S. dollars [1][4]. The fund replicates that benchmark through U.S.-listed iShares MSCI South Korea ETF swaps [1]. As a result, every daily return embeds both local KOSPI equity moves and daily USD/KRW exchange-rate shifts [1][4]. During global risk-off shocks across emerging Asia, the Korean Won often depreciates against the U.S. dollar while Korean equities decline [4]. This dynamic creates a compounding double-negative daily impulse that KORU multiplies by three [1]. Conversely, when semiconductor exports surge and foreign capital enters Seoul, Won appreciation provides a secondary tailwind alongside 3x equity beta [4].

The sponsor's institutional pedigree also shapes how the vehicle behaves under market stress [1]. Rafferty Asset Management, LLC manages dozens of daily leveraged and inverse series within the Direxion Shares ETF Trust [1]. Unlike boutique single-product issuers, Direxion maintains standardized master ISDA swap agreements with Tier-1 global banks [1]. This infrastructure protects KORU against sponsor insolvency or single-day tracking failure [1]. Instead, the primary operational risk remains the unforgiving arithmetic of daily compounding over multi-month holding periods [5].

A simple two-day numerical illustration from the SEC's Investor Bulletin framework demonstrates why KORU's past cycles appear asymmetric [5].
