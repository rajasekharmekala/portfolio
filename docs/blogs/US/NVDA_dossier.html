Chapter 1

NVIDIA was founded on April 5, 1993, in San Jose, California, by Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky, and Curtis Priem [20]. Their founding bet was that PCs would evolve from text processors into 3D graphics and multimedia machines. Early survival was anything but assured. The company's first chip, the NV1 (1995), used quadratic texture mapping—a dead-end standard that Microsoft's Direct3D rejected—and its follow-on NV2 failed to ship [20]. By 1997, NVIDIA was weeks from running out of cash before betting its remaining capital on the RIVA 128, which shipped over one million units in four months and rescued the firm [20]. Two years later, in 1999, NVIDIA went public on January 22 and launched the GeForce 256, integrating hardware transform and lighting (T&L) onto a single chip and coining the term GPU [20].

Between 2000 and 2026, NVIDIA's corporate evolution unfolded across four distinct operational cycles, each marked by severe drawdowns that forged its current full-stack architecture. The first crisis struck in 2008: defective underfill packaging on mobile GeForce 8 GPUs ('Bumpgate') triggered massive warranty charges just as the Global Financial Crisis froze PC demand [20]. Simultaneously, Huang committed R&D capital to CUDA (launched in 2006), a parallel-computing software layer that let researchers program GPUs in C/C++ for non-graphics math. Wall Street initially panned CUDA as a margin drag, yet it quietly seeded two decades of developer lock-in.

Every major NVIDIA hardware crisis—from 1997 NV1 to 2018 crypto and 2022 gaming inventory gluts—forced deeper vertical integration into software (CUDA) and networking (Mellanox/NVLink), culminating in the November 2026 test of whether that institutional machine outlives its co-founder.

The second and third cycles exposed NVIDIA's historical vulnerability to consumer and crypto boom-busts. In 2018, Ethereum mining inflated GeForce demand before crashing, leaving channel partners awash in used GPUs and pushing FY2020 revenue down 6.8% to $10.92 billion [18]. A similar pandemic-era PC and crypto surge ended in 2022 (FY2023), when gaming inventory gluts forced steep channel price cuts and halved GAAP operating income from $10.04 billion in FY22 to $4.22 billion in FY23 [19]. Crucially, during that exact downturn, NVIDIA closed its $6.9 billion acquisition of Mellanox (2020) and launched the Hopper H100 architecture, shifting its center of gravity from PC graphics cards to rack-scale AI clusters.

The fourth cycle—from FY2024 through Q3 FY2027—produced the fastest industrial scaling in modern semiconductor history, alongside an abrupt leadership shock. Revenue exploded from $26.97 billion in FY23 to $60.92 billion in FY24, $130.50 billion in FY25, and $215.94 billion in FY26 [4], [18]. In Q3 FY27 (ended October 25, 2026), quarterly revenue alone reached $115.8 billion (+103% YoY), propelled by Blackwell and the debut of the Vera Rubin NVL72 rack-scale platform (~$20 billion in first-quarter shipments) [1], [6]. Yet on November 12, 2026—five days before Q3 earnings—co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as President, CEO, and director following disclosures of personal conduct violations [5], [6]. Longtime CFO Colette Kress stepped in as Interim CEO, Harvey C. Jones became NVIDIA's first independent Board Chair, and the Board formed a National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee [5], [7].

NVIDIA operates a fabless manufacturing model [8], [9]. It designs silicon, systems, and software in-house, while outsourcing wafers, packaging, and memory to concentrated Asian partners [8], [9]. TSMC fabricates Rubin GPUs on its N3 (3nm) node and Vera CPUs on advanced nodes [12]. It assembles multi-die packages using CoWoS-L (Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate with Local Silicon Interconnect) [12]. SK Hynix and Micron supply High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM3e for Blackwell, HBM4 for Vera Rubin) [9], [12]. ODMs and OEMs like Foxconn, Quanta, Dell, Supermicro, and HPE integrate HGX boards and NVL72 liquid-cooled racks for end buyers [9], [12].

This capital-light model yields extraordinary P&L unit economics. However, it now requires massive off-balance-sheet supply commitments to lock in capacity. In FY2026, NVIDIA generated $215.94 billion in revenue with only $6.04 billion in CapEx (2.8% of revenue) [4], [11]. It converted 44.8% of revenue ($96.68 billion) into free cash flow [4], [11]. In Q3 FY27, total supply and capacity commitments jumped sequentially from $279 billion to ~$390 billion [8], [9]. Roughly $69 billion is due in the rest of FY27, and ~$203 billion is due in FY28 [8], [9]. Management noted that multi-year HBM memory procurement drove most of this surge [9]. Rising memory prices and the Vera Rubin ramp will compress Q4 FY27 gross margins to a guided 71.0%–72.0% trough [2], [9]. That marks a ~340 bps drop from 74.8% in Q3 FY27, before an expected rebound toward 72%–73% in FY28 [2], [9].

Chapter 4

NVIDIA commands roughly 70%+ of the AI accelerator market, yet the industry is increasingly bifurcated [14]. At the frontier, customers train multi-trillion-parameter reasoning models, multimodal agents, and complex enterprise inference. Here, NVIDIA's moat rests on four interlocking layers. First, CUDA and software libraries (cuDNN, TensorRT-LLM, NIM) have trained millions of engineers over 20 years. Second, NVLink 6 rack-scale coherent interconnect delivers 260 TB/s per NVL72 rack, turning 72 Rubin GPUs into one logical processor [12]. Third, end-to-end networking via InfiniBand and Spectrum-X Ethernet surpassed $21B per quarter in Q3 FY27 [2]. Fourth, an annual architectural cadence (Hopper to Blackwell to Vera Rubin) resets performance-per-watt before rivals catch up.

However, three structural challengers are chipping away at the edges of that moat. First, hyperscalers are deploying custom AI ASICs co-designed with Broadcom and Marvell. Together, those two chipmakers hold ~95% of custom AI ASIC co-design share [14]. Google TPUs, AWS Trainium, Microsoft Maia 200 (on TSMC 3nm), and Meta MTIA are growing faster in percentage terms than merchant GPUs [14]. Hyperscalers want to avoid the 'NVIDIA tax' on predictable internal inference and recommendation workloads [14]. Second, AMD's Instinct MI-series GPUs and ROCm software offer a credible merchant second source for memory-bound inference. Third, U.S. export controls have locked NVIDIA out of China's Data Center compute market. That restriction pushes Chinese cloud buyers toward domestic silicon like Huawei's Ascend series [15].
