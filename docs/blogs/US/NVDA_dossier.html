1. What the company does

NVIDIA sells computing power for artificial intelligence. A graphics processing unit (GPU) is a chip that does thousands of small calculations at once. That is what training and running an AI model needs. NVIDIA invented the GPU in 1999 and opened it to general programming with its CUDA software in 2006[1]. It now calls itself a data center scale AI infrastructure company. Its chips, networking, software and full server racks work as one system[1]. A buyer who writes code for CUDA must rewrite it to switch chips. That switching cost is the main reason customers stay.

The company reports two segments, Compute & Networking and Graphics. In the second quarter of fiscal 2027, the three months ended July 26, 2026, Compute & Networking made $88.3 billion of the $96.2 billion total. Graphics made $7.9 billion[2]. A second view splits revenue by market platform. Data Center was $89.0 billion, up 117% from a year earlier. Edge Computing, which includes workstation and consumer PC products, was $7.2 billion[2]. NVIDIA's fiscal year ends on the last Sunday of January. Fiscal 2027 therefore ends on January 31, 2027 and has 53 weeks. Every fiscal period in this report carries that label[2].

Customers fall into two main groups. The first is the hyperscalers, the giant cloud companies such as Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft that rent computing power to others. They bought $48.7 billion in the quarter, up 102%[2]. The second group is AI Clouds, Industrial and Enterprise. It includes specialist AI cloud firms, AI model builders, governments and large companies. It bought $40.3 billion, up 138%[2]. A few direct customers dominate. One was 16% of second-quarter revenue. In fiscal 2026 two were 22% and 14% of the year[2][1].

A dollar of NVIDIA revenue passes through a long supply chain that the company does not own. TSMC, the Taiwanese contract chip maker, makes the silicon wafers. SK Hynix, Micron and Samsung supply the memory. NVIDIA uses chip-on-wafer-on-substrate (CoWoS) packaging, the advanced method that joins logic chips to memory on one carrier[1]. Hon Hai, Wistron and Fabrinet assemble, test and package the final products[1]. TSMC said on October 15, 2026 that demand for AI chips and advanced packaging still exceeds its capacity. That supports NVIDIA's pricing. It also caps how fast NVIDIA can ship[27].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

NVIDIA is strong because it earns far more on each sale than almost any rival. It kept doing so while revenue more than doubled. The gross margin, which is revenue minus the direct cost of making the product, as a share of revenue, was 75.0% in the second quarter of fiscal 2027. A year earlier it was 72.4%. The operating margin was 66.2%[2]. AMD's gross margin was about 53.8% in its second quarter of 2026, with an operating margin near 17.3%. Intel's were about 40.4% and 11.1%[16][18]. Broadcom, which designs custom AI chips and networking gear, earned 69.1% and 53.9% in its quarter ended August 2, 2026. It is the closest peer on profit[17]. Micron is the exception. It earned an 80.7% gross margin and a 74.6% operating margin in its fiscal year ended September 3, 2026, during a memory price surge[19].

The advantage has three layers. The first is software. CUDA and hundreds of libraries make NVIDIA the default choice for AI developers[1]. The second is system scale. NVIDIA now sells whole racks that combine GPUs, CPUs, switches and network cards. Networking revenue was $31.4 billion in fiscal 2026, up from $13.0 billion a year before. The company captures more of each data center build[1]. The third is supply. At July 26, 2026 NVIDIA had $279 billion of supply and capacity commitments, up from $119 billion three months earlier[2][6]. It has reserved memory and manufacturing capacity that a smaller rival cannot easily match. Management says the commitments cover the next several years of demand[2].

The weaknesses are about who the customers are and how they pay. The 10-K names large cloud companies with in-house chip teams, among them Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft, as competitors as well as customers[1]. A customer that designs its own chip is a customer that can leave. Concentration is also high. One direct customer was 16% of second-quarter revenue. Five direct customers held 22%, 14%, 13%, 11% and 10% of receivables, which adds to 70%[2]. Days sales outstanding rose to 60 from 45. The CFO blamed longer payment terms on large multi-quarter deals with investment-grade customers. The 10-Q says terms can run from 90 days up to one year[4][2].

The newest weakness is that NVIDIA now helps customers pay. In August 2026 it signed guarantees capped at $105 billion. They back leases for about 4.25 gigawatts of data center capacity at an Ohio campus. An affiliate of OpenAI is the tenant[13][2]. Earlier guarantees for AI cloud partners add $3.5 billion. The maximum gross exposure is $108.5 billion, or 47% of shareholders' equity[2]. The 10-K, filed six months earlier, said NVIDIA had not entered into any financing arrangements[1]. The guarantees pay out only if the tenant defaults. The first phase is expected in fiscal 2029[2]. Still, some demand now rests on the customer's access to outside capital. That kind of worry weighed on chip stocks on October 22, 2026[26].

How cyclical is the business

This is a cyclical business in an unusually long upswing. In fiscal 2023 revenue was flat at $27.0 billion, against $26.9 billion. Operating income fell from $10.0 billion to $4.2 billion. The gross margin fell from 64.9% to 56.9%. The company also took a $1.4 billion charge to end an acquisition[11]. Growth then restarted. Revenue was $60.9 billion in fiscal 2024, $130.5 billion in fiscal 2025 and $215.9 billion in fiscal 2026[1]. The lesson is that earnings can fall by more than half when customers stop ordering. NVIDIA's commitments today are many times larger than in fiscal 2023. Operating expenses were $23.1 billion in fiscal 2026, against $11.1 billion in fiscal 2023[11][1]. A pause would meet a cost structure twice as large as the last one. That is why we treat the cycle as a live risk and not a historical footnote.

In February 2026 it guided first-quarter fiscal 2027 revenue to $78.0 billion and reported $81.6 billion, 4.6% above. 3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter sets out what the company earns, what it keeps in cash and what it owes. The headline is a profit machine of a scale the market has rarely seen. In the twelve months to July 26, 2026, NVIDIA earned $303.0 billion of revenue, $197.6 billion of operating income and $192.9 billion of net income. The gross margin was 74.7%[1][2].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate has a weakest link. For the reverse DCF it is the starting point. Trailing free cash flow of $127.0 billion is a record, and 14.67% growth is measured from a record. For the $286.38 target it is fiscal 2028 EPS of $15.91. That needs 68% more revenue after a fiscal 2027 forecast to rise 90.6%. For the probability-weighted value it is the 30% bear weight, which is a judgment and not a measurement. The same evidence tests all three: receivables, supply commitments and the guarantee balance. They appear in the next 10-Q, which should follow the third-quarter report expected on November 18, 2026. NVIDIA has not confirmed that date[24][25].

The red-team bear case is simple. NVIDIA sells to a few buyers who are spending faster than their own businesses earn. Those buyers are increasingly financed by outside capital, partly with NVIDIA's credit support. If that capital tightens, orders slow just as $279 billion of supplier commitments and $31.6 billion of inventory arrive. The margin and cash flow gains of three years then reverse.

Fatal flaw 1: the funding chain breaks

Scenario: an AI model builder or specialist cloud cannot raise its next round of financing, and orders from that group stop. The tenant under the Ohio guarantee then defaults. NVIDIA's maximum gross guarantee exposure is $108.5 billion, or 47% of equity. Receivables are $63.1 billion, and five customers hold 70% of them[2]. The weakest assumption is that the guarantees are a modest side issue. A rising guarantee total or longer payment terms in the next 10-Q would show it is wrong. The AI Clouds group is 45% of Data Center revenue. A slowdown there removes about $40 billion of quarterly revenue at the current run rate[2]. This is the only flaw that can hit revenue, cash flow and the balance sheet in the same quarter, so it carries the most weight in our bear case.

Fatal flaw 2: a digestion year and a commitment overhang

Scenario: the hyperscalers decide they have enough capacity for twelve months, as customers did in fiscal 2023. Revenue was flat then and operating income fell 58%[11]. NVIDIA would own $279 billion of supply commitments. Of that, $92 billion falls due in the rest of fiscal 2027 and $87 billion in fiscal 2028. Trailing free cash flow is $127.0 billion[2]. Some agreements can be canceled or adjusted, but changes may carry costs. The company already booked a $4.5 billion H20 charge when demand vanished[2][1]. The weakest assumption is that supply stays the bottleneck forever. Rising inventory against revenue, or inventory provisions above the $784 million booked in the second quarter, would show it is wrong[2].

Fatal flaw 3: custom chips, export rules and the memory cost squeeze

Scenario: the large cloud companies move more work to their own chips, which the 10-K names as a competitive threat. US export rules or a Chinese ban keep China at zero. Memory suppliers raise prices[1]. Micron earned an 80.7% gross margin on $133.2 billion of revenue, which shows how much of the memory price surge goes to suppliers. NVIDIA's own guide already assumes its gross margin slips from 75.0% to 74.0%[19][3]. The weakest assumption is that the 74% to 75% margin is stable. A margin below 72.5% would show it is wrong. Our Layer 2 rule would then move the KPI off PASS.

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT on a CORE_ALPHA, COMPOUNDER basis with Tier 3 conviction. The rule behind it is the framework's technical routing rule. Every Layer 1 floor and the Layer 2 score pass[2]. The momentum gate returns NO_MOMENTUM because Test C fails on the short-term turn. A Stage 3 pullback above a rising 200-day average routes to a limit-order entry and not to rejection. The price of $210.96 is 0.13 ATR above the swing low, inside the full-size band of the support ladder[20]. Support distance is not what holds us back. The missing turn is.

The entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy. It runs from $204.00 to $210.18, from just above the rising 200-day average of $203.67 up to the 20-day swing low. We buy with a limit order, which fills only at the stated price or better, so it does not chase the market. The reward-to-risk ratio, also called asymmetry, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop. At the top of the zone it is 2.90 to 1, with a target of $286.38 built from consensus EPS and a stop of $183.95[20][21]. At the current price of $210.96 it is 2.79 to 1. The setup therefore has limited room for a lower multiple or lower earnings. The stop is the 63-day base low of $190.01 minus one ATR of $6.06.
