1. What the company does

NVIDIA sells computing power for artificial intelligence. A graphics processing unit (GPU) is a chip that does thousands of small calculations at once, which is what training and running an AI model needs. NVIDIA invented the GPU in 1999 and opened it to general programming with its CUDA software in 2006[1]. It now describes itself as a data center scale AI infrastructure company, and its chips, networking, software and full server racks are built to work as one system[1]. A programmer who writes code for CUDA must rewrite it to switch chips, and that switching cost is the main reason customers stay.

The company reports two segments, Compute & Networking and Graphics. In the second quarter of fiscal 2027, the three months ended July 26, 2026, Compute & Networking made $88.3 billion of the $96.2 billion total and Graphics made $7.9 billion[2]. A second view splits revenue by market platform, and it is the more useful one. Data Center was $89.0 billion, up 117% from a year earlier, and Edge Computing, which holds workstation and consumer PC products, was $7.2 billion[4]. NVIDIA's fiscal year ends on the last Sunday of January. Fiscal 2027 ends on January 31, 2027 and is a 53-week year, so its fourth quarter has 14 weeks, one more than usual[1].

Customers fall into two groups. Hyperscalers are the giant cloud companies, such as Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft, that rent out computing power to others. Their bucket, called Hyperscale, bought $48.7 billion in the quarter, up 102% from a year ago. The second bucket, AI Clouds, Industrial and Enterprise, holds specialist AI cloud firms, AI model builders, governments and large companies. It bought $40.3 billion, up 138%[4]. NVIDIA reclassified one company from the second bucket to the first during the quarter and restated the earlier period to match[4]. Direct customers, the companies that buy from NVIDIA itself, are few. One was 16% of second-quarter revenue, and in the first half three were 16%, 15% and 13%[2]. In fiscal 2026 two were 22% and 14% of the year[1].

A dollar of NVIDIA revenue passes through a supply chain that the company does not own. TSMC, the Taiwanese contract chip maker, and Samsung make the silicon wafers. SK Hynix, Micron and Samsung supply the memory. NVIDIA uses CoWoS packaging, which stands for chip-on-wafer-on-substrate and is the advanced method that joins logic chips and memory on one carrier. Hon Hai, Wistron and Fabrinet assemble, test and package the final products[1]. TSMC raised its 2026 revenue growth outlook to 44% on October 15, 2026, which shows demand for advanced chips is still strong[27]. That supports NVIDIA's pricing, and it also caps how fast NVIDIA can ship.

What is new in the product line

Blackwell is the current generation of NVIDIA data center systems, and Blackwell Ultra, its upgrade, drove the second-quarter growth[4]. Vera Rubin is the next generation. NVIDIA said on August 26, 2026 that Vera Rubin is ramping into full production, with racks running at CoreWeave, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, Oracle Cloud Infrastructure and Nebius[3]. On October 27, 2026, the day of this report's last price bar, NVIDIA and Oracle said Vera Rubin NVL72 systems are generally available on Oracle's cloud and will expand to multi-gigawatt scale[28]. The CFO said inventory rose to $31.6 billion from $25.8 billion in the first quarter because NVIDIA is preparing for the introduction of Vera Rubin in the third quarter[4]. Raw materials inventory alone rose from $3.8 billion in January to $11.3 billion, while finished goods fell from $8.8 billion to $6.9 billion[2].

NVIDIA is also spending cash outside its own product line. It paid $13.0 billion in fiscal 2026 for a non-exclusive license to technology from Groq, whose chips target fast AI answers[1]. On September 2, 2026 it agreed to buy Hugging Face, a hub where developers share AI models, for about $11.9 billion, with closing expected in the first half of 2027[14]. It held $99 billion of equity investments and had committed another $25 billion at July 26, 2026[2]. These items matter because they turn some customers and suppliers into part-owned partners, a change that chapters 2 and 6 examine.

What NVIDIA does not sell to China

US export rules blocked NVIDIA's top data center chips from China. The company took a $4.5 billion charge in the first quarter of fiscal 2026 for H20 chips it could no longer sell[1]. In February 2026 the US government granted a license to ship small amounts of H200 chips to specific China-based customers, and the 10-K said NVIDIA had earned no revenue under it[1]. Hopper shipments to China were less than 1% of second-quarter Data Center revenue, and the third-quarter outlook assumes no Data Center compute revenue from China[4][3]. So China is a possible source of upside, and it is not in the numbers a buyer is paying for.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

NVIDIA is strong because it earns far more on each sale than almost any rival, and it kept doing so while revenue more than doubled. The gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of making the product, shown as a share of revenue, and it was 75.0% in the second quarter of fiscal 2027 against 72.4% a year earlier[3][2]. The operating margin is what remains after research and administration costs are also paid, and it was 66.2%[2]. AMD's gross margin was 53.8% in its latest quarter, with an operating margin near 17.3%. Intel's were 40.4% and 11.1%[16][18]. Broadcom, which designs custom AI chips and networking gear, earned 69.1% and 53.9%[17]. Micron is the exception: its fiscal 2026 gross margin was 80.7% and its operating margin 74.6%, during a memory price surge[19].

The advantage has three layers. The first is software: CUDA and hundreds of libraries make NVIDIA the default choice for AI developers[1]. The second is system scale. NVIDIA now sells whole racks that combine GPUs, CPUs, switches and network cards, and Data Center networking revenue was $31.4 billion in fiscal 2026 against $13.0 billion the year before[1]. The third is supply. At July 26, 2026 NVIDIA had $279 billion of supply and capacity commitments, up from $119 billion three months earlier and $95.2 billion at the end of fiscal 2026[2][6][8]. It has reserved memory and manufacturing capacity that a smaller rival cannot easily match. The company says these contracts cover the next several years of demand, but some of them can be cancelled or rescheduled only at a cost[2].

The weaknesses are about who the customers are and how they pay. The 10-K names large cloud companies with in-house chip teams, among them Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft, as competitors as well as customers[1]. A customer that designs its own chip is a customer that can leave. Concentration is also high. Five direct customers held 22%, 14%, 13%, 11% and 10% of receivables at July 26, 2026, which adds to 70%[2]. Days sales outstanding rose to 60 from 45, and the CFO blamed longer payment terms on large multi-quarter agreements with investment-grade customers. The 10-Q says terms can run from 90 days up to one year[4][2].

The newest weakness is that NVIDIA now helps customers pay for what they buy. In August 2026 it signed guarantees capped at $105 billion that back leases for about 4.25 gigawatts of data center capacity at an Ohio campus, with an affiliate of OpenAI as the tenant[13][2]. OpenAI agreed to reimburse NVIDIA for any payment it makes, which helps only if OpenAI itself can pay[13]. Older guarantees for AI cloud partners add $3.5 billion, so maximum gross exposure is $108.5 billion, or 47% of shareholders' equity[4]. The February 2026 10-K said NVIDIA had not entered into any financing arrangements[1]. The guarantees pay out only if a tenant defaults, and the first phase is expected in fiscal 2029[2]. Still, part of the demand now rests on a customer's access to outside capital.

Is NVIDIA funding its own demand?

This question has become sharper since our October 23 report. The 10-Q shows $99 billion of equity investments and $25 billion of further commitments, and it shows $36 billion of agreements to buy cloud services back from AI cloud partners that can unilaterally stop providing them[2]. In August NVIDIA also signed memorandums of understanding with large capital providers to set up financing platforms meant to mobilize more than $500 billion of outside money, which the filing says may not become definitive agreements[2]. On the October 22 sell-off, when NVIDIA fell more than 4% and the Philadelphia semiconductor index fell more than 6%, the financing worry was named as the driver[26].

New facts arrived on October 26 and 27. Meta said it would raise $35 billion of equity at once and up to $60 billion more over time, and NVIDIA said it would invest $3 billion in the offering while Meta expands its use of Vera Rubin systems[30][29]. CNBC reports that NVIDIA has invested in OpenAI, Anthropic, Intel and xAI and joined 121 funding rounds in 2026, against 13 in 2022[30]. None of this is unusual for an ecosystem builder, and Meta is among the strongest buyers of AI chips. It still means a rising share of NVIDIA's future revenue is tied to customers it also finances, and a reader should treat the revenue and the equity stakes as one risk, not two.

How cyclical is the business

This is a cyclical business in an unusually long upswing. In fiscal 2023 revenue was flat at $27.0 billion, against $26.9 billion the year before. Operating income fell from $10.0 billion to $4.2 billion, and the gross margin fell from 64.9% to 56.9%[11]. The company also took a $1.35 billion charge to end an acquisition that year[11]. Growth then restarted: revenue was $60.9 billion in fiscal 2024, $130.5 billion in fiscal 2025 and $215.9 billion in fiscal 2026[1]. The lesson is that earnings can fall by more than half when customers stop ordering. NVIDIA's commitments today are many times larger than in fiscal 2023, and operating expenses were $23.1 billion in fiscal 2026 against $11.1 billion in fiscal 2023[1][11]. A pause would meet a cost structure twice as large as the last one, which is why we treat the cycle as a live risk and not a historical footnote.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management gave revenue and margin guidance, which is the company's own forecast for the coming quarter, in each of the last four quarters. Three times it delivered more than it said. In November 2025 it guided fourth-quarter fiscal 2026 revenue to $65.0 billion and reported $68.1 billion, 4.8% above. In February 2026 it guided $78.0 billion and reported $81.6 billion, 4.6% above. In May it guided $91.0 billion and reported $96.2 billion, 5.7% above[9][7][5][3]. Gross margin guidance was met in each case, and the opex guides were met with room to spare: the second-quarter guide was about $8.5 billion on a GAAP basis and the result was $8.4 billion[5][2]. Each outlook assumed no Data Center compute revenue from China, and actual China Hopper shipments were under 1% of Data Center revenue, so no hidden China shortfall sat in the beats[4].

The fourth quarter before that was the exception, and the cause was outside management's control. In February 2025 it guided first-quarter fiscal 2026 revenue to $43.0 billion with a 70.6% GAAP gross margin[10]. Revenue came in at $44.1 billion, 2.5% above, our arithmetic from the half-year and second-quarter figures in the 10-Q[2]. The gross margin was about 60.5% on the same arithmetic, roughly 10 points short, because export rules forced a $4.5 billion H20 charge that the guide could not have included[1]. We count it as delivered on revenue and missed on margin for a reason the company did not control.

Two statements need a closer look. In May 2026 the CFO said days sales outstanding were 45 because customers had paid early, against 51 the quarter before, and that the figure would return to more normal levels the next quarter[6]. In August it was 60, above both earlier readings, and the reason given was longer payment terms on large agreements[4]. Second, the February 2026 10-K said NVIDIA had not entered into any financing arrangements, while the August 10-Q says that financing arrangements with certain investment-grade customers, including extended payment terms, will keep affecting the timing of operating cash flow[1][2]. Management disclosed the change openly, which counts in its favor. The direction is still the opposite of the earlier statement. Capital return promises were kept: the company returned $41.1 billion in fiscal 2026 and $45.3 billion in the first half of fiscal 2027, raised the quarterly dividend from $0.01 to $0.25 and added $80.0 billion to the repurchase authorization[8][2].

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter sets out what the company earns, what it keeps in cash and what it owes. The headline is a profit machine of a scale the market has rarely seen. In the twelve months to July 26, 2026, NVIDIA earned $303.0 billion of revenue, $197.6 billion of operating income and $192.9 billion of net income, at a gross margin of 74.7%[1][2]. These trailing figures are our sums: fiscal 2026 plus the first half of fiscal 2027 minus the first half of fiscal 2026, each taken from the filings.

The same filings hold reasons for caution. Second-quarter net income of $59.7 billion includes $7.8 billion of other income, nearly all of it gains on equity investments, and the first-half figure includes $23.7 billion of such gains[4][2]. NVIDIA owns $42.8 billion of marketable equity securities and $51.2 billion of non-marketable securities, which are shares it holds in other companies[2]. When their value rises, profit rises, and when it falls, profit falls. So we judge earning power by operating income and free cash flow, not by net income. Capital return is large and steady: in fiscal 2026 NVIDIA paid $40.1 billion of share repurchases and $974 million of dividends, and in the first half of fiscal 2027 it paid $39.0 billion and $6.3 billion[8][2]. In the last five weeks of the second quarter, from June 22 to July 26, NVIDIA bought 58.5 million shares at an average of $202.40, below today's price[2].

Layer 1 uses four yardsticks, and each one has a plain meaning. ROIC, or return on invested capital, is the after-tax profit a company earns on the money invested in it. SBC, or stock-based compensation, is the cost of paying staff in shares, and it matters because it dilutes owners. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating cash profit. Net debt is debt minus cash and debt securities, so a negative figure means the company holds more cash than it owes. EPS is earnings per share, which is profit divided across all shares outstanding.

5. What could happen next

This chapter asks what the price already assumes, how analyst forecasts are moving and where our view differs. We start with a reverse DCF. A DCF, or discounted cash flow model, adds up the cash a company will earn in future years after reducing each year's amount for the time it takes to arrive. Run backward, it takes today's price and finds the growth rate that would justify it. The inputs are set by our scoring rules: a 10% discount rate, a terminal value of 18 times year-ten free cash flow, and ten years. We use NVIDIA's enterprise value of $5,239 billion and trailing free cash flow of $127.0 billion[2][1].

The answer is about 15.1% a year of free cash flow growth for ten years. That sounds low for a company growing revenue 106%, and it is the key fact of this chapter. The price does not assume the boom continues. It assumes cash flow grows at the rate of a good large company. The consensus estimate is the average of analyst forecasts, and the consensus for fiscal 2028 EPS is $15.91. At 24.1 billion shares that is about $384 billion of profit, which is already 3.0 times trailing free cash flow[21]. The reverse DCF needs only $518 billion of free cash flow in year ten. The market is pricing a real chance that earnings fall back from a peak, and that is the debate we are paid to answer.

Our view has two parts. First, consensus is more reliable for the next two quarters than the price implies. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate has a weakest link. For the reverse DCF it is the starting point: trailing free cash flow of $127.0 billion is a record, and 15.1% growth is measured from a record. For the $286.38 target it is fiscal 2028 EPS of $15.91, which needs 68% more revenue after a fiscal 2027 forecast to rise 90.6%[21]. For the probability-weighted value it is the 30% bear weight, which is a judgment and not a measurement. The same evidence tests all three: receivable days, supply commitments and the guarantee total. They appear in the next 10-Q, which NVIDIA filed on the day of its last earnings release, August 26, so it should arrive with the third-quarter report[35]. That report is expected on November 17 or 18, 2026. One public calendar calls November 17 confirmed and two others call November 18 an estimate, and NVIDIA's own site, as we could read it, lists no dated event[35][23][24][25][36].

The red-team bear case is simple. NVIDIA sells to a few buyers who are spending faster than their own businesses earn. Those buyers are increasingly financed by outside capital, partly with NVIDIA's credit support and partly with NVIDIA's own equity checks. If that capital tightens, orders slow just as $279 billion of supplier commitments and $31.6 billion of inventory arrive, and the margin and cash flow gains of three years reverse[2][4].

Fatal flaw 1: the funding chain breaks

Scenario: an AI model builder or specialist cloud cannot raise its next round of financing, and orders from that group stop. The tenant under the Ohio guarantee then defaults. NVIDIA's maximum gross guarantee exposure is $108.5 billion, or 47% of equity. Receivables are $63.1 billion, and five customers hold 70% of them[4][2]. The AI Clouds, Industrial and Enterprise group is 45% of Data Center revenue, so a halt in its orders would remove about $40 billion of quarterly revenue at the current run rate[4]. NVIDIA has also put $99 billion into equity stakes, with $25 billion more committed, and it announced a $3 billion investment in Meta's share offering this week[2][29]. When customers, investees and guarantee beneficiaries are the same names, one failure hits revenue, cash flow and the balance sheet in the same quarter. This is the flaw that carries the most weight in our 30% bear case.

The weakest assumption is that the guarantees are a modest side issue. OpenAI has agreed to reimburse NVIDIA for any guarantee payment, which is a real protection and also a claim on the same company whose lease is in doubt[13]. The first guarantee phase is not expected until fiscal 2029, so the accounting effect is far away, but the market effect of a broken financing would be immediate. A receivable-days reading above 60, a guarantee total above $108.5 billion or a largest customer above 25% of revenue in the next 10-Q would show the assumption is wrong[2].

Fatal flaw 2: a digestion year and a commitment overhang

Scenario: the hyperscalers decide they have enough capacity for twelve months, as customers did in fiscal 2023. Revenue was flat then and operating income fell 58%[11]. NVIDIA would own $279 billion of supply commitments. Of that, $92 billion falls due in the rest of fiscal 2027, $87 billion in fiscal 2028 and $88 billion in fiscal 2029, against trailing free cash flow of $127.0 billion[2]. Some agreements can be cancelled or adjusted, but changes may carry costs. The company already booked a $4.5 billion H20 charge when demand vanished[1].

The weakest assumption is that supply stays the bottleneck for years. Inventory is $31.6 billion, up 48% since January, and raw materials tripled from $3.8 billion to $11.3 billion while finished goods fell, which is consistent with a build ahead of Vera Rubin and also with a stock of memory that has to be used[2]. Inventory provisions of $784 million in the second quarter, rising above that, or a fourth-quarter outlook below the Street's lowest estimate of $117.29 billion would show the assumption is wrong[2][21].

Fatal flaw 3: custom chips, memory costs and export rules

Scenario: the large cloud companies move more work to their own chips, which the 10-K names as a competitive threat. AMD says its Helios racks are running at Meta, a sign that rival systems are winning large deployments[1][30]. Memory suppliers raise prices, and Micron's 80.7% gross margin on $133.2 billion of revenue shows how much of a memory price surge goes to suppliers[19]. NVIDIA's own outlook already assumes its gross margin slips from 75.0% to 74.0%[3]. US export rules keep China at zero, and the H200 license has produced no revenue[1].

The weakest assumption is that the 74% to 75% margin is stable. A gross margin outlook below 73.5%, which is more than 150 basis points under the current level, would lose the PASS test that allows a guided decline of no more than 150 basis points. The scorecard defines WATCH only by margin level (40% to 50%), so we would score that case WATCH and say so. A fall in the Data Center share of revenue from 92.5%, with Edge growing faster, would be an early sign that accelerator demand is slowing[3][4].
