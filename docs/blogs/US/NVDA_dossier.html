1. What the company does

NVIDIA is a company in Santa Clara, California that designs computer chips and sells them, together with networking equipment, complete server racks and software, mainly to the companies that build artificial-intelligence data centers.[8] It does not own chip factories. It pays Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, known as TSMC, and Samsung to make its chips, it buys memory from SK Hynix, Micron and Samsung, and it uses contract manufacturers such as Hon Hai to assemble systems.[8] Its fiscal year ends in late January, so fiscal 2027 runs from late January 2026 to late January 2027. The latest quarter, the second of fiscal 2027, ended on July 26, 2026.[1]

Who pays NVIDIA, and for what, is now a short list. In the second quarter the Data Center platform brought in $89.0 billion, which is 92.5 percent of revenue.[2] Inside it, Hyperscale customers (the public clouds and the largest consumer internet companies) paid $48.7 billion. Customers in a group called AI Clouds, Industrial and Enterprise (AI-focused cloud firms, factories, companies and governments) paid $40.3 billion.[2] The Edge Computing platform covers PCs, game consoles, workstations, robots and cars, and it added $7.2 billion.[2] The buyers are companies, not consumers, and there are few of them. One direct customer made up 16 percent of second-quarter revenue, and three direct customers made up 16, 15 and 13 percent of first-half revenue.[3]

NVIDIA makes money by selling hardware at a price far above its cost. The gross margin, which is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of making the product, was 75.0 percent in the quarter.[1] The hardware runs on CUDA, NVIDIA's own software platform, and on a large library of tools built on top of it.[8] Software is what keeps customers returning, because programs written for CUDA run on NVIDIA chips and moving them to a rival chip costs engineers time. Payments that arrive before NVIDIA delivers support or software are also growing. That balance, called deferred revenue, was $6.4 billion at the end of July 2026 against $2.0 billion a year earlier.[3]

What changed in 2026 is that customers want a place to put the chips as well as the chips themselves. Land, electric power and building space are now the scarce items, and many AI companies cannot borrow enough to secure them. NVIDIA has started to help. It has guarantees of $3.5 billion for AI cloud partners, and in August 2026 it guaranteed up to $105 billion of lease payments for a data center campus in Ohio whose tenant is an affiliate of OpenAI.[2][4] It also agreed to buy Hugging Face, a platform for sharing open-source AI models, for about $11.9 billion.[5] These steps widen NVIDIA's reach and raise the amount of money it stands behind. 2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case that NVIDIA is a strong business rests on three things that show up in filed numbers: it sells a whole system, it earns a very high margin, and it has secured supply. The case that it is a fragile business rests on three others: its customers are few, its suppliers are few, and it is putting more of its own money behind its customers. This chapter takes each in turn.

Strength one: a system, not a part

NVIDIA says its data center products use what it calls extreme co-design, which means the chips, networking, systems, software and algorithms are designed together.[8] Networking is the clearest proof. Networking revenue rose from $12.99 billion in fiscal 2025 to $31.38 billion in fiscal 2026, an increase of 141.5 percent, because racks full of chips need fast links between them.[8] A customer that buys the chips, the links and the software from one supplier has fewer reasons to try a second supplier.

The software side is the lock. The 10-K describes CUDA as the foundational platform that runs on all NVIDIA GPUs, with hundreds of libraries and tools on top.[8] A rival can build a fast chip. It is harder to build the years of tools that programmers already know.

Strength two: price, and what it says about demand

NVIDIA kept a 75.0 percent gross margin while revenue doubled.[1] The peers in Chapter 3 earned less in their latest quarter: Broadcom 69.1 percent, AMD 53.8 percent, Marvell 53.1 percent and Intel 40.4 percent.[20][19][22][21] A buyer that keeps paying a 75 percent margin is telling you that it has few close substitutes today. The spending also looks sustainable on NVIDIA's side. Research and development cost $7.05 billion in the second quarter, more than twice AMD's $2.53 billion and more than Broadcom's $2.90 billion in their latest quarters.[1][19][20]

NVIDIA has also tried to lock up the parts it needs. It says it has partnered with suppliers to secure the critical components needed to meet demand for the next several years, and its supply and capacity commitments rose from $119 billion to $279 billion in one quarter, mostly for memory.[2][3] The same fact has a second reading, which is that NVIDIA now owes suppliers a very large sum whether or not demand holds. We score that under Layer 2 in Chapter 2B.

Weakness one: few customers, few suppliers

In fiscal 2026 one direct customer made up 22 percent of revenue and another 14 percent.[8] In the first half of fiscal 2027 three direct customers made up 16, 15 and 13 percent.[3] Losing or delaying one of them would be felt in a single quarter. On the supply side, NVIDIA relies on TSMC and Samsung for wafers, on SK Hynix, Micron and Samsung for memory, and on a packaging method called CoWoS (chip-on-wafer-on-substrate), which joins the logic chip to memory.[8] The latest 10-Q says NVIDIA is currently experiencing certain supply constraints.[3]

Competition is real but, so far, has not shown up in the margin. The 10-K names AMD, Huawei and Intel as chip rivals, and it names large cloud companies including Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft as firms with internal teams designing their own AI hardware.[8] Regulators in the European Union, the United States, the United Kingdom, China and South Korea have asked NVIDIA for information about its sales and its investments in AI model makers, and the 10-Q says more requests are expected.[3]

Weakness two: China is shut out, and the cycle has turned down before

Shipments of Data Center Hopper products to China were less than 1 percent of Data Center revenue in the second quarter, and the outlook for the third quarter assumes no Data Center compute revenue from China.[2][1] That makes China an option rather than a need. Customers headquartered in China still accounted for $7.9 billion of revenue in the quarter, so the exposure has not vanished.[3]

The last downturn is a reminder of how fast the numbers can move. In fiscal 2023, revenue was flat at $26.97 billion against $26.91 billion the year before, the gross margin fell to 56.9 percent from 64.9 percent, and the operating margin fell to 15.7 percent from 37.3 percent.[15][14] Net income fell 55 percent in that year.[14][15] That was a smaller company and a different demand source, but it shows what an inventory-and-demand mismatch does to a chip maker's profit.

Management guidance is the forecast a company publishes for the next quarter. The test of management is whether it delivers what it says. NVIDIA's record on its own quarterly forecasts is clean and, in each of the last three quarters, conservative. On capital return, the record is also kept. In fiscal 2026 NVIDIA returned $41.1 billion to shareholders.[7] In the first quarter of fiscal 2027 it returned about $20.0 billion, and in the second about $26.0 billion, so two quarters exceeded the whole prior year.[9][1] In May the board raised the quarterly dividend from $0.01 to $0.25 a share and added $80.0 billion to the repurchase authorization.[9]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, describes the strongest case against our own view, names the one claim the whole conclusion depends on, and says when we will learn more. The aim is to say what evidence would prove the work wrong before that evidence arrives.

Risk one: customers are being financed, in part, by NVIDIA

The weakest assumption behind the base target is that demand is organic. The 10-Q says that if AI clouds do not sell committed capacity to third parties, NVIDIA has agreed to purchase that capacity, and that customers may fail to meet financial commitments or become insolvent.[3] NVIDIA also guarantees up to $105 billion of lease payments at the Ohio campus where an OpenAI affiliate is the tenant, and OpenAI has agreed to reimburse NVIDIA for any payments made.[4][3] A reimbursement promise is only as good as the party making it. If end demand for AI computing falls short of the cost of the capacity, NVIDIA could lose both the sale and the guarantee.

The evidence that would show the risk is real is rising collection days, which went from 45 to 60 in one quarter, new or larger guarantees, or a customer asking for longer payment terms.[2] We will see it in the third-quarter report and 10-Q.

Risk two: $279 billion of supply promises meet demand that may slow

The weakest assumption behind the free cash flow estimate is that supply commitments can be sold through. The 10-Q says NVIDIA may be unable to reduce them if demand differs from expectations, and that some supplier agreements may be cancelable, rescheduled or adjustable only before firm orders are placed.[3] Inventory already rose to $31.6 billion from $25.8 billion in one quarter.[2] A mismatch between supply and demand is what cut the gross margin to 56.9 percent in fiscal 2023.[14]

The company's own outlook for the third quarter is a gross margin of 74.0 percent, 100 basis points lower than the second quarter, and memory prices are already described as elevated.[1][2] If the next outlook shows another step down, our base case weakens.

Risk three: few customers, and a market that has stopped paying for growth

The weakest assumption behind the scenario probabilities is that the market will pay about 18.7 times FY2028 profit once the stock breaks out. Today it pays 13.5 times, and three direct customers were 44 percent of first-half revenue.[3] One of them cutting orders would move the whole company's revenue in a single quarter. Custom chips from cloud companies are a second threat, since the 10-K names Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft as firms designing their own AI hardware.[8]

Export rules are a third. The outlook already assumes no Data Center compute revenue from China, so a ban would not change the forecast, but regulators in five jurisdictions have asked for information and the 10-Q says more requests are likely.[1][3]

The red-team bear case, in one paragraph

A sceptic would write it this way. NVIDIA's revenue doubled because a few customers raced to build AI capacity, and NVIDIA lent them its balance sheet to do it. Cash from operations fell from $50.3 billion in the first quarter to $24.1 billion in the second as receivables grew by $22.3 billion.[2][1] Commitments and guarantees of $530.5 billion are 9.4 times its cash and debt securities. Profit includes $23.7 billion of equity gains that will not repeat.[1] The stock has already stopped rising, and the market is in correction. If any one large customer stumbles, the guarantees turn from a sales tool into a liability, and the stock falls toward the 52-week low of $164.27. The sceptic reads today's 13.5 times multiple on FY2028 estimates as the market refusing to believe those estimates. Chapter 5 reads the same multiple as room to re-rate if the estimates are met.
