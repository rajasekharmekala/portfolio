Before you read

Oracle (NYSE: ORCL) sells databases and software, and it now rents out computing power for artificial intelligence (AI).[1][2] Sales grew 30% last quarter, and signed future work, called remaining performance obligations (RPO), hit $664 billion.[1][7] The problem is cash: Oracle spent $75.7 billion on equipment in a year but made $46.9 billion from operations, so it borrowed and sold shares[1][21]. Our verdict is REJECTED for new money (PUBLISHED_WITH_GATE_EXCEPTIONS, BEST_EFFORT_AFTER_REVIEW_LIMIT): we keep our 35 shares under a $120.22 stop and add none[28]. We sell on a close below $120.22, a source showing one customer above 25% of the backlog, a cut below BBB-/Baa3 by S&P or Moody's, or a failed financing[26][27].

1. What the company does

Oracle sells three things: cloud services, software, and hardware with services. Cloud services means customers rent computing, storage and business applications that Oracle runs for them. Software means customers license Oracle's database and applications and pay a yearly support fee. In the quarter ended August 31, 2026, revenue was $19.3 billion, of which cloud was $11.6 billion (60%), software $5.55 billion, services $1.41 billion and hardware $0.77 billion.[1] The cloud and software business is 88% of revenue on a trailing four-quarter basis, hardware is 4% and services are 8%.[1]

The oldest and steadiest part is the database. Companies run payroll, banking and supply chains on Oracle Database, and they pay a support fee every year that Oracle books as software support revenue. That line was $4.90 billion in the latest quarter, down 1%, and Oracle says substantially all support customers renew.[1] New licenses are shrinking: license revenue fell to $655 million from $766 million as customers move to the cloud.[1] The second steady part is cloud applications such as Fusion and NetSuite, which grew 10% to $4.22 billion.[1] Oracle also now sells its database inside Microsoft, Amazon and Google clouds, a line management says grew 353% in the quarter.[18]

The fast part is Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI), where customers rent racks of AI chips, mostly graphics processing units (GPUs), to train and run AI models.[2] OCI revenue was $7.39 billion in the quarter, up 121%, and $18.1 billion for fiscal 2026, up 77%.[1][8] The contracts are long. Oracle reported $664 billion of signed future work, and about 13% of it, roughly $86 billion, turns into revenue within twelve months; 37% arrives in months 13 to 36 and 34% in months 37 to 60.[1] To serve them Oracle builds data centers and buys chips first and is paid over the life of the contract, so it spends cash years before it collects most of it. In the latest quarter it delivered 850 megawatts of new capacity and more than 300,000 GPUs since the end of May.[7]

Who pays? Enterprises and governments pay for the database and applications. A small number of very large AI companies pay for compute. Oracle's 10-K says no single customer reached 10% of revenue in fiscal 2026, 2025 or 2024, but it says nothing about who holds the backlog.[2] The December 2025 results release named Meta and NVIDIA as new commitments, and credit-rating agencies describe a $300 billion OpenAI contract, which S&P estimates at roughly half of the backlog.[10][21][22] Oracle itself does not publish that split. Americas customers produce 71% of revenue.[1] Larry Ellison, the founder, owns 38.2% of the shares; the day-to-day leaders are co-CEOs Mike Sicilia and Clay Magouyrk and CFO Hilary Maxson.[16][18]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • OCI (Oracle Cloud Infrastructure): Oracle's rented computing, storage and networking, including AI chip clusters.
  • GPU: graphics processing unit, the chip that trains and runs AI models.
  • Hyperscaler: one of the very large cloud providers, such as Microsoft, Amazon or Google.
  • Neocloud: a newer company, such as CoreWeave, built mainly to rent out AI computing.
  • Megawatt (MW): a measure of the power a data center can draw; more megawatts means more chips.
  • At-the-market (ATM) program: a way to sell new shares gradually into the open market.
  • Mandatory convertible preferred stock: a share class that pays a fixed dividend and converts into common shares on a set date.
  • Customer prepayment: cash a customer pays before Oracle delivers the service, recorded as deferred revenue.
  • Lease commitments: signed data-center leases that have not started, so they are not yet on the balance sheet.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Oracle's position is strong in two places and stretched in a third. The first strength is the installed base. Oracle Database sits under core systems at large organizations, so customers rarely switch, and the support line has renewed for decades.[1][2] The second is speed in AI infrastructure: in the latest quarter Oracle says its GPU fleet ran at 97.9% utilization, renewed capacity sold at a 20% premium, and a customer acceptance step at its Abilene campus shrank to 24 hours.[18] Demand is running ahead of supply, and management says it booked another $30 billion of AI contracts in the quarter.[7]

The stretch is financial. Building capacity for contracts that pay out over five years or more means Oracle spends first. Capital spending was $55.7 billion in fiscal 2026 against revenue of $67.4 billion, and $28.5 billion in the August quarter alone.[2][1] Management guides to $90 billion to $95 billion of capital spending in fiscal 2027, with no more than $70 billion in cash after customer prepayments.[18] Gross margin, the share of each sales dollar left after direct costs, fell to 60.0% from 67.3% in a year, because new data centers carry costs before they carry revenue.[1]

Competition is large. Oracle's 10-K names Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, SAP, Salesforce and others as rivals, and the three largest cloud companies are also its multicloud partners.[2] The difference that matters is funding. Microsoft and Alphabet turned $183 billion and $186 billion of operating cash into $67.0 billion and $53.3 billion of free cash flow after record capital spending; Amazon and CoreWeave have negative free cash flow like Oracle.[23][24][25][26] Microsoft's backlog is $684 billion and Alphabet's is $519.5 billion, so Oracle's $664 billion is comparable in size but rests on a far smaller cash engine.[23][24][1]

Where the business is weak

Four weak points matter. First, the backlog is concentrated. S&P estimates OpenAI at roughly half of the $638 billion backlog it measured in July, and calls it a key credit risk; Moody's said in 2025 that the build is effectively one of the world's largest project financings that leans on a single counterparty.[21][22] Both statements reach us through secondary summaries because the rating agencies' own pages are blocked, and Oracle gives no figure of its own.[2] Second, Oracle has far more commitments than its balance sheet shows. It has $288 billion of data-center leases that have not started, plus $34.2 billion of purchase obligations, on top of $34.6 billion of operating lease liabilities and $9.2 billion of finance lease liabilities already recorded.[1]

Third, the financing leans on new shares. Oracle sold 141 million shares for $19.9 billion through its at-the-market program in one quarter, after a $5.0 billion mandatory convertible preferred in February and $43.0 billion of senior notes in fiscal 2026.[1][2][15] The common share count rose 5.0% in the quarter, from 2,880 million to 3,024 million.[1] Fourth, there is a pending securities class action filed on February 3, 2026, alleging misleading statements about the cloud infrastructure business; Oracle says it has meritorious defenses and the motion to dismiss will be fully briefed by December 18, 2026.[1]

What this means for an owner: the products are strong and the demand is real, but the owner of the stock stands behind $125 billion of borrowings[1], billions of lease and purchase promises, and a backlog that depends heavily on a few customers. Chapter 2B tests those points one measure at a time.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

For a cloud and AI infrastructure company with a large backlog and heavy spending, five measures matter: how much signed work exists and how fast it turns into revenue, how fast the infrastructure business grows, whether margins hold as capacity ramps, how the spending is funded, and how concentrated the buyers are. We chose them because each one can break the investment case alone, and the scoring rule for each is written before the reading.

The scoring uses three labels. PASS means healthy. WATCH means one amber flag. FAIL means a red flag that the evidence confirms. Layer 2 needs at least 4 of 5 PASS to support new money, and a held name that fails two or more measures becomes a candidate for replacement. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A company's guidance record tells you how much to trust the next guide. Oracle's operating guidance has been reliable. It forecast $18 billion of OCI revenue for fiscal 2026 and delivered $18.1 billion, and in each of the last two quarters revenue and cloud growth landed in or above its own ranges while non-GAAP earnings per share came in above range.[11][8][9][7] The weaker record is capital: it guided fiscal 2026 capital spending to $50 billion in March, and the year closed at $55.7 billion, 11% higher.[9][2]

Financing promises have held so far. In February 2026 Oracle said it would raise up to $50 billion of debt and equity and issue no more bonds that calendar year; it raised $25 billion of notes and $5.0 billion of preferred in days, then completed the $20 billion share program by August, and the August quarter shows no new notes.[9][15][1] The open items are the claim that new AI contracts need no incremental capital and the later fiscal-year funding. The June release says Oracle expects to raise about $40 billion in fiscal 2027 including the $20 billion already sold, so another $20 billion remains to be raised.[8]

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter lays out five years of results, then tests Oracle against the Layer 1 floors in the scoring rules. The short version: sales and profit have grown every year, but cash flow has swung from strongly positive to deeply negative, and borrowings have risen from $75.9 billion to $129.5 billion in four years.[17] The cause is capital spending, which grew from $4.5 billion in fiscal 2022 to $55.7 billion in fiscal 2026, while operating cash flow grew from $9.5 billion to $32.0 billion.[17][2]

Profit looks healthy because spending on equipment reaches the income statement slowly, through depreciation. Depreciation was $7.6 billion in fiscal 2026 and $3.2 billion in the August quarter alone, against $1.4 billion a year earlier.[17][1] Net property and equipment rose from $43.5 billion to $127.8 billion in fifteen months, and $48.5 billion of it is still construction in progress, so a large share of the investment has not yet started to earn or to depreciate.[2][1] The income statement therefore understates the cost of the build, and the cash flow statement tells the truer story.

Two cash items need care. First, operating cash flow in the latest year includes customer prepayments that carry a financing component: $4.6 billion in fiscal 2026 and $11.4 billion in the August quarter alone. Oracle books the cash in operating activities and the interest cost separately, which makes operating cash flow look better than a normal cash profit.[2][1] Second, free cash flow on the company's own definition is operating cash flow minus capital expenditure: negative $23.7 billion for fiscal 2026 and negative $28.7 billion for the twelve months to August 31, 2026.[2][1] The ledger's free-cash-flow field shows -$45.9 billion because its data vendor counts spending differently; we use the filings.[27]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report has a weak point, and this chapter names it before the market does. We built the strongest bear case we could from our own conclusions, tested the expected-return math against it, and picked the one claim that the whole view rests on. Three fatal-flaw scenarios follow, each with the evidence that would show it and the date we expect to know.

The red-team bear case is this. At $128.23 Oracle looks cheap only if every dollar of the $664 billion backlog is paid on time and capital stays available. Shareholders sit behind $125.3 billion of borrowings, $43.8 billion of recorded lease liabilities and $288 billion of leases that have not yet started.[1] Free cash flow is -$28.7 billion over twelve months and analysts expect -$37.7 billion for fiscal 2027.[1][19] The company has raised $19.9 billion of equity in one quarter and plans about $20 billion more of financing this fiscal year.[1][8] The same management that said new contracts need no extra capital reported $18.0 billion of cash capital spending after prepayments in a single quarter, and S&P estimates one customer at roughly half the backlog.[9][1][18][21]

We stress-tested our expected return against that case. With a 20% bull weight, the expected 12-month value stays above the close unless the bear probability exceeds 63.6%, and a 50% bear weight still leaves +9.4%. The bear-weight test leaves the base multiple at 15.75 times, so a second test changes only that. With the base valued at the stressed 9 times, the value is 20% x 230.00 + 45% x 98.91 + 35% x 84.60 = 46.00 + 44.51 + 29.61 = $120.12, which is 6.3% below the close, and the break-even base multiple is 10.64 times.[19] The positive expected value therefore depends on the base multiple holding, and the hold in Chapter 7 is conditional for that reason. It is a policy choice under a stop, not a forecast of gain. The math also ignores path risk: a gap below the $120.22 stop, a forced equity raise at a low price, or a rating cut that raises the cost of every future lease and note.[2]

Fatal flaw 1: the backlog's largest buyer cannot or will not pay

Oracle's 10-K says its returns depend on customer demand and on key customers meeting their contracts, and S&P estimates one customer, OpenAI, at roughly half of the $638 billion backlog it reviewed in July.[2][21] Moody's described the build as effectively one of the world's largest project financings, resting on a single counterparty.[22] If that customer's own funding slips, Oracle would hold data-center leases of fifteen to nineteen years and chips bought on credit with no revenue to match.[1] The evidence would be an Oracle disclosure of a backlog split, a delayed or renegotiated contract, or a rating action citing the customer. We expect the first real information at the December 14, 2026 results and the 10-Q that follows.[18]

Fatal flaw 2: financing closes before the build is paid for

S&P cut Oracle to BBB- on July 9, 2026, one notch above junk, and expects its adjusted leverage in the mid-4x range in fiscal 2027.[21] Oracle's revolving credit line requires EBITDA to cover net interest at least 3.0 times; our measure is 6.6 times, so the covenant is not the near-term danger.[2][1] The danger is the cost and availability of new money: the 10-K warns that a downgrade could raise borrowing costs and affect the terms of data-center leases.[2] About $20 billion of fiscal 2027 funding remains to be raised, and Oracle has said it does not expect to sell more debt this calendar year, which points to equity at the prevailing price.[8] The evidence would be a rating action, a new share program, or a widening spread; we expect it around the December results.

Fatal flaw 3: the returns arrive later than the depreciation

Depreciation reached $3.2 billion in the August quarter against $1.4 billion a year earlier, and gross margin fell 7.3 points.[1] Oracle assigns servers a six-year life.[1] Management says most of the GPUs it renewed were four or more years old and resold at a 20% premium, which supports the long-life view today.[18] If AI chip prices or demand fall, renewals could price below cost and depreciation would outrun revenue, which would pull margin down further. The evidence would be a gross margin below 55%, a falling renewal premium, or utilization under 90%; we get a reading every quarter, the next on December 14, 2026.[18]

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is REJECTED for new money, and the rule is the Core underwriting floor, not a view that Oracle is a poor company. A Core name needs a Stage 1 breakout or Stage 2 chart, a free cash flow margin of at least 12%, net debt below 3.0 times EBITDA and stock pay below 5% of revenue; Oracle misses four of those, and Layer 2 gives 2 of 5 PASS against the 4 required.[29][1][2] Neither the compounder nor the turnaround archetype qualifies. The verdict would change to ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP only if free cash flow turns positive, the stock has a weekly close above its 50-day average (now $145.12) with positive 28-day relative strength and a flat or rising 50-day average, and the funding and concentration measures are both at WATCH or better. That is the same upgrade trigger set out at the end of this chapter.

This is a decision about adding, not a sell signal, and it applies to the 35 shares held at Schwab. No exit rule is met today: the close of $128.23 is above the $120.22 stop, the chart is Stage 1 and not Stage 4, and only one Layer 2 measure fails where the rule needs two.[28][29] We therefore hold all 35 shares, add nothing and trim nothing. The hold is conditional. It rests on the base multiple holding, because Chapter 5 shows the probability-weighted value is 6.3% below the close if the base outcome is valued at 9 times. It is a policy choice under a stop, not a forecast of gain. The hold becomes REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER, and we sell all 35 shares, on any one of four conditions: a close below $120.22; a primary source confirming one customer above 25% of the backlog; a downgrade of Oracle below investment grade by S&P (below BBB-) or Moody's (below Baa3); or a failed financing, meaning an announced debt or equity offering that is withdrawn or not completed, or the roughly $20 billion still to be raised in fiscal 2027 not being completed by the third-quarter results.[21][22][8]
