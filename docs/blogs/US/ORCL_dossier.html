Before you read

Oracle sells databases and business software, and it now builds data centres that rent computing power to artificial-intelligence labs. The business is real and growing fast, but cash flow is deeply negative, debt is large, and about half of the signed backlog reportedly sits with one customer, so the position is too big for the risk[42]. On 2026-10-28 the stock rose 10.7 percent after management dated the turn to positive cash flow and capped new funding[7][42]. We replace the earlier plan to sell all 35 shares with a smaller one: sell 20, hold 15, and set a stop at $115.40[43]. The view is wrong if cash flow is not positive by late fiscal 2028, or if a rating agency cuts Oracle to junk.

1. What the company does

Oracle makes money in four ways. It sells databases and business applications, it rents computing power from its own data centres, it sells servers, and it sells consulting. Fiscal 2026, the year to 31 May 2026, brought in $67.4 billion of revenue[1]. Cloud was $34.0 billion of that and software licences and support were $24.5 billion[1]. In the first quarter of fiscal 2027, to 31 August 2026, revenue grew 30 percent to $19.3 billion. The rented-computing part, called Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI), grew 121 percent to $7.4 billion[2].

Three kinds of customer pay. Large companies and governments pay for the database and applications they already run, mostly on yearly support contracts. Artificial-intelligence labs pay to use rows of graphics chips, called GPUs, that Oracle buys, houses and powers. A third group pays early: in the first quarter customers prepaid $11.4 billion, and some customers buy the chips themselves and ask Oracle to run them[2][4]. The best single measure of what has been signed is RPO (remaining performance obligations), which means contracted revenue that has not yet been delivered. RPO was $664 billion on 31 August 2026, about 9.2 times the last twelve months of revenue[2]. Oracle expects 13 percent of it to become revenue within twelve months and about half within 36 months[2][14].

This business is expensive to build. Oracle spent $55.7 billion on capital expenditure (money spent on buildings and equipment that last for years) in fiscal 2026 and $28.5 billion in the first quarter of fiscal 2027 alone[1][2]. Cash from operations in that quarter was $23.1 billion, so after the spending the company was $5.4 billion short[2]. Free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operations minus capital expenditure, and it is the number to watch in this report. It is negative, and most of this report is about when it turns positive.

Management is new at the top. Clay Magouyrk and Mike Sicilia became co-chief executives in September 2025, and Hilary Maxson became chief financial officer on 6 April 2026[20][19]. Larry Ellison, the founder, is executive chair and owns 38.2 percent of the shares[17]. The people who set the Investor Day targets on 2026-10-28 have therefore run the company for about a year, not a decade.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares. Analysts quote it on a non-GAAP basis, which leaves out items such as share-based pay.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a rough measure of operating cash profit before financing costs.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money invested in the business. A figure below the cost of capital means growth is not yet paying for itself.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is pay given in shares or options. It costs existing owners because new shares are issued, even though it needs no cash.
  • dilution means each existing share owns a smaller slice of the company after new shares are issued.
  • buyback is a company purchasing its own shares, the opposite of an issue.
  • net debt is borrowings minus cash. gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of delivering it, as a share of revenue. operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue.
  • guidance is the forecast management gives to investors. A consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow the stock.
  • price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share. cash flow from operations is the cash a business produces from day-to-day activity before it buys equipment.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has moved: above 70 is hot and below 30 is washed out. ATR (average true range) is the typical size of one day's price move.
  • A 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 sessions; the 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average use 50 and 200 sessions.
  • Stage 2 is the advancing phase of a stock in the Weinstein method, and Stage 4 is the declining phase. Stage 1 is a flat floor after a decline and Stage 3 is a topping phase.
  • A base is a flat stretch of trading after a fall. The pivot is the price level where the base would end. A breakout is a close above that level, and a retest is a later pullback to it.
  • A stop loss is a price at which we sell to limit a loss. A limit order is an order to buy or sell only at a set price or better. The entry zone is the price band where we would be willing to add.
  • asymmetry or reward-to-risk is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop. At 2.0 you win two dollars for each dollar at risk.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Oracle's oldest strength is the database it sells to the world's largest organisations. Customers keep that software for decades because moving it means rewriting the programs built on top of it. Yearly support fees show how sticky it is: software support revenue was $4.9 billion in the first quarter of fiscal 2027, down 1 percent from a year earlier, so it holds steady rather than falling fast[2]. Oracle is also placing its database inside the Amazon, Microsoft and Google clouds, and management says multicloud database revenue grew 353 percent year over year in the quarter[14]. That software base is a cash engine that most AI-cloud start-ups do not have.

The second strength is speed in AI computing. Management says demand exceeds supply: the GPU fleet ran at 97.9 percent utilisation, and chips whose contracts ended were rented again at a 20 percent premium[14]. Oracle delivered 850 megawatts of capacity in one quarter, almost three times the fourth quarter[3][14]. In the latest reported quarter OCI grew 121 percent, against 103 percent at Google Cloud, 48 percent at Microsoft Azure and 37 percent at Amazon Web Services[2][30][28][31]. The quarters differ: Oracle's ends 31 August, Microsoft's and Alphabet's 30 September (both reported after the 2026-10-28 close) and Amazon's 30 June. OCI grew fastest because it started smallest. Growth is not a moat: anyone with enough money can buy capacity, and a customer can leave when a contract ends.

The weaknesses are about money more than demand. Oracle must borrow, lease and issue shares to build what it has promised, and its credit ratings sit near the bottom of investment grade. S&P cut Oracle to BBB- on 9 July 2026, Moody's rated it Baa2 with a negative outlook as of its December 2025 page, and Fitch rated it BBB with a stable outlook in February 2026 according to a press summary[23][22][25]. Oracle does not name its largest backlog customer. S&P has said OpenAI is about half of the $638 billion backlog reported in June, a figure we could source only from press reports of the S&P note, because S&P's own page is behind a paywall[24][23]. The 10-K says that in some OCI offerings Oracle is more concentrated among a number of large customers, and that no single customer was 10 percent or more of revenue in fiscal 2026[1]. Both can be true: revenue so far is spread out, and the backlog is not.

Execution risk is physical. In September Oracle sent a force majeure notice (a contract clause that excuses delay caused by events outside a party's control) to the developer of its New Mexico site, and Oracle said the project remains on its planned schedule[26]. On the 10 September call management said neither New Mexico nor Wisconsin would change fiscal 2027 guidance[14]. Control is a second point. Larry Ellison owns 38.2 percent, and in fiscal 2026 Oracle sold $32.2 million to, and bought $10.4 million from, firms linked to him, which is small[17]. He cancelled a plan to sell shares in September 2026[18]. A securities class action filed on 3 February 2026 alleges false statements about OCI. It is an allegation, not a finding[1].

3. The numbers, in plain words

The five-year record shows a company that grew faster each year and then changed what it spends. Revenue went from $42.4 billion in fiscal 2022 to $67.4 billion in fiscal 2026, an average of 12 percent a year, and the three-year growth rate to fiscal 2026 was 10.5 percent[1][15]. Operating margin rose from 25.7 to 30.6 percent over the same years. Earnings per share (GAAP) went from $2.41 to $5.83[1][15].

Cash tells a different story. Cash flow from operations rose from $9.5 billion to $32.0 billion, but capital expenditure rose faster, from $4.5 billion to $55.7 billion, so free cash flow fell from +$5.0 billion in fiscal 2022 to -$23.7 billion in fiscal 2026[1][15]. Borrowings rose from $75.9 billion to $129.5 billion in the same years and equity went from a $5.8 billion deficit to $43.1 billion positive[16][1]. In the first quarter of fiscal 2027 Oracle sold $19.9 billion of new shares under a $20 billion at-the-market programme and repaid $8.1 billion of debt, which cut borrowings to $125.3 billion[2][21].

Two measures need care. Analysts quote non-GAAP EPS, which leaves out share-based pay, and Oracle's guided $8.10 for fiscal 2027 is on that basis, while trailing GAAP EPS is $6.38[3][2][1]. The second is cash flow from operations, which in the trailing year includes $16.0 billion of customer prepayments that Oracle must repay by delivering service[1][2]. Section by section, we show the static level and the direction side by side, because a stock that scores badly on level and well on direction is a different case from one that scores badly on both.

Layer 1 asks three questions. Is the business solvent, does it fit a style of investing (compounder, turnaround or breakout), and does the price give enough reward for the risk? Oracle passes the first, fits no style at today's price, and fails the third at $144.54. The number

We estimate Oracle's return on invested capital at 12.6 percent when only borrowings count as debt, and 9.8 percent when leases also count.

How it was calculated

Operating income for the last twelve months was $23.1 billion and the tax rate in the latest quarter was 15.1 percent, so after-tax operating profit is about $19.6 billion. Invested capital is equity of $67.2 billion plus borrowings of $125.3 billion minus cash of $37.1 billion, which is $155.5 billion. Adding $43.8 billion of leases gives $199.3 billion.

The inputs

Operating income, interest and tax: 10-K and 10-Q[1][2]. Equity, borrowings, cash and leases at 31 August 2026[2]. A data vendor shows 11.34 percent and a 10.82 percent cost of capital[33].

The range

If the $48.5 billion of construction in progress is left out, because it earns nothing yet, invested capital is $106.9 billion and the return is 18.3 percent. If leases count and the new sites arrive late, the return stays near 10 percent, which is no better than the 10 percent discount rate used in this report.

If the number is off

If the true figure is the lower one, growth is not yet creating value and the stock needs the plan to work to earn a premium. If it is the higher one, the compounder floor of 12 percent is met and the problem is only cash timing.

If EBITDA grows more slowly than the lease start dates, leverage rises past the 4.5 times S&P trigger and a downgrade to junk becomes likely. The revolving credit facility also needs EBITDA of at least three times net interest, which is met by a wide margin today[1].

How it was calculated

Cash flow from operations of $46.9 billion minus capital expenditure of $75.7 billion gives -$28.7 billion. Customer prepayments with a financing component were $4.6 billion in fiscal 2026 and $11.4 billion in the first quarter of fiscal 2027, or $16.0 billion in total, and they are a liability to deliver service later. The range

On management's FY27 figures, capex of $90 to $95 billion against a net cash outlay of about $70 billion leaves a gap of $20 to $25 billion that prepayments and timing cover. If the number is off

If prepayments stop, FCF is worse than the headline by the prepayment amount, and the $20 billion funding cap in the Investor Day plan comes under pressure. If prepayments keep arriving, they reduce borrowing but add a delivery obligation of the same size.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, says what evidence would show it is wrong, and gives the date by which we would know. It then builds the strongest bear case we can against our own conclusions and names the one claim the verdict depends on.

Fatal-flaw scenario 1: the backlog is worth much less than $664 billion

The weakest assumption behind the base and bull cases is that the customers behind the backlog can pay. S&P calls OpenAI a key credit risk for Oracle and says it is about half of the backlog reported in June, and press reports add that SoftBank had to cut a loan backed by OpenAI shares from $10 billion to $6 billion and that OpenAI pushed its IPO to 2027[24][23]. Oracle's own filings do not name the customer, and they warn that some customers may be highly leveraged[1]. Oracle also says the vast majority of new contracts came with prepayment or customer-supplied hardware, which lowers its cash risk, and it expanded its OpenAI relationship in the quarter[14].

Evidence of failure would be slowing additions to RPO, a fall in prepayments, a contract change or a late payment. We would see the first signs in the RPO figure and the prepayment line on 14 December 2026. We give this scenario most of the 30 percent bear weight. Its cost is the $99.00 case.

Fatal-flaw scenario 2: funding fails before cash flow turns

The weakest assumption behind the $20 billion cap is that vendor financing, customer prepayments and customer-supplied chips fill the gap without new borrowing. The market is not paying for that confidence: five-year credit default swaps are at 277 basis points, the ten-year Treasury yields 5.05 percent, and the senior notes trade at 85 cents on the dollar (fair value $105.7 billion against $125.0 billion carrying value)[9][35][2]. S&P would cut again if leverage stays above 4.5 times or it doubts positive free cash flow by FY29, and Fitch's triggers are 3.5 times EBITDA or 4 times with rents[25].

Evidence of failure would be a new bond issue before the end of 2026, which Oracle said it did not expect, a rating action, or a larger equity programme than the $10 billion promised[4][7]. The share price is part of the plan: at $99 the $10 billion equity raise is 101 million shares, not 69 million.

Fatal-flaw scenario 3: delivery, cost and margin do not behave

The weakest assumption behind FY28 EPS is that sites open on time at planned cost. Capex guidance for fiscal 2026 rose from over $25 billion to an actual $55.7 billion[10][1]. Power and permits can delay sites, as the New Mexico notice showed[26], and the largest sites are phased over many quarters. Servers are depreciated over six years[2], while the company says chips that came up for renewal were rented again at a 20 percent premium[14]. If useful lives turn out shorter, EPS falls without any change in cash.

Evidence of failure would be gross margin below 60 percent, a capex run-rate above the $90 to $95 billion guide, or a delay notice at a second site. We would see the first of these on 14 December 2026 and again with the March 2027 results.

The strongest bear case against our own view

Oracle is a leveraged claim on a handful of AI customers, and the customers are young. Revenue is guided up 34 percent while EPS is guided up 6 percent, so each extra dollar of revenue is earning little for shareholders[3][4]. The FY28 EPS step of 35 percent needs the margin ramp that the last two quarters have not shown. The backlog converts slowly (13 percent in twelve months), the leases start before the revenue does, and the rating sits one notch from junk. A bear would say that even a $186.83 base target needs everything to go right, that the 30 percent bear weight is too low, and that a position larger than the 2.5 percent risk-unit size, 15 shares, is a bet on the best case. Our response is the trim: it keeps a position that benefits if the plan works and cuts the loss if it does not.
