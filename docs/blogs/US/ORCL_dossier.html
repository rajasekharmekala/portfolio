1. What the company does

Oracle is best known for the database that large companies use to store and search their most important records, and for business applications such as finance, human resources and supply-chain software. Customers pay for these products in two ways. They buy a software license once, then pay a yearly support fee, or they subscribe to a cloud version and pay for it over time. Oracle says substantially all software support customers renew their contracts, which is the steady base under the whole company.[1] In the fiscal year ended May 31, 2026, Oracle reported total revenue of $67.4 billion, and cloud services were 51% of that total, up from 43% a year earlier.[1]

The fast-growing part is Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI), which rents servers, storage and networking, including the graphics processors that train and run AI models. OCI revenue was $18.1 billion in fiscal 2026, up 77%, and it reached $7.4 billion in the first quarter of fiscal 2027, up 121%.[1][3] Oracle sells much of this capacity under multi-year commitments. The value of signed work that has not yet been billed is called remaining performance obligations (RPO), and it stood at $664 billion on August 31, 2026, up from $455 billion a year earlier.[2] Only about 13% of that backlog is expected to turn into revenue over the next twelve months, 37% in months 13 to 36 and 34% in months 37 to 60.[2]

Oracle also sells its database inside other companies' clouds. Management said multicloud database revenue grew 353% in the first quarter of fiscal 2027, and that Oracle has reached 70 multicloud database regions across Azure and Amazon Web Services.[11] That business matters because it extends the old license-and-support franchise into the clouds where customers now put their applications.

The business model has changed in a way that matters more than any product. Oracle used to earn large cash profits with modest spending on equipment. Now it builds or leases data centers and buys chips ahead of revenue. Capital expenditure — cash spent on long-term plant and equipment — was $55.7 billion in fiscal 2026 and $28.5 billion in the first quarter of fiscal 2027 alone, and management guides to $90 billion to $95 billion for the full fiscal 2027.[1][2][11] Customers prepaying for chips or supplying their own chips now cover part of that cost, and Oracle puts the prepaid and customer-supplied share of its large AI contracts at $75 billion.[4]

Who pays Oracle, and when

Legacy software customers pay Oracle each year and are slow to leave, because moving a large database is expensive and risky. Software support revenue was $19.8 billion in fiscal 2026 and grew 1%, which tells you the base is stable but not growing.[1] Cloud application customers sign one-to-five-year subscriptions and pay as the service runs.[1] AI infrastructure customers are different: Oracle's own filing says that in certain OCI offerings it is more concentrated among a number of large customers, and that the economic return on its data center investment depends on those key customers meeting their contracts.[1] The same filing says no single customer was 10% or more of total revenue in fiscal 2026, 2025 or 2024, so the concentration sits in the backlog and in future revenue, not yet in the reported sales.[1]

How the AI rental business works

Oracle signs a long contract, builds or leases a data center, buys the chips and servers late in construction, and then bills the customer as the capacity is used. Oracle says it does not pay for leased data centers until they are delivered, and that it buys equipment very late in the build, so most of its own spending is on the equipment that goes inside.[8] Oracle has also said that new contracts signed in the first quarter of fiscal 2027 mostly came through prepayment or customer-supplied hardware, and that this new backlog will not affect its capital spending or revenue until fiscal 2028 or later.[11] That structure lowers the cash Oracle must raise, but it does not remove the risk that a customer cannot pay, and it ties Oracle to dates set by power companies and construction crews.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Oracle's strongest asset is the installed base of its database and applications. Customers renew support year after year, and the multicloud database growth shows that the product still wins new workloads inside rival clouds.[1][11] Operating income was $20.6 billion in fiscal 2026 on $67.4 billion of revenue, a 31% operating margin, which is the share of each sales dollar left after operating costs.[1] That margin was 31% in fiscal 2025 as well, so the legacy franchise absorbed the new data center costs without a drop in the headline figure.[1]

The weak points are cost and funding. Gross margin, meaning revenue minus direct costs as a share of revenue, fell from 79.1% in fiscal 2022 to 65.8% in fiscal 2026, and it was 60.0% in the first quarter of fiscal 2027 against 67.3% a year earlier (our calculation from the cited statements).[5][1][3] Management said gross margin stepped down about five points in fiscal 2026 and will step down again in fiscal 2027 as data centers ramp before they earn full revenue.[10] The cloud and software segment margin, which excludes stock pay and some allocations, was 59% in fiscal 2026 versus 63% a year earlier and 55% in the first quarter of fiscal 2027 versus 60%.[1][2]

Competition is with the largest companies in technology. Microsoft and Alphabet each produced more than $53 billion of free cash over the last twelve months, Amazon's was negative $11.6 billion after it spent $173 billion on equipment, and CoreWeave, a pure AI-rental peer, carries $51.6 billion of debt on $5.0 billion of book equity.[26][28][27][29] Oracle sits between the two groups. It has the customer base of a software company and the capital needs of a data center builder, but not the balance sheet of the biggest hyperscalers. The peer matrix in chapter 3 puts numbers on that gap.

Evidence of strength

Demand is real and growing. Total revenue rose 30% in the first quarter of fiscal 2027, cloud revenue rose 62%, and Oracle delivered 850 megawatts of new data center capacity in the quarter.[3] The company also booked more than $30 billion of new AI cloud contracts in that quarter and says it needs no incremental capital from Oracle for them.[3][13] The backlog was $523 billion at the end of November 2025, $638 billion at May 31, 2026 and $664 billion at August 31, 2026.[9][1][2] Operating cash flow was $23.1 billion in the quarter, but $11.4 billion of that came from customer prepayments that carry a financing component, so the underlying figure is lower.[2] Management also raised its full-year guidance, which is its own published forecast, to at least $90 billion of revenue.[3]

Evidence of weakness

Oracle's own filing says it could be locked into multi-year commitments for excess data center space, and the associated financing, if customers do not pay or perform.[1] It also reports $288 billion of lease commitments that have not yet started, up from $260 billion three months earlier, on top of $43.8 billion of lease liabilities already on the balance sheet.[1][2] S&P Global Ratings cut Oracle to BBB- on July 9, 2026, one notch above speculative grade, and its summary named high customer concentration among the reasons.[15][17] Reports based on S&P and Bank of America put roughly half of the backlog, then $638 billion at May 31, 2026, with one customer, OpenAI.[16][12] No share has been published for the $664 billion at August 31, 2026. Shareholder returns have also thinned. A buyback — company repurchases of its own shares —, where a company buys its own shares, was $95 million in fiscal 2026 against $1,202 million in fiscal 2024, and there were none in the first quarter of fiscal 2027.[1][5][2]

7. What we would do, and when

Layer 3 is the third of our checks, and it turns the first two into an action. Layer 1 tested financial levels and trends, and Layer 2 scored the business measures. Layer 3 asks how close the price is to support, how big a position the risk allows, and what the order should look like. Support is a price level where buyers have stepped in before. Portfolio heat means the share of your net worth you would lose if the stop were hit. Risk-unit sizing sets the position so that loss equals a fixed budget, measured in basis points (bps), where one bps is one hundredth of one percent of net worth. A conviction tier sets that budget: TIER_1 allows 100 bps, TIER_2 75, TIER_3 50, TIER_4 35, and TIER_0 allows none, which means zero size.

Two more terms matter here. A structural invalidation stop is a price below which the reasons for owning the stock no longer hold, and ours is $107.94, which is the 63-day low of $114.50 minus one ATR of $6.56. A simple moving average (SMA) is the plain average of closing prices over a set number of days, and an exponential moving average (EMA) gives recent days more weight. The 50-day SMA and the 21-day EMA are the lines we trail a stop under for a winning position. The let-winners-run rule says we never sell a rising Stage 2 stock only because its RSI is high or its weight passed 15%, and we let it grow to 22% while trailing the stop. Oracle is not that case, because it is below every one of those averages. CMP means current market price, which is $126.16.

The decision is REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER for a current holding. The rule behind it is that the stock is in Stage 4, Layer 1 and Layer 2 both fail, and the asymmetry at the current price is 1.52 to 1, below the 2.0 floor for a core position. The tier is TIER_0, so the ceiling for this name is zero percent. The 1.52 to 1 figure is the asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, which is the gain to our base target divided by the loss to the stop. The support ladder places Oracle 0.52 ATR above support, which is the full-size band, but that band sizes eligible names and Oracle is not eligible.
