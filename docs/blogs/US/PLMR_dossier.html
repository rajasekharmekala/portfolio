1. What the company does

Palomar Holdings is a specialty insurer. It sells property and casualty cover to people and businesses in five product groups: earthquake, inland marine and property, casualty, crop, and surety and credit.[1] Specialty means it goes after risks that large generalist insurers price crudely or avoid. Its insurance companies carry an A (Excellent) financial strength rating from A.M. Best, the main rating agency for insurers, and it had 439 employees at the end of 2025.[7] About 52% of its premium is written on an admitted basis, which means a state regulator approves the rates and policy forms. The rest is excess and surplus (E&S) business, where the insurer sets its own terms for risks the admitted market declines.[3]

Its customers are homeowners, small and mid-sized businesses, builders, farmers and contractors. They reach Palomar through retail agents, program administrators, wholesale brokers and partnerships with other insurers rather than buying directly.[7] Program administrators are outside firms that run a book of policies for a niche and place the risk with an insurer. Palomar has been taking over several such books, and management says that rollover helps casualty growth.[3] The largest state is California, at 25.6% of first-half 2026 premium, down from 32.3% a year earlier.[1]

An insurer makes money in two ways. The first is underwriting profit, which is premium collected minus claims and costs. The second is investment income on the premium it holds before claims are paid. The main score is the combined ratio, which is claims plus costs divided by premium. Below 100% means an underwriting profit. In the second quarter Palomar's combined ratio was 83.3%, made of a 34.5% loss ratio and a 48.8% expense ratio, so it kept about 16.7 cents of each premium dollar before investment income.[1] Net investment income was $37.9 million in the first half, up from $25.4 million a year earlier.[1]

Palomar also passes a large share of its premium to reinsurers, which are insurers of insurers. In the second quarter it ceded $305.3 million of $630.5 million of gross premium, so it kept about 52% as net premium.[1] Reinsurance lets it write large earthquake limits while capping its own loss from one event at $20.0 million for an earthquake and $11.0 million for a hurricane. Those caps were about 2.1% and 1.2% of equity at the end of 2025.[7] The price is a reinsurance bill and a credit risk on the reinsurers, which Chapter 2B scores.

How earthquake cover works at Palomar

Earthquake was the largest single product in 2025, at $571.4 million of premium, 28.2% of the total, and it is the second largest in 2026.[6] About 64% of the earthquake book is residential, and 36% is commercial.[3] The residential part is steady. Premium retention (the share of customers who renew) was above 96%, and renewals carry a 10% inflation guard that lifts the insured amount each year.[3] The commercial part is under price pressure. Management says average rates on the commercial earthquake book fell by more than 20%, and rates on large layered property business fell 16%.[3]

Layered business means several insurers each take a slice of a big building, and those slices compete hard on price. Management says some new large accounts are being priced below its own technical level, meaning the price that covers expected claims, reinsurance, costs and capital, and that it is walking away from them.[3] That discipline protects margin but explains why total earthquake premium grew only 0.9% in the first half.[1]

Why a bigger casualty, crop and surety book changes the profile

Casualty premium rose 45.5% in the first half and crop rose 88.3%.[1] Crop is the clearest example of a different business. Most crop premium is written and earned in the third quarter, but crop losses show up earlier in the year, so the first half carries a high loss ratio. Management says it books 2026 crop closer to a 100% net combined ratio early in the year, then trues up.[3] It now expects more than $400 million of crop premium in 2026, up from a prior outlook of about $320 million.[3]

Surety bonds are promises to a project owner that a contractor will finish the work. The business grew 235.6% in the second quarter because Palomar bought Gray Surety on January 31, 2026.[1][2] Management says it expects the loss ratio to keep rising as these attritional lines grow, and it would not be surprised if the ratio rose again next year or the year after.[3] Chapter 2B tests whether margins hold while that happens.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Gross written premium is the premium charged to customers before reinsurance. Net written premium is what Palomar keeps after paying reinsurers.
  • The combined ratio is claims plus costs divided by premium. Below 100% means the insurer made an underwriting profit.
  • Reinsurance is insurance bought by an insurer to cap its own losses. The retention is the loss the insurer keeps from one event before reinsurers pay.
  • Attritional losses are the steady, everyday claims, as opposed to rare catastrophe losses such as earthquakes and hurricanes.
  • IBNR (incurred but not reported) is the reserve for claims that have happened but have not yet been reported or fully measured.
  • Adjusted net income is Palomar's own profit measure. It adds back stock-based pay, amortization of purchased intangibles, deal costs and some other items, and removes investment gains.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business is strong on returns and loss control, and weaker on growth quality and on how clearly its profit can be read. The returns are real: return on equity rose from 12.1% in 2021 to 23.6% in 2025, and management reports an adjusted figure of 26.3% for the second quarter.[15][6][1] The five peers in Chapter 3 earn between 17.3% and 26.6%, so Palomar sits in the upper half of a strong group.[25][26][27][28][29]

The first source of strength is the design of the risk transfer. Palomar buys $3.92 billion of earthquake cover and $135 million of continental U.S. hurricane cover, and it keeps only $20 million and $11 million of a single event.[3][4] Hawaii hurricane business sits in a reciprocal called Laulima, which keeps $1.5 million per event and has $865 million of cover.[4] Catastrophe losses were a net credit of $0.7 million in 2025 and a cost of $27.8 million in 2024, which is small against earnings of $197.1 million and $117.6 million.[6] Reinsurers also renewed 14 treaties in the second quarter at better or similar terms, which is a market vote on the quality of the book.[3]

The second source is diversification by line. No product was more than one third of premium, about half of the book is property, about 19% comes from crop and surety that do not follow the usual insurance price cycle, and 52% is admitted.[3] That mix lets management shrink lines where prices have fallen and grow lines where they have not. In the second quarter it cut back on transactional liability and cyber because pricing no longer met its return hurdles.[3] The model is not free of cost. Newer lines have higher expected claims, so the second-quarter loss ratio was 34.5% against 25.7% a year earlier.[1]

The weaknesses are specific. First, the original franchise is shrinking in price: commercial earthquake rates fell by more than 20%.[3] Second, the profit that investors quote is the adjusted figure, and it grew 27.1% in the first half while net income under accounting rules grew 6.8%. The gap is stock-based pay, amortization of purchased intangibles and deal costs.[1] Third, Palomar relies on reinsurers: reinsurance recoverables of $586.0 million equal 60% of equity.[2] Fourth, the Gray Surety purchase for about $314.0 million was funded with a $300 million term loan and lost money in its first full quarter.[2][17]

What the competitive evidence says

A real edge in insurance shows up as returns that last through a price cycle. Palomar's combined ratio ran between 76.6% and 80.4% in each year from 2021 to 2025 while premium grew from $535.2 million to $2,028.3 million.[15][14][12][11][6] The company grew four-year premium at about 39.5% a year without letting the ratio slip, and that is the strongest single piece of evidence for underwriting skill.

Two cautions apply. A younger casualty book has not yet shown how it behaves over several years, and management itself holds 84% of casualty reserves as IBNR because the claims are still developing.[3] And first-half 2026 was a quiet catastrophe period: catastrophe losses were a net credit of $0.1 million.[1] Chapter 2B scores reserves and catastrophe exposure directly. What this means for an owner: the franchise is good, and the evidence for it is a multi-year record, but part of today's margin comes from a quiet catastrophe period and from reserve releases.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of results are the starting point. For an insurer, revenue is premium earned plus investment income and gains, so the more useful lines are net income, EPS (EPS stands for earnings per share, which is profit divided by the number of shares), book value per share and the combined ratio. Book value per share is the owners' equity divided by shares, and for an insurer it is the closest thing to what the business would be worth if it stopped writing new policies. The table shows all of these from the filings, with the first half of 2026 in the last column.[15][14][12][11][6][1]

The portfolio's quality tests were written for industrial companies, so several need an insurer's version. ROIC stands for return on invested capital, and for an insurer we use return on equity and return on tangible equity, which strips out goodwill. FCF stands for free cash flow, the cash left after running costs and equipment, and it is not meaningful when premium arrives years before claims are paid. Net debt is borrowings minus cash, and EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. Net debt divided by EBITDA is a standard leverage test, and for an insurer we use debt-to-capital and interest cover instead. SBC stands for stock-based compensation, which is pay in shares or options and is a real cost to owners. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by EPS.[34]

The rules also use price levels. A stop loss is a price at which we sell to cap a loss. A limit order is an order to buy only at a set price or lower, and the entry zone is the price range where we would place it. Asymmetry means the gap between upside and downside, and reward-to-risk is the upside to our target divided by the fall to the stop. Charts are sorted into four stages: a Stage 2 stock is in a rising markup phase, and a Stage 4 stock is in a falling markdown phase, which is the only chart pattern that triggers an automatic reject. A breakout is a close above a prior high that shows buyers are taking control.[34]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 5 gave a $154 central value, a $101 bear value and a $191 bull value. This chapter plays the skeptic. It builds the strongest honest case against buying, names the three ways the thesis could fail outright, and says what single claim the whole call rests on.

Flaw one: the profit is flattered by what is left out and by quiet catastrophe years

Adjusted net income grew 27.1% in the first half, but reported net income grew 6.8%.[1] Reserve releases supplied $24.7 million, about 20% of pre-tax income, and catastrophe losses were a net credit of $0.1 million.[1] In 2024 catastrophe losses were $27.8 million, which was 5.5% of net earned premium.[6] If net earned premium reaches about $1.2 billion in 2026 (our estimate) and the catastrophe ratio returned to 5.5%, losses would be about $66 million against the $8 million to $12 million guided. After tax that is about $44 million, or about $1.67 a share, roughly 17% of our FY2027 EPS.[35]

Two Hawaii hurricanes already hit in the third quarter. Hurricane Lala passed the Big Island in August and Hurricane Lowell damaged Kauai on September 7, and a trade-press headline put the losses at about $1 billion.[30][31] Palomar's Hawaii reciprocal keeps only $1.5 million per event, so the direct hit should be small, but the third quarter is also the seasonal peak for losses, and the first look at the damage comes on November 4.[3][4][19]

Flaw two: growth comes from the lines where the edge is thinnest

Casualty, crop and surety supplied the growth, and each has weaker evidence of an edge than earthquake. Crop is booked near a 100% net combined ratio early in the year, casualty is partly rolling over books from outside administrators, and surety was bought.[3] The reported loss ratio rose from 24.7% to 33.9% in the half, and management says it will probably keep rising next year and the year after.[1][3]

If those lines mature worse than reserved, the 84% casualty IBNR cushion shrinks. A rising combined ratio would also remove the main support for a 16-times multiple. The Gray Surety unit lost $4.6 million in its first full quarter, and the term loan costs about $4.0 million of interest a quarter.[3][2]

Flaw three: the original franchise stops paying while debt and a rich multiple remain

Earthquake premium grew 0.9% in the first half, and commercial earthquake rates fell by more than 20%.[1][3] The shares trade at 3.64 times book and 4.80 times tangible book.[35] A multiple like that assumes the reported return on equity stays above 20%, and it was 19.9% in the first half.[1]

If prices keep falling and the new lines earn less, reported return on equity could drift toward the high teens. At 3.0 times book the shares would be near $112, and at the 2022 multiple on flat EPS they would be near $101, our bear case.[35][21]
