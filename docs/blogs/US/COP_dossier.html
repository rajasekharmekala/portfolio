Before you read

ConocoPhillips is the largest US independent oil and gas producer, with output of 2,248 thousand barrels of oil equivalent a day in the second quarter of 2026.[1] It is financially strong: it made $10.1 billion of free cash flow (cash left after capital spending) in the last four quarters, and its net debt (debt less cash) is only $15.6 billion.[2][3] The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: the $149.06 close sits 2.57 ATR (average true range) above support, and our entry price is $127.95 or lower.[39] The stock trades at 13.8 times 2027 EPS (earnings per share), against 9.4 to 12.2 times for four oil peers, so the price already assumes strong oil.[25][33][34][35][36] The call is wrong if oil falls toward $75 and the stock loses value, or if oil stays near $100 and the stock rises without us[32][31].

1. What the company does

ConocoPhillips finds, develops and sells crude oil, bitumen (a heavy oil from Canadian oil sands), natural gas liquids (NGLs, such as ethane and propane), natural gas and liquefied natural gas (LNG). It earns the market price for each product, and it is unhedged, which means it holds no contracts that fix prices in advance.[3] In the second quarter of 2026 its average sale price was $62.33 per barrel of oil equivalent, against $45.77 a year earlier, because WTI (the US benchmark crude price) averaged $92.79 against $63.74.[1][2][3] A barrel of oil equivalent counts 6 thousand cubic feet of gas as one barrel, so it lets a reader add gas to oil. The company does not set these prices, so its profit moves with them every quarter.

The company is large. It operates in 15 countries, employs about 9,600 people and held $124 billion of assets at June 30, 2026.[3] The Lower 48 states supplied 1,479 of its 2,248 thousand barrels of oil equivalent a day in the second quarter (66 percent): 720 from the Delaware Basin, 363 from the Eagle Ford, 202 from the Midland Basin and 189 from the Bakken.[1] At the end of 2025 it held about 782,000 net acres in the Delaware Basin, 489,000 in the Eagle Ford, 799,000 in the Bakken and 416,000 in the Midland Basin.[6] Proved reserves, the oil and gas that engineers are reasonably certain can be produced at current prices and costs, were 7,637 million barrels of oil equivalent at the end of 2025, which is 8.8 years of that year's 867 million barrels of production by our arithmetic.[6]

The company changed shape in 2024. On 2024-11-22 it closed the all-stock purchase of Marathon Oil, issuing about 143 million shares and taking on $4.6 billion of Marathon's debt.[8][15] Production rose from 1,987 thousand barrels of oil equivalent a day in 2024 to 2,375 in 2025, an increase of 388, but only 57 of those barrels (2.5 percent) were underlying growth after removing the effect of purchases and sales.[5][6] Since then the company has sold noncore Lower 48 assets, $3.2 billion in 2025 and $1.7 billion that closed in July 2026, which met its $5 billion disposal target ahead of schedule.[5][1] Production fell to 2,248 in the second quarter of 2026 because the war in the Middle East cut output from its Qatar gas investments and because higher oil prices raised royalties at Surmont, its Canadian oil sands project.[1]

The next phase rests on four projects. Willow, an Alaska oil field, has a capital budget of $8.5 billion to $9.0 billion, was about 50 percent complete after the 2026 winter season and is due to produce its first oil in early 2029.[7][4][6] In LNG, the company owns 25 percent of each of the North Field East and North Field South projects in Qatar, 30 percent of the operating N3 plant there and 30 percent of Port Arthur LNG on the US Gulf Coast, and it has contracted 12 million tonnes a year of LNG offtake to resell.[3][1] In July 2026 it agreed to buy 42 percent of a joint venture that is redeveloping four oil fields in Kirkuk, northern Iraq, with $0.3 billion to $0.5 billion of cash due at closing and a cost of supply near $30 a barrel according to one report of the call, and it signed an agreement to re-enter Syria.[3][1][20] Management's headline promise is an extra $7 billion of free cash flow by 2029, which Chapter 2B tests against the record.[5]

What the volumes are made of

In the second quarter of 2026 the company produced 1,094 thousand barrels a day of crude oil, 420 of NGLs, 115 of bitumen and 3,710 million cubic feet a day of natural gas, which is 618 thousand barrels of oil equivalent.[2] Oil and bitumen together were about 54 percent of the total, NGLs 19 percent and gas 27 percent, by our arithmetic. Oil moves earnings most, but gas matters in a different way: the Lower 48 realized gas price was negative $1.44 per thousand cubic feet in the quarter, against positive $1.60 a year earlier, and the supplement prints this without explanation.[2] Gas from Qatar and Australia, sold as LNG, is priced separately and averaged $5.62 per thousand cubic feet from equity affiliates.[2]

Because ConocoPhillips pays tax in many countries, a higher oil price does not all reach shareholders. The consolidated income tax rate was 35.4 percent in the second quarter, and it was 74.9 percent in Europe, Middle East and North Africa, where Norway and Libya take a large share of each barrel.[2] Alaska also pays production-based taxes that rose with price, so Chapter 2 treats a $1 move in oil as worth less to ConocoPhillips than a simple volume-times-price estimate would suggest.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • WTI and Henry Hub are the benchmark prices for US crude oil and US natural gas.
  • NGLs are natural gas liquids such as ethane and propane, pulled out of wet gas and sold separately.
  • LNG and MTPA: LNG is natural gas cooled to a liquid for shipping; MTPA is million tonnes per year of capacity or offtake.
  • MBOED means thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day, the unit this report uses for production.
  • CFO is the company's cash from operations before working capital changes, a non-GAAP measure the company reports.
  • Run-rate synergies are savings counted at their yearly pace once fully in place, not savings already banked in the income statement.
  • Limit order and stop loss: a limit order buys only at a stated price or lower, and a stop loss is the price at which we would sell to end a losing trade.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

ConocoPhillips' competitive position rests on four things: scale, a deep Lower 48 drilling inventory, a strong balance sheet and a spread of long-life projects. The balance sheet is the easiest to check. Total debt was $23.3 billion at June 30, 2026, against $6.6 billion of cash and $1.1 billion of short-term investments, so net debt was $15.6 billion, or 0.57 times trailing EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) by our calculation (Chapter 3 shows the working).[2][3] Debt was 26 percent of total capital, the credit ratings are A from Fitch, A- from S&P and A2 from Moody's, all with stable outlooks, and total liquidity was $13.2 billion including a $5.5 billion revolving credit line that runs to February 2030 and was undrawn.[3] A company with this much room can keep paying out cash through a price drop, which is the point of the framework it calls its value proposition.

The second strength is cost discipline that is starting to show in the numbers. Production and operating expenses were $2,431 million in the second quarter of 2026 against $2,572 million a year earlier, even though prices and activity rose, and for the half year they fell $371 million, which the 10-Q attributes mainly to efficiencies.[2][3] Management says it will reach more than $1 billion of extra cost and margin gains on a yearly basis by the end of 2026 and describes the program as ahead of plan.[3][19] It also reports that Lower 48 drilling and completion efficiency improved by more than 15 percent in 2025.[5] These are company statements and unaudited, so we weigh the reported cost line more heavily than the claims, and the reported line does agree with them.

The cost of the Marathon Oil purchase shows up in a different place: depreciation. DD&A (depreciation, depletion and amortization, the yearly write-off of capital spent on wells and fields) was $11,500 million in 2025 against $8,270 million in 2023. Per barrel it rose from about $12.41 in 2023 to $13.26 in 2025 and $14.55 in the second quarter of 2026, by our arithmetic from the filings. Two causes are named in the 10-Q: the purchase price was allocated to assets at market value, and proved developed reserves fell at the end of 2025.[11][6][3][2] That is why return on capital fell even as cash flow held up. It also means reported earnings carry a higher write-off per barrel than the 2021 to 2023 results did, which matters when the market compares the earnings multiple with peers.

The competitive position is therefore a good one with a visible weak point. ConocoPhillips is the largest US independent producer and it sells at market prices. It generated $10.1 billion of free cash flow over the last four quarters. Its return on invested capital has not recovered to pre-purchase levels, and its underlying production is shrinking. The stock closed at $149.06, which is $63.49 above its 52-week low of $85.57 and 2.4 percent below its 52-week high of $152.77, and Yahoo's page shows a 52-week gain of 69 percent, so the market has already paid for much of the recovery.[39][26]

Where the business is weak

ConocoPhillips is a price taker. Its earnings follow WTI, and the Middle East war that lifted oil prices in 2026 also cut the output of its Qatar gas investments, which produced about four percent of company volumes in 2025.[3] Gas from the equity affiliates fell from 1,166 million cubic feet a day in the first quarter of 2026 to 865 in the second. Output was constrained in that quarter. The 10-Q says the Qatar investments were not damaged and show no sign of impairment.[2][3] Management's timing language on the Qatar LNG projects has already moved. The February 2026 release expected the North Field East startup in the second half of 2026. The August call said the projects will contribute in 2027. It added that any delay in first gas or first cargo should be 'months, not a full year'.[5][19]

Willow is the single largest dependency in the growth plan. Reuters and CNBC, as quoted by Yahoo Finance, say it underpins nearly 75 percent of the free cash flow growth plan, and the budget has already risen to a range of $8.5 billion to $9.0 billion, about $1.5 billion above the earlier figure according to the same article.[17][7] Management says peak Willow capital is behind it and that first oil is on track for early 2029, but the field is 2.3 years from production, and a delay would push the cash flow inflection out with it.[19][6] The chief executive also changed on 2026-09-01: Andy O'Brien, previously chief financial officer, replaced Ryan Lance, who stays as executive chair, and Konnie Haynes-Welsh became chief financial officer.[16] The people who made the 2029 promise are therefore not all the people who must deliver it.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has made specific promises in the last two years, and most can be checked against filed figures. The first promise was the Marathon Oil synergy: in May 2024 the company said it expected at least $500 million of run-rate savings within the first full year after closing, split $250 million of general and administrative cost, $150 million of operating cost and $100 million of capital.[14] At closing in November 2024 it raised the figure to over $1 billion within 12 months, and in February 2026 it reported that it had completed the integration and doubled synergy capture to more than $1 billion on a run-rate basis in 2025, plus about $1 billion of one-time benefits.[15][5] The figure is the company's own and unaudited, but the reported production and operating cost line fell $371 million in the first half of 2026 against the first half of 2025, which agrees with it.[3]

The second promise is the cash return. In August 2024 management committed to return at least $9 billion for the year and delivered $9.1 billion.[10][9] For 2025 it set a target of $10 billion in February 2025 and delivered $9.0 billion, 45 percent of CFO, which is 10 percent short in dollars; WTI averaged $64.81 against $75.72 in 2024, and the company then restated the goal as a percentage of CFO for 2026.[9][5][6] The 2026 promise is the same 45 percent, but the first half came in near 40 percent, so the second half has to carry it: the CFO said on the call that the company would raise the percentage in the second half of the year.[19]

The third test is plan discipline. In 2025 the company guided production of 2.34 to 2.38 million barrels of oil equivalent a day, raised it to 2.375 million in November 2025 and delivered 2,375 thousand; guided capital spending of about $12.9 billion and spent $12.55 billion; and cut its operating cost guidance from $10.9 to $11.1 billion to $10.6 billion.[9][7][5][6] In 2026 the record is mixed: production guidance of 2.33 to 2.36 million was cut to 2.295 to 2.325 million in April because of Qatar and higher Surmont royalties, and capital spending guidance moved from about $12 billion to a range of $12 billion to $12.5 billion to cover extra Permian activity, while Willow's budget rose to $8.5 billion to $9.0 billion in November 2025.[5][4][7]

What this means for an owner: the operating KPIs support owning the business, and the record shows a team that sets public targets and mostly meets them. None of this says anything about the price. A reverse DCF (discounted cash flow) turns the usual valuation question around. Instead of forecasting cash flow and finding a price, it takes the price and asks what yearly growth in cash flow, for ten years, a 10 percent discount rate and an 18 times exit multiple would need to justify it. A low implied growth rate means the price asks for little, and a high one means the price already assumes a lot. The frozen ledger carries no reverse DCF for ConocoPhillips (its fields are empty), so we computed our own from the filings.[39]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter makes the bear case against our own work. It takes each estimate in Chapter 5, names its weakest assumption, says what evidence would show it wrong and when that evidence arrives. The bear case in one sentence is that ConocoPhillips' earnings follow an oil price held up by a war, the stock has risen 18.8 percent in a month on that price and is already priced like an integrated major, and the company's biggest promises (Willow, Qatar LNG, a doubled free cash flow by 2029) have not yet produced cash.

Fatal flaw 1: the conflict ends and oil gaps down

EIA spot WTI was $103.37 on 2026-10-27 and $69.73 on 2026-07-02, so oil has moved by $34 in four months, and the stock followed: it was about $132 at March 31, about $104 at June 30 and $149.06 at the October 28 close.[29][26][28][39] A settlement could take $20 off the oil price in days, which on our sensitivity is $5 of yearly EPS and about $66 a share at 13.16 times. A stop order does not cap that loss, because the stock can open below the stop and the order then fills at the next price. Our bear value of $92.51 is $29.96 below the $122.47 stop, so a gap from the zone top to that value would cost $35.44 a share, or 27.7 percent.

Fatal flaw 2: the projects slip and the 2029 cash flow does not arrive

Willow underpins nearly 75 percent of the $7 billion free cash flow growth plan according to the Reuters and CNBC reports quoted by Yahoo, its budget has already risen to $8.5 billion to $9.0 billion, and first oil is more than two years away.[17][7] The Qatar LNG projects, which carry $1.9 billion of book value, have already moved from a second-half 2026 startup to 'contributing in 2027', and the 10-Q lists further escalation of the conflict as a risk to operations, LNG transport and construction.[3][5][19] If Willow slips a year, the cash flow target moves a year and the multiple the market pays for it should fall.

Fatal flaw 3: the payout promise is met at the top of the cycle, or it is not met

The 45 percent of CFO promise requires about $3.4 billion a quarter of payouts in the second half, against $3.0 billion in the second quarter, and it comes with buybacks at $140 to $150 a share after $2.0 billion in the second quarter alone.[19][2] Management rejects the charge that it buys back stock at the wrong time, and says a doubling of free cash flow should lift the share price.[19] If oil falls and CFO drops, either the payout shrinks below 45 percent or the buyback has spent cash at the highest prices. A new chief executive and chief financial officer since 2026-09-01 add to this risk, because the team that made the promise is not the team that must deliver it.[16]

Fatal flaw 4: the 13.16 times multiple is a proxy, and the stock already sits above it

Our base target uses 13.16 times, the median of 12 trailing P/E values on one data page, and not a published three-year median.[28] The stock trades at 13.83 times 2027 EPS and 13.06 times 2026 EPS, above that median, and the independents trade at 9.4 to 12.2 times.[33][34][35][36] Producers usually earn lower multiples when earnings are lifted by a price spike, so 13.16 may be generous at the top of an oil cycle. The effect on the entry zone is direct: at the independents' mean of 10.2 times, EPS of $10.78 is worth $110 and the weighted value falls to about $116, which is below our entry zone and well below the close.

Layer 3 is the third test, which sets the entry posture, the position size and the exit rules. CMP stands for current market price, here the $149.06 close. A basis point (bps) is one hundredth of a percent, so 50 bps is 0.5 percent of net worth. Portfolio heat is the share of net worth that would be lost if every open position hit its stop at once, and the framework caps it at 12 percent. Risk-unit sizing sets a position by what a loss would cost and not by how much the buyer likes the idea: the size is the lower of the tier's maximum weight and the tier's risk budget divided by the distance to the stop. We use TIER_3, which allows 2 to 3.5 percent of net worth with a 50 bps budget, because the quality is high but the price asks for patience and an oil shock can hit the whole position at once.

The 63-day base low of $114.71 is the next level down.

The support-distance ladder is the framework's table that turns the distance from a price to its nearest verified support, measured in ATR, into an action. Under 0.6 ATR is the full-size band, under 1.2 is standard size, under 1.6 is the limit-order band with reduced size, and beyond 1.6 is the NO_BUY band where we place an order and wait. For ConocoPhillips we would raise the stop to the 50-day average once the price closes above the base target of $141.86, and hold for up to 12 months, to about October 2027.
