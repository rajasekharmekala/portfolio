1. What the company does

ORCX is the Defiance Daily Target 2X Long ORCL ETF, listed on Nasdaq. An exchange-traded fund, or ETF, is a basket of holdings that trades like a stock. Defiance sells it under its own brand, and Tidal Investments LLC is the investment adviser.[1] The fund began trading on 6 February 2025 and its stated aim is to deliver two times (200%) the daily percentage change in the share price of Oracle Corporation, before fees.[1][3] The words 'daily' and 'single trading day' matter, because the prospectus says plainly that the fund does not seek that result for any other period.[1] Defiance described the launch as the first 2X long ETF for Oracle, and says an investment in the fund is not an investment in Oracle itself.[12]

The fund gets its doubled exposure from derivatives, mainly total return swaps. A swap is a contract in which a bank pays the fund the return on a stated amount of Oracle stock in exchange for a financing charge. The fund may also use short-dated listed call options, and each day it values these contracts and rebalances so that its exposure is about 200% of Oracle's share price again.[1] It holds Treasury bills and similar assets as collateral, and the prospectus expects 40% to 60% of assets to sit as collateral for swaps or as option premiums.[1] At 30 April 2026 the swap schedule listed seven banks as counterparties, meaning the other side of each contract: BMO Capital Markets, Marex, Clear Street, Cantor Fitzgerald, Nomura, Morgan Stanley and National Bank of Canada. Their swaps added up to $575.6 million of notional exposure against $287.9 million of net assets, a ratio of 2.00 to 1 (our calculation), and the financing rates ran from the Overnight Bank Funding Rate plus 1.25% to plus 4.50%.[4]

The price of owning this is a fee table with two layers. The stated total is 1.31% a year: a 1.29% management fee plus 0.01% of other expenses and 0.01% of acquired-fund costs. The prospectus then says the embedded cost of the swaps is not in that table.[1] The year to 30 April 2026 cost holders 1.30%, and portfolio turnover (how often the holdings are replaced) was 1,829%.[3][1] That turnover is the daily rebalancing at work, and it is why the fund is built for trading, not for holding.

The fund is also small and young. Net assets were $287.9 million at 30 April 2026, and the market data page shows $193.33 million of assets and 12.17 million shares on 23 October 2026.[4][6] Dividing the April net assets by the April share value of $31.09 gives about 9.26 million shares, so the share count has risen about 31% while the price fell (our calculation).[4][6] On 20 March 2026 the fund merged every three shares into one, a reverse split, and the daily closes we use show no jump on that date (our check).[2][3] The prospectus names a newer-fund risk, in its words a 'limited operating history', and warns there is no assurance the fund keeps an economically viable size.[1]

Oracle, the stock behind the fund

Oracle sells business software and, increasingly, cloud computing capacity for artificial intelligence work. In its fiscal year to 31 May 2026 it reported revenue of $67.4 billion, with cloud revenue of $34.0 billion, up 39%, and cloud infrastructure revenue of $18.1 billion, up 77%.[21] In the quarter to 31 August 2026 revenue rose 30% and cloud infrastructure revenue rose 121%, while the order backlog, which Oracle calls remaining performance obligations, reached $664 billion.[20][19] A fund that doubles Oracle's daily move therefore doubles a stock whose growth is fast and whose spending is faster, a point Chapter 3 quantifies. Oracle's own next dated events are an Investor Day on 28 October 2026 and a quarterly report that StockAnalysis estimates for 9 December 2026; Oracle has not confirmed that date.[27][28]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Daily reset means the fund re-sets its exposure to twice Oracle at every close, so each day's gain or loss compounds from a new starting value.
  • Volatility decay is the loss that comes from daily resetting when the stock zigzags. If Oracle rises 10% and then falls 10%, it ends 1% lower, but a 2x daily fund rises 20% and falls 20% and ends 4% lower (our calculation).
  • Swap is a contract in which a bank pays the fund the return on a stated amount of Oracle stock, and the fund pays a financing charge in return. The counterparty is the bank on the other side.
  • Notional amount means the face amount a swap is written on. It is what gives the fund twice its net assets in exposure.
  • Reverse split means the fund merges shares, for example three into one, which raises the price per share and leaves the total value unchanged.
  • Average true range (ATR) is the typical size of one day's price swing, measured over 14 days. For ORCX it is $1.75, or about 11.6% of the $15.10 close (our calculation).
  • Relative strength index (RSI) is a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast the price has been rising or falling over 14 days; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • Simple moving average (SMA) is the average closing price over a set number of days, such as 20, 50 or 200. An exponential moving average (EMA) is the same idea with more weight on recent days.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The fund does what the label says on a daily basis. Over the last 252 trading days to 23 October 2026, the slope of ORCX's daily returns against Oracle's was 2.00 and the correlation was 0.9993; over all 429 daily returns since launch the slope was 1.99 and the correlation 0.9988 (our calculation).[6] A slope is the number of fund points gained for each Oracle point, so 2.00 is a perfect double, and a correlation of 1.0000 would be a perfect match. The extremes agree: on 10 September 2025, the day after Oracle's results, Oracle rose 35.9% and ORCX rose 71.3%, and on 11 December 2025 Oracle fell 10.8% and ORCX fell 22.1%.[6] On tracking alone, the fund passes.

The weakness is what the daily reset does to a stock that moves as much as Oracle. Since 7 February 2025 Oracle's price is down 27.7%, so a naive doubling would suggest a fall of about 55.4%, yet ORCX is down 75.5%. A perfect daily double of Oracle's actual daily moves would be down 70.0%, so the reset itself explains most of the extra loss and fees and financing explain the rest (our calculation).[6] The prospectus gives a table for this: with no change in the stock over a year, the fund would lose 6.1% at 25% volatility, 22.1% at 50%, 43.0% at 75% and 63.2% at 100%.[1] Those four figures all match the simple formula e raised to the power of minus volatility squared, minus one, so we use that formula for Oracle's own volatility (our calculation). Oracle's volatility, the typical size of its daily swings stated per year, was 54.3% over the last 63 sessions and 56.9% over the last 252. The prospectus reports 41.47% as the five-year figure to 30 June 2026, with 62.75% in its worst calendar year.[1] At 54.3% the formula gives a one-year loss of 25.5% even if Oracle ends flat, before any fee.

Costs sit on top of that. Measured against a perfect daily double of Oracle's closes, ORCX has lagged by about 11.2% a year over the last 252 sessions and 11.3% a year since launch (our calculation).[6] That lag is larger than the 1.31% fee on its own, and it is consistent in direction with the financing rates on the swaps, which run from the Overnight Bank Funding Rate plus 1.25% to plus 4.50%.[4] We did not rebuild the lag line by line from the swap schedule, so we call it an observed cost and not a proven breakdown. The competing Direxion fund on the same stock lagged by a similar amount, which suggests the cost belongs to the product type, not to Defiance alone (Chapter 3 peer table).

The structure adds two risks that a stock does not have. First, the swap terms can let a bank close a contract at once if Oracle makes a dramatic move that cuts the fund's net assets, and the fund may then be unable to rebuild its exposure even if Oracle recovers.[1] Seven counterparties spread that risk, which is a strength. Second, the sponsor has been shrinking its range. Defiance announced the closure of eight funds on 16 January 2026, two on 29 May 2026 and eight more on 21 August 2026, and it reverse-split twenty four funds in March, ORCX among them.[9][10][11][7] ORCX is not on any closure list we found, and it was not among the eleven funds in the July 2026 reverse-split release.[8] Its net assets nevertheless fell from $287.9 million at 30 April to $193.33 million, a drop of 33% (our calculation), which is why we score fund durability as a watch item.[4][6]
