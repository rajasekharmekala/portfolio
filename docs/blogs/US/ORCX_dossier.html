1. What the company does

ORCX is the Defiance Daily Target 2X Long ORCL ETF. It is an exchange-traded fund, a basket that trades on a stock exchange like a share, listed on Nasdaq and managed by Tidal Investments under the Defiance name.[1][2] Its stated goal is to deliver 2 times (200%) the daily percentage change in the share price of Oracle Corporation (NYSE: ORCL), before fees and expenses.[2] If ORCL rises 3% in one day, the fund aims to rise 6%. If ORCL falls 9%, the fund aims to fall 18%. The fund began trading on February 6, 2025.[7] On March 20, 2026 it did a 1-for-3 reverse split, which combined every three old shares into one new share and left the value of each holder's stake unchanged.[6] On October 16, 2026 the fund had net assets of $188.77 million, 12,029,976 shares and a net asset value (NAV, the per-share value of what the fund owns) of $15.69, equal to its closing price.[1]

The fund does not own Oracle shares. It gets the 2x exposure through total return swaps, which are contracts with banks in which the fund receives Oracle's gain or loss and pays a financing charge. On October 16 the fund held seven swaps, with Morgan Stanley, Cantor Fitzgerald, Marex, Clear Street, Nomura, National Bank of Canada and BMO (names matched to the April 30 swap schedule by maturity date), worth $377.30 million in total, which is 2.00 times net assets (our arithmetic: $377,298,309 divided by $188.77 million).[1] The largest, with Morgan Stanley, is $76.35 million, or 20.2% of the swap total.[1] The rest of the fund is cash and Treasury bills: $169.43 million of Treasury bills (89.76% of net assets) plus money-market cash.[1] The prospectus says 40% to 60% of assets is expected to serve as collateral, so the actual cash-like share is above the expected range.[2] Each trading day the fund resets its exposure to 200% of its net assets. Picture a driver whose cruise control is reset every evening to twice the speed reached that day. That reset suits a one-day bet and punishes a long hold.

The people who pay the fund are traders who want a short-term, amplified bet on Oracle. The fee table shows a management fee of 1.29% and total annual operating expenses of 1.31%.[2] The prospectus adds that the cost of the swaps, including the embedded financing, is an indirect expense that is not in that table.[2] At April 30, 2026 the swaps paid between the overnight bank funding rate (OBFR, 3.64% that day) plus 1.25% and OBFR plus 4.50%, which is a notional-weighted spread of 3.36% (our arithmetic) on a notional amount of $575.56 million, twice net assets.[5] The fund's portfolio turnover was 1,829% in the year to April 30, 2026, which means it replaced its whole portfolio about eighteen times.[2] In Chapter 3 we measure the all-in carrying cost from real prices and get about 11.5% a year, roughly nine times the headline expense ratio.

The engine behind the fund is Oracle, which sells database and business software and now rents large amounts of AI computing power through Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI). In the first quarter of fiscal 2027 (the three months to August 31, 2026) Oracle reported revenue of $19.3 billion, up 30%, cloud infrastructure revenue up 121%, and remaining performance obligations (RPO, the value of signed contracts not yet recognized as revenue) of $664 billion.[21] It also spent $28.5 billion on equipment in that one quarter and ended with free cash flow of negative $5.4 billion.[15] ORCL closed at $128.23 on October 16, down 4.64% on the day and about 60% below its 52-week high of $322.54.[31] A fund that doubles each day's move turns that fall into a much larger one for ORCX, which closed 89.9% below its own split-adjusted 52-week high of $154.95.[7] This book follows the rule for leveraged funds: it studies the stock behind the fund, the fund's structure, the chart and the decay, and it does not value the fund as a business.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Leveraged ETF — a fund that aims to return a multiple (here 2 times) of one day's move in something else.
  • Daily reset — the fund rebuilds its 2x exposure at every close, so gains and losses compound day after day.
  • Swap — a contract with a bank that pays the fund the stock's gain or loss in return for a financing fee.
  • Volatility drag — the slow loss a daily-reset fund suffers when the stock swings up and down, even if the stock ends where it began.
  • NAV (net asset value) — the per-share value of everything the fund owns, after liabilities.
  • OBFR — the overnight bank funding rate, the base interest rate used in the fund's swaps.
  • Reverse split — combining several shares into one, which raises the share price and cuts the share count without changing total value.
  • ORCL — the ticker of Oracle Corporation, the stock ORCX follows.
  • Operating margin — the share of revenue left after operating costs; net debt is total borrowings minus cash and liquid securities.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) — a measure of operating profit before those charges.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) — operating profit after tax divided by the money invested in the business.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is cash from operations less equipment spending; SBC (stock-based compensation) is pay given to staff in shares.
  • RSI (relative strength index) — a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has been moving. ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in dollars.
  • Stage 4 — the falling downtrend phase of a stock, with the price below falling averages. A breakout is a move above the top of a recent trading range, and the pivot is that top.
  • Asymmetry — the reward-to-risk ratio of a trade, which is (target - price) divided by (price - stop). A stop loss is the exit price if the trade is wrong, and a limit order is an order capped at a chosen price.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A fund has no brand or factory, so its strength is how well it does the one job it promises, and how it compares with rivals doing the same job. On the job itself ORCX is faithful: it matches twice Oracle's daily move closely. On cost and on survival over weeks it is weak, because the daily reset turns Oracle's swings into a steady loss. Two funds offer 2x daily exposure to ORCL. ORCX has $188.77 million of net assets and a 1.31% expense ratio. Direxion's ORCU has $142.06 million and a 0.97% expense ratio.[1][11][12] ORCU fell 9.22% on October 16 against 9.41% for ORCX, so the day's result does not separate them.[11][8] ORCX's edge is size, and it has no edge on price.

Strength: the fund does what it says each day

The strongest evidence is the daily match. Over the 250 trading days from October 17, 2025 to October 16, 2026, we compared each ORCX daily return with twice the ORCL return. The average gap was -0.04 percentage points, the average absolute gap was 0.16 points, and only 3 days had a gap above 1 point.[9][10] The worst gap was 2.22 points. On October 14, 15 and 16 ORCL moved -8.83%, +3.00% and -4.64%, so a perfect 2x fund would have moved -17.67%, +6.01% and -9.28%. ORCX moved -17.73%, +6.06% and -9.41%.[7][10] The fund also trades at its value: the premium to NAV was -0.01% on October 16 and the median bid-ask spread was 0.15%.[1] The fund's annual report shows it kept its swap exposure at 2.00 times net assets on April 30, 2026, the same ratio we compute for October 16.[5][1]

Weakness: the daily reset eats value when the stock is volatile

The prospectus states the weakness plainly: for periods longer than a day the fund's return is the product of each day's compounded return, and it will lose money if Oracle is flat. Its table shows a loss of 6.1% over one year if the stock goes nowhere at 25% volatility, about 43% at 75% volatility and 63.2% at 100% volatility.[3] The Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) wrote in 2009 that leveraged and inverse ETFs that reset daily typically are unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them for longer than one trading session.[34] Oracle's realized volatility, measured from 250 daily closes, is 57.25% a year, with 56.75% over the last 63 days and 70.33% over the last 10.[10] At 57.25% the prospectus formula gives a flat-stock loss of about 28% a year, before fees.

Real prices show what that does. From October 17, 2025 to October 16, 2026 ORCL fell 55.98% (from $291.31 to $128.23) while ORCX fell 87.55% (from $126.00 to $15.69, split-adjusted).[8][9][10] Compounding twice each day's ORCL return gives -86.04%. The gap of 1.5 points between that figure and the actual -87.55% is the fund's fees, financing and small tracking gaps. A fund with no volatility drag would have ended at the square of ORCL's ratio (0.4402 squared, or 19.4% of its start); the actual ending value was 12.5% of the start. We show the working in Chapter 3.

The stock behind the fund: strong demand, heavy spending

Oracle's demand numbers are very strong. First-quarter fiscal 2027 revenue grew 30% to $19.3 billion, cloud revenue grew 62%, cloud infrastructure revenue grew 121%, and RPO rose $209 billion in a year to $664 billion, of which about 13% should become revenue in the next twelve months.[21][16] Oracle booked more than $30 billion of new AI cloud contracts in the quarter and said they require no incremental capital because most are prepaid or use customer-supplied hardware.[21][26] Management lifted fiscal 2027 revenue guidance to at least $90 billion.[21] The cost of that growth is the weakness. Capital expenditure was $28.5 billion in the quarter and $75.7 billion over the last twelve months, which is 105% of revenue, so free cash flow (cash from operations less equipment spending) was negative $28.7 billion over the last twelve months.[15][17] Oracle funded part of the gap with $19.9 billion of new shares sold through an at-the-market program (it issued about 141 million shares) and with $43 billion of debt raised in fiscal 2026.[15][22] Borrowings are $125.3 billion against cash and securities of $37.1 billion.[14]

What the chart says about the fund itself

ORCX's own chart is the second weak point. The close of $15.69 sits below the 20-day ($18.94), 50-day ($20.97) and 200-day ($30.60) averages and 89.87% below the 52-week high.[31] The ledger labels the fund Stage 1 (basing), but the 200-day average has fallen from $51.10 to $30.60 in 63 trading days, and the label rests on price sitting just above its recent low of $15.50, not on a floor that has had time to form. A buyable chart would be Stage 2, a confirmed uptrend with the price above rising averages. ORCX is nowhere near that, so the fund inherits all of Oracle's risk and adds its own, which Chapter 2B scores.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means checking what management promised one or two years ago against what it delivered. Two sets of people made promises here: Oracle's management about the business, and Defiance about the fund. Oracle's record is strong on revenue, backlog and earnings targets, and weaker on cash and capital spending.

The clearest example of a promise delivered is backlog. In June 2025 Oracle said RPO would grow more than 100% in fiscal 2026, and it grew from $138 billion to $638 billion.[24][17] The clearest miss is the cloud growth rate: Oracle said total cloud growth would increase to over 40% in fiscal 2026, and it was 38.7% (cloud revenue $33,989 million against $24,506 million, our arithmetic).[24][17] Capital spending is the open test. Oracle's guide for fiscal 2026 was $50 billion, and it spent $55.7 billion, 11% more, while promising $90 billion to $95 billion for fiscal 2027.[23][17][26] On the fund side, Defiance promised a 2x daily result and delivered it within a fraction of a point on most days.

How it was calculated

A 2x daily-reset fund's value is approximately its start value, times the stock's price ratio squared, times e to the power of minus (volatility squared plus yearly cost) times the years. Setting that equal to the start value gives a break-even price ratio of e to the power of ((volatility squared plus cost) x years / 2). With volatility 0.5725 (squared 0.3278), cost 0.1146 and 0.25 years, that is e to the power of 0.0553, or 1.0569. This is the standard approximation for a 2x fund and our simplification of it, not a number from the fund.

The inputs

Volatility of 57.25% from 250 daily closes.[10] A carrying cost of 0.1146 a year (11.5%), which is the gap between the fund's actual one-year result and twice each day's ORCL return compounded: ln(0.1245) minus ln(0.1396) = -0.1146.[8][9][10] Start price $128.23.[10] The prospectus table matches the volatility term: e to the power of minus 0.25 squared gives the 6.1% loss it reports at 25% volatility.[3]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, the evidence that would show it wrong, and when we will know. It then gives the strongest case against our own conclusion and the single claim the conclusion depends on.

Estimates 1 and 2: volatility of 57% a year

We assumed Oracle's volatility stays near 57%. The measurement is stable (56.75% over 63 days, 56.26% over 20 days) but the last 10 days show 70.33%, and the figure rose in the week of October 14 to 16.[10] The formula also assumes steady price swings and no large gaps. Oracle moved by -8.83% on October 14 and the fund by -17.73%, and the largest single-day tracking gap in the year was 2.22 points, so real paths can differ from the formula.[9][10] The evidence that would show us wrong is realized volatility falling below 40% for a month, which would cut the break-even to about +3.5%. We will know within 20 trading days and by the December report.

Estimate 3: the scenario prices and weights

The $160.19, $139.44 and $100 prices are anchored to chart levels in the ledger, not to a valuation, and the 20/50/30 weights are a judgment rounded from a statistical model, not a forecast.[31] Analyst targets ($110 to $400, mean $237.36) say the stock could do far better, and 43 analysts is a real counterweight.[27] The evidence that would show our weights wrong is Oracle's Q2 report, expected between December 10 and 14 (not confirmed by Oracle in the sources we read), and any new financing news.[29][30] If Oracle shows capex funded by prepayments and cash flow improving, the bull weight should rise; if capex runs above $95 billion or a rating agency downgrades Oracle, the bear weight should rise.

Structural risks the model leaves out

The formula does not include fund-level events. ORCX has seven swap counterparties, and the prospectus warns that a counterparty's inability to perform could cause a significant loss and a limited or delayed recovery.[3] The fee table does not include swap financing, which ranged from OBFR plus 1.25% to plus 4.50% at April 30 and could change when a swap is renewed.[5] The fund has also done a reverse split, and Defiance announced reverse splits for twenty-four of its funds on March 12, 2026.[6][7] A fund that shrinks or loses its authorized participants could trade at a discount or close.[2] Nothing in the filings says closure is imminent: net assets are $188.77 million and the share count rose 30% since April.[1][4] These are tail risks that make the downside worse than the formula, and we cannot put a probability on them from the documents.

The red-team case against selling

The strongest argument against our verdict is this.
