The three questions that decide this investment

First, can the capital structure be carried? Every favourable fact — the backlog, the customers, the capacity — reaches the shareholder only if the build is financed at a cost the cash flows can service, and today the interest bill exceeds the operating profit it is drawn against. Second, is the backlog real revenue or merely contracted intention? It is real, but only to the extent delivery occurs on schedule through power, supply and construction constraints the company does not fully control. Third, is the equity actually cheap? At 3.58 times trailing sales it looks cheap; at an enterprise value of $73B against a $27B market cap it is a leveraged call, and leveraged calls are cheap only when the underlying is safe — which this one is not yet.

1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Stock Cycles

Core Investor Question: How did a 2017 Ethereum-mining outfit become a $5.1 billion AI cloud — and what do its first full stock cycle and financing pivots tell us about the risk we are underwriting today?

CoreWeave looks like a company that appeared overnight. It did not. What you are really underwriting is a nine-year arc that already includes one near-death cycle, two strategic pivots and a violent first eighteen months as a public stock. Understanding that arc is the difference between reacting to the chart and pricing the business. A crypto miner owns depreciating hardware and hopes a token rises; a GPU cloud owns depreciating hardware and sells contracted compute hours. Same buildings, entirely different quality of earnings. Everything that follows in this book depends on which one you believe CoreWeave now is. We will start where the company started, follow the money through each pivot, and only then ask what the stock is worth. By the end of this chapter you should be able to say, without hedging, which financial history you think the next five years will rhyme with.

From Ethereum Mining to AI's Essential Cloud

The company was founded on 21 September 2017 as Atlantic Crypto, an Ethereum miner run by Michael Intrator, Brian Venturo, Brannin McBee and Peter Salanki — former commodities and oil traders who already knew how to price scarce, volatile, capital-heavy assets. When the 2018 crypto crash wiped out most of their peers, this team did something quieter and far more consequential: in 2019 they renamed the business CoreWeave and began renting the very same GPUs out for rendering, machine learning and AI work. In April 2023 NVIDIA invested $100 million, and by May 2023 the company was valued at roughly $2 billion. Then, in August 2023, it raised a $2.3 billion debt facility led by Magnetar Capital and Blackstone, collateralised by NVIDIA H100 GPUs — the first time H100 hardware anywhere had been used as loan collateral. That single structure tells you everything about how this company grows: not from retained earnings, but by borrowing against equipment. Notice who is doing the lending and what is doing the collateralising: this is not a bank underwriting a software company's future cash flows, it is a syndicate underwriting the resale value of physical hardware. That distinction returns, loudly, in the depreciation debate later in this book.

The operating record is extraordinary on the top line and sobering below it. Longer lives mean lower annual charges and higher reported profit today — and a larger book value for the collateral those same GPUs back. Hold that thought.

Why adjusted EBITDA flatters a lessor

Adjusted EBITDA adds back depreciation and interest — exactly the two costs of owning an asset and financing it. For a company that exists to own expensive GPUs with borrowed money, that measure describes the business before it pays for the business.

The balance sheet sharpens the point. At 30 June 2026 CoreWeave held $5.54 billion of cash against $51.61 billion of total debt — over three times the debt a year earlier — for net cash of negative $46.03 billion. Trailing-twelve-month free cash flow was negative $13.66 billion and shares outstanding had grown 75.59% year over year. The company is funding a capital build faster than it generates cash, leaning on debt and equity markets that are themselves repricing AI credit.

One pair of ratios to keep beside you: the shares trade at roughly 1.4× forward sales while EV/EBITDA sits near 19×. Neither number is absurd on its own; together they say the market is paying a growth multiple on a business with a leveraged, capital-hungry balance sheet — the whole debate compressed into a single line.

Then there is the stock cycle itself. CoreWeave listed at $40.00 on 28 March 2025, peaked intraday at $187 on 20 June 2025, and closed our as-of session at $48.98 on 9 November 2026 — roughly 74% below the peak. It now screens as a Stage 4 breakdown with roughly 20% of the float sold short, and Beta is 7.52: this is not a stock that moves with the market, it moves against it. Our judgment for this chapter is deliberately two-sided. The industrial achievement is not in doubt, and neither is the financial fragility. CoreWeave proved it can pivot from mining to rental and from one anchor to several; what it has not yet proved is that the spread between its revenue and its cost of capital is wide enough to hand anything back to equity holders. That is the burden every later chapter has to test. One more asymmetry to file away: the company guides to a year-end 2026 revenue run-rate of $18.5–19.5 billion and capex of $35–39 billion. If the demand behind those numbers is real, today's price could look cheap; if it is not, the same numbers describe a company spending its way into a refinancing wall.

2. What the Company Does: Segments, Revenue Mix & Concrete Examples

Core Investor Question: Which parts of CoreWeave actually earn the money, who pays for them, and how does a take-or-pay GPU contract turn a data centre into a predictable revenue stream?

Everyone calls CoreWeave 'an AI cloud.' That label is true and useless. Four distinct revenue engines run on top of the same GPU fleet, and separating them is the fastest way to understand where the money comes from — and where it is concentrated. Think of the company as a single machine — powered, networked, NVIDIA-accelerated capacity — sold four different ways to four different kinds of buyer, at four different sizes and durations. The order matters: the first engine moves the revenue needle today, while the last three are where the company hopes to prove it is more than a hardware landlord. We will take them one at a time, then look at how the dollars actually split across customers and continents.

Three of the four engines are really one product — powered NVIDIA capacity — sold to different customers at different sizes and durations.

Start with the biggest engine: hyperscaler and AI-lab reserved capacity. These customers reserve enormous blocks of compute for years on take-or-pay terms — they pay whether or not they use the capacity. Microsoft was 35% of revenue in FY2023, 62% in FY2024 and 67% in FY2025. Next to it sit the two largest AI-lab contracts: OpenAI, worth up to about $22.4 billion in total, and Meta at $14.2 billion (September 2025) plus a further $21 billion (March 2026). The customer is buying guaranteed supply of the scarcest input in AI. Trace the money and the model becomes visible: the hyperscaler signs a multi-year take-or-pay agreement it cannot build for itself fast enough; CoreWeave borrows against that signed contract — the $8.5 billion Meta-backed DDTL 4.0 facility closed 31 March 2026 — and spends the proceeds on GPUs, power and fit-out. The customer gets capacity on a schedule it could never achieve alone; CoreWeave gets contracted revenue and the debt to fund it. The contract is the collateral. The customer list behind this engine keeps widening: OpenAI's commitment alone was expanded three times before the Meta pact arrived, and the pattern is now multi-anchor rather than single-tenant. This is why the backlog figure is the number management points to first: revenue backlog rose from $66.8 billion at the end of 2025 to $99.4 billion in March 2026 and $104 billion at 30 June 2026, excluding more than $25 billion of net new commitments booked in early Q3 2026. A backlog is a promise, not cash — but it is the promise the debt is built on.

The second engine is enterprise and AI-native compute. These customers are smaller individually but more numerous, buying for their own training and inference rather than wholesale resale: Jane Street, Anthropic, Cohere, Mistral, Perplexity, Databricks, IBM, Caterpillar, Bentley Systems, Grammarly, Isomorphic Labs, Runway and Hudson River Trading. This is where the 'essential cloud' claim earns its keep — a hedge fund or a manufacturer rents elite capacity without owning a data centre. Take Jane Street: a quant trading firm that needs unusually large model runs and refuses to be rack-constrained signs a $6 billion capacity deal in April 2026 — and invests $1 billion of equity in CoreWeave itself. CoreWeave delivers the compute from its own fleet, orchestrated by its own software, on negotiated terms. Notice the structure: the customer is simultaneously a financier, blurring the line between revenue and capital, exactly the kind of arrangement a careful underwriter flags. The deal also shows how CoreWeave competes against the pure hyperscalers: it offers flexibility and speed a large cloud's queue cannot always match, and is willing to shape commercial terms around one sophisticated customer.

The third engine is managed inference and software, the one investors most often miss. CoreWeave sells not just raw GPUs but the layer that makes them usable: SUNK, Mission Control, ARIA and the Weights & Biases platform. It was among the first with NVIDIA GB200/GB300 NVL72, the first NVIDIA Exemplar Cloud for training, and the sole Platinum provider in SemiAnalysis's ClusterMAX ranking. Software is where a hardware lessor can begin to earn a margin that is not just rent. The fourth engine is storage and networking: the high-performance fabric and storage AI workloads demand, sold alongside compute rather than standalone. A training cluster that cannot feed its GPUs fast enough is worthless, so this connective tissue is what makes the headline compute sellable — even though it rarely appears as its own line item. Weights & Biases in particular — a widely used experiment-tracking and model-management platform — signals intent: buy the tools AI teams already rely on, then run them on your own silicon. Together these engines are the reason CoreWeave can claim a software moat rather than a pure-commodity rental business — though treat that claim with care, because the disclosed record does not yet break software revenue out separately.

When the customer is also the competitor

CoreWeave's four largest prospective customers — Microsoft, Amazon, Google and Oracle — control roughly 65% of the cloud market and can build the same capacity themselves. Today they buy because CoreWeave is faster; tomorrow the calculus may change.

Read the Jane Street arrangement with the same caution. A customer that both pays you and owns a slice of you is a strong signal of conviction — and a structure that makes it harder for an outsider to tell operating revenue from capital inflows.

There is a mirror risk too. Each of these customers can shift demand between building and renting quarter by quarter; if Microsoft or Meta absorbs more capacity in-house, CoreWeave loses revenue exactly when it most needs it to service the debt raised against that contract.

The geography line deserves its own moment. In FY2025, $4,801 million of revenue came from the United States against just $330 million from everywhere else combined — a startlingly domestic book for a company whose pitch is global essential infrastructure — small enough that a single European or Indian anchor deal could change the story, though it has not happened yet. Our judgment: the segment picture genuinely supports the quality argument, with real software, real enterprise logos and a real neutral-specialist position. But the take-or-pay mechanics that make the revenue predictable are the same mechanics that make the debt possible, and the concentration in three customers and one continent means any single shock arrives at the levered equity first. Diversification here is real but young; concentration is real and structural. Watch how management reports the customer split in each quarterly update from here — it is the single cleanest read on whether the quality thesis is winning.

3. Distribution, Supply Chain & Unit Economics

Core Investor Question: Does CoreWeave's distribution model turn signed contracts into cash — or only into more signed contracts?

The discipline we will use for the rest of this book: take $100 of CoreWeave revenue and walk it, line by line, until you reach the cash. It is the fastest way to tell whether a distribution model is actually a business. CoreWeave sells a reservation — a claim on NVIDIA-accelerated compute for a fixed term, paid whether or not the customer uses it — so the economics must be read from the signature all the way to the lender's desk.

How the Compute Is Sold

CoreWeave reaches buyers through four channels. Direct enterprise sales to AI labs and corporates (OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic, Cohere, Mistral, Perplexity, Databricks, IBM, Caterpillar, Bentley Systems, Grammarly, Isomorphic Labs, Runway, Hudson River Trading). Hyperscaler and AI-lab master services agreements that anchor the backlog: Microsoft (67% of FY2025 revenue), OpenAI (up to ~$22.4B total), Meta ($14.2B plus $21B) and Jane Street ($6B plus $1B equity). Take-or-pay capacity reservations, where the customer pays regardless of usage. And partner, reseller and software channels (Weights & Biases, SUNK, Mission Control, ARIA) that widen reach and create lock-in. Concentration is improving — the top three customers were 36%, 26% and 10% in Q2 2026, with Microsoft down from 72% in Q1 2025 — but 'Customer A' was still 68% of net receivables at 31 December 2025.

Within those channels the company sells three grades of the same compute. Reserved capacity is the longest, cheapest and most committed — it is what gets securitised and what lenders advance against. On-demand is flexible and priced higher, serving workloads that cannot commit for years. Inference is the growing, latency-sensitive end, where the company hopes a full reservation book will give it room to prove pricing power. The mix matters because a business that is mostly reserved is a bond-like utility; a business that can charge a premium for inference is closer to a software margin.

The Supply Chain: Silicon, Sites and Watts

The supply chain is the strategy made physical. NVIDIA allocation is the crown jewel — CoreWeave was among the first with GB200/GB300 NVL72, the first NVIDIA Exemplar Cloud for training, and the sole SemiAnalysis Platinum ClusterMAX provider. Dell and data-centre partners build and house the racks; long-term leases secure the sites with no single partner above 17% of capacity; and the company is shifting toward self-build. But the binding constraint is power: active capacity rose from ~70 MW (end 2023) to ~850 MW (end 2025) to 1.5 GW (30 June 2026), with ~3.7–4.2 GW contracted across 51 AI data centres. Revenue is gated by megawatts.

Two consequences follow. First, the shift to self-build trades near-term margin for control of cost and schedule — strategically correct, but it puts more of the capex on CoreWeave's own balance sheet at the very moment debt is already $51.61B. Second, power is a permitting and interconnection problem that no sales team can solve; the runway to 8 GW by 2030, and to more than 5 GW of AI factories with NVIDIA, depends on utilities and land as much as on GPUs. The dispersal of capacity across partners — none above 17% — is deliberately lender-friendly because it reduces single-site concentration risk.

The rule to carry forward

For a neocloud, distribution is not a sales problem; it is an electricity and financing problem wearing a sales suit. Watch megawatts, watch contracted power, watch the next dollar of debt — in that order.

Financing: Capex First, Cash Later

The billing cycle does not match the build cycle. Capex is spent up front — guided at $35–39B for FY2026 — while revenue arrives over the contract term, bridged by debt secured against GPUs and contracts: the $8.5B DDTL 4.0 (A3/A(low), backed by the Meta contract) and the $2.6B DDTL 5.5 (SOFR+5.50%, ~5-year maturity against ~3-year underlying contracts). Resolution depends on backlog converting to cash on schedule and the debt cost staying below the asset return. Total debt reached $51.61B at 30 June 2026 — more than three times the year-earlier level — against equity of $5.02B and net cash of −$46.03B.

The link that turns contracts into cash is days-sales-outstanding discipline. Revenue backlog climbed from $66.8B (31 December 2025) to $99.4B (31 March 2026) to $104B (30 June 2026), up 246% year on year and excluding more than $25B of net new commitments booked in early Q3 2026. That is an extraordinary forward book — but a book is not a bank balance. If customers stretch payment terms or defer start dates, the debt service does not wait. The metric to watch is not the headline backlog; it is the conversion of backlog into operating cash flow and the trend in receivables.

Step back and the distribution model reads clearly. CoreWeave has industrialised the sale of compute and built a genuine multi-year backlog, but the machinery that funds it — debt against GPUs and contracts — now costs more in interest than the operations beneath it earn. The channel is a strength; the unit economics are the constraint. A distribution model is only as durable as the credit markets' willingness to refill it, and that willingness is being tested in 2026.

Two truths must be held together. CoreWeave is the largest independent neocloud by a wide margin — its 50.8% share of dollars in Q1 2026 is dominant within the category — yet the category is a fraction of a cloud market where AWS, Azure, Google Cloud and Oracle control roughly 65%. Leadership of a small, fast-growing perimeter is valuable only if the perimeter keeps expanding faster than the incumbents can enclose it. The rivals are not idle: Nebius pairs roughly $575–582M of quarterly revenue and a $17.4B Microsoft deal with a four-year depreciation policy that produces more conservative reported profit and collateral, while IREN, Lambda, Crusoe and the private players compete for the same NVIDIA allocation.

The most uncomfortable feature of the structure is that CoreWeave's largest customers — Microsoft, Amazon, Google and Oracle — are also the four companies best equipped to replace it. Microsoft alone was 67% of FY2025 revenue, and every quarter those hyperscalers finish more of their own capacity, the value of the bridge CoreWeave provides narrows. Concentration has improved: Microsoft fell from 72% of revenue in Q1 2025 toward the mid-40s, and the top three customers were 36%, 26% and 10% by Q2 2026. But the strategic fact is not the percentage; it is that the buyers have both the motive and the means to become the sellers. A customer who can insource is not a customer in the ordinary sense.

Switching Costs and Pricing Power

CoreWeave's lock-in is contractual: multi-year reserved capacity, customer prepayments and software (SUNK, Mission Control, ARIA, Weights & Biases). That raises switching costs. But pricing power needs scarcity, not just stickiness — and sophisticated AI labs run multi-cloud precisely to keep leverage and press on price at renewal. The contracts prove demand; they do not, by themselves, prove durable pricing power.

The subtlest point in the moat debate is that switching costs and pricing power are different muscles. A take-or-pay reservation keeps the customer in the building because leaving is expensive; it does not keep the customer from asking for a lower rate the next time the contract comes up. During an AI supply shortage CoreWeave has both the lock-in and the leverage. As hyperscaler capacity catches up and the GPU supply loosens, the contractual switching costs remain — contracts are contracts — but the pricing power fades, and renewals reset toward the market. Model the two separately or you will pay a premium for a moat that only half exists.

The moat is partly rented

NVIDIA's $2B equity stake at $87.20, the plan for more than 5 GW of AI factories by 2030, the ~$6.3B cloud-services purchase and first access to GB200/GB300 NVL72 are advantages rivals cannot simply buy — but they are granted by a supplier that also funds the buyers. That is both the deepest moat and the sharpest dependency, and the circular-financing critique the 2026 AI-debt debate centres on.

The Depreciation Debate

CoreWeave depreciates GPUs over six years (policy since 2023); Nebius uses four. The difference changes reported profit, book value and loan collateral — and it underpins the bear argument that the industry understates depreciation (Michael Burry's claim of roughly $176B across the hyperscalers for 2026–2028). CoreWeave's rebuttal: contracts repay asset-level debt over about five years and the GPUs are redeployed, with A100 contracts running into 2029. Read both sides; the number you choose moves the valuation more than most operating assumptions.

Our verdict, stated plainly: CoreWeave has a real operating lead and a partial moat, but the moat is narrower than the market share suggests. The defensible parts are execution speed, power and supply access, and the software layer that roots it in the customer's workflow. The non-defensible parts are price, the depreciation convention, and the willingness of hyperscalers to build their own capacity. Underwrite CoreWeave as a superb operator in a brutal, capital-intensive business riding a temporary supply-demand imbalance — not as an anointed toll road. The strongest part of the moat is a licence from NVIDIA, and licences can be renegotiated.

CoreWeave depreciates GPUs over six years, a policy since 2023, versus European peer Nebius at four years. For a company that borrows against GPU collateral, useful-life assumptions move both reported profit and the value of the assets lenders rely on. Charge SBC ($630M in FY2025) and D&A ($3.99B trailing) against adjusted EBITDA before you treat any of it as distributable cash.

Reverse DCF / enterprise-value sensitivity: what $48.98 already assumes

The number:

At a $27.03B equity value and $73.05B enterprise value, the market is already capitalising most of the $104B backlog converting into revenue, funded at a cost of capital it expects to stay durable. The equity is not priced as a free option on the entire backlog; it is priced as a levered residual that stays valuable only if execution is close to flawless.

How it was calculated:

Start from the enterprise value of $73.05B and the market capitalisation of $27.03B; the difference of roughly $46B is the net debt (reported net cash of −$46.03B). The company guides fiscal 2026 revenue to $12.4–$13.2B with a year-end run-rate of $18.5–$19.5B. Capitalising that run-rate on a modest enterprise multiple broadly supports the current enterprise value; the equity is the thin layer that remains after $51.61B of total debt. Because equity value equals enterprise value minus net debt, and the equity is only about 37% of enterprise value, the implied equity value is far more sensitive than the enterprise value to any change in the assumed cost of capital or terminal multiple. Using 551.54M shares, equity ≈ $27.0B (about $49/share, near the $48.98 close); a purely illustrative 20% haircut to the $73.05B enterprise value removes roughly $14.6B from a ~$27B equity base, a ~54% decline in the residual. These are sensitivity illustrations and judgmental assumptions, not a calibrated discounted-cash-flow output.

If the number is off:

The single most sensitive input is not revenue growth but the cost of capital, because it sits above the equity in the capital stack. Guided Q3 2026 interest expense of $860–940M already exceeds guided adjusted operating income of $200–260M. The reverse DCF makes the market's bet explicit. At $48.98, investors are not paying for the whole $104B backlog — they are paying for its orderly conversion at a cost of capital that does not spiral. The equity is a leveraged call on flawless execution: wide torque if the spread holds, and first-loss exposure if it does not.
