1. What the company does

Arch Capital Group is a holding company registered in Bermuda that owns a worldwide group of insurers. Its shares trade on the Nasdaq exchange and sit in the S&P 500 index. At September 30, 2026 it reported about $27.3 billion of capital, and in February 2026 it employed about 8,000 people in North America, Europe, the U.K. and Asia.[1][4] It sells three products: specialty insurance to businesses, reinsurance to other insurers, and mortgage insurance to lenders.

An insurer earns money in two ways. First, it collects premiums up front and pays claims later, and the gap is called float. Arch invests that float, and it held $49.2 billion of investable assets at the end of the third quarter.[3] Second, it earns an underwriting profit when premiums exceed claims and costs. The yardstick is the combined ratio: claims plus expenses, divided by premiums earned. Below 100 percent is an underwriting profit. Arch's combined ratio was 82.5 percent in the third quarter, which means it kept about 17.5 cents of each premium dollar before investment income and tax.[1]

The three segments are very different in size and in profit. In 2025, insurance wrote $10.4 billion of gross premiums, reinsurance $11.1 billion and mortgage $1.3 billion.[4] Mortgage is the surprise: in the third quarter it brought in 5.9 percent of gross premiums but produced 28.6 percent of underwriting income ($201 million of $702 million).[1] That is why this report spends time on mortgage credit, even though it is the smallest piece.

Who pays, and for what

In insurance, businesses pay Arch to cover risks that standard insurers avoid or price badly. That includes excess and surplus casualty, construction, marine and the lines Arch leads at Lloyd's of London. In August 2024 Arch bought the U.S. MidCorp and Entertainment insurance businesses from Allianz, which added middle-market commercial business and, on purpose, a block of programs that it chose not to renew in 2026.[1][3][14]

In reinsurance, other insurers pay Arch to take part of their losses. Property catastrophe is the best-known line, but it was only 14 percent of reinsurance net premiums written in the twelve months to September 2025.[15] In mortgage, lenders and borrowers pay for private cover that pays out if a home loan defaults. The company said the share of its U.S. loan insurance that stays in force was about 80 percent, and the delinquency rate was 2.2 percent at September 30.[3]

What the company does with its profit

Arch pays no regular dividend on its common shares. It paid a one-time special dividend of $1.9 billion in 2024, and in 2025 it spent $1.9 billion buying back its own stock.[4] In the first half of 2026 it bought back about $1.9 billion of stock, and in the third quarter another $1.4 billion. Through nine months it repurchased about 109 percent of its net income.[7][1][3] A buyback is when a company buys its own shares, which shrinks the share count; the diluted count was 341.4 million in the third quarter against 376.1 million a year earlier, a 9.2 percent fall.[1]

What changed in 2026

Three things changed. First, the insurance market softened: property and short-tail rates fell, reinsurance renewals at January 1 were down 10 to 20 percent for property catastrophe and short-tail excess-of-loss cover, and Arch has been cutting its share of those risks.[14][3] Second, Arch raised its debt: in June 2026 it sold $2.0 billion of senior notes (the notes have coupons of 5.250 and 5.950 percent), and it launched a tender offer to buy back up to $417.9 million of older notes.[9][10][11] Third, management changed: the president who ran the insurance segment left on June 3, 2026, and the president who ran reinsurance and mortgage took over insurance as well.[13]

Two smaller facts matter for later chapters. Bermuda's new qualified refundable tax credits lowered the expense ratio, and management said they are not a one-time event.[3] And the quarter included losses tied to the Iran conflict, partly offset by extra marine war premium.[1][3]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio: claims plus expenses divided by premiums earned; below 100 percent is an underwriting profit.
  • Net premiums written: premiums the company keeps after paying other insurers to share its risk.
  • Book value per share: shareholders' equity divided by the number of shares; it is the main scoreboard for an insurer.
  • Operating return on equity: profit from the insurance business and investments, excluding market gains and foreign-exchange swings, divided by average equity.
  • Catastrophe losses: claims from large events such as hurricanes and wildfires.
  • Prior-year development: changes to estimates of old claims; favorable means earlier estimates turned out too high.
  • Float: premiums collected now that will be paid out as claims later, and invested in the meantime.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is profit earned on each dollar invested in the business; for an insurer we use return on equity instead.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before financing and accounting charges; insurers do not publish it.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying employees in shares.
  • Net debt is interest-bearing debt minus cash; for an insurer we use debt to total capital instead.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast the price has moved; above 70 is hot and below 30 is cold.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend phase on the Weinstein scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • A base is a sideways stretch the price builds before a move, and a pivot is the top of that stretch.
  • A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the potential gain divided by the potential loss: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Arch is a strong business with a weakening growth story. The strength is in underwriting and in how it uses capital. Over five years its combined ratio stayed between 79.3 and 85.2 percent, book value per share rose from $33.56 to $65.11, and the company's five-year annualized return on equity was 18.6 percent.[6][4][5][8] Management ties pay to growth in tangible book value per share, with a target of 11 percent a year for 2023 to 2025; growth over that period was 33.65 percent as printed, which paid the performance shares at 200 percent of target.[8]

The weakness is the cycle. Arch calls itself a cycle manager: it grows where prices are good and steps back where they are not. In the third quarter that meant reinsurance net premiums written fell 9.3 percent and group net premiums written fell 3.0 percent, while net premiums earned, which drive profit, fell 6.3 percent.[1] Earned premium lags written premium by months, so the earned decline is the number that will weigh on 2027 profit.

Where the competitive position is strong

The first strength is breadth. Arch can move capital between insurance, reinsurance and mortgage, and the call says it did exactly that: it shifted capital toward specialty lines where returns met its hurdles and used third-party capital to manage its net exposure in reinsurance.[3] The second is mortgage, a niche that earned $1,000 million of underwriting income in 2025, the fourth year in a row above $1 billion.[4][14]

The third is the investment book. Net investment income was $451 million in the third quarter against $408 million a year earlier, a rise of 10.5 percent, at a pre-tax yield of 4.09 percent.[1] Management said new money earns 65 to 70 basis points more than the current book yield, which should keep lifting income even if premiums fall.[3] Net income also includes returns on investments accounted for under the equity method ($224 million in the quarter), which added about 340 basis points to annualized net return on equity year to date. That part is less steady than interest income.[3]

Where the competitive position is thin

Insurance is the weak segment. Its combined ratio was 94.4 percent in the third quarter, and 89.2 percent excluding catastrophes and reserve changes, which is far above the 78.4 percent in reinsurance.[1] Management said property and short-tail markets are more competitive and that rate increases have moderated in some casualty lines, and in July it said competition is increasing, particularly in property and short-tail lines.[3][16] Casualty is the area where industry reserves have been the problem in recent years, and the company itself said adverse development is emerging across the industry from recent accident years.[3]

Reinsurance faces falling prices. Management said abundant capacity in property and short-tail lines cut premiums, and in February it said that if property catastrophe rates fell further, in the mid-teens, it would have to decide case by case where to keep writing.[3][14] The mortgage segment faces slow origination because mortgage rates are above 7 percent, and its reserve releases are shrinking: 15.4 points of favorable development this quarter against 18.1 a year earlier.[3][1]

Reserves: how honest are the numbers?

Reserves are the money an insurer sets aside for claims it has not yet paid. If they were set too high, the insurer later releases the excess as profit, which is called favorable development. Arch released money in all three segments: $43 million in insurance, $322 million in reinsurance and $235 million in mortgage in 2025, about $600 million in total, against about $507 million in 2024.[4] In the third quarter the net release was $179 million, which is 4.5 points off the group combined ratio.[1]

A cushion of this size is a good sign, but it is also part of the profit. Without the $179 million release, the third-quarter combined ratio would have been about 87.0 percent rather than 82.5 percent, which we compute as 82.5 plus 4.5.[1] The insurance segment's release was small, only 0.7 points, so the casualty cushion there is thin.[1] We treat reserve strength as real but not as a source of future growth.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter takes the weakest assumption behind each estimate, builds the strongest honest case against our conclusion, names the one claim the verdict depends on, and says when we will know.

Red-team bear case: the earnings are better than they look

A skeptic would start with quality of earnings. The third-quarter operating combined ratio of 81.2 percent excludes catastrophes and reserve changes, but reserve releases are real profit: the $179 million release lowered the reported combined ratio by 4.5 points, so without it the reported ratio would have been about 87.0 percent.[1] Catastrophe losses of $234 million (5.8 points) were described by management as relatively low for the third quarter, which is normally the heaviest, and the quarter was helped by extra marine war premium.[3][1]

Net income of $1,025 million also included $224 million of equity-method fund returns, which are not interest income and can reverse. Meanwhile premiums earned fell 6.3 percent, and analysts already expect 2027 earnings per share to be 3.5 percent below 2026. If that decline were 10 percent instead, with a combined ratio near 85 percent, the multiple would likely fall toward the 1.2 times that we use in the bear case, and the stock would trade near $84.[1][18]

Assumption 2: the multiple holds near its median

Our base target multiplies book value per share by 1.50, the three-year median of year-end price-to-book ratios. That median was earned while operating return on equity averaged about 19 percent (21.6, 18.9 and 17.1 percent in 2023 to 2025).[22][5][4] Consensus earnings for 2027 imply an operating return of about 13 percent (9.84 divided by an average book value of about $73), which we compute from the cited inputs. A lower return usually earns a lower multiple, so 1.50 times is the weakest part of our number.

A one-factor stress shows how much rests on that multiple. Keep book value at $76 and use the FY2025 year-end multiple of 1.43 instead: $76 x 1.43 = $108.68. At $97.50 the reward-to-risk is (108.68 minus 97.50) divided by 7.36, which is 1.52, below the 2.0 floor. No earnings miss is needed for that. So we lowered the top of the buy zone to $96.30, where the stressed ratio is 2.01. At the FY2021 multiple of 1.26, the value is $95.76 and no price in the zone passes.[22]

The cross-check on earnings points the same way. The price-to-earnings ratio is the price paid for each dollar of expected earnings. The three-year median forward ratio is 10.48 times. Applied to 2027 consensus EPS of $9.84, it gives about $103. That is no upside from the close, and only 1.09 to 1 at $96.30. If the market values Arch on earnings, not on book, our $114 is too high.[19][18]

The reserve cushion is real but thin in insurance (0.7 points in the third quarter) and shrinking in mortgage (15.4 against 18.1 points). Management itself said adverse development is emerging across the industry from recent accident years.[1][3] A hurricane or wildfire season larger than the $1.9 billion modeled single-event loss, or a casualty reserve charge at Everest's scale, would hit book value per share directly.[3][25]

The buyback authorization stood at $2.2 billion at June 30; after $1.4 billion in the third quarter and about $100 million in October, about $0.7 billion remains on our arithmetic, so the pace needs a new authorization to continue.[7][1][3] And the head of the insurance segment left in June; the reinsurance and mortgage president now runs all three segments, which concentrates decisions in one person.[13]
