1. What the company does

Cencora, Inc. (NYSE: COR, formerly AmerisourceBergen) is a drug wholesaler and pharmaceutical services company. Its fiscal year ends on September 30. In fiscal 2025 it reported revenue of $321.3 billion, up 9.3 percent[10]. A wholesaler buys medicines from drug makers, holds them in warehouses, and delivers them to the pharmacies, hospitals and physician offices that treat patients. It earns a small markup for doing so. Its gross margin, the share of each sales dollar left after paying for the goods, was 3.57 percent in fiscal 2025 (our calculation from gross profit of $11,478.5 million and revenue of $321,332.8 million[1]). Its operating margin, the share left after running costs, was 0.82 percent under US accounting rules (GAAP) and 1.31 percent on the company's adjusted basis, which removes items management calls unusual[1][10].

Since October 1, 2025 the company reports three groups. U.S. Healthcare Solutions distributes brand, specialty and generic drugs to hospitals, pharmacies and clinics, and serves physicians in oncology and retina[1]. International Healthcare Solutions holds Alliance Healthcare, Innomar, World Courier and the core parts of PharmaLex, and sells mainly in Europe[1][10]. A third group, called Other, holds businesses the company is exploring options for: MWI Animal Health, Profarma, US consulting services and the rest of PharmaLex[1]. Who pays, and why the customer list matters

Cencora's customers include hospitals and health systems, retail drugstore chains, independent pharmacies, mail-order pharmacies and physician practices, and the company says it is typically the primary source of supply for its healthcare provider customers[1]. Walgreens and Boots together accounted for about 25 percent of fiscal 2025 revenue, Evernorth Health Services for about 13 percent, and the ten largest customers for about 66 percent[1]. A wholesaler with customers this large has little room to refuse a price request, which is one reason the margin is thin.

Growth comes from more prescriptions, new drugs and price changes set by manufacturers. In fiscal 2025 sales of products labeled for diabetes or weight loss in the GLP-1 class (a family of injected drugs) rose $7.7 billion, or 26.9 percent[1]. Those drugs pull revenue up but earn a lower margin than the average product, as the company said again in its June 2026 quarter[7]. Generic drugs fill about 90 percent of US prescriptions, and the company calls the rise of generics and biosimilars (near-copies of complex biologic drugs) favorable because they have historically carried a greater gross profit margin opportunity than brand-name drugs[1]. The company does not publish biosimilar sales volumes in the filings we read, so we cannot size that tailwind.

Specialty, oncology and portfolio changes

Specialty drugs are costly medicines given mostly in physician offices. To sell more of them, Cencora has bought into management services organizations (MSOs). An MSO is a company that runs the business side of independent physician practices, such as billing, purchasing and technology, so that the doctors can focus on patients. On January 2, 2025 Cencora bought an 85 percent interest in Retina Consultants of America for $4,042.0 million in cash[1]. On February 2, 2026 it bought the majority of OneOncology that it did not already own, at a fair value of $7,387.1 million that included $4,648.7 million in cash[5]. In its first five months, the OneOncology management company and some consolidated practices added $820.0 million of revenue[5].

On October 21, 2026 Cencora announced an agreement to buy American Oncology Network, a physician-led network of more than 350 providers in 21 states, at an enterprise value of $2.4 billion. Cencora expects to pay about $1.7 billion in cash if certain sellers roll over 25 percent, and it expects the deal to add to adjusted earnings per share in the second year after closing[13]. At the same time the company is selling animal health: MWI Animal Health is to merge with Covetrus at an enterprise value of $3.5 billion, with Cencora receiving $1.25 billion in cash and $800 million of preferred equity and keeping a 34.3 percent common stake[20]. The Federal Trade Commission asked for more information on May 20, 2026[5], so closing is not assured. In May 2026 the company also announced an agreement to acquire the retina business of EyeSouth Partners[8].

We also checked Cencora's annual report, quarterly report, releases and proxy statement for the name Mercalis and found no mention. Public pages describe Mercalis as an independent life-sciences services company that announced a merger with PharmaCord on March 12, 2025[27]. We therefore do not count Mercalis as part of Cencora and give it no weight in this report.

  • Wholesaler: a company that buys goods in bulk from makers and resells them to the stores and clinics that serve the final customer.
  • Gross margin and operating margin: the share of each sales dollar left after the cost of goods, and after running costs, respectively.
  • GAAP and adjusted: GAAP is the official US accounting rulebook. Adjusted figures remove items management calls unusual, so they are a company choice, not a rule.
  • Fiscal year: Cencora's accounting year, which runs from October 1 to September 30. Fiscal 2026 ended on September 30, 2026 and has not been reported yet.
  • MSO: a management services organization that runs the back office of independent physician practices.
  • GLP-1: a class of injected drugs for diabetes and weight loss that sells in large volume at lower distributor margins.
  • Prime vendor agreement: a contract that makes one wholesaler the main supplier to a pharmacy chain.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for Cencora rests on scale and routine. Pharmacies and hospitals need medicines delivered every day with few errors, and a wholesaler that already runs a national network of warehouses is hard to replace. Cencora's own filing says its warehouse operating systems have improved productivity and operating leverage in the US group[1], and it plans $1 billion of network investment through 2030, including a second national distribution center and more cold-chain storage for specialty drugs[10]. The agreements with its largest pharmacy customer run until 2029 in the US and 2031 for Boots in the United Kingdom[1], which gives revenue some visibility.

The profit trend supports the case. Adjusted operating income, which is profit from running the business before items management excludes, rose 11.4 percent in the nine months to June 2026, and the adjusted operating margin rose from 1.35 to 1.43 percent[7]. In the US group the operating margin rose from 1.17 to 1.27 percent, helped by the February 2026 OneOncology purchase[7]. Competitors face the same economics: McKesson and Cardinal Health, the two rivals Cencora names, also earn between one and two cents of free cash flow (the cash left after paying for equipment and software) per dollar of sales, as the peer table in chapter 3 shows[1][22][23]. That similarity means thin cash margins describe the industry, not a Cencora failure.

The weak points are concentration, mix, money owed and policy. First, concentration: Walgreens and Boots were about 25 percent of fiscal 2025 revenue and about 38 percent of accounts receivable, which are bills customers owe[1]. Second, mix: GLP-1 drugs lift sales but carry lower margins[7]. Third, thin margins magnify small changes. One tenth of one percentage point of adjusted operating margin on trailing revenue of $332.8 billion is about $333 million, or 7.3 percent of trailing adjusted operating income of $4,587.2 million (our calculation from the bridge table in chapter 3). Fourth, policy. The filing flags the Inflation Reduction Act, which sets Medicare negotiated prices from 2026 for Part D drugs and 2028 for Part B drugs. It also flags possible Section 232 tariffs on imported pharmaceuticals[1].

The Walgreens relationship after the Sycamore buyout

Walgreens Boots Alliance (WBA) was acquired by the private equity firm Sycamore Partners on August 28, 2025, at $11.45 per share in cash plus a right to up to $3.00 more from later asset sales[1][21]. Cencora has a US distribution agreement with Walgreens, a generics purchasing services arrangement with a WBA unit, and a separate agreement to supply Boots in the United Kingdom[1]. The filing warns that the new owners may seek changes, for example by closing more of the roughly 1,200 stores WBA planned to close over three years[1].

On the ownership side, Walgreens Boots Alliance had pledged 20.0 million Cencora shares as collateral for forward transactions from May 2023[2], and the 2026 proxy statement says it stopped controlling more than 5 percent of Cencora's stock in June 2025[19]. The filings we read do not say how the stake was sold, so we state only that it is no longer above 5 percent. On August 12, 2026 the company disclosed that, beginning July 1, 2026, certain Walgreens volume that Cencora had served outside the prime vendor agreement began moving elsewhere. It said the prime vendor agreement itself, which is the vast majority of the Walgreens business, is unchanged, and that its fiscal 2026 profit range was reaffirmed[15]. In the June quarter, US segment revenue growth also reflected lower sales to a large mail-order customer and the 2025 loss of an oncology customer[7].

Cash, working capital and the opioid settlement

Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and paying for equipment and software. For a wholesaler it moves with working capital, the cash tied up in inventory and customer bills minus the credit suppliers extend. Cencora's days sales outstanding were 27.5 and days inventory on hand 28.0, against days payable outstanding of 61.3, in the nine months to June 2026[5]. Adding the first two and subtracting the third gives minus 5.8 days (our calculation), which means Cencora pays suppliers about six days after it has already collected from its own customers. That is why accounts payable of $54.7 billion exceed receivables of $25.4 billion plus inventory of $21.0 billion[7]. A quarter with a bad timing swing can move cash flow by billions, so we judge cash over a year, not a quarter.

The opioid settlements are a fixed claim on that cash. The accrued liability was $4.2 billion at June 30, 2026, to be paid over 13 years, with $396.2 million expected within a year[5]. In the June 2026 quarter the company cut the liability by $102.0 million after the dismissal of opioid litigation[7], and the City of Baltimore separately dropped its claims with prejudice on May 8, 2026[5]. Payments reduced operating cash flow by $404.1 million in fiscal 2025[1]. We treat the liability as debt-like in our stress tests, because it must be paid before shareholders see the cash.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of filings tell one story: revenue grew quickly while profit barely moved. Revenue rose from $214.0 billion in fiscal 2021 to $321.3 billion in fiscal 2025, a gain of 50.2 percent and a compound rate of 10.7 percent a year (our calculation)[3][1]. Gross margin improved from 3.24 percent to 3.57 percent, but operating margin under GAAP fell from 1.10 percent to 0.82 percent, and net income attributable to Cencora was $1,539.9 million in fiscal 2021 and $1,554.2 million in fiscal 2025[3][1]. Opioid settlement charges, deal costs and goodwill write-downs absorbed the gains, including a $723.9 million write-down of PharmaLex goodwill in fiscal 2025[1].

The company's adjusted figures remove those items and tell a cleaner story. Adjusted EPS rose from $13.76 in fiscal 2024 to $16.00 in fiscal 2025, an increase of 16.3 percent[10]. GAAP results for fiscal 2026 look better than the business is, because the nine months to June 2026 include a $1.1 billion gain from remeasuring the earlier OneOncology investment, which is worth $4.28 per share of the $15.21 nine-month GAAP EPS[7]. We therefore use adjusted profit to read the trend, and GAAP cash and debt to read safety.

Cash is the third thread. Cash from operations ranged from $2.7 billion to $3.9 billion a year over five years and swings with working capital. Free cash flow, which is cash from operations minus capital expenditures (money spent on equipment and software), has been between 0.9 and 1.3 percent of revenue every year[4][3][1]. The trailing-twelve-month figure of 1.22 percent is helped by a fiscal fourth quarter 2025 in which operating cash flow was $3,133.4 million, against only $1,687.5 million in the first nine months of fiscal 2026[1][5].

The balance sheet changed most in the last year. Total debt rose from $7.7 billion at September 30, 2025 to $11.7 billion at June 30, 2026 as the company issued $3.0 billion of senior notes and $1.5 billion of term loans to fund OneOncology[7][5]. Stockholders' equity is small ($3,239.1 million) because years of buyback programs, which are purchases of the company's own shares, and the opioid charges used it up. That makes return on equity meaningless for this name, since the ledger shows 96.87 percent on that small base, so we use return on invested capital instead. The 2025 purchase of Retina Consultants of America was also financed with $1.8 billion of senior notes and a $1.5 billion term loan[7].

It asks what yearly growth in cash flow, for ten years, would justify today's market value at a 10 percent required return and a terminal value of 18 times cash flow. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate rests on one assumption that could fail. This chapter names that assumption for each estimate. It states the bear case in full and describes three ways the thesis could fail outright. It then names the single claim everything hinges on and the date we will know. The bear case is the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion, so it is written as if by someone who wants the stock to fall.

Weakest assumption in the profit estimate

The EPS estimate assumes that the Walgreens volume now leaving is small and that the physician-practice businesses keep adding profit. The company said on August 12, 2026 that the leaving volume was served outside the prime vendor agreement and was already included in its fourth-quarter view[15], but it did not say how large the volume is. Walgreens and Boots were about 25 percent of fiscal 2025 revenue[1], so even a small share is large in dollars. The evidence that would show us wrong is a US group adjusted operating income growth rate below 10 percent in the November 4 outlook, against 13.4 percent in the nine months to June 2026[7]. The fiscal 2026 floor of $17.90 also implies at least $4.59 of adjusted EPS in the fourth quarter, which is nine-month adjusted EPS of $13.31 subtracted from the floor. That is 19.5 percent above the $3.84 of a year earlier (our calculation)[7][13][10], so the company has to grow faster in the quarter than the 9.5 percent it delivered in the nine months.

Weakest assumption in the price target

The target assumes the stock keeps a multiple of 17.37 times profit, our proxy for the three-year median. That proxy mixes years in which the company grew at different speeds and carried different debt, so it is a rough anchor. The evidence that would show us wrong is a multiple that stays at 14 to 15 times after the November 4 report, which would put the price at roughly $277 to $296. We will know within days of that report.

Weakest assumption in the probabilities

The weights assume the next year looks like the last four quarters, each of which beat consensus[24]. Four quarters is a short record, and a new Chief Financial Officer arrived on June 29, 2026[17]. A first guide from a new CFO tends to be set with room to spare, which would support the central case, but we have no filing that says so, and we do not rely on it.

The bear case

A bear would say the quality is overstated. In the nine months to June 2026, 78 percent of Cencora's segment profit came from one US group that depends on a few customers[7]. One of them, with about 25 percent of revenue, is now owned by a private equity firm that may reshape its pharmacy business[1][21]. Growth is coming from GLP-1 drugs that earn lower margins[7]. It also comes from purchases funded with debt, which has risen by $4.1 billion in nine months[5][1]. Adjusted profit leaves out opioid-related charges, while cash payments of about $400 million a year still go out[1][5]. Reported free cash flow has stayed near 1 percent of sales for five years, so owners keep little of each sales dollar. If the market stops paying 17 times profit, the price falls toward $277 even if profit meets consensus.

Three ways the thesis could fail outright

Layer 3 turns the analysis into timing and size. Support is the price area where buyers have stepped in before. We use the 20-day moving average of $324.55 as the support anchor, because it is the highest verified level at or below the price. The close is 0.91 average true ranges above it, which falls in the STANDARD band of our ladder (0.6 to 1.2 ATR), so timing alone would allow a standard-size starter. The stop uses a different level because it answers a different question. Timing asks where buyers are likely to return. The stop asks where the idea is wrong, and for a tactical name that is below the 20-day swing low of $295.01, the lowest price of the past 20 sessions, less half of one ATR, which is $291.46. That is the structural invalidation point: a price at which the recovery pattern is broken, not merely tested.

Position size comes from risk-unit sizing, also called portfolio heat. Portfolio heat is the share of your net worth you would lose if every stop were hit. The formula is weight = the smaller of the tier cap and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. A tier is a conviction grade with a size cap and a risk budget measured in basis points (bps; 100 bps is one percentage point of net worth). TIER_0 means zero size and is the tier we assign, because the verdict is REJECTED. The yardstick for a tactical name would be TIER_3, with a cap of 3.5 percent of net worth and a risk budget of 50 bps. At a stop 10.20 percent below the $324.55 zone top, 50 bps divided by 10.20 percent allows 4.9 percent, so the 3.5 percent cap would bind. That would be $2,883 of the $82,368.77 net worth, with a planned loss at the stop of $294, or 35.7 bps. The head of this report shows 0.0 percent because the tier is TIER_0.

CMP means the current market price, which here is the $331.00 close of October 23, 2026. The generic table below prints wording for several chart types, so we state the variant used. The 20-day swing low is the lowest of the three candidates, so it sets the stop at $291.46.
