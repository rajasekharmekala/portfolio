1. What the company does

Gulfport Energy is an independent company that drills for natural gas, and for the small amounts of oil and natural gas liquids that come out of the same wells. Its main field is the Utica and Marcellus shale in eastern Ohio. Its second field is the SCOOP area of central Oklahoma. In the second quarter of 2026 it produced 962.8 million cubic feet of gas equivalent a day, of which 800.0 came from Ohio and 162.8 from Oklahoma[3]. Gas equivalent, written Mcfe, counts each barrel of oil or liquids as six thousand cubic feet of gas, so one number covers everything sold. The mix that quarter was about 91% natural gas, 6% natural gas liquids and 3% oil[3]. Over the full year 2025 Ohio gave 81% of production and Oklahoma 19%[1].

Who pays, and for what? Gulfport sells to refiners, resellers called marketers, and end users, and one buyer took 14% of its 2025 sales[1]. The price is the New York futures price for gas at the Henry Hub in Louisiana, less a regional discount. For 2026 the company guides to a gas discount of $0.15 to $0.30 per thousand cubic feet, oil $6 to $7 a barrel below the New York oil price, and liquids at 40% to 50% of that oil price[4]. The company does not set any of these prices. That is why its quarterly numbers swing: realized gas, before hedges, was $4.90 per thousand cubic feet in the first quarter and $2.48 in the second[7][4]. The first quarter was lifted by winter storm Fern, when the Henry Hub price averaged $7.72 in January[28].

How does it make money? It sells gas at the price above and pays costs per unit. In the second quarter those costs were $0.23 per thousand cubic feet for lease operations (the cost of keeping wells running), $0.08 for production taxes, $0.97 for moving, gathering and compressing the gas, $0.13 for cash overhead and $0.18 for interest[3]. Lease operations, taxes and midstream together came to $1.28 against an unhedged price of $2.99[4]. The biggest single cost is the pipeline and compression bill, not the wells themselves. That matters because Gulfport has firm transportation and gathering commitments[1], so the bill does not shrink when the gas price falls.

Gulfport also uses hedges: contracts that fix a future price for part of its gas so that a price drop is cushioned. As of July 28, 2026 it had swaps for 225 billion British thermal units a day in 2027 at an average $3.89 per million British thermal units, plus collars (a floor and a ceiling) for another 117 at a $3.75 floor[4]. The accounting has a trap. Gains and losses on open hedges are booked inside revenue, so reported revenue is not sales. In 2023 total revenue was $1,791.7 million, of which $740.3 million was a derivative gain[13]. In the first half of 2026 it was $760.8 million, of which $45.9 million was a derivative gain[4]. We therefore track oil, gas and liquids sales excluding derivatives, and we say so each time.

How to read a per-share number that looks very large

Gulfport's stock trades at $165.91 and its profit per share is about $26 over the last twelve months, which looks unusual for a gas producer. The reason is history. The company filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on November 13, 2020[1]. On emerging in May 2021 the old 160.8 million shares were cancelled and 21.5 million new shares were issued[13]. Since then buybacks have cut the count to about 17.7 million[4]. A small count means each share carries a large slice of the business: $496.7 million of net income over the last twelve months, spread over the average share count, is about $26 a share, which matches the $26.19 a share that the data vendor reports[17].

The count is not a one-way street. In 2025 preferred stock was converted into 2.555 million shares, and the remaining preferred was redeemed on September 5, 2025, which pushed year-end shares up from 17.8 million to 18.8 million[1][2]. Per-share growth is therefore smaller than the headline suggests when you compare across 2025. We use the market value of $2.93 billion and total dollar figures first, and per-share figures only where the share count is stated[17]. Earnings per share is the company's profit divided across all shares; dilution, the fall in your slice when new shares are issued, and the buyback, the company buying shares back, work in opposite directions on that number.

What Gulfport spends its cash on

The company has three main uses of cash. The first is drilling. Its 2026 base capital expenditure, meaning spending on wells and equipment, is guided at about $430 million, including $35 million for lease maintenance and seismic work[3]. The second is buying more acreage: a new $140 million discretionary program for the rest of 2026, after $40.3 million in the second quarter[3]. The third is buying back its own shares: $242.8 million in the first half of 2026, against $336.3 million in all of 2025[3][5]. The money for all three comes from operating cash flow plus borrowing on a revolving credit line, so spending beyond cash flow shows up as rising debt.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided across all of its shares.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before financing costs, tax and the accounting write-down of assets; it approximates operating cash earnings.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is the cash left after running the business and paying for wells and equipment.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is the profit earned on each dollar of debt and equity put into the business.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying employees in shares.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast a price has been moving; above 70 is hot, below 30 is washed out.
  • ATR (average true range) is the average daily price swing in dollars; here it is the yardstick for how far the price sits from support.
  • Mcfe, MMcfe, Bcfe are a thousand, a million and a billion cubic feet of gas equivalent, with each barrel of oil or liquids counted as six thousand cubic feet.
  • Henry Hub is the Louisiana pipeline hub whose futures price is the US natural gas benchmark; basis is the local discount to it.
  • Hedge is a contract that fixes a future price; a swap fixes one price, a collar sets a floor and a ceiling.
  • PV-10 is the present value, discounted at 10% a year, of the cash the company expects from its proved reserves at set prices.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Gulfport's competitive position rests on three things: a deep inventory of drilling locations, costs that are low for a small producer, and a location near growing gas demand. On the first, management says total net inventory is roughly 15 years with break-evens below $2.50 per million British thermal units[5]. A break-even is the gas price at which a new well earns its cost of capital. In 2025 the company added 701 billion cubic feet equivalent of proved reserves by extension and drilling against 379 produced, so reserves grew from 3,969 to 4,253[1]. The August 2026 release adds 4,700 net Ohio acres from state land and a $140 million program that together should raise Utica net inventory by more than 20% and extend the drilling runway by more than 2.5 years[3].

On the second, the evidence is operational. Drilling footage per day in the Marcellus improved 50% and Oklahoma drilling cycle times ran 25% better than plan in the first quarter[7]. Management said on the second-quarter call that longer laterals cut drilling and completion cost per foot by about 25% against older, shorter wells[23]. Lateral length is the horizontal distance a well runs through the rock; longer laterals reach more gas for one rig move. These are claims by the company, and the proof is in the unit costs that follow in chapter 2B.

On the third, Gulfport sits inside the Appalachian demand pocket that data-center and power buyers are now chasing. The new chief executive named in-basin demand and artificial-intelligence data centers as the reason to stay focused on cost and market access[23]. Forward power prices support the theme: the October 2026 market update says calendar 2027 on-peak power rose in every zone it tracks, led by PJM Dominion at $114.90 a megawatt-hour, even as gas prices fell[30]. That demand is a reason to expect better regional prices over time. It is not yet visible in Gulfport's realized prices, which were $2.48 in the second quarter[4].

The honest summary is a good asset base held by a company without a pricing edge. Gulfport has no pipeline of its own to sell at a premium, one buyer takes 14% of sales[1], and the company is small beside its peers, with a $2.9 billion market value against $5.63 billion for CNX and $12.95 billion for Antero[17][31][32]. Small size cuts both ways. It means a few good wells move results, and it also means the company has less bargaining power with pipelines, which is why the $0.97 pipeline and compression cost is the line to watch.

Where the cost position is strong

Lease operating expense, the cost of running producing wells, was $0.23 per thousand cubic feet equivalent in the second quarter, and cash overhead was $0.13[3]. Those are low numbers. Total lease, tax and midstream cost was $1.28[4], and over 2025 the average unhedged price of $3.49 sat well above the $1.25 those three lines cost, a margin above $2[5]. Reserve value supports the view that the assets are worth more than the market is paying: proved reserves are worth $3,622 million at a 10% discount rate (PV-10 is the present value of expected cash from proved reserves, discounted at 10% a year), measured at a 2025 gas price of $3.39, against an enterprise value of $3.86 billion[1][17]. Today's strip is lower than $3.39, so that reserve value is probably lower now, and we treat it as a sanity check, not a target.

Where the position is weak

Lease operating expense has crept up from $0.19 to $0.23 over twelve months and the pipeline and compression cost from $0.94 to $0.97[3]. Production is also soft against last year: 962.8 million cubic feet equivalent a day in the second quarter against 1,006.3 a year earlier, a fall of 4%[4]. Full-year guidance, the company's own forecast, of 1.030 to 1.055 billion cubic feet equivalent a day needs the second half to average roughly 1.08 to 1.13 because the first half averaged 0.98, which is a heavy ramp that rests on new Ohio wells coming on schedule[4]. Finally, about 19% of production is in Oklahoma, where the company has less advantage and which analysts rarely credit.

What the hedges do, and what they cannot do

The hedge book is a real support. Gulfport held a net derivative asset of $96.5 million at June 30, 2026[2], and its 2027 swaps at $3.89 sit well above the $3.16 average Henry Hub price that the US Energy Information Administration forecasts for 2027[28]. With about a quarter of second-quarter gas volume swapped and another 13% under collars, the contracts add something like $85 million a year at that price, our own estimate from the volumes and prices. The limit is that about 60% of 2027 gas is unhedged, and the hedge asset can fall as quickly as it rose if prices recover, so hedges soften the cycle and do not remove it.

It fits TURNAROUND_VALUE_INFLECTION only narrowly, with two of four trajectory signals positive. The test that decides the final label is asymmetry, the ratio of possible gain to possible loss from the entry price.

5. What could happen next

A reverse DCF works backwards. Instead of forecasting cash flow and finding a value, it takes today's price and asks what growth in cash flow the price already assumes. The scoring file sets the terms for US stocks: a 10% yearly discount rate, ten years, and a terminal value of 18 times year-ten free cash flow. The ledger holds no reverse DCF for Gulfport, so we solved our own. A natural gas producer's multiple often stays low for as long as the gas price is low, because investors treat current profit as a peak. The 6.93 is a data-vendor forward multiple. On 2027 profit alone the current multiple is 6.55, which is $165.91 divided by $25.33. If the multiple stays at 6.93, the same $25.33 of profit is worth $175.54, and the reward-to-risk is 0.52 to 1 at the close and was 0.84 to 1 at the old zone top of $162.77. That is why the buy zone now ends at $155.00, where the ratio is 2.73 to 1 even with no re-rating. This is the assumption that carries the most weight in our target.

The evidence that it is wrong would be a report in which profit meets estimates and the stock still does not move, or estimate cuts that take 2027 profit below $22. We would know after the November 4 report and again when 2027 guidance is published with the fourth-quarter results, which last year came on February 24[5]. Until then the entry zone, not the target, carries the asymmetry.

Weakest assumption 2: free cash flow of $250 million is repeatable

Our reverse DCF starts from $250.0 million of free cash flow over the last twelve months. That total includes the first quarter, when the Henry Hub price averaged $7.72 in January after winter storm Fern[28] and Gulfport's unhedged gas price was $4.90[7]. The second quarter, at $2.48 unhedged, produced adjusted free cash flow of only $6.4 million[4]. At a $3.10 price, a year could earn well under $250 million, and the 2027 hedges are worth roughly $85 million a year at the EIA's price, our estimate.

A lower base does not make the stock expensive on the Layer 1 test, but it narrows the margin. On a $150 million stress base the price assumes 5.3% growth a year for ten years. That is below the 9.6% growth analysts forecast for 2027 profit, but the two are not like for like, because one is ten years of cash growth and the other is one year of profit growth. The $150 million is a stress case, not the worst case: the second quarter's $6.4 million of adjusted free cash flow annualizes to far less. The risk is a mix of lower cash flow and a low multiple together. We will see the third-quarter free cash flow on November 4 and the full year in February.

Weakest assumption 3: the second-half production ramp and the cost trend

Production guidance of 1.030 to 1.055 billion cubic feet equivalent a day for 2026 was repeated in August, but the first half averaged 0.980. To hit the range, the second half has to average roughly 1.08 to 1.13, and the fourth quarter has to be about 5% above the fourth quarter of 2025[4][7]. Management points to two wet gas Utica pads completed near its new Ohio state land[3] and says liquids volumes will be more than 50% higher than in the first half[23]. Second-quarter volume was 4% below a year earlier[4], and lease operating cost has risen from $0.19 to $0.23 over twelve months[3].

Evidence that the assumption is wrong would be third-quarter volume below about 1.0 billion cubic feet equivalent a day, a cut to the production range, or unit costs above $1.30. We will know on November 4 for the third quarter and in the February guidance for 2027. A cut to guidance would turn the fifth Layer 2 measure from watch to fail. That alone lowers the score from 3.5 to 3.0 out of 5.0, which still qualifies. The verdict would move to REJECTED only if a second measure also failed or the score fell below 3.0.

Weakest assumption 4: leadership and capital allocation stay disciplined

Both the chief executive and the chief financial officer have changed within six months: the chief executive left on March 9 and the chief financial officer on August 5[9][3]. A successor finance chief has not been named. The new chief executive has said capital allocation is the most important decision and that it must be competitive, with the balance sheet kept conservative[23]. Against that, debt rose $134 million in six months, buybacks of $242.8 million in the first half exceeded adjusted free cash flow of $125.4 million, and the company has committed another $140 million to acreage[4][3].

The evidence that this is going wrong would be net debt above 1.2 times adjusted EBITDA, a buyback pace that outruns cash flow again in the third quarter, or a large acquisition. We will see the balance sheet on November 4 and the new leadership's first full plan in early 2027.

If the November 4 report or the February 2027 guidance puts 2027 profit near $20, the case fails and we step aside.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, and it is a call to place limit orders below the market, not to ignore the stock. Here is why the support ladder does not simply give ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP. By distance the price is 0.63 average true ranges above its nearest support, the 20-day average at $162.77. That is the STANDARD band (up to 1.2 ranges), where ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP is allowed, and reward-to-risk is 2.68 to 1 at the close. But the band says only that the price is near support. It does not say the 2.5 floor holds on a defensible target. The 2.68 needs an 8.5 times multiple, which is a proxy built from three year-end readings. If the multiple stays at 6.93, $25.33 of profit is worth $175.54 and the ratio is 0.52 to 1 at the close and 0.84 to 1 at $162.77. The floor holds on that stricter test only at $155.50 or lower. The chart also passes only Test C, which calls for a starter and a limit order, and results arrive in a week. The trigger that ends the wait is a fill at or below $155.00.

The entry zone is $151.00 to $155.00. The top is the highest round price at which the floor holds with no re-rating: at $155.00 the ratio is 2.73 to 1 on the $175.54 target, and 8.03 to 1 on the $215.30 base target. At $152.75 or lower it holds even at 6.55 times 2027 profit. The bottom, $151.00, sits just above the $149.97 swing low and the $149.59 base low, because below them the structure breaks. We would use two limit orders: half near $155 and half between $151 and $153. Position size follows our risk-unit rule. The conviction tier is TIER_3, with a risk budget of 50 basis points of the portfolio. At the zone top the stop distance is 4.85%, which would allow 10.3%, so the tier cap of 3.5% governs. Because only Test C passes and results are due, only the first half, 1.75%, is placed before the November 4 report. The second half waits until that release keeps the 2026 production range.

The stop is $147.49, which is half an average true range ($2.48) below the $149.97 swing low and below the 63-day base low of $149.59 and the 52-week low of $149.18. It follows the rule for the tactical bucket. We judge the tactical bucket appropriate because the thesis rests on a turn near support and on events, not on a long hold of a stable compounder. The targets are $215.30 base, $255.00 bull and $135.25 bear. The base is 2027 profit of $25.33 times 8.5, the bull is $30.00 times 8.5, and the bear is the lowest 2027 estimate of $19.89 at 6.8 times. The stop is a risk budget, not a loss ceiling. At 3.92% the gap loss would reach 50, so we do not size above the 3.5% cap.
