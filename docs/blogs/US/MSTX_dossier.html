1. What the company does

This chapter explains what MSTX is, what it holds and why a fund that resets every day behaves differently from the stock it follows. A short quick-reference list of terms comes first, and later chapters explain each term again where it matters.

Quick reference. The RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast a price has been moving. The ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing. A Stage 2 stock is in a rising uptrend, and a Stage 4 stock is in a falling downtrend. Why a daily reset changes the maths

A daily-reset fund is a fund whose leverage is restored to the same level at the end of every trading day. That is the opposite of a margin loan, where the loan stays fixed and the leverage changes with the price. After a fall the fund must shrink its dollar exposure, and after a rise it must grow it, so it buys after gains and sells after losses. In a market that swings up and down, those trades lock in losses. The prospectus calls the result compounding and states that the fund will lose money if the underlying security is flat over a period longer than one day.[1] The prospectus table shows the size of the effect: with a flat underlying stock over one year, the fund returns -22.1% at 50% volatility, -43.0% at 75% and -63.2% at 100%.[1] Chapter 3 recomputes this with Strategy's actual volatility, and Chapter 7 turns it into a plan.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Daily-reset fund: a fund that rebalances every day to hold a fixed multiple of one day's price change, here two times.
  • Swap: a contract in which a bank pays the fund the return on a stated amount of Strategy stock in exchange for a financing fee.
  • Counterparty: the bank or broker on the other side of a swap; if it fails to pay, the fund can lose the gain it is owed.
  • NAV: net asset value, the value of everything the fund owns minus what it owes, divided by the number of shares.
  • Volatility decay: the loss a daily-reset fund suffers when the underlying price moves up and down, even if it ends where it began.
  • Reverse split: a change that merges old shares into fewer new ones, here ten old for one new, which raises the price and cuts the share count.
  • Preferred stock: shares that pay a fixed or set dividend ahead of common shares; Strategy's series are called STRF, STRC, STRK and STRD.
  • USD Reserve: a cash reserve Strategy keeps to pay preferred dividends and interest on its debt.
  • mNAV: here, the market value of Strategy's shares divided by the market value of the bitcoin it holds; below 1.0 means the shares trade for less than the bitcoin behind them.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could fail. Fatal flaw 1: a gap that the stop cannot catch

Our stop works on closing prices and executes at the next open, so a gap can skip it. In the 252 sessions to October 28, Strategy's worst one-day fall was 17.1% on 2026-02-05, which was a 34.5% fall for the fund (our calculation).[14][13] Bitcoin trades through the weekend while Strategy's shares do not, so news arrives on Saturday and shows up in the stock on Monday. Across 48 Mondays, the stock opened more than 5% lower than the prior Friday close on 5 of them, and the lowest opening gap was -6.7% on 2026-06-01 (our calculation).[14]

A repeat of the worst day in our sample would cost $526, or 60 basis points of net worth, which is 1.7 times the 35 basis point budget for a TIER_4 name. A one-day fall of 50% in the stock would take the fund to zero, because 2 times -50% is -100%, and the prospectus says an investor could lose the full principal within a single day.[1] That total loss would cost 173 basis points. The stop limits a slow decline; it does not limit a gap. If the stop fails and Strategy falls 35% in one session, the fund falls about 70% and the 98.480594 shares lose about $1,066, or 121 basis points. If you cannot accept a loss of that size overnight, sell the remaining shares as well. The Strategy report on 2026-10-29 after the market closes is the next obvious gap risk, and the plan sells the trimmed shares before it.

Fatal flaw 2: a failure at the fund, a bank or the sponsor

The fund depends on six swap counterparties, and on April 30, 2026 they owed it $160.2 million, with no collateral shown against that amount in the offset table.[3] The prospectus lists counterparty risk and says the fund may obtain only a limited or delayed recovery from a counterparty in distress.[1] If a bank failed or terminated its swap, the fund would have to replace it with another bank or with listed options, which the prospectus calls less efficient and likely to make the 2x goal harder to meet.[1][7] The sponsor's adviser has also closed other Defiance 2X funds this year, and a closure of MSTX would give holders a set date and a payout at NAV, which could land at an inconvenient price.[9][10][11][12] Closure of MSTX is not announced, and we have no evidence that it is planned. The warning sign is flow, and the part of the 9.8% fall in assets that is flow is the 3.5% fall in shares over five calendar days (three trading sessions); the rest is a lower share price.[8][34]

Fatal flaw 3: Strategy's financing stops working

Strategy's shares trade at 0.89 times the value of its bitcoin by our calculation, so the market values the shares below the bitcoin behind them. The company needs cash to pay about $1.76 billion a year in preferred dividends and interest and has raised it by selling shares, selling preferred stock and selling bitcoin.[19] If bitcoin fell far enough that the company could not sell shares and had to sell bitcoin into a falling market, the stock would fall faster than bitcoin and the fund twice as fast. The company did also say its software business will not cover its obligations, so the cushion depends on capital markets and bitcoin.[19]
