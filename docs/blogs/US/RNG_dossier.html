1. What the company does

RingCentral, Inc. runs business phone systems, messaging, video meetings and contact-center software from the cloud, so a company does not buy phone hardware and servers. It pays a monthly or yearly subscription per user instead. The company was founded in 1999, is based in Belmont, California, and lists more than 7,300 employees on its investor page.[25] In the quarter ended September 30, 2026, subscriptions were $652.9 million of $676.0 million in revenue, or 97% of the total. The rest comes from phones and professional services.[1][3]

The company's own filing says that more than 90% of subscription revenue comes from two product families: RingEX, the unified business phone and messaging product, and its contact-center products. The contact-center line includes RingCX and RingCentral Contact Center. The 10-Q does not split revenue between them, so we cannot say how fast RingCX alone grows.[3] The filing says its AI products, AI Receptionist (AIR) and AI Conversation Expert (ACE), are sold on either a usage basis or a per-seat basis, which means the customer pays for calls handled or for people using the tool.[3] North America is 87% of revenue in the third quarter of 2026, down from 89% a year earlier, so international sales are growing a little faster than the base.[3]

Customers reach RingCentral through its own sales force and through partners. The 10-Q names the partner model: global service providers, resellers and channel partners sell the product, and the company also resells software from NICE, a contact-center vendor, and uses NICE technology inside its contact-center offer.[3] In June 2026 the company and Avaya amended their partnership so that Avaya Cloud Office customers move to a RingCentral-branded offer, and RingCentral booked a $15 million gain on releasing Avaya from certain obligations.[3] The company has two classes of common stock, Class A and Class B, plus Series A convertible preferred stock. On the November 10, 2025 record date there were 75,820,797 Class A shares, 9,804,538 Class B shares and 200,000 preferred shares outstanding.[16] This is a mature subscription business that sells to existing and new accounts, so the key measure of health is whether recurring revenue keeps growing after customers leave.

The 2026 story is about AI phone agents. In its release for the third quarter the company said that customers who use at least one of its own paid AI products make up approaching 15% of ARR (annual recurring revenue, meaning the monthly subscription charges at the end of a period multiplied by twelve), and that this share doubled from a year earlier.[1] On the call, management said AIR has more than 22,000 customers and ACE about 7,000, and that the paid-AI group is over $400 million of ARR.[22][23]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ARR, or annual recurring revenue, is the month-end subscription charge multiplied by twelve. It is a leading indicator of future subscription revenue.
  • Net monthly dollar retention is a monthly measure of how much recurring revenue the company keeps from existing customers after upgrades and cancellations. Above 100% means existing customers spend more than they cancel.
  • Free cash flow is cash from operations minus the cash spent on equipment and capitalized software. It is what is left for debt, buybacks and dividends.
  • SBC, or stock-based compensation, is pay in shares. It costs the owners something even though no cash leaves the bank.
  • Non-GAAP operating margin is operating profit before stock pay, purchase-accounting amortization and a few other items, divided by revenue. GAAP operating margin keeps those costs in.
  • A buyback is when a company spends cash to purchase its own shares, which lowers the share count.
  • Working capital is the cash tied up in day-to-day bills: money customers owe the company, less money the company owes its suppliers.
  • A breakout is a move of the share price above a level where it had stalled before.
  • Reverse DCF asks what yearly cash-flow growth the current price already assumes, so we can judge whether the assumption is demanding.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of the business is scale and stickiness in a product that companies treat as plumbing. ARR reached $2.80 billion on September 30, 2026, up from $2.63 billion a year earlier, a gain of about 6.5% on the company's rounded figures.[3] ARR was $1.80 billion at the end of 2021, $2.10 billion in 2022, $2.33 billion in 2023, $2.49 billion in 2024 and $2.67 billion in 2025, so yearly growth slowed from about 17% in 2022 to about 7% in 2025.[7][6][5][3] A year before the latest quarter, the company reported free cash flow of $130 million and a non-GAAP operating margin of 22.8%.[12] The company's net monthly dollar retention has stayed above 99% in each of the last five quarters in its filings, which means that after upgrades and cancellations the recurring base shrank by less than 1% in a month. A monthly figure printed only as 'above 99%' cannot be scored finely.[3] Gross margin was 73.1% in the third quarter (gross profit $494.3 million on revenue of $676.0 million), up from 71.8% a year earlier, by our calculation.[1]

The weakness is growth. Total revenue grew 4.8% in 2025 after 9.0% in 2024 and 10.8% in 2023, and the nine months of 2026 grew 5.7%.[4][6][3] Competition is wide: the 10-Q lists 8x8, Zoom, Microsoft, Five9, Genesys, Cisco, Alphabet and others as rivals, and says many have longer histories, more resources and larger customer bases.[3] Sales and marketing cost 42% of revenue on a GAAP basis in the third quarter, so a lot of money goes to winning and keeping customers in a market that grows slowly.[3]

Dependence on a partner is the specific risk to watch. The 10-Q says the company resells and relies on solutions from NICE and that it cannot assure it will renew agreements with third-party providers.[3] On the October 22 call an analyst asked what happens when the one-year NICE extension ends. The CEO said he was not sure where the one-year figure came from, that the company had not disclosed specifics, and that the contract still had multiple years left. He added that RingCX is 'getting stronger and stronger'.[22] We read this as an open question, not a threat that has happened.

Balance-sheet structure is better than a year ago but still unusual. The stockholders' deficit was $12.4 million at September 30, 2026, up from a $588.1 million deficit at December 31, 2025, because the company released $544.9 million of a tax valuation allowance, a non-cash accounting entry.[1][3] The 10-K statements show the earlier deficit followed net losses in 2021 to 2024 and share repurchases in 2022 to 2025.[4][6] A deficit makes book-value measures such as return on equity unusable for this company. We use cash flow and debt instead.

3. The numbers, in plain words

The record has three parts. The business grew fast and then slowed. Cash flow improved sharply. The balance sheet was rebuilt in 2025 and 2026.
