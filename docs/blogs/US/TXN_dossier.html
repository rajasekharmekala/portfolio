Chapter 1: How the Business Got Here — From Oil Seismographs to 300mm Analog Dominance

Texas Instruments Incorporated (TI) has reinvented itself several times over nearly a century [1], [7]. Along the way, it shed lower-return units to focus on one durable economic engine [1], [7]. The company began in 1930 as Geophysical Service Incorporated (GSI), using reflection seismographs to find oil [7]. During World War II, it shifted into defense gear and adopted the name Texas Instruments in 1951 [7]. That engineering culture produced two landmark breakthroughs in modern electronics. TI built the first commercial silicon transistor in 1954, and Jack Kilby demonstrated the integrated circuit (IC) in 1958 [7]. Yet for today's investors, TI's defining moves were not its 1950s inventions. They were the disciplined portfolio exits executed between 1995 and 2012.

For decades, TI ran as a sprawling defense and chip conglomerate. It made military radar, consumer calculators, laptops, DRAM memory chips, and OMAP phone processors [7]. Every one of those units shared a fatal flaw. They suffered from short product lives, heavy buyer concentration, or steep Moore's Law spending that eroded returns every few years. Under CEOs Tom Engibous and Rich Templeton, TI exited those traps step by step [7]. It sold defense electronics to Raytheon in 1997 and DRAM memory to Micron in 1998 [7]. It divested sensors and controls in 2006, then wound down its OMAP wireless unit in 2012 [7]. Management shifted capital and engineers into Analog and Embedded Processing. In these fragmented markets, a single chip win in a factory drive or car powertrain can ship for 10 to 20 years.

The biggest deal in that pivot came in 2011, when TI bought National Semiconductor for $6.5 billion [7]. That purchase added about 12,000 analog parts and deepened TI's power-management and signal-chain catalog. It also set a clear template for buying assets during slowdowns. Ten years later, in 2021, TI applied the same playbook to wafer fabs. It bought Micron Technology's 300mm fab in Lehi, Utah (LFAB1) for $900 million [4], [7]. That deal secured cleanroom space at a fraction of new-build cost [4], [7]. In April 2023, Rich Templeton handed the CEO role to Haviv Ilan [4], [7]. Ilan was a 24-year TI veteran who had run Analog, Embedded Processing, and the 2022–2026 300mm buildout plan [4], [7].

TI spent 25 years pruning short-cycle, capital-hungry businesses to concentrate on long-lifecycle Analog and Embedded chips, then used the 2022–2025 cycle to lock in a generational 300mm U.S. manufacturing footprint.

To read TI's recent results, you must separate the demand cycle from the factory-build cycle. During the 2020–2022 chip shortage, TI's revenue jumped to $18,344 million in 2021 and $20,028 million in 2022 [7]. In 2022, operating profit reached $10,487 million and free cash flow hit $5,923 million [7]. Then came the post-pandemic inventory glut. Industrial and auto buyers spent 2023 and 2024 working down excess part stockpiles. TI's revenue fell 12.5% to $17,519 million in 2023, then dropped another 10.7% to $15,641 million in 2024 [1], [7]. Operating profit fell by nearly half from its 2022 peak to $5,350 million in 2024 [1], [7].

Most chipmakers slash capital spending when sales drop 22% from peak to trough. TI did the exact opposite. Management bet that U.S. supply security and 300mm wafer math would shape the next twenty years. It pushed gross capex to $5,071 million in 2023, $4,820 million in 2024, and $4,550 million in 2025 [1], [7]. As a result, free cash flow shrank from $6,294 million in 2021 to $1,349 million in 2023 and $1,498 million in 2024 [1], [7]. At the same time, TI raised internal inventory from ~$2 billion pre-pandemic to $4,804 million by year-end 2025 [1]. That 222-day buffer let TI ship parts right away when buyers returned [1].

By 2026, that twin bet on 300mm capacity and ready stock began to pay off. Revenue grew 13.0% in 2025 to $17,682 million [1]. Growth sped up through 2026, hitting $4,825 million in Q1, $5,463 million in Q2, and $6,055 million in Q3 [2], [3]. That Q3 figure rose 28% year-over-year and topped the prior 2022 quarterly peak [2], [3]. Gross margin climbed from 57.0% in FY2025 to 64.2% in Q3 2026 as fab load rose and Sherman's SM1 fab started output [1], [2], [4]. On February 4, 2026, TI also announced an all-cash deal to buy Silicon Labs for about $7.5 billion in enterprise value [6]. The deal targets a first-half 2027 close and adds ~1,200 wireless parts to Embedded Processing [6].

Why Analog Lifecycles Reward Patient Capital: Consumer logic and memory chips often turn obsolete within 24 months. By contrast, TI builds analog and embedded parts on mature nodes from 28nm to 130nm [4], [7]. Products on those nodes often stay in production for 10 to 20 years [4], [7]. An industrial motor drive or car battery system takes years of safety testing to qualify. Once TI wins a socket, the revenue stream acts like a long-term royalty. That long life is why building 300mm cleanrooms during a downturn makes economic sense. Fabs built in 2023–2025 will still stamp out high-margin chips in 2040 [4].

Capital Discipline Across Three Decades: TI's shift from a broad conglomerate to a focused analog supplier did not happen by chance. Each divestiture removed a unit that demanded constant node shrinks or relied on one or two large buyers. In wireless basebands, a single phone cycle could wipe out two years of R&D work. In DRAM, industry oversupply crushed margins every few years regardless of execution. By leaving those markets, TI freed cash to buy National Semiconductor and build its 300mm U.S. fabs. That multi-decade focus explains why TI can spend $14.4 billion across 2023–2025 without losing its balance-sheet footing [1], [7].

Chapter 2: Inside the Machine — Analog, Embedded Processing & Real-World Silicon

Headline AI chipmakers sell a handful of $30,000 processors to four cloud giants. Texas Instruments runs the opposite model. It sells roughly 80,000 distinct chip products to more than 100,000 buyers worldwide [1], [4]. TI reports two core operating segments—Analog and Embedded Processing—plus a small Other unit [1]. Every modern machine needs these chips. A factory robot, an electric car battery, and a cloud server rack all rely on TI parts. These parts regulate voltage, sense heat and current, isolate high-voltage rails, and run real-time control loops.

1. Analog ($14,006M in FY2025, 79.2% of revenue; $4,911M in Q3 2026, 81.1% of revenue): Analog is TI's core profit engine [1], [2]. It covers two main product groups: Power and Signal Chain [1], [7]. Analog chips handle real-world inputs like voltage, current, heat, pressure, and sound. Analog operating margin fell to 37.9% in FY2024 and 38.6% in FY2025 on low fab load and higher 300mm depreciation [1], [7]. As volume returned, margin snapped back to 51.0% in Q3 2026 [2].

Concrete End-to-End Analog Example — 800V Electric Vehicle Traction & Battery Management: Picture an automaker building an 800-volt electric SUV. Inside the battery pack, TI's BQ79616 monitor chip tracks cell voltage and heat across 16 or more cells in series. It reads values within a fraction of a millivolt and sends data over an isolated link to prevent thermal runaway. Downstream, in the charger and DC/DC converter, TI's UCC5880 gate drivers and 650V GaN power stages step 800V pack power down to 48V and 12V rails. Those rails run power steering, brakes, and cabin screens. One EV can hold $300 to $500+ of TI analog chips across dozens of qualified sockets. Those parts often stay in production for the vehicle's full 7-to-10-year platform life.

2. Embedded Processing ($2,697M in FY2025, 15.3% of revenue; $858M in Q3 2026, 14.2% of revenue): Embedded Processing sells microcontrollers (MCUs), digital signal processors (DSPs), and system processors [1], [7]. These chips run precise, real-time tasks inside physical machines [1], [7]. Embedded margins fell hard during the 2024–2025 slump, dropping from 38.4% in 2022 to 11.3% in FY2025 [1], [7]. That drop came from moving foundry wafers into TI's Lehi 300mm fab (LFAB1) while fab load stayed low [1], [4]. By Q3 2026, Embedded margin recovered to 26.5%, with $227M in operating profit on $858M in sales [2]. In February 2026, TI also agreed to buy Silicon Labs for $7.5 billion [6]. Expected to close in H1 2027, that deal adds ~1,200 wireless IoT chips covering Bluetooth LE, Zigbee, Thread, Matter, and Wi-Fi [6].

Concrete End-to-End Embedded Processing Example — Industrial Robotic Servo Drive & Edge Vision: Picture a smart factory running a 6-axis robot arm. At each joint, a TI C2000™ real-time MCU (such as the TMS320F28388D) checks motor current and position in nanoseconds. It runs fast control loops so the arm moves with micron-level accuracy. At the main hub, a TI Sitara™ AM64x or Jacinto™ TDA4x chip blends camera and LiDAR feeds to spot obstacles. That lets robots work safely near human staff. Customers write millions of lines of custom control code on TI's Code Composer Studio and C2000 tools. Switching to a rival MCU would force years of new coding and safety tests.

3. Other ($979M in FY2025, 5.5% of revenue; $286M in Q3 2026, 4.7% of revenue): The Other unit holds DLP® light chips, school calculators, custom ASICs, and corporate items such as restructuring costs [1], [2], [7]. This small unit acts as a steady cash earner while absorbing corporate items [1], [2].

Concrete End-to-End Other Segment Example — Automotive Head-Up Displays (DLP®) & STEM Calculators: Inside a luxury car's head-up display, a TI DLP5530-Q1 chip uses hundreds of thousands of tiny tilting aluminum mirrors. Those mirrors project clear turn arrows onto the windshield even in bright sun. Elsewhere, in North American high schools and colleges, TI's TI-84 Plus CE and TI-Nspire™ CX II calculators remain required tools for AP Calculus, SAT, and ACT exams. That classroom standard brings high-margin, repeat fall sales with very low R&D spend.

Chapter 3: The 300mm Cost Machine — Distribution, Fabs & Unit Economics

Most analog chipmakers rely on outside distributors like Arrow or Avnet to sell their parts. They also pay outside foundries like TSMC or UMC to make their wafers. Over the past decade, Texas Instruments replaced that model with two linked advantages. First, it built direct-to-customer sales (>80% of revenue) [1], [4]. Second, it scaled internal 300mm wafer fabs [1], [4].

On distribution, TI ended most broad-line distributor deals and moved its 100,000+ buyers onto direct accounts [4]. Those direct accounts run through TI.com and consignment stock programs [4]. Today, over 80% of TI's revenue flows straight from TI to the end customer [4]. Cutting out middleman markups saves several points of gross margin. Even better, TI sees live order flow, sample downloads, and factory demand without distributor lag. To ship fast without distributors, TI holds large internal stock. Inventory reached $4,804 million (222 days) at year-end 2025, then eased to $4.7 billion (194 days) in Q3 2026 [1], [2].

On manufacturing, TI's unit-cost edge comes from wafer size. Most analog rivals still build chips on 150mm or 200mm (8-inch) wafers, often at outside foundries. TI pioneered analog chips on 300mm (12-inch) wafers at RFAB1 in Richardson, Texas [4]. A 300mm wafer has 2.25x the surface area of a 200mm wafer and loses fewer chips at the edge. As a result, each 300mm wafer yields roughly 2.5x as many unpackaged dies. That geometry delivers about 40% lower cost per die and a 20%–40% structural gross-margin edge at scale [4].
