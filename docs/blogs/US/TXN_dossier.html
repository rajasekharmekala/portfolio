1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Semiconductor Cycles

Core Investor Question: How did a 1930 Texas oilfield seismic contractor reinvent itself into the world's dominant analog semiconductor compounder. Also, what does the 2021–2026 cycle teach us about its earnings power?

To understand why Texas Instruments commands a $254.57 billion market capitalization and a 64.0% gross margin in late 2026, an investor must trace a 96-year institutional evolution that repeatedly discarded glamorous, short-lived digital businesses in favor of long-lived, high-return manufacturing moats. The company's roots date back to December 1930 in Dallas, Texas, when J. Clarence 'Doc' Karcher and Eugene McDermott founded Geophysical Service Incorporated (GSI), pioneering reflection seismography—using sound waves to map underground rock strata for petroleum exploration[4]. During World War II, GSI expanded into military submarine-detection sonar and radar electronics for the U.S. Navy and Army Signal Corps. Recognizing that precision electronics held far broader commercial potential than oilfield contracting alone, management — led by Eugene McDermott, Cecil Green, J. Erik Jonsson. Also, Patrick Haggerty — reorganized the enterprise and renamed it Texas Instruments Incorporated in 1951, listing on the New York Stock Exchange on October 1, 1953[4][6].

Two foundational inventions in the 1950s turned Texas Instruments from a regional defense contractor into the birthplace of the modern silicon age. In May 1954, TI research director Gordon Teal developed the world's first commercial silicon transistor, overcoming the severe temperature limitations of earlier germanium transistors and enabling the Regency TR-1 pocket radio and high-reliability aerospace electronics[4]. Four years later, in the summer of 1958, newly hired TI engineer Jack Kilby worked alone in the Dallas laboratory while colleagues were on vacation and solved the 'tyranny of numbers' by demonstrating that resistors, capacitors. Also, transistors could all be fabricated on a single monolithic piece of semiconductor material. Kilby's September 12, 1958 demonstration of the first integrated circuit (IC)—an achievement later recognized with the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics—laid the physical foundation for every microprocessor, memory chip. Also, analog signal-chain component in existence today[4].

The Templeton–Ilan Pivot: Exiting Wireless to Build the Analog & 300mm Monopoly

For four decades after Kilby's breakthrough, Texas Instruments competed across nearly every arena of electronics: defense radar systems, minicomputers, DRAM memory chips, consumer calculators, digital signal processors (DSPs). Also, 2G/3G mobile phone baseband and OMAP application processors for Nokia and Motorola. Yet by the mid-2000s, then-CEO Richard K. Templeton (who served as Chief Executive Officer from 2004 to April 2023 and Chairman through 2025) recognized a profound economic divergence within the semiconductor industry. Digital logic and wireless baseband chips required bleeding-edge, multi-billion-dollar Moore's Law node shrinks at external foundries every twenty-four months, only to suffer brutal customer concentration and obsolescence whenever a handset OEM switched suppliers or integrated its own silicon (as Apple and Samsung ultimately did)[4][16]. Analog and embedded processing chips, by contrast, operated on mature process nodes (45nm to 180nm+), served tens of thousands of fragmented industrial and automotive customers, remained designed into customer boards for ten to thirty years. Also, required virtually zero post-qualification R&D maintenance.

Executing one of the boldest capital reallocation programs in corporate history, Templeton and his leadership team—including Haviv Ilan, who joined TI in 1999 when TI acquired butterfly wireless startup Butterfly Communications and rose through engineering, COO. Also, ultimately CEO in April 2023 and Chairman in 2026—systematically divested non-core divisions[4][5]. TI sold its defense electronics business to Raytheon in 1997, exited commodity DRAM, divested its sensors and controls unit in 2006. Also, between 2008 and 2012 wound down its multi-billion-dollar cellular baseband and OMAP smartphone processor businesses[4]. Simultaneously, TI went on the offensive in analog manufacturing during industry panics. During the 2008–2009 global financial crisis, TI bought bankrupted Qimonda's 300mm DRAM tools for pennies on the dollar to open RFAB1 in Richardson, Texas—the world's first 300mm analog wafer fab. In September 2011, TI acquired arch-rival National Semiconductor for $6.5 billion, adding over 12,000 high-performance power management and precision signal-chain products to its catalog[4][16]. And over the subsequent decade, TI pivoted its R&D and salesforce relentlessly toward Industrial and Automotive end markets. That lifted their combined revenue share from roughly 32% in 2011 to over 70% prior to the 2026 Data Center explosion.

Anatomy of the 2021–2026 Full Cycle: Boom, Trough, Elliott Activism. Also, Breakout

2. The Physics & Die Economics of 300mm vs. 200mm Analog Wafer Fabrication

While digital logic companies like Apple, NVIDIA. Also, AMD migrated to 300mm wafers at TSMC two decades ago to build 3nm–7nm processors, almost the entire global analog industry remained stuck on legacy 150mm and 200mm wafers running 90nm–350nm bipolar-CMOS-DMOS (BCD) and precision analog processes. Why? Because an individual analog chip is physically tiny and sells for $0.30 to $2.00. Only a company shipping tens of billions of units per year across 80,000 part numbers possesses the volume required to keep a 300mm wafer fab loaded. Competitors such as Analog Devices, NXP. Also, Microchip either rely heavily on external foundries (where wafer prices rose sharply after 2021) or operate older 200mm internal lines[4][11][16].

The geometry of a 300mm circular silicon wafer creates an insurmountable unit-cost advantage over a 200mm wafer. Because the area of a circle scales with the square of its radius ($\pi r^2$), increasing wafer diameter by 1.5x (from 200mm to 300mm) increases total surface area by 2.25x (from 31,416 square millimeters to 70,686 square millimeters). Also. This is because less percentage of the perimeter edge is lost to curved edge-die waste, a 300mm wafer yields roughly 2.3x to 2.4x as many usable chips per wafer pass[11][16]. Yet processing a 300mm wafer through photolithography, ion implantation, etch. Also, chemical vapor deposition costs only about 1.35x to 1.40x as much as processing a 200mm wafer. The net result, verified in TI's capital management disclosures, is an approximate 40% reduction in unpackaged die cost and an ~800-basis-point structural gross margin uplift (raising chip-level gross margin from ~60% on 200mm to ~68% on 300mm once packaged and tested){cite(4, 16, 17)}.
