1. What the company does

Texas Instruments sells two kinds of chips. Analog chips sense and clean up real-world signals such as heat, pressure and sound, and they manage power inside a device. Embedded processors, such as microcontrollers, are small computers that run a machine's controls. In 2025 Analog made about 79% of revenue, Embedded Processing about 15%, and a group called Other (projector chips, calculators and custom chips) made $979 million of the $17,682 million total.[4]

Customers fall into five market groups. In 2025 industrial was 33% of revenue, automotive 33%, personal electronics 21%, data center 9%, communications equipment 3%, and calculators about 1%.[4] Data center is now bigger: it was 13% of third-quarter 2026 revenue and grew about 95% from a year earlier.[13] By customer location, the United States was 39% of third-quarter revenue and China 23%, up from 21% for the whole of 2025.[3][4] One end customer accounted for 12% of revenue in both 2025 and 2024.[4]

TI owns most of its factories. A chip made on a 300mm wafer costs about 40% less than one made on a 200mm wafer, according to the company's own filing.[3] A large part of the cost of running a factory is fixed, so profit margins rise when factories run fuller and fall when they run emptier.[3] That fixed-cost structure is why TI's margins swing with the chip cycle, and why a rebound in demand lifts profit faster than revenue. In February 2026 management said it expects more than 95% of its wafers to come from its own factories by 2030, with more than 80% on 300mm.[21]

TI says its objective is the long-term growth of free cash flow (FCF) per share.[3] Free cash flow is cash from operations minus the money spent on plant and equipment. TI's own non-GAAP version also adds back CHIPS Act cash incentives, which are U.S. government support for domestic chip factories.[2] This report shows both versions and says which one it uses. Management has said it aims to return 100% of free cash flow to owners over time, through dividends and buybacks.[22]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • An analog chip is a chip that works with real-world signals (power, sound, temperature) instead of only ones and zeros.
  • An embedded processor is a small computer chip that controls a machine, such as a motor drive or a car module.
  • A 300mm wafer is a 12-inch silicon disc on which many chips are printed at once; more chips per disc means a lower cost per chip.
  • Capital expenditure (capex) is cash spent on factories and equipment rather than on running costs.
  • The CHIPS Act is the 2022 U.S. law that gives chipmakers cash grants and a tax credit for building U.S. factories.
  • The investment tax credit (ITC) is a credit that lowers a tax bill; for TI it is 35% of qualifying U.S. factory spending.
  • Gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of making the chips.
  • A reverse DCF is a calculation that asks what yearly growth in cash flow the current share price already assumes.
  • Inventory days is the stock of unsold chips measured in days of sales; a low number risks missed orders and a high one risks write-downs.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

TI's competitive position rests on cost, supply and breadth. TI names four advantages: manufacturing and technology, a broad analog and embedded portfolio, the reach of its market channels, and diverse, long-lived products and customer positions.[3] The cost advantage is measurable: 300mm output costs about 40% less per chip than 200mm output.[3] Owning the factories gives TI control of supply, which matters when customers are short of parts. On the third-quarter call the CEO said there has been little addition to analog capacity outside China and Taiwan in the last four years, and that TI built inventory during the downturn so it can help customers who ask for supply.[13]

The cycle is the second thing to understand. Revenue was $18,344 million in 2021, peaked at $20,028 million in 2022, fell to $15,641 million in 2024, and has since climbed to $20,766 million over the last twelve months.[6][4][2] Gross margin followed: 68.8% in 2022, 57.0% in 2025 (both our calculations from the income statements), and 64.2% in the third quarter of 2026.[6][4][2] Industrial revenue grew more than 40% in the third quarter and reached a new peak after four years, which the CEO tied to more chip content in customers' systems.[13] That gives industrial room to run, though it also means TI now sells at the top of the last cycle's revenue range.

The third quarter showed how strongly profit follows volume. Revenue rose $1,313 million from a year earlier and gross profit rose $1,164 million, so about 89% of each extra revenue dollar reached gross profit (our calculation); the CFO put it at about 90%, above the 75% to 85% range TI uses for long-term modelling.[2][13] Days of inventory fell to 194 from 222 at the end of 2025, inside the 150 to 250 day band TI set in February.[3][21] Lower inventory days and a higher margin together say the extra sales are shipping from fuller factories, not piling up in a warehouse.

The weak points are real. Depreciation, the yearly charge for the cost of factories, rose from $925 million in 2022 to $1,918 million in 2025 and $2,180 million over the last twelve months, about 10.5% of revenue.[6][4][3] China was 23% of third-quarter revenue, and the 10-K warns that China is promoting and reshaping its own chip industry, which could cut TI's ability to compete.[3][4] One customer is 12% of revenue.[4] Finally, TI has agreed to buy Silicon Labs for $231.00 per share in cash, an enterprise value of about $7.5 billion; the U.S. antitrust waiting period has expired and Silicon Labs stockholders approved on April 30, 2026, but other regulatory approvals remain and the close is expected in the first half of 2027.[22][23][24]

Evidence for the cost and supply advantage

The best evidence is the gross margin recovery alongside rising depreciation. Depreciation in the third quarter was $555 million against $497 million a year earlier, up about 12%, while revenue rose 28% and gross margin rose from 57.4% to 64.2% (our calculations).[2] Factories filled with more output spread their fixed costs, and the numbers show exactly that pattern. TI's guidance for the fourth quarter (its own forecast), $5.80 billion to $6.30 billion of revenue, sits above the usual seasonal dip, which the CEO linked to data center, industrial and automotive demand.[2][13] A peer shows the same pattern from a lower base: NXP's non-GAAP gross margin was 58.6% on 19% revenue growth.[28]

Where the advantage is thinner

Pricing power is not proven yet. TI said analog pricing was flat in the first half of 2026 and rose a little in the second half.[13] The advantage also has a cost: TI spent heavily to build it, and the filing says a significant portion of its operating cost is fixed, so a demand drop would hit profit hard.[3] The next chapter tests whether TI delivered on what it promised about this spending, using five operating measures chosen for an analog chip maker.

Third-quarter 2026 results are already out, so no report is pending for this plan. TI reported after the market closed on October 21, 2026 and filed its 10-Q on October 23.[2][3] Revenue was $6.06 billion and EPS (earnings per share) was $2.62 against a consensus of $2.40, and the fourth-quarter outlook is revenue of $5.80 billion to $6.30 billion and EPS of $2.45 to $2.79.[2][14] The share price has since risen from $284.69 on 2026-10-23 to $309.83.[1][37]

Management guides one quarter ahead and gives a capital plan once a year, so both can be checked. On the quarterly numbers TI has been accurate to conservative: revenue landed inside the range in two of the last four quarters and above the top of the range in two, and EPS landed above the top of the range in three of four.[11][10][9][8][2] On the multi-year plan, the 10-K for 2024 said capital spending would stay elevated, and the February 2026 plan then set 2026 spending at $2 billion to $3 billion, with $1.65 billion spent in the first nine months.[5][21][3]

The promise most relevant to this stock is free cash flow per share. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Two points stand out before the tables. First, profit and cash moved in opposite directions for three years: operating profit fell 46% from 2022 to 2024, while capital spending rose from $2.8 billion to $5.1 billion in 2023.[6][4] Second, cash flow has now turned. Over the last twelve months operating cash flow was $9,896 million and capex only $2,578 million.[2] That swing is why the ledger's cash-flow field, which is empty this time, needed our own calculation, as the reverse DCF discussion in chapter 5 explains.

Shareholder returns and CHIPS Act cash complete the picture. Over the last twelve months TI paid $5,174 million of dividends and bought back $636 million of shares, against $1,611 million of buybacks a year earlier.[2] A buyback is a company purchase of its own shares. The dividend now runs at $1.52 a quarter, or $6.08 a year, which is the 23rd straight yearly increase and about 1.96% of the share price; the next payment goes to holders of record on October 30, 2026 and is paid on November 10.[12] On 913.77 million shares the new rate costs about $5.56 billion a year, or 76% of the last twelve months' operating cash flow less capex (our calculation; the data vendor shows a 75.92% free cash flow payout).[3][17] The dividend is covered, but with less room than a 35% margin suggests: at $3 billion of capex and flat operating cash flow the payout would be about 81% (our arithmetic).

The CHIPS Act supports the plan in two ways. TI expects a 35% investment tax credit on qualifying U.S. factory spending and up to $1.6 billion of direct funding, of which $630 million has been received.[3] Cash benefits were $588 million in 2024, $670 million in 2025 and $1,484 million in the first nine months of 2026.[5][4][3] The 10-K for 2024 expected $7.5 billion to $9.5 billion through 2034, and management has said the amounts depend on U.S. capex, which TI is now cutting.[5] Beyond 2026, TI says capex will depend on revenue and growth expectations.[3]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in chapter 5 has a weakest assumption, and a good decision names it before the money is committed. The bear case below argues against our own central view as hard as the evidence allows. It then ranks three ways the thesis could fail outright, names the one claim everything hangs on, and gives the date it will be known.

Fatal flaw 1: the upturn is a peak, not a recovery

Industrial revenue has only just regained its 2022 level, and TI's gross margin fell from 68.8% in 2022 to 57.0% in 2025.[6][4][13] If customers have been ordering ahead of real demand, a pause would hit revenue while depreciation stays at about $2.2 billion a year. In 2023 revenue fell 12.5% and gross margin dropped 5.9 points.[6] If 2027 revenue were flat at $22.4 billion rather than $26.2 billion, 2027 EPS would be near $9.1 on our fall-through arithmetic, and at the 10-year median P/E the stock would be about $218. Evidence to watch: lead times, pricing, and the fourth-quarter outlook. TI said lead times ticked up and that automotive customers hold little inventory, which argues against double ordering in that market, and NXP said 80% of its portfolio now has lead times over 16 weeks, but the same facts also describe the early stage of a shortage that can reverse.[13][28]

Fatal flaw 2: China turns a risk into a loss

China was 21% of 2025 revenue ($3,781 million) and 23% of third-quarter 2026 revenue ($1,392 million), and the 10-K says products shipped into China were about 50% of 2025 revenue.[4][3] China opened an anti-dumping probe in September 2025 into certain U.S. analog chips, covering interface and gate driver chips made at 40 nanometers and above; it was due to finish by September 13, 2026, with a possible six-month extension, and we could not verify the outcome.[34][35] If it was extended, the latest date is March 13, 2027. The 10-K also warns that China is reshaping its domestic chip industry with policy support.[4] The Silicon Labs purchase still needs approvals outside the United States, with the close expected in the first half of 2027.[24] A ruling with duties, or a block on the deal, would hurt both revenue and the use of the $7.5 billion of cash and debt.

Fatal flaw 3: capex returns before cash flow does

The free cash flow case rests on capex staying near $2 billion to $3 billion. The CEO said TI wants to aim higher in that range, that tool lead times are lengthening, and that 2027 capex depends on how much growth TI wants to support.[13] TI's filing says capex beyond 2026 depends on revenue and growth expectations, and NXP guided 2027 capex to about 5% of its revenue, so the industry is moving toward more spending, not less.[3][28] The factory shells are built, so TI can add tools quickly, but each added $1 billion lowers free cash flow by about $1.09 per share. If 2027 capex returned to $4 billion to $5 billion, 2027 free cash flow would look like the 2025 pattern again, and the 2.58% yield would fall further.

Red-team bear case: the strongest honest argument against our own plan

The bear says TI is a good company bought at a cyclical top. Gross margin of 64.2% is still below the 2022 peak of 68.8%, but revenue is already above its 2022 level, and the stock trades at 39.93 times trailing earnings, which is 56% above its 10-year average.[6][2][20] Analyst targets average only 1.8% above the close, DZ Bank's upgrade stops at hold with a $300 target, and the lowest target on the MarketBeat page is $240, or $250 on a second page.[15][19] In that view the Silicon Labs deal adds leverage and integration risk at the moment capex is meant to be falling. If 2027 EPS lands at the Street's lowest figure of $9.79 and the market pays the 10-year median of 23.99 times, the stock is worth $234.86, 24.2% below the close and 17.2% below our order price.[16][20]

Our response is that the order price already assumes this risk. A fill at $283.79 loses 17.2% in the bear case instead of 24.2%, the position is capped at 2% of net worth, and the stop at $262.66 limits an ordinary decline to about 7.5%. The bear is right that there is no margin of safety at the market price, and that is the reason we are not buying at the market price.

Position size follows from risk units. Portfolio heat is the total amount the portfolio would lose if every stop were hit; the rules cap it at 12% of net worth. Risk-unit sizing sets each new position so that a stop-out costs a fixed number of basis points (bps; one bp is one hundredth of one percent) of net worth, with a ceiling that depends on the conviction tier. Tiers run from TIER_1 (the most conviction, 7% to 10% of net worth) to TIER_4 (1% to 2%), and TIER_0 means zero size. TXN is TIER_4: 1% to 2% of net worth and a 35 bps risk budget. TIER_3 would have been the yardstick for a case with a clean weighted ratio; we kept the lower tier because the weighted ratio is under the floor and the median-multiple test loses money. The formula gives the lower of the tier ceiling (2%) and 35 bps divided by the 7.45% stop distance (4.7%), so the ceiling binds. A stop-out then costs about $106, or 12.7 bps, against the 35 bps budget.[36]

A stop does not cap a loss when a stock gaps. If we held 5 shares bought at $283.79 and TXN opened 15% lower, at about $241.22, the position would lose about $213, or 26 bps, which is about three quarters of the 35 bps budget. A fall from the fill to the bear value of $234.86 would cost about $245, or 29 bps, close to the whole budget. A stop-loss order turns into a market order when the price touches it, and it fills at the next available price, which can be well below the stop. The portfolio is already running at about 2.78 times market exposure, so we do not raise the size to chase the ratio.[36]
