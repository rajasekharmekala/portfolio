1. What the company does

Texas Instruments sells two kinds of chips. Analog chips sense and clean up real-world signals such as heat, pressure and sound, and they manage power inside a device. Embedded processors, such as microcontrollers, are small computers that run a machine's controls. In 2025 the Analog segment made $14,006 million of the company's $17,682 million of revenue, Embedded Processing made about 15%, and a group called Other (projector chips, calculators and custom chips) made $979 million.[3] The three parts add to the total, and Analog alone is about 79% of it (our calculation).

Customers fall into five market groups. In 2025 industrial made up 33% of revenue, automotive 33%, personal electronics 21%, data center 9%, communications equipment 3%, and calculators about 1%.[3] TI realigned its market groups in 2025 to show data center separately, and the 10-K table has no line called enterprise systems, so this report uses the five current groups.[3][22] By customer location, the United States was 38% of 2025 revenue and China 21%; about 20% of revenue came from end customers headquartered in China and about 50% from products shipped into China.[3] One end customer accounted for 12% of revenue in both 2025 and 2024.[3]

TI owns most of its factories. A chip made on a 300mm wafer costs about 40% less than one made on a 200mm wafer, according to the company's own filing.[2] A large part of the cost of running a factory is fixed, so profit margins rise when factories run fuller and fall when they run emptier.[2] That fixed-cost structure is the reason TI's margins move so sharply with the chip cycle, and it is the reason a rebound in demand can lift profit faster than revenue.

TI says its objective is the long-term growth of free cash flow (FCF) per share.[2] Free cash flow is cash from operations minus the money spent on plant and equipment. TI's own non-GAAP version also adds back CHIPS Act cash incentives, which are U.S. government support for domestic chip factories.[1] This report shows both versions and says which one it uses. Management has also said it aims to return all free cash flow to owners over time, through dividends and buybacks.[12]

How TI makes money from each chip

TI sells a very large number of low-priced parts into many designs, so no single product drives the result. The 10-Q says new products do not tend to move revenue in any one period, and that changes in revenue come from changes in customer demand, which show up as shipment volumes.[2] In the third quarter of 2026 revenue rose 28% from a year earlier, and TI attributes that to higher demand, although the CEO also said analog prices ticked up in the second half after a flat first half.[2][13] For an owner this means the investment case rests on volume recovery and factory fill, with price as a smaller extra.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • An analog chip is a chip that works with real-world signals (power, sound, temperature) instead of only ones and zeros.
  • An embedded processor is a small computer chip that controls a machine, such as a motor drive or a car module.
  • A 300mm wafer is a 12-inch silicon disc on which many chips are printed at once; more chips per disc means a lower cost per chip.
  • Capital expenditure (capex) is cash spent on factories and equipment rather than on running costs.
  • The CHIPS Act is the 2022 U.S. law that gives chipmakers cash grants and a tax credit for building U.S. factories.
  • The investment tax credit (ITC) is a credit that lowers a tax bill; for TI it is 35% of qualifying U.S. factory spending.
  • Gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of making the chips.
  • A reverse DCF is a calculation that asks what yearly growth in cash flow the current share price already assumes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

TI's competitive position rests on cost, supply and breadth. TI names four advantages: manufacturing and technology, a broad analog and embedded portfolio, the reach of its market channels, and diverse, long-lived products and customer positions.[2] The cost advantage is measurable: 300mm output costs about 40% less per chip than 200mm output, and TI said in February 2026 that it expects more than 95% of its wafers to be made in its own factories by 2030, with more than 80% on 300mm.[2][22] Owning the factories gives TI control of supply, which matters when customers are short of parts. On the third-quarter call the CEO said there has not been much added to analog capacity in the last four years, and that TI built inventory during the downturn so it can help customers who ask for supply.[13]

The cycle is the second thing to understand. Revenue was $18,344 million in 2021, peaked at $20,028 million in 2022, fell to $15,641 million in 2024, and has since climbed to $20,766 million over the last twelve months.[5][3][1] Gross margin followed: 68.8% in 2022, 57.0% in 2025 (both our calculations from the income statements), and 64.2% in the third quarter of 2026.[5][3][2] Industrial revenue only reached its 2022 level again in the third quarter of 2026, which the CEO called a new peak after four years, and he said customers' systems now carry more chip content than they did then.[13] That gives industrial room to run in his telling, though it also means TI now sells at the top of the last cycle's revenue range.

The third quarter showed how strongly profit follows volume. Revenue rose $1,313 million from a year earlier and gross profit rose $1,164 million, so about 89% of each extra revenue dollar reached gross profit (our calculation); the CFO put it at about 90%, above the 75% to 85% range TI uses for long-term modelling.[1][13] Days of inventory fell to 194 from 222 at the end of 2025, inside the 150 to 250 day band TI set in February.[2][22] Lower inventory days and a higher margin together say the extra sales are being shipped from fuller factories, not built up in a warehouse.

The weak points are real. Depreciation, the yearly charge for the cost of factories, rose from $925 million in 2022 to $1,918 million in 2025 and $2,180 million over the last twelve months, about 10.5% of revenue.[5][3][2] China is 21% of revenue, and the 10-K warns that China is promoting and reshaping its own chip industry, which could cut TI's ability to compete.[3] One customer is 12% of revenue.[3] Finally, TI has agreed to buy Silicon Labs for $231.00 per share in cash, an enterprise value of about $7.5 billion, which needs regulatory approval and is expected to close in the first half of 2027.[2][29]

Evidence for the cost and supply advantage

The best evidence is the gross margin recovery with rising depreciation. Depreciation in the third quarter was $555 million against $497 million a year earlier, up about 12%, while revenue rose 28% and gross margin rose from 57.4% to 64.2%.[1][2] Factories that are filled with more output spread their fixed costs, and the numbers show exactly that pattern. TI's guidance for the fourth quarter (its own forecast), $5.80 billion to $6.30 billion of revenue, is above the usual seasonal dip, which the CEO linked to data center, industrial and automotive demand.[1][13]

Where the advantage is thinner

Pricing power is not proven yet. TI said analog pricing was flat in the first half of 2026 and rose a little in the second half, and that 2027 pricing talks with customers are under way, with results due in the January call.[13] The advantage also has a cost: TI had to spend heavily to build it, and the filing says a large part of its operating cost is fixed, so a demand drop would hit profit hard.[2] The next chapter tests whether TI delivered on what it promised about this spending.

Management guides one quarter ahead and gives a capital plan once a year, so both can be checked. On the quarterly numbers TI has been accurate to conservative: revenue landed inside the range in two of the last four quarters and above the top of the range in two, and earnings per share (EPS, profit divided by shares) landed above the top of the range in three of four.[10][9][8][7][1] On the multi-year plan, the 10-K for 2024 said capital spending would stay elevated, and the 10-K for 2025 then set 2026 spending at $2 billion to $3 billion, with $1.65 billion spent in the first nine months.[4][3][2]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 puts numbers behind the story. Five fiscal years show a full swing: the 2021 and 2022 boom, the 2023 to 2025 slowdown while TI spent heavily on new 300mm factories, and the rebound in 2026. The last-twelve-months column (TTM) is fiscal 2025 plus the first nine months of 2026 minus the first nine months of 2025, and it is our calculation from the 10-K and 10-Q.[3][2] Diluted earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the shares outstanding plus the extra shares that stock awards could create.

Two points stand out before the tables. First, profit and cash moved in opposite directions for three years: operating profit fell 46% from 2022 to 2024, while capital spending rose from $2.8 billion to $5.1 billion in 2023.[5][3] Second, cash flow has now turned. Over the last twelve months operating cash flow was $9,896 million and capex only $2,578 million.[1] That swing is the reason the ledger's cash-flow figure needed a correction, which the reverse DCF discussion in chapter 5 explains.

Shareholder returns and CHIPS Act cash complete the picture. Over the last twelve months TI paid $5,174 million of dividends and bought back $636 million of shares, against $1,611 million of buybacks a year earlier.[1] A buyback is a company purchase of its own shares. The dividend now runs at $1.52 a quarter, or $6.08 a year, which is the 23rd straight yearly increase and about 2.1% of the share price.[12] On 913.77 million shares the new rate costs about $5.56 billion a year, or 76% of the last twelve months' operating cash flow less capex (our calculation; the data vendor shows a 75.94% free cash flow payout).[2][17] The dividend is covered, but with less room than a 35% margin suggests: at $3 billion of capex and flat operating cash flow the payout would be about 81% (our arithmetic).

The CHIPS Act supports the plan in two ways. TI expects a 35% investment tax credit on qualifying U.S. factory spending and up to $1.6 billion of direct funding, of which $630 million has been received.[2] Cash benefits were $588 million in 2024, $670 million in 2025 and $1,484 million in the first nine months of 2026, and the balance sheet carries $2,136 million of incentives still to collect.[4][22][2] The 10-K for 2024 expected $7.5 billion to $9.5 billion through 2034, and management has said the amounts depend on U.S. capex, which TI is now cutting.[4][22] Beyond 2026, TI says capex will depend on revenue and growth expectations.[2]

5. What could happen next

A reverse DCF works backward from the price. A normal discounted cash flow model takes growth forecasts and produces a value; a reverse one takes the price and asks what yearly growth in cash flow, over ten years, would make that price fair at a 10% discount rate and an 18 times exit multiple. If the answer is higher than the business can plausibly deliver, the price is demanding. The session ledger printed 18.8% a year for TXN, and we tested it against TI's filings before using it.

The ledger started from a cash-flow figure of $4.874 billion. TI's filed numbers for the twelve months to September 2026 are $9.896 billion of operating cash flow and $2.578 billion of capex, which gives $7.318 billion.[1] That figure looks like one that still carried the heavier capex of the building years. With the filed figure and the ledger's own enterprise value of $265.62 billion, the implied growth falls from 18.8% to 13.4% a year (our calculation by the same method as the ledger). The rest of this chapter uses 13.4% and shows two cross-checks.

Our variant perception

The popular view has two halves. Where we differ from a simple read of consensus is the source of the 2027 earnings. Consensus asks for earnings per share to grow from $9.06 to $11.55 on revenue growth from $22.4 billion to $26.2 billion.[16] On about 918 million diluted shares and a 13.5% tax rate (TI guided 13% to 14% for 2027), that growth in profit implies roughly 69% of added revenue reaching pre-tax profit (our calculation).[13] TI's own range for added gross profit on added revenue is 75% to 85%, and depreciation and operating costs will absorb some of it, so the consensus step looks reasonable rather than aggressive. Our variant view is therefore not that 2027 earnings are too high or too low: it is that the stock is a fair price for a good recovery, and the margin for error sits in the multiple.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in chapter 5 has a weakest assumption, and a good decision names it before the money is committed. The bear case below argues against our own central view as hard as the evidence allows. It then ranks three ways the thesis could fail outright, names the one claim everything hangs on, and gives the date it will be known.

Fatal flaw 1: the upturn is a peak, not a recovery

Industrial revenue has only just regained its 2022 level, and TI's gross margin fell from 68.8% in 2022 to 57.0% in 2025.[5][3][13] If customers have been ordering ahead of real demand, a pause would hit revenue while depreciation stays at about $2.2 billion a year. In 2023 revenue fell 12.5% and gross margin dropped 5.9 points.[5] If 2027 revenue were flat at $22.4 billion rather than $26.2 billion, 2027 earnings per share would be near $9.1 on our fall-through arithmetic, and at the 10-year median P/E the stock would be about $218. Evidence to watch: lead times, pricing, and the fourth-quarter outlook. TI said lead times ticked up and that automotive customers hold little inventory, which argues against double ordering in that market, but the same data do not rule it out elsewhere.[13]

Fatal flaw 2: China turns a risk into a loss

China is 21% of revenue ($3,781 million in 2025), and about 50% of revenue comes from products shipped into China.[3] China opened an anti-dumping probe in September 2025 into certain U.S. analog chips, covering interface and gate driver chips made at 40 nanometers and above, where the applicant's preliminary evidence showed U.S. imports up 37% and prices down 52% from 2022 to 2024; it was due to finish by September 13, 2026, with a possible six-month extension, and we could not verify the outcome.[28] The 10-K also warns that China is reshaping its domestic chip industry with policy support, and it lists tariffs specific to the products TI sells among its trade risks.[3] The Silicon Labs purchase needs China's regulator to approve it and is expected to close in the first half of 2027.[2] A ruling with duties, or a block on the deal, would hurt both revenue and the use of the $7.5 billion of cash and debt.

Fatal flaw 3: capex returns before cash flow does

The free cash flow case rests on capex staying near $2 billion to $3 billion. The CEO said he wants the higher end for 2026, that tool lead times are lengthening, and that 2027 capex depends on how much growth TI wants to support, with a mid-teens growth plan needing more than a 10% plan.[13] TI's filing says capex beyond 2026 depends on revenue and growth expectations.[2] The factory shells are built, so TI can add tools quickly, but each added $1 billion lowers free cash flow by about $1.09 per share.[2] If 2027 capex returned to $4 billion to $5 billion, 2027 free cash flow would look like the 2025 pattern again, and the 2.8% yield would fall.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT with a conditional order: buy 5 shares of TXN at $283.84 or lower, only after a same-day check passes. Here is the logic in the order the rules apply. Layer 1 (financial floors) passes, and Layer 2 (operating KPIs) passes with one watch item. Layer 3, the entry plan, is where the verdict is set: the current market price (CMP) of $284.69 is 0.09 ATR above the 20-day average, which puts it in the best band of the entry ladder, but the reward-to-risk ratio at the close is 1.89, below the 2.0 floor for CORE_ALPHA. Under the rules an IMMEDIATE_BUY or ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP verdict needs the floor met at the current price, so the verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The ratio reaches 2.0 at about $283.91, so the order sits 0.3% below the close.

The order depends on the central target, and we say so plainly. The ratio of 2.01 at the zone top uses the central value of $326.06, and it clears the floor by less than 0.01. The probability-weighted ratio, which counts the bear case, is 1.41 at the close and 1.51 at the zone top, and it does not clear 2.0 at either price. A return to the 10-year median multiple of 23.99 on full consensus EPS gives $277.08, which is 2.67% below the close and 2.38% below the zone top; the ratio on that value is negative (-0.35 at the close, -0.32 at the zone top). We still do not reject the stock, because the business passes every floor and the central case needs only a multiple above about 24.65 times 2027 EPS. We do lower the size one tier because of the shortfall, as the sizing paragraph shows. China's decisions on the Silicon Labs deal and on U.S. analog chips are unverified, so they are a reason to check the calendar before any fill.

Reward-to-risk means the gain to the central target divided by the loss to the stop. The stop is $262.83, found with the core-alpha rule for a stock in a rising trend: one ATR ($9.18) below the lower of the 50-day average ($272.40) and the 20-day swing low ($272.01), which is the structural invalidation level, meaning the price at which the idea is shown to be wrong. The tactical rule, half an ATR below, would give $267.42 and a ratio of 2.40 at the close and 2.57 at the zone top. We use the core rule because TI is a core-quality business and because the tactical floor of 2.5 is not met either way. The support used for the entry is the 20-day average of $283.84; the stop uses lower levels because a stop placed at the entry support would be hit by ordinary daily moves.

The entry zone, the price range in which we would buy, runs from $272.40 (the 50-day average) to $283.84 (the 20-day average). At the zone top the stop distance is $21.01, or 7.40% of the zone top, and the ratio is 2.01. At the zone bottom the stop distance is $9.57, or 3.51% of the zone bottom, and the ratio is 5.61. Measured from the $284.69 close, the stop is 7.68% away.

The same-day check comes before every order. Reaching the zone never triggers a buy by itself. Each session, after the close of the previous one and before the order is placed, five things are confirmed. First, the price is inside the zone from $272.40 to $283.84. Second, the trend and the gate are rechecked: the close is above a rising 50-day average that is above a rising 200-day average, which keeps the chart out of Stage 4, and Test C still passes. Third, support, ATR, stop and size are recomputed from that day's data, and the central-target ratio is still at least 2.0 at the order price. Fourth, the event calendar is clear: no TI report within five trading days, and no announced ruling on the Silicon Labs deal or on China's trade actions against U.S. analog chips. Fifth, nothing has changed in the consensus 2027 EPS that moves the central target below the level that gives a 2.0 ratio. If any part fails, the order is cancelled for that session and nothing is bought.

Position size follows from risk units. Portfolio heat is the total amount the portfolio would lose if every stop were hit; the rules cap it at 12% of net worth. Risk-unit sizing sets each new position so that a stop-out costs a fixed number of basis points (bps; one bp is one hundredth of one percent) of net worth, with a ceiling that depends on the conviction tier. Tiers run from TIER_1 (the most conviction, 7% to 10% of net worth) to TIER_4 (1% to 2%), and TIER_0 means zero size. TXN is TIER_4: 1% to 2% of net worth and a 35 bps risk budget. TIER_3 would have been the yardstick for a case with a clean weighted ratio; we dropped one tier because the weighted ratio is under the floor and the median-multiple test loses money. The formula gives the lower of the tier ceiling (2%) and 35 bps divided by the 7.40% stop distance (4.73%), so the ceiling binds. With a net worth of $82,368.77 (from the 2026-10-23 positions replay) the ceiling is about $1,647, and 5 shares at $283.84 cost $1,419.20, or 1.72% of net worth. A stop-out then costs about $105, or 12.8 bps, against the 35 bps budget.

A stop does not cap a loss when a stock gaps. If TXN opened 15% below the zone top, at about $241.26, the 5 shares would lose about $213, or 25.8 bps, about three quarters of the budget; a fall to the bear value of $234.86 would cost about 30 bps. A stop-loss order turns into a market order when the price touches it, and it fills at the next available price, which can be well below the stop. The portfolio is already running at about 2.68 times market exposure, so we do not raise the size to chase the ratio.

The stress tests remove one variable at a time. If the exit multiple is 10% lower (25.41 instead of 28.23) with EPS unchanged, the central target becomes $293.45 and the ratio falls to 0.40 at the close and 0.46 at the zone top. If EPS is 10% lower at $10.40 with the multiple unchanged, the target is $293.45, which is the same figure. If the multiple falls to the 10-year median of 23.99 with EPS delivered, the value is $277.08, a loss of 2.67% from the close. A 10% miss on either input removes almost all of the reward, which is why the rule asks for a pullback rather than the market price.

Order details leave nothing open. Because a resting order cannot be checked before it fills, the order is a day order, placed fresh each session from Monday, October 26 through Friday, November 6, 2026, and only after the same-day check passes. Each order is to buy 5 shares at $283.84 or lower, in either the Schwab or Robinhood account, because the plan does not depend on which. If only part fills in a session, keep the shares that filled, cancel the rest, and size the stop to the filled quantity; the total bought across all sessions never exceeds 5 shares, and the limit is never raised. After any fill, place a stop at $262.83 for the filled shares, and treat a close below $262.83 as the signal to sell the whole position at the next open with a market order, not a limit order, so that a failed limit cannot leave exposure open. If a report date is confirmed and a sale is planned within two sessions of it, the sale becomes a market order placed in the last 30 minutes of the session before the report. If no fill happens by the close on November 6, the plan lapses and a fresh full review decides what comes next. Shares go ex-dividend on October 30, 2026, so the price will open about $1.52 lower that day, all else equal.[12][16]

TXN is not held, so there is no holding audit. The transaction logs for both accounts and the options book contain no TXN trades, no recent closed position and no TXN options, and the watchlist tag TACTICAL_MOMENTUM did not match what we found: the chart is at support but has not led the market over 63 days. If the stock rises, the let-winners-run rule applies. This rule says never to trim a rising Stage 2 stock only because RSI passes 70 or because its weight passes 15%, and to trail the stop up to the rising 21-day exponential moving average (EMA, an average that weights recent days more) or the 50-day simple moving average (SMA, a plain average of the last 50 closes) until the trend or the fundamentals break. Weight may run up to 22% on this rule; at 1.72% of net worth this is far away. Buying is not automatic beyond the first fill: any add needs a fresh full review.
