1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Stock Cycles

Core Investor Question: How did a 1930 oil exploration outfit invent the silicon age, survive brutal semiconductor commoditization cycles, and emerge as an analog monopoly?

The history of Texas Instruments Incorporated over the past nine decades is a study in strategic foresight and capital discipline. The enterprise was founded in 1930 during the Great Depression as Geophysical Service Inc. by Eugene McDermott, J. Erik Jonsson, Cecil Green, and Dr. Patrick Haggerty. It began as an acoustic signal exploration contractor mapping subsurface petroleum deposits for wildcat drillers. The founders applied reflection seismology principles to defense electronics during World War II. In doing so, the company laid the engineering foundation for its historic transition into commercial silicon.

In May 1954, Texas Instruments commercialized the world's first silicon transistor, replacing fragile germanium devices. Four years later, in September 1958, TI engineer Jack Kilby invented the integrated circuit in Dallas. This breakthrough earned him the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics. It laid the physical cornerstone of modern computing. Over the subsequent four decades, TI navigated consumer calculators, digital watches, and commodity DRAM memory chips. Management soon learned that commodity consumer and memory markets consumed enormous capital while delivering depressed through-cycle economic returns.

THE STRATEGIC GENIUS OF THE DRAM EXIT (1998)

In 1998, under CEO Tom Engibous, Texas Instruments sold its entire memory business to Micron Technology for $800 million. At the time, Wall Street questioned the move, as DRAM accounted for more than a third of company revenues.

However, DRAM was a commodity product defined by vicious boom-and-bust cycles driven by government-subsidized Asian rivals. By exiting memory, TI avoided decades of brutal capital destruction and redeployed that capital toward high-margin analog and signal processing architectures that yielded superior economic returns.
