1. What the company does

Kirby is the nation's largest domestic tank barge operator.[1] A tank barge is a floating steel tank that a towboat pushes along a river or canal. It carries liquids such as chemicals, heating oil and asphalt. At the end of the third quarter of 2026 the company had 1,148 active inland tank barges holding 25.7 million barrels, plus 27 coastal tank barges.[3] All of its vessels fly the US flag and qualify for domestic trade under the Jones Act, a federal law that reserves shipping between US ports for vessels built, owned and crewed in the United States.[1] At the end of 2025 the 1,105 inland barges were about 28% of all domestic inland tank barges, and one typical 15-barge tow on the lower Mississippi carries the cargo of roughly 216 railroad tank cars or 1,050 tank trucks.[1]

Kirby reports two segments. Marine transportation produced $1,935.3 million, or 58%, of 2025 revenue of $3,364.1 million, and distribution and services produced $1,428.7 million, or 42%.[1] Marine is the profit engine. It earned $374.5 million of operating income at a 19.3% margin in 2025, while distribution and services earned $130.8 million at a 9.2% margin, so marine supplied about three of every four dollars of segment profit.[1] The 2026 pattern is similar. In the third quarter marine revenue was $568.7 million at a 19.7% margin, and distribution and services was $408.5 million at an 11.9% margin.[3]

The marine segment sells time on a barge in two ways. A time charter is a daily rate for a barge and tug. A contract of affreightment is a set price to move a cargo from one point to another.[1] Agreements of a year or longer count as term contracts, and shorter ones are spot. In 2025 about 70% of inland revenue was term and 30% was spot.[1] In the third quarter of 2026 term contracts were about 65% of inland revenue, with 56% of that in time charters and 44% in contracts of affreightment, and about 95% of coastal revenue was term, almost all time charters.[5] By cargo, petrochemicals were 48% of 2025 marine revenue, black oil 26%, refined products 23% and agricultural chemicals 3%, and no single customer reached 10% of revenue.[1] Fuel is a pass-through cost in most contracts, but the escalator clauses lag: management says some lag by as much as 120 days.[5]

The distribution and services segment sells, rebuilds and services engines, transmissions, pumps and electrical equipment. In the third quarter power generation was about 46% of segment revenue, commercial and industrial about 44%, and oil and gas about 10%.[3] Management said on the call that the power generation backlog is north of $1.5 billion and probably two thirds behind-the-meter, meaning generators placed on the customer's side of the electric meter, for example at a data center that cannot wait for a grid connection.[5] Oil and gas matters less than it did: its revenue fell 32% in 2025.[1] Supply of engines from outside makers limits how fast the backlog turns into revenue.[5]

For an owner this is a cyclical, asset-heavy business with two engines. The barge market earns more when supply is tight and refineries and chemical plants run hard. The power equipment business grows as fast as engine makers can deliver. Net property and equipment was $4.18 billion at June 30, 2026 against trailing revenue of about $3.60 billion,[3][6] so each dollar of sales needs a lot of capital behind it. That is why Chapter 3 spends time on return on capital and free cash flow, and why the verdict rests on price as well as quality.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business is supply. A new clean 30,000-barrel inland barge costs about $4.6 million to $4.7 million, a black oil barge $5.8 million to $6.0 million, and a 10,000-barrel barge $2.5 million, and management says barge prices are still about 40% below the level that would justify building new ones.[5] Industry deliveries are about 65 barges this year, most of them replacements.[5] The 10-K counts roughly 600 industry tank barges that are 30 years old or older, about 350 of them 40 or older, against an industry average age of about 18 years, which points to steady retirements.[1] Kirby's own fleet averages 17.8 years.[1] Barges also win on cost: one ton moves 675 miles on a gallon of fuel by barge, 472 miles by rail and 151 miles by truck.[1]

The evidence from the cycle supports the story. Marine operating margin, the share of revenue left after running costs, rose from 13.9% in 2023 to 19.0% in 2024 and 19.3% in 2025, and the fleet was running in the low 90% utilization range in the third quarter of 2026.[1][3] But the cycle is not one-way. Inland utilization ran in the low to mid 90% range in the first half of 2025, then fell to the mid 80% range in the third quarter and the mid to high 80% range in the fourth, because feedstocks got lighter and fewer barges were in maintenance across the industry,[1] which shows that utilization can drop several points in a single quarter. The third quarter of 2026 also shows where the growth came from. Inland ton miles, the measure of cargo moved over distance, rose 19.6% to 4,183 million while revenue per ton mile rose only 1.9% to 11.0 cents, and the average number of towboats rose to 294 from 270.[3] Most of the 21% inland revenue growth was therefore volume and a bigger operating fleet, not higher prices.[5] What this means for an owner: the business is strong, but price gains are still to come, and they are the part of the story that has not yet been proven.

The weaknesses are cost, capital and lumpiness. Diesel cost $3.91 a gallon in the third quarter against $2.46 a year earlier, and management said fuel recovery through contract clauses has lagged, with an earlier belief that it was recovered proving wrong when fuel "did another tick up".[3][5] Skilled mariner wages and steel for shipyard work are also rising.[5] Return on capital is thin for the size of its assets: the aggregator's weighted average cost of capital for Kirby is 8.02%, against an ROIC of 8.6% to 8.9%, so the business earns only a little more than it costs to fund.[3][7] Distribution and services is lumpy because engine deliveries arrive in batches, and oil and gas revenue fell 32% in 2025 and another 9% in the third quarter of 2026.[1][3] Past accounting scars show the same asset risk: the company took $340.7 million of non-cash charges in 2021 on coastal equipment and goodwill, and a $56.3 million charge in 2024 on fracturing equipment inventory.[1][8]

On the market backdrop, the ledger marks the S&P 500 index fund as Stage 3, a topping or pullback phase, with a flat or falling 50-day average.[2] Kirby itself is Stage 2, the confirmed uptrend phase, and its 63-day return beat the index fund by 6.25 percentage points.[2] The stock spent months in a flat base, a sideways price floor from which a move can start. The top of that floor is the pivot, and the jump through it on earnings day is the breakout. The run instructions we were given describe the Industrials sector fund as Stage 4, the confirmed downtrend phase, in the Improving quadrant, which would make Kirby an exception to its sector. The ledger holds no Industrials fund series, so we could not verify that label ourselves. We therefore treat Kirby as a stock-specific story driven by refinery utilization, chemical volumes and data-center power demand, not as a sector call.[5]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number above rests on assumptions. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, the evidence that would show it wrong, and when we will know. It also gives the red-team bear case, the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion. We found three ways the case could fail badly; each is tied to a number we can check.

Fatal flaw 1: costs outrun price

The central case needs barge prices to rise faster than costs. In the third quarter diesel cost $3.91 a gallon against $2.46 a year earlier, up 59%, and management admitted that fuel recovery through contract clauses lags, with some clauses taking up to 120 days.[3][5] Skilled mariner wages and steel are also rising. Nine-month marine margin was 18.1% against 18.9% a year ago even though revenue was up 10.3%, which shows cost is already taking part of the price gain.[3] Third-quarter inland term renewals rose only in the low single digits.

If the fourth-quarter renewals, which cover 35% to 40% of the term book, also come in near inflation, the spread narrows in 2027 and our $8.05 becomes about $7, which is the bear case. The signs would be an inland margin below the high-teens to low-20% range the company guided for the year, and a weaker 2027 pricing comment. We will know at the fourth-quarter report; the date is not published, and the last two were on 2026-01-29 and 2025-01-30.[3][5][9][11]

Fatal flaw 2: utilization and the chemical cycle turn

Management lists strong reasons for tight barges: near-record refinery utilization, heavier Venezuelan crude, chemical plant closures in Europe, damage to Middle East infrastructure and continuing barge retirements.[5] Each of these can reverse. Utilization was in the mid 80% range in the third quarter of 2025 and the mid to high 80% range in the fourth, when feedstocks lightened and fewer barges were in maintenance, and 2025 earnings per share growth landed at the low end of the guided range.[1][11] If utilization falls five points, spot rates, now 10% to 15% above term, would fall below term and the 2027 renewal cycle would reprice down, not up.

What shows it early: utilization commentary below 90%, ton miles falling from the 4,183 million reached in the third quarter, spot rates turning down, and coastal margins staying in the mid-teens.[3][5] We will see it in each quarterly report and, in between, in management's comments on the 10-Q.
