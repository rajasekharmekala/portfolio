1. What the company does & day-to-day vertical operations

Arista sells Ethernet switches and routers. It sells EOS, network-management software and support. Cloud, AI, campus and routing customers use these systems. Arista reports one operating segment. It does not split revenue or profit by vertical. Q2 2026 revenue was $3.036B. Product sales were $2.605B, and services were $430.5M. Products were the larger sales line. Arista does not report hardware units or customer renewal rates.[1]

Product engineering starts with system design and EOS software. Arista selects silicon and optics, checks hardware and software together, tests releases and supports deployments. The Q2 release added 7060XE7 Etherlink systems for AI fabrics. Arista says the family supports 1.6 Tbps and up to 100 Tbps system bandwidth. These are product specifications. They do not show how many systems had shipped by October 30.[3]

Manufacturing is mostly outsourced. Contract manufacturers buy parts and assemble systems. Arista manages design, supplier relationships and customer programs. The filings say several parts come from limited or sole sources. Broadcom is its main merchant switching-silicon supplier. By June 30, Arista had $9.7B of non-cancellable purchase commitments. $9.4B was due within 12 months. Those are orders Arista owes suppliers, not orders from customers. They are not backlog or remaining performance obligations. A customer delay can leave Arista with inventory before revenue comes in.[1][2]

After assembly, customer teams agree on configuration, port speed, optics and software. Arista ships a system; the customer tests and accepts it under the contract. Service revenue includes support and related services. Large orders can move or be delayed. Acceptance clauses can also delay revenue. Deferred revenue was about $6.9B at June 30. It is not the same as backlog. It does not show when a product will ship or if more orders will follow.[1][4]

Arista does not report vertical-level margins, installed-base size, renewal rates, churn, port volumes or use rates. The one-segment report and services line are the available cuts. That makes it hard to measure software returns apart from hardware cycles. It also limits the view of AI sales beyond large buyers. I track reported revenue, margins, customer mix, purchases, stock, cash flow and management goals instead. I do not fill unreported unit economics.[1][2]

Cloud and core data-center switching

The sales process starts with a customer design and qualification. Arista assigns sales teams by territory, major account or vertical. Systems engineers provide pre-sales support to customers, integrators, OEMs and channel partners. As an operating inference, a cloud buyer qualifies switch capacity, port speed, optics and EOS against its network plan. The filing does not disclose the exact test plan or customer economics.[2]

After design, a system is built, shipped and tested by the customer. Many orders arrive through a partner after the end customer has placed an order; partners generally do not hold Arista inventory. The company says some new products require long trials and acceptance periods. Revenue is recognized when control of the promised product transfers, so a delayed test can push revenue into a later period. Accepted shipments matter more than launch counts.[2]

AI fabrics: qualifying and scaling the cluster

Management describes Arista 7800 systems as a spine for large AI networks. The Q2 call explains a fabric design in which MRC can spread one flow across paths. SRv6 tags the path for each packet, and congestion signals can move traffic away from a busy link. These are management descriptions of the design, not independent performance tests.[4]

The operating inference is that a customer qualifies capacity, traffic paths and recovery behavior before accepting a cluster build. The Q2 release says the new 7060XE7 family supports 1.6 Tbps and up to 100 Tbps of system bandwidth, but it gives no shipped-unit count by this cutoff. The filing says trials and acceptance clauses can defer revenue. That makes accepted revenue, margin and customer concentration the useful tests.[2][3][4]

Campus networks

The company sells into wired and wireless campus networks, but it does not report campus revenue as a separate segment. The CEO describes new logos and land-and-expand activity as growth sources. As an operating inference, a campus rollout qualifies site-edge capacity and wired or wireless access needs, then tests the configuration at the customer site. The supplied evidence does not establish Arista’s exact site rollout steps.[3][4]

A useful test is whether a campus win becomes reported revenue and support work, rather than a product announcement or customer count. The company does not disclose campus shipments, site counts, renewal rates or profit by vertical. Its FY2026 campus target remains a forecast until reported results are available.[1][2][4]

Routing and scale-across networks

Routing connects sites and carries traffic between data centers. Management calls edge routing and data-center interconnect adjacencies to the core switch business. Its Q2 call describes contextual routing, traffic engineering and SRv6 across distributed AI fabrics. As an operating inference, customer teams qualify link capacity, routing policy and failover across locations before acceptance.[4]

The call describes congestion, packet loss and physical failures as conditions the design should handle. Management says the system can recover very quickly, but the supplied evidence has no independent recovery benchmark. A deployment can still fail if links, software, component supply or customer acceptance do not meet requirements.[2][4]

EOS, CloudVision and post-sale support

Arista sells hardware that incorporates EOS software. The release describes a shared software stack for automation, analytics, security and network availability. The Q2 call says EOS supports the same SRv6 intelligence from AI fabrics through scale-across routing. The CloudVision product page could not be retrieved in this run, so its detailed daily workflow, customer use and revenue contribution are not independently established here.[2][3][4]

Post-contract support is a recurring service obligation. The 10-K says it includes technical support, hardware repair or replacement beyond warranty, bug fixes, patches and unspecified upgrades. Fees are deferred and recognized evenly over the support contract; support requests arise when customers need them. Q2 services revenue was $430.5M, but renewal rates and support margins are not disclosed.[1][2]

Where the workflow can break

The process can break at design, supply, trial or acceptance. A product may miss requirements, contain a defect, or arrive late when a key component is scarce. The 10-K says trials, acceptance clauses and returns can delay or reverse product revenue. Supplier commitments and inventory can remain on Arista’s balance sheet while customer demand changes. These risks make shipment and margin evidence more useful than a goal alone.[1][2]

Two end customers accounted for 42% of FY2025 revenue. The filing does not name them or report customer-level backlog and churn. The key question is whether AI and campus sales broaden demand while margins hold. The available filings do not give enough detail to answer that by product group.[2][1]

2. Competitive moat, industry structure & external learning hub

Arista sells a combined system: Ethernet platforms, EOS, management tools and support. Buyers value uptime, planned upgrades and clear network signals. A customer using one workflow across a large estate may face test costs and staff retraining when switching. That is a switching-cost inference, not proof that customers cannot use more than one vendor. Arista does not publish customer retention for these products.[2][11][12]

Arista faces established rivals. Its FY2025 filing names Cisco as the historic market leader. It also names Dell, Extreme, HPE, Huawei, Juniper, Nvidia and white-box vendors. The filing warns that rivals can bundle hardware and software after acquisitions. AI buyers can split work across vendors or choose a different network design. The company also lists price pressure, large-customer discounts and long sales cycles as risks. The filing gives no current market-share or win-rate series, so I make no share claim.[2][1]

The near-term product case is strongest in high-capacity Ethernet. The Q2 release introduced 1.6 Tbps Etherlink platforms and up to 100 Tbps system bandwidth. On the call, CEO Jayshree Ullal said, Our AI fabrics momentum with Etherlink switches now exceeds 100 cumulative customers. That supports an adoption story. It does not show customer revenue, shipment backlog or renewal economics. The test is whether sales grow while margin holds and the business wins beyond a few large accounts.[3][4]

Architecture and industry structure

Data-center decisions combine chips, port speed, optics, routing and network software. Arista's one-EOS positioning can reduce tool and training needs across supported systems. Cisco, HPE/Juniper, Dell, Extreme, Nvidia and white-box vendors compete through installed base, breadth, price or an AI stack. Buyers can also divide campus, WAN and AI work among vendors. That makes shipped systems, renewals and margin better tests than a product launch alone.[1][2]

Broadcom is a key supplier, which is a risk beside the software story. Arista relies on Broadcom for most merchant switching silicon. It also names limited component sources. A strong software layer cannot remove a silicon delay or a price increase. A rival may bundle silicon, systems and software. A white-box design may cut cost when a buyer has the staff to build and run it.[2]

Is the company reaching its potential & are the CEOs walking the talk?

The FY2026 plan is the clearest current test. In May, management projected $11.5B in revenue. On August 4, the CFO raised it to $12.6B and called it 40% growth. She said this was the third increase in 2026. The change suggests demand exceeded the prior view. It is not delivery. FY2026 actuals were not available by October 30. Q3 results were due November 3 after the close.[4]

A completed top-line promise can also be checked. At the February 12, 2024 Q4 call, CEO Jayshree Ullal recalled that Arista had first guided for 25% FY2023 revenue growth and finished at 33.8%, or $5.86B. The FY2025 10-K reports FY2023 revenue of $5.860B, consistent with that recap. The company exceeded the initial growth guide by 8.8 percentage points. This is one completed revenue win; it does not establish delivery of today’s AI and campus targets or a recovery in margins.[2][38]

The AI target has a customer-count signal but not a full revenue audit. The CEO said Etherlink customers topped 100, up from four to five customers in 2024. That shows wider adoption. It does not show revenue per customer or the share of the $3.5B AI goal already shipped. The right verdict is credible growth guidance with incomplete delivery evidence, not a completed target scorecard.[4]

Margin execution is the counterpoint. CFO Chantelle Breithaupt said, overall gross margin in Q2 was 63.4%, down from 65.6%. She linked the decline to customer mix and other costs. The quarter improved from Q1, but the year-over-year comparison remains lower. Margin must stay above the 62% lower end of the FY2026 range. That would test growth quality, but it would not fix valuation or the broken trend.[4]

The completed FY2023 guide was met with a beat, while the FY2026 revenue, AI and campus targets remain open. Revenue grew from $5.860B in FY2023 to $7.003B in FY2024 and $9.006B in FY2025. That record supports execution, but it does not prove a durable AI run rate or higher future margins. Compare the next reported results with the relevant target before treating guidance as delivered.[2][4]

6. Key risks, thesis breakers & bear case

The bear case is that a concentrated, capital-intensive supply chain is being valued as if high AI growth and premium margins can persist together. The company has strong cash and historical growth, but the market is exposed to customer timing, procurement commitments, component concentration and a price trend already below key averages.

Demand and customer concentration

Two end customers represented 42% of FY2025 revenue. A deferral, price concession, qualification delay or network-design change at either could move quarterly revenue and gross margin. The company does not disclose a customer-level AI pipeline or binding backlog that would allow investors to quantify that risk independently.[2]

AI customer count grew from the four-to-five initial customers discussed in 2024 to more than 100 cumulative Etherlink customers, according to the CEO. That adoption count is encouraging, but it says nothing by itself about how many customers have deployed at scale or the average revenue per customer. If those deployments are smaller than the revenue target assumes, the market may be capitalizing early interest as realized growth.[4]

Margin, supply and working capital

Q2 non-GAAP gross margin was 63.4%, versus 65.6% a year earlier, and management’s FY2026 range is 62%–64%. If customer mix, pricing or component costs keep margins near the low end, earnings will grow more slowly than revenue. Inventory stood at $2.535B at June 30, while non-cancellable purchase commitments were $9.7B. A product transition or demand pause could leave Arista carrying inventory and supply obligations through a weaker shipment window.[1][4]

Competitive and valuation reset

Cisco and a broad set of large systems vendors compete for data-center and campus business; the company also faces AI-specific systems and white-box options. Competition can bring price pressure even when total network spending rises. At 18.94x TTM EV/sales, a fall to the 13x base multiple in this dossier offsets 20% modeled FY2027 revenue growth. The bear case does not require a balance-sheet failure: a growth miss and multiple compression are enough to produce substantial equity downside.[2][8]

Technical breakdown and event risk

The October 30 close was 13.50% below the 50-day average and 4.17% below the 30-week average; the one-day decline was 8.34%. The close remained above the 200-day average, so this is not a unanimous Stage 4 classification. The stricter consumer screen is sufficient to bar fresh entry, especially with results due November 3 after the close. A single rebound could be short covering rather than trend repair.[8][10]
