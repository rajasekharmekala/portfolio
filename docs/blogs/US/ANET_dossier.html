1. What the company does

Arista Networks designs and sells the equipment and software that move data between computers inside a data center. Picture a data center as a very large warehouse of servers, and Arista's switches as the road system between them: every web request, every file and every AI training job crosses those roads. The switches speak Ethernet, the same standard that links office computers, and they all run one operating system, called EOS, that Arista writes itself on top of standard Linux.[2] The shares trade on the New York Stock Exchange under the symbol ANET, although the holdings file shows a Nasdaq code; the 10-K cover page names the New York Stock Exchange.[1]

The company's $9.006 billion of 2025 revenue came from three groups of buyers. Cloud and AI Titans, the largest cloud and AI companies, supplied about 48%. Enterprise and financial-services customers supplied 32%. AI and specialty providers, a group that includes Apple, Oracle and the newer AI-focused cloud companies called neoclouds, supplied 20%.[3][22] By product, the core data-center and AI switches were 65% of revenue, campus and routing products 18%, and software and subscription services 17%.[3] Product sales were 84.1% of revenue ($7,576.9 million) and service contracts were 15.9% ($1,428.8 million).[4] About 79% of sales were in the Americas.[3]

Two customers each bought more than 10% of the company's revenue. They accounted for 26% and 16% of 2025 revenue, a combined 42%, up from 35% in 2024 and 39% in 2023.[3] The 10-K does not name them. On earnings calls the chief executive has named Microsoft and Meta as her two longest partners, and she expects one or perhaps two more customers to pass 10% in 2026.[21][20][22] This is the central fact about the business: a handful of the world's largest companies buy most of the AI-related equipment, so Arista's growth follows their spending plans.

Arista does not build its own boxes. Contract manufacturers Jabil, Sanmina and Foxconn assemble the switches, and the main switching chips come from one supplier, Broadcom.[2] The company sells through its own sales force and through channel partners, and it earns service revenue from support contracts that often run for several years.[6] Large AI orders frequently carry a customer acceptance clause: the customer tests the equipment for six to eighteen months before it counts as delivered.[22] Until then the cash can arrive but the revenue waits in a liability called deferred revenue, which stood at $6.87 billion on June 30, 2026, up from $4.06 billion a year earlier.[13]

The AI part of the business has three jobs, which management calls scale-up, scale-out and scale-across. Scale-up links chips inside one rack and is still dominated by Nvidia's proprietary NVLink. Scale-out links racks to each other, where Ethernet is replacing the older InfiniBand standard. Scale-across links data centers that sit far apart because no single site has enough power.[2][20] Management says it passed its 2025 goal of $1.5 billion of AI center revenue and has set a 2026 goal of at least $3.5 billion, the figure in the finance chief's formal outlook; the chief executive once said $3.6 billion on the same August call, and we use $3.5 billion.[22][20] In June 2025 it also bought the VeloCloud branch-office networking business from Broadcom for $300.0 million in cash, to sell alongside its campus products.[8]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Ethernet: the common standard for sending data over cables between computers; Arista sells Ethernet switches.
  • Switch: a box that receives data from many cables and forwards each piece to the right cable.
  • EOS: Arista's own operating system, the software that runs every Arista switch.
  • Hyperscaler or cloud titan: one of the very largest cloud companies, such as Microsoft or Meta.
  • Neocloud: a newer cloud company built mainly to rent out AI computing power.
  • Scale-up, scale-out, scale-across: three ways to link AI chips, inside a rack, between racks, and between buildings.
  • InfiniBand: an older networking standard, sold mostly alongside Nvidia chips, that Ethernet is competing to replace in AI clusters.
  • White box: a low-cost generic switch that the customer loads with its own or open-source software.
  • Deferred revenue: money billed or collected before the sale counts as revenue, usually because the customer has not yet accepted the product.
  • Purchase commitments: binding orders placed with suppliers for parts the company has not yet received.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A strong position in a market is not the same as a safe one. Arista's position is strong because customers say they choose it for reliability and software, and because, according to management citing outside market researchers, it now holds the largest share of fast Ethernet switching.[21] The company reports a net promoter score of 89, which it says means 94% of customers are strongly positive,[15] and it counts more than 10,000 cumulative customers.[22] The research firm Gartner named it a Leader for enterprise wired and wireless networks in 2026.[14] These are company-reported claims. We treat them as evidence of how customers see Arista, not as audited measurements.

The technical argument is simple. Every Arista product runs the same software image, so a feature written once works on every box, and a switch can be upgraded without taking it offline. Management calls this Smart System Upgrade, and its engineering chief told investors in August that it lets customers patch security holes “without sacrificing even a single minute” of expensive chip time.[20][2] For a customer that has spent billions on AI chips, an idle chip costs far more than a switch, which is why reliability earns Arista a higher price than a generic box.

Who competes with Arista, and how

The 10-K names Cisco as the historical leader in data-center and campus networking, with Dell, Extreme, Hewlett Packard Enterprise (HPE), Huawei, Juniper, Nvidia and white-box vendors as other rivals.[2] Cisco is not shrinking. In the quarter ended July 25, 2026, Cisco's networking product revenue rose 28% to $9.79 billion, and it reported $9.3 billion of AI infrastructure orders from hyperscalers in fiscal 2026, against about $4 billion of revenue delivered, with $7.5 billion of revenue expected in fiscal 2027.[33] The market is growing fast enough for several winners. It also means the incumbent is winning large AI orders from the same buyers Arista needs.

HPE completed its purchase of Juniper, and the 10-K lists that as a competitive change.[2] HPE's networking segment sold $2.9 billion in its latest quarter, up 74.9%, with data-center networking up 112.2% to $382 million; the release does not separate purchased growth from organic growth, so those rates may overstate the underlying pace.[36] Broadcom, which bought VMware, is both Arista's main chip supplier and, because its chips can also power white-box switches, an indirect competitor.[2] Nvidia sells networking as part of its AI systems. Its Compute & Networking segment had $88.3 billion of revenue in the July 2026 quarter, but it does not report networking on its own, so we cannot size the overlap.[34]

In AI the rivals are technologies as well as companies. The 10-K says Ethernet faces competition from InfiniBand and NVLink in AI back-end clusters, both usually sold with Nvidia chips.[2] The chief executive told analysts in August, “we live in an NVIDIA world”: where Nvidia supplies the whole stack, Arista has little part in scale-up, and she compared the coming shift to the move from InfiniBand to Ethernet, which took two to three years.[20] No source we can cite publishes the split of AI back-end ports between Ethernet and InfiniBand, so we put no number on it. White-box switches, generic hardware with open-source software, are a cheaper option. Management calls them “a tactical solution” for simple designs and says most neoclouds lack large networking teams, but the 10-K warns that white box could push prices down.[20][2]

Where the business is weak

Three weak points matter. First, two customers produced 42% of 2025 revenue, so one customer's budget cut would show up quickly in Arista's results.[3] Second, the company depends on Broadcom for its main chips, on three contract manufacturers for assembly, and on a memory market that the 10-K says is tight and is pressuring gross margin, the share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of making the product.[2][3] The chief executive told investors the industry has “a 2-year problem” on supply that she does not expect to clear until 2028.[20] Third, revenue is lumpy: large AI deployments can sit in acceptance windows of six to eighteen months, and the finance chief said the deferred balance can move “independent of underlying business drivers.”[22][20]

Gross margin has slipped. On the standard accounting (GAAP) basis it was 64.1% in 2025 and 62.9% in the June 2026 quarter, against 65.2% a year earlier.[3][14] The 10-K ties part of the pressure to sales to the largest customers, who receive volume discounts, and to supplier costs for memory and chips.[3] A business that keeps 63 cents of each dollar is still highly profitable, but the direction matters because today's valuation assumes the margin holds.

What this means for an owner: the business is strong on product quality and financial health and weak on dependence. The company depends on few buyers, one chip supplier and a supply chain that is tight until at least 2028. Chapter 2B scores that mix one measure at a time.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue grew from $2.948 billion in 2021 to $9.006 billion in 2025, a compound rate of 32% a year, and the twelve months to June 30, 2026 reached $10.54 billion.[10][4][12] Operating margin, the share of revenue left after all running costs, rose from 31.4% to 42.8% over the same four years and reached 43.1% in the latest twelve months.[10][4][12] Net margin is profit after every cost as a share of sales, and it rose from 28.5% to 39.0%. Three groups of terms need plain definitions before the tests. The Weinstein stage scale names the phase of a stock: Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend, Stage 3 is a topping or pullback phase, and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend. A base is a flat stretch of trading that comes before a move. The pivot is the top of that stretch, and a breakout is a move above it on heavy volume. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the gain to a target divided by the loss to the stop. A measured move projects the height of a base above the pivot as a target.
