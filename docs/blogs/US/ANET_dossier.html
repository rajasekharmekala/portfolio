1. What the company does

Arista makes the boxes that move data between servers. A data center holds thousands of servers, and each one must talk to the others at very high speed. Arista's Ethernet switches do that job, and one operating system, called EOS, runs across the whole product line. In 2025 the company sold $7,576.9 million of products and $1,428.8 million of support services, for total revenue of $9,005.7 million, up 28.6% from $7,003.1 million in 2024[1]. Think of Arista as the interchange builder for AI traffic: the chips do the thinking, and Arista's switches decide how fast the chips can share what they know.

Three groups of customers pay the bills. Cloud and AI titans, the giant cloud and AI companies, supplied 48% of 2025 revenue. Enterprise and financial companies supplied 32%. A third group called AI and specialty providers supplied 20%, and the company says it includes Apple, Oracle and newer GPU-rental clouds known as neoclouds[11]. Two end customers each passed the 10% mark: one bought 26% of 2025 revenue and the other 16%[1]. The chief executive calls Microsoft and Meta Arista's two longest partners[9]. Customers this large buy in big, lumpy orders, so one quarter can look very different from the next.

Arista splits its sales into three product groups. Core cloud, AI and data center switches made up 65% of 2025 revenue. Campus and routing products, which the company calls adjacencies, made up 18%. Network software and subscription services made up 17%[11]. The company also describes AI networking in three zones. Scale-up links chips inside one rack, scale-out links racks inside one cluster, and scale-across links separate data centers. Management sizes the scale-across market at $15 billion to $20 billion by 2030[9]. More than 100 customers now use its Etherlink AI switches, up from four or five in 2024[9].

Arista does not build its own chips or run its own factories. It buys switching chips, mainly from Broadcom[1], and contract manufacturers assemble the products. On many large AI contracts, revenue waits until the customer accepts the product, so cash arrives first and revenue follows later. That is why deferred revenue, which is cash billed for products or services not yet counted as sales, stood at $6,865.9 million on June 30, 2026[2]. It also explains why the company has promised its suppliers $9.7 billion of purchases that it cannot cancel[2]. The two numbers matter together: the first is what customers have prepaid, and the second is what Arista owes its suppliers.

The newest chapter is speed. Second-quarter 2026 revenue was $3,035.7 million, the first quarter above $3 billion, and it rose 37.7% from a year earlier[2][5]. The company introduced 1.6 terabit switches in the quarter. They will be in trials in the second half of 2026 with very large customers, and the chief executive says real production comes in 2027[9]. A report on the third quarter arrives on November 3, 2026, after the market closes[21]. The chapters that follow ask whether the business deserves the price, and what the shares and the call you own should do before that date.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Arista's main strength is a product that is hard to copy at its quality level. EOS is one program that runs unchanged from a small campus switch to the largest AI spine, so a customer's staff learn one system. The company reports a customer satisfaction score of 89 for 2026, which it says means 94% of customers are strongly positive[6]. Operating margin tells the same story. GAAP operating margin was 45.4% in the second quarter of 2026, against 44.7% a year earlier, even as revenue rose 37.7%[5]. A business that grows this fast and still widens its margin has pricing power or very good cost control.

The second strength is position in AI. Management says more than 100 customers now use Etherlink AI switches, and it raised its 2026 target for AI networking revenue from $2.75 billion to $3.25 billion in February and then to at least $3.5 billion by May[11][10][9]. It also says it beat the 2025 goals of $1.5 billion in AI networking and $800 million in campus[11]. The company's annual report says the industry move from InfiniBand to Ethernet is an opening for share gains, and it names Cisco, Dell, Extreme, Hewlett Packard Enterprise, Huawei, Juniper and NVIDIA as rivals[1].

The weaknesses are real. The first is margin. Product gross margin, which is product revenue minus product cost, divided by product revenue, was 59.8% in the second quarter of 2026, using $2,605.2 million of product revenue and $1,047.5 million of product cost. A year earlier it was 62.3%, using $1,877.0 million and $707.3 million[2]. The company blames large customers, who generally receive higher discounts, and it cites tight memory and silicon supply[2]. The chief executive says the industry faces what she calls a two-year problem that will not clear until 2028[9]. The chief financial officer adds that Arista is not looking to accrete margin, which means it will use price increases to protect volume, not to raise profit per unit[17].

The second weakness is concentration. Two customers supplied 26% and 16% of 2025 revenue, which is 42% together. The 2024 figures were 20% and 15%, and the 2023 figures were 18% and 21%[1]. In February management flagged a chance of one or two additional 10% customers in 2026[11], and in August it again said it expected one, maybe two[9]. That would widen the customer list at the top, but it would also put a larger share of sales behind a handful of buyers, each of which controls its own spending plan and can pause it.

How strong is the Ethernet case against NVIDIA?

Arista says it works with NVIDIA, and it calls today's market an NVIDIA world for the processors it connects. Where NVIDIA sells the whole stack over its own NVLink links, the chief executive says there is very little participation from Arista or anybody else in scale-up[9]. Where customers use AMD chips, Google's TPUs or their own chips, she expects Arista to work on both scale-up and scale-out and to build custom racks. She compares the shift away from NVLink to the earlier move from InfiniBand to Ethernet, which took two to three years[9]. That is an opening, not a result: the company gives no scale-up revenue figure. For an owner, this means 2027 and later growth depends on products that are not yet in volume.

How much does the white box threat matter?

A white box switch is a basic box that a customer pairs with free software. The chief executive calls it a tactical solution that fits simple designs. She says customers who value operational excellence and cannot hire large network teams pick EOS[9]. Peer growth rates show why the question matters. Celestica, which builds switches for large customers, reported revenue up 62% in its second quarter, and its Hardware Platform Solutions unit grew about 58%[25][26]. Arista's 37.7% growth is slower than that, so share loss is not visible in Arista's sales. It would show up first in gross margin, which is why margin is a scored measure in chapter 2B.

What the peers say about share

Cisco said it took $4 billion of AI infrastructure orders from large cloud customers in its latest quarter, and it expects $7.5 billion of such revenue in fiscal 2027[23]. HPE reported networking revenue up 74.9% to $2.9 billion, but that figure includes Juniper Networks, which HPE bought, so it is not a like-for-like gain[27][37]. Broadcom, Arista's chip supplier, grew AI semiconductor revenue 221% to $16.7 billion in its latest quarter[24]. Taken together, the peers show that AI networking demand is large and that several rivals are growing quickly. The evidence does not show Arista losing share, but it does show that Arista is one of several winners, not the only one. That is why we score concentration and margin in the next chapter.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 shows what Arista earns, what cash it keeps and what it owes, over five years. Revenue grew from $2,948.0 million in 2021 to $9,005.7 million in 2025[3][1]. Growth was uneven: 48.6% in 2022, 33.8% in 2023, 19.5% in 2024 and 28.6% in 2025, then 37.7% in the second quarter of 2026 against a year earlier[3][1][5].

Profit grew faster than sales. GAAP operating margin rose from 31.4% in 2021 to 42.8% in 2025 and 43.1% over the twelve months to June 2026. Net income rose from $840.9 million to $3,511.4 million[3][1]. Arista split its stock four for one in December 2024, so earnings per share are shown split-adjusted: $0.66 in 2021 and $2.75 in 2025[3][1]. The company guides to a different measure, which adds back share-based pay and other items. Analysts' earnings numbers in chapter 5 use that adjusted measure, and the tables here use GAAP.

Part of that prepayment is offset by a cost. When Arista ships a product it cannot yet count as revenue, it also parks the product's cost on the balance sheet as deferred cost of goods sold. Hard red lines come first: a distressed balance sheet, a fraud or going-concern warning, or a Stage 4 downtrend with no base. Stage 4 is the phase of a long and confirmed decline. A base is a sideways shelf where a falling stock stops and rests, and a breakout is a move up out of that shelf. The entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy, and the stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the potential gain divided by the potential loss. Most of it arrives on one date, November 3, 2026, when Arista reports the third quarter after the close, and in the days before it, when the three largest cloud companies update their spending plans.

Weakest assumption behind the reverse DCF

We assume the filing-based free cash flow of $5,155.3 million is repeatable. Deferred revenue supplied $2,804.1 million of it in the last twelve months, though part of that was offset by $424.9 million of deferred cost in the first half of 2026 alone[1][2].
