1. What the company does

Arista makes the boxes that move data between servers. A data center holds thousands of servers, and every one must talk to the others at very high speed. Arista's Ethernet switches do that job, and its operating system, called EOS, runs across its whole product line.[1] In 2025 the company sold $7,576.9 million of products and $1,428.8 million of support services, for total revenue of $9,005.7 million, up 28.6% from $7,003.1 million in 2024.[1] Think of Arista as the road builder for AI: the chips do the thinking, and Arista's switches decide how fast the chips can share what they know.

Three groups of customers pay the bills. Cloud and AI titans, the giant cloud and AI companies, supplied 48% of 2025 revenue. Enterprise and financial companies supplied 32%. AI and specialty providers, which include Apple, Oracle and newer GPU-rental clouds called neoclouds, supplied 20%.[11] Two end customers each passed the 10% mark: one bought 26% of 2025 revenue and the other 16%.[1] The company named Meta Platforms and Microsoft as its two 10% customers in its 2023 annual report,[3] and the chief executive still calls Microsoft and Meta Arista's two longest partners.[9] Customers this large buy in big, lumpy orders, so one quarter can look very different from the next.

Arista splits its sales into three product groups. Core cloud, AI and data center switches made up 65% of 2025 revenue. Campus and routing products, the "adjacencies", made up 18%. Network software and subscription services made up 17%.[11] The company describes AI networking in three zones: scale-up (links inside one rack, mostly proprietary today), scale-out (links across racks in one cluster) and scale-across (links between separate data centers).[1] Management wants Ethernet to replace the older InfiniBand technology in scale-out, and it hopes the open ESUN standard will open up scale-up over time.[1]

Arista does not build its own chips or run its own factories. It buys switching chips, mainly from Broadcom,[1] and third-party contract manufacturers assemble the products.[2] On many large AI contracts, revenue waits until the customer accepts the product. Cash arrives first and revenue follows later. That timing is why deferred revenue (billed but not yet recognized as revenue) reached $6.9 billion at June 30, 2026.[9] The next paragraphs use five measures. Gross margin is the share of sales left after the direct cost of making and delivering the product. Operating margin is the share left after running costs such as research and sales. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the share count. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash from operations minus spending on equipment and buildings. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is the cost of paying staff in shares.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Arista's main strength is a product that is hard to copy at its quality level. EOS is one software program that runs unchanged from a small campus switch to the biggest AI spine, so a customer's staff learn one system.[1] The company reports a customer satisfaction score of 89 in 2026, which it says means 94% of customers are strongly positive.[6] Operating margin tells the same story: GAAP operating margin was 45.4% in the second quarter of 2026, against 44.7% a year earlier, even as revenue rose 37.7%.[5] A business that grows this fast and still widens its margin has pricing power or very good cost control.

The second strength is position in AI. Management says more than 100 customers now use its Etherlink AI switches, up from four or five in 2024.[9] It beat its 2025 goals of $1.5 billion in AI networking and $800 million in campus,[11] and it has raised its 2026 AI goal from $2.75 billion to $3.25 billion to at least $3.5 billion.[11][9] The industry is also moving its way: the annual report says the shift from InfiniBand to Ethernet in scale-out clusters "creates an opportunity for us to gain share".[1]

The weaknesses are real. Competition is heavy: the annual report names Cisco, Dell, Extreme, Hewlett Packard Enterprise, Huawei, Juniper, NVIDIA and white box makers, and it warns that Ethernet faces InfiniBand and NVLink in AI clusters.[1] Broadcom is Arista's chief chip supplier, and the annual report also lists Broadcom's VMware purchase among the deals competitors have made.[1] In scale-up, NVIDIA often supplies the whole stack, and the chief executive says "there's very little participation from Arista or anybody else's scale up" in that case.[9] She compares it to the move from InfiniBand to Ethernet, which took two to three years, and says the shift away from NVLink "will take time".[9]

Margins are the second weak spot. Product gross margin (product revenue minus product cost, divided by product revenue) was 60.7% in 2025, using $7,576.9 million of product revenue and $2,978.7 million of product cost.[1] It was 59.8% in the second quarter of 2026, against 62.3% a year earlier, using $2,605.2 million and $1,047.5 million, then $1,877.0 million and $707.3 million.[2] The company blames large customers, who "generally receive higher discounts".[2] It also cites tight memory and silicon supply.[2] The chief executive says the industry faces "a 2-year problem" that will not clear until 2028.[9]

How strong is the Ethernet case against NVIDIA?

Arista says it interoperates with NVIDIA and calls the networking world an "NVIDIA world" for the GPUs it connects.[9] Where customers use AMD chips, Google TPUs or their own chips, Arista expects to work on both scale-up and scale-out and to build custom racks.[9] That is a real opening, but it is a plan, not a result: the company gives no scale-up revenue figure. The 1.6 terabit switches will be in trials in the second half of 2026, with production in 2027, and only a few very large customers are trialing them.[9] For you as an owner, this means the growth in 2027 and later depends on products that are not yet in volume.

How much does the white box threat matter?

A white box switch is a basic box that a customer pairs with free software. The chief executive calls it "a tactical solution" that fits simple designs, and says customers who value "operational excellence" and cannot hire large network teams pick Arista's EOS.[9] The Celestica results show the other side: Celestica reports Hardware Platform Solutions revenue of about $1.9 billion, up 58%, and lists 800G and 1.6T networking among its programs.[27][26] Arista's 37.7% growth beats Cisco's 18% but trails Celestica's 62%,[23][26] so share loss is not obvious in its sales. It would show up first in gross margin, which is why that measure is a scored KPI in chapter 2B.

The seed record for this name claimed 32% ROIC (return on invested capital, the profit earned on each dollar put into the business), a 39.3% free cash flow margin, $8.6 billion of net cash and a 27.2% five-year revenue growth rate. Revenue grew from $2,948.0 million in 2021 to $9,005.7 million in 2025.[3][1] Using 2020 revenue of $2,317.5 million,[4] that is a five-year compound growth rate of 31.2% a year, a four-year rate of 32.2%, and a three-year rate of 27.1%. Profit grew faster than sales. Operating margin rose from 31.4% in 2021 to 42.8% in 2025, and net income rose from $840.9 million to $3,511.4 million. The 2025 cash flow needs one caution. Under acceptance terms, customers were billed ahead of revenue, and deferred revenue added $2,452.0 million to operating cash flow in 2025, which is 56% of the $4,371.9 million total.[1] In the first half of 2026 it added another $1,493.5 million, against $1,141.4 million a year earlier.[2] Management says this balance can "move significantly on a quarterly basis, independent of underlying business drivers".[9] The balance sheet has no debt and $13,343.3 million of cash and securities at June 30, 2026.[2]

The number

We would add shares only if the stock falls into a band from $161.13 to $168.25, and we would sell everything if it closes below $148.26.

How it was calculated

The top of the band is the 20-day swing low of $168.25, which is the lowest price of the last 20 sessions and the nearest verified support. The bottom is the 63-day base low of $156.84 plus half an average true range (the base low is the lowest price of the last 63 sessions). The stop is the base low minus one average true range. The average true range (ATR) is the average daily price swing, now $8.58, or about 5% of the price.

The inputs

The close of $172.37, swing low of $168.25, base low of $156.84, ATR of $8.58 and 200-day average of $163.77 come from the session price data for October 23, 2026. The 200-day average sits inside the band, which adds a second reason to expect buyers there. Rule used: for a core holding in a Stage 3 base, the stop goes one ATR under the base low, giving 156.84 minus 8.58, or $148.26.

The range

A tighter stop under the 20-day swing low, 168.25 minus 8.58, would be $159.67 and would lift the reward-to-loss ratio to 5.25 at $168.25. We reject it because one ordinary 5% day would trigger it. At the top of the band the stop is 11.88% away and the ratio is 2.25. At the bottom the stop is 7.99% away and the ratio is 4.05. At today's price the stop is 13.99% away and the ratio is 1.70.

If the number is off

If $168.25 fails, the next support is the base low of $156.84, and the band would need to be rebuilt lower. A close below $148.26 would mean the base has failed and the stock is moving toward a Stage 4 downtrend, which is the phase of a long and confirmed decline. That is the point at which the position is sold.

Portfolio heat is the share of your net worth you would lose if every stop were hit at once. A tier is a risk class that sets how large a position may be and how much of your net worth you may lose if its stop is hit. Arista is TIER_2 because it is a high-quality business with real risks, mainly two customers at 42% of sales and a falling gross margin. TIER_2 allows 4% to 6.5% of net worth in one name and a loss budget of 75 basis points, where a basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so 75 basis points is 0.75%. On your net worth of $82,376.82, the 6.5% ceiling is $5,354.49 and the 75 basis point budget is $617.83. CORE_ALPHA is the portfolio bucket for long-term compounders held for years, as opposed to short-term momentum trades.

Risk-unit sizing sets each position from its stop instead of from a round number. The weight in percent equals the loss budget in basis points divided by the stop distance in percent. At an entry of $168.25 and a stop of $148.26 the stop distance is 11.88%, so the weight is 0.75 divided by 11.88%, which is 6.31% of net worth, under the TIER_2 ceiling. A wider stop automatically gets a smaller position. That is the sizing rule.

Two chart terms matter for what comes next. A simple moving average (SMA) averages closing prices over a set number of days, and an exponential moving average (EMA) gives more weight to recent days. The let-winners-run rule says you should not trim a rising stock only because its RSI passes 70 or because its weight passes 15%. You may let the weight reach 22% while the stop trails the rising 21-day EMA or the 50-day SMA. The rule does not apply today, because the position is below cost and the stock is not in a Stage 2 advance. Structural invalidation is a price that proves the reason for owning the stock wrong. For Arista it is $148.26, one average true range under the 63-day base low. A close below it ends the case.
