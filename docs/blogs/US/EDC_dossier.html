1. What the company does

EDC is a fund, not a company. It is the Direxion Daily MSCI Emerging Markets Bull 3X ETF, an exchange-traded fund (a basket product whose shares trade on a stock exchange all day, like a share) that began trading on December 17, 2008 on NYSE Arca.[2] Its stated aim is 300% of the daily performance of the MSCI Emerging Markets Index, before fees and expenses.[1] Rafferty Asset Management, LLC is the investment adviser, and Paul Brigandi has been a portfolio manager since the start.[1] The 2026 summary prospectus calls the fund an ETF, formerly the Direxion Daily MSCI Emerging Markets Bull 3X Shares, and the watchlist still carries the old name.[1]

The word daily is the whole story. At the close of each trading day, Rafferty rebalances the fund so that its exposure to the index is again three times its net assets.[1] If the index rose that day, the fund has more money and must add exposure; if the index fell, the fund must cut exposure.[1] Over any stretch longer than one day, the result is each day's gain or loss multiplied together, which the prospectus says will very likely differ from 300% of the index's return.[1] Its table says a flat index with 25% volatility (volatility is the typical size of daily price swings, stated as a yearly percentage) costs the fund 17.1% over one year, and 100% volatility costs 95%.[1]

EDC gets its exposure mainly through swaps. A swap is a private contract in which a bank pays the fund the return of an asset and the fund pays the bank a financing charge.[1] The October 23 holdings file lists six swap lines that all point at shares of EEM, the iShares MSCI Emerging Markets ETF, plus 159,148 EEM shares held directly.[4] The six lines cover 5,714,992 EEM shares (our sum), and the file's own percentages for them add to 291.9% of net assets; the direct EEM holding adds 8.13%, for 300.0% in all (our calculation).[4] Net assets were about $120.9 million (our calculation: 2,031,598 shares on the holdings file times the October 22 NAV of $59.49).[4][2] At the October 31, 2025 year end the swaps were 292.8% of net assets and at April 30, 2026 they were 296.0%.[5][6]

A buyer pays in three ways. The stated fee is 1.12% before the sponsor's waiver and 1.09% after it, and 0.95% of that is the part the sponsor capped through September 1, 2027; the other 0.14% is the fees of funds EDC itself holds.[1][3] The second cost is the financing charge on the two extra dollars of exposure for each dollar invested. It is paid inside the swaps, so it shows up in returns and not in the fee line, and the filings we could reach do not give EDC's spread over the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR, the overnight US bank funding rate, 3.90% on October 22, 2026).[23] The third cost is volatility decay, which chapter 3 measures. In return the fund paid four quarterly distributions that add to $1.0973 per share (our sum).[2]

How a daily-reset fund behaves, in plain numbers

Take a simple case. The index rises 10% on one day and falls 10% the next. The index ends the two days at 0.99 of where it started, a loss of 1%. A fund that gives three times each daily move rises 30% and then falls 30%, which leaves it at 0.91, a loss of 9% (our arithmetic). Nothing went wrong with the fund. The fund gains 30% on day one, so day two's 30% loss falls on a larger amount of money, and that is volatility decay.

The loss grows with volatility. The prospectus table, which assumes no fees and no financing, shows a flat index costing the fund 3.0% a year at 10% volatility, 17.1% at 25%, 52.8% at 50% and 95.0% at 100%.[1] Chapter 3 checks that those numbers follow one formula and then applies it to what emerging markets actually did.

Splits, closures and the sponsor's record

Leveraged funds that lose value over time often reverse-split, which swaps several old shares for one new share at a higher price and changes the price without changing the investment. EDC did a 4-for-1 split on May 5, 2010, a 1-for-5 reverse split on November 10, 2011, a 3-for-1 split on April 2, 2013 and a 1-for-4 reverse split on March 24, 2016.[9] Every price in this book is split-adjusted, and none of those events falls inside the five-year window we measure. The sponsor reverse-split the bear fund, EDZ, 1-for-10 after the close on September 26, 2025.[7]

On March 13, 2026 Direxion announced that it would close ten funds on April 10, 2026 because they could not attract enough assets.[8][28] The ten tickers were FRDU, FRDD, BOED, BRKD, ELIS, LMTS, SHPD, XOMZ, LMBO and REKT, so EDC is not among them.[8] That notice shows the sponsor does close funds that stay small, so chapter 2 compares EDC's size with that standard.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Leveraged ETF: a fund that aims for a multiple of one day's move in an index, here three times.
  • Daily reset: the fund restores its 300% exposure at each close, so gains and losses compound along the path.
  • Swap: a private contract that pays one side the return of an asset in exchange for a fee; the other side is the counterparty.
  • Volatility decay: the loss a daily-reset fund suffers when the index rises and falls repeatedly, even if it ends where it started.
  • NAV: net asset value, the per-share value of everything the fund owns minus what it owes.
  • SOFR: the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, the overnight US dollar borrowing rate that swap financing is priced from.
  • Slope: how many points the fund moves for each point the index moves; a perfect 3x fund has a slope of 3.0 against its index.
  • ATR: the average true range, the typical size of one day's price swing in dollars, used here to place stops.
  • RSI: the relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast the price has moved; under 30 is called oversold.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A fund has no moat of its own, so its competitive position is a matter of price, size and safety against funds that sell the same exposure. The index it follows is cheap on one measure and narrow in what drives it. The MSCI Emerging Markets Index held 1,165 companies across 24 countries on September 30, 2026, but information technology was 43.78% of it and three chip names, Taiwan Semiconductor at 15.63%, Samsung Electronics at 7.55% and SK Hynix at 5.90%, were 29.08% together (our sum).[10] The ten largest holdings made up 39.03%.[10]

How EDC compares with its rivals

The same exposure can be bought for much less. VWO charges 0.06% and holds $123.19 billion, IEMG charges 0.09% and holds $153.18 billion, and EEM charges 0.72% and holds $28.69 billion.[17][18][13] The only leveraged rival on the same index that we found is EET, which targets twice the daily move, charges 0.95% and holds $34.07 million.[16] EDC charges 1.09% and holds about $120.9 million (our calculation).[1][4][2] The sponsor's bear fund on the same index, EDZ, holds $24.87 million.[20]

Counterparties, closure and sponsor risk

If a bank fails to pay what a swap owes, the fund can lose that amount, and the prospectus warns that the fund may use a limited number of counterparties.[1] It adds that a swap agreement may let the bank close the contract immediately if the index moves sharply in one day, and that the fund may then be unable to replace the exposure.[1] Under conditions where leverage is expensive or unavailable, the fund may reduce its leverage or close.[1]

The holdings file labels the six swap lines with codes that end in BPL, JPL, UBL, MLL, CTL and BCL, and it gives no bank names.[4] The annual and semi-annual reports we could reach carry swap schedules for other funds in the trust and not for EDC, and the filing list shows no other annual report for the trust.[5][6][27] So we cannot say which banks stand behind EDC, how much each owes it or what financing spread it pays, and that gap lowers our confidence in the safety rating. The reports do show the swaps at 292.8% of net assets at October 31, 2025 and 296.0% at April 30, 2026.[5][6]

Closure risk is mostly about size. At $118.2 million at the last year end, $197.8 million at April 30 and about $120.9 million now, EDC has stayed above the $100 million line we use in chapter 2B, but it has not grown.[5][6] The sponsor closed ten funds in April 2026 for lack of assets.[8] FINRA, the brokerage industry regulator, has said that funds reset daily are typically unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them longer than one trading session.[22] We treat EDC only as a short-term trading satellite.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in chapter 5 rests on a weakest assumption, and chapter 6 names it, says what evidence would show it wrong, and gives the date by which we would know. The central value assumes the index drifts at its five-year average of 8.94% a year, which is not a forecast: the index returned 23.38% in the nine months to September 30, 2026 and -20.09% in 2022.[10] The bull value assumes volatility stays near 25%, and a calmer market would help the fund more than our formula credits. The bear value assumes a one-standard-deviation quarter, which understates the fund's worst days.

Here is the bear case in plain words: the downside of owning EDC is not the 13-week bear value of $40.05 but the speed at which it can arrive. In the last five years EDC fell 10% or more in a single session 14 times and 15% or more 3 times, and in the same period it opened 10% or more below the prior close 8 times (our calculation).[25]

Fatal flaw 1: the stop fails in a gap

A stop at $55.37 sits 8.25% below the close, which is less than the 8.64% the fund fell on October 22 alone.[2] About 65% of EDC's squared daily moves come from the gap between one close and the next open, and its worst openings in the last five years were 17.14% below the prior close on June 23, 2026 and 17.06% on March 3, 2026 (our calculation).[25] If the fund closed at the stop and then opened 17.14% lower, at $45.88, a position of 3.5% of net worth, the ceiling of tier TIER_3, would lose 84 basis points of net worth against a 50-basis-point budget, which is 1.68 times the budget. The stop is an exit instruction and not a loss limit. The evidence that this is wrong would be a run of gaps that stay above the stop; we would know at each session open.

Fatal flaw 2: the index is a chip bet

The verdict is REJECTED, and two rules produce it. The first is the reward-to-risk floor. For a leveraged satellite we use the tactical floor of 2.5, because the scoring rules set no separate one. EDC's ratio at the $60.35 close is -0.14, because the central 13-week value of $59.65 is below the price. The second is the archetype test, and none of the three archetypes fits. A compounder needs business quality that a fund lacks. A turnaround needs two improving signals and a ratio of 2.5 or better. A tactical breakout needs a Stage 2 chart, and the momentum gate says NO_MOMENTUM. The fund is not in Stage 4, so the technical red line is not what rejects it.

Some terms first. CMP is the current market price, here the $60.35 close of October 23, 2026. Portfolio heat is the total money at risk across all positions if every stop were hit at once. Our ceiling for it is 12.0% of net worth. Risk-unit sizing sets a position's weight from the loss we accept if the stop is hit, called the risk budget. Weight equals the risk budget divided by the stop distance, capped at the tier maximum. A basis point (bps) is one hundredth of a percent, so 50 bps is 0.50% of net worth. The tiers set the budget. TIER_1 is 100 bps, TIER_2 is 75, TIER_3 is 50 and TIER_4 is 35. TIER_0 is zero, which means no position at all. EDC is TIER_0 because it is rejected. We use TIER_3 as the yardstick for the most a 3x fund could earn.

The stop rule matters because it sets the stop distance. A stop is the price at which the idea is judged wrong and the position is cut. We place it at structural invalidation, the level that, once broken, shows the chart pattern behind the idea has failed. We applied the Stage 1 base variant. The 63-day base low of $58.87 minus 1.0 ATR of $3.50 gives $55.37. The stop-rule cell in the table also mentions the Stage 2 variant, which does not apply because the fund is not in Stage 2. The tighter tactical stop is 0.5 ATR below the swing low, at $57.12. It gives a ratio of -0.22 at the close, so the choice of stop does not rescue the trade. Every stop distance in this chapter uses $60.35 as its basis, the top of the reference zone.
