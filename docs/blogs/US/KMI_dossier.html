1. What the company does

Kinder Morgan is a toll road for energy. It does not drill wells or sell gasoline to drivers. It owns the pipes, storage tanks and docks that sit between producers and users, and it charges a fee to use them. In the trade this is called midstream, which means the part of the energy chain between the well and the customer. At the end of 2025 the company owned an interest in or operated about 78,000 miles of pipelines, 136 terminals and about 706 billion cubic feet of working natural gas storage.[8] Its natural gas network is the largest piece: management says it delivers about 40% of all the natural gas in America, about 40% of the gas that feeds LNG export plants, and more than half of the gas exported to Mexico.[20] LNG means liquefied natural gas, which is gas cooled into a liquid so it can travel by ship.

The customers are local gas utilities, power plants, LNG exporters, gas producers, refiners and industrial users. Most pay under contracts that charge for reserved space whether or not the customer uses it. The industry calls this take-or-pay, and it works like paying rent on a parking space that sits empty some days. At December 31, 2025, the company reported about $36 billion of future revenue under contracts of this kind: $5 billion for 2026, $5 billion for 2027 and $26 billion for 2028 and later.[8] That is about 2.1 times the 2025 revenue of $16,937 million, our calculation,[7] and it is the main reason the cash flow is steady.

The company reports one earnings number that this report uses often: adjusted EBITDA, which is EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) after removing items management calls unusual and adding the company's share of joint ventures. It is a company measure, not an accounting standard. Adjusted EBITDA was $8,391 million in 2025 and $9,189 million over the twelve months to September 30, 2026.[1] The company also reports adjusted earnings per share, which strips the same unusual items from profit. It was $1.30 in 2025 and $1.59 over the twelve months to September, our calculation from the quarterly releases.[7][1]

How a pipeline fee works, and why it matters for a stock

A pipeline owner can charge in two ways. A reservation fee is paid whether or not gas flows. A usage fee is paid per unit moved, like a toll. Kinder Morgan's natural gas contracts lean toward the first kind, which is why its earnings do not swing with the gas price the way a producer's do.[8] The weak spot is the CO2 segment and some gathering and marketing activity, where oil, gas and gas-price spreads still matter. In 2025 the CO2 segment earned $608 million of adjusted segment earnings, down from $647 million in 2024, while volumes held near 26 thousand barrels of oil a day.[7] A stock that pays for stability is usually valued like a bond with growth. The dividend is $1.19 a share for 2026, a 2% rise on 2025, which is a yield of 3.5% at today's price.[1][28]

What changed in 2026: a record year with some one-time help

The third quarter of 2026 was a record. Adjusted EBITDA reached $2,180 million, up 9%, and adjusted earnings per share were $0.35, up 21%.[1] For the nine months, adjusted earnings per share were $1.20 against $0.91 a year earlier, a 32% rise.[1] Management is candid about the quality of that gain. On the call, the chief financial officer listed $225 million of nonrecurring items (winter weather, a contract buyout, a regulatory rate true-up and favorable gas price spreads in the Permian Basin), and said $175 million of the outperformance came from commodity prices that may not last.[20] He added that 2026 "is going to be a difficult year to replicate."[20] That matters for the price, because the market pays for the earnings it expects to see next year, not the record already printed.

The growth engine: a $14.4 billion project backlog

A backlog is the list of approved expansion projects that are not yet earning. Kinder Morgan's backlog reached $14.4 billion at September 30, 2026, up $4.7 billion in a quarter, with $5.4 billion of new projects less $700 million placed in service.[1] Natural gas projects make up $13.2 billion of it, and more than 75% is tied to power generation and gas utilities.[1] Two projects reached a final investment decision in the quarter. The Southeast Connector, about 470 miles of new pipe carrying up to 2.6 billion cubic feet a day, costs the company $3.1 billion and starts in the fourth quarter of 2030.[1] The Permian Link, a $4.2 billion pipe (the company's share is $1.6 billion) carrying 2.8 billion cubic feet a day to an A-rated hyperscaler under a 20-year take-or-pay contract, is due in the third quarter of 2030.[1][20] A hyperscaler is a very large cloud company that builds data centers. Management says the $13.7 billion stable portion of the backlog costs about 5.7 times the EBITDA it should earn in its first full year, which is a return of about 17.5% a year before depreciation and tax (our calculation), and that the whole backlog adds about $2.5 billion a year of EBITDA when built.[1][20]

How the backlog has moved over five quarters

The backlog is not a straight line. It stood at $9.3 billion in September 2025, rose to $10.0 billion by December, reached $10.1 billion in March 2026, fell to $9.6 billion in June as large projects entered service, and then jumped to $14.4 billion in September.[9][7][5][3][1] A backlog that shrinks when projects finish is healthy, because it means the pipes were built. The risk is the opposite: a backlog that grows faster than the company can finish and fund it. Chapter 3 shows that funding.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Kinder Morgan is a strong business because its assets are hard to copy and its customers are committed in writing. A new interstate pipeline takes years of permits, landowner agreements and construction, so a competitor cannot easily build beside a full pipe. The network is also connected to almost every producing basin and to the main demand centers, which lets the company offer a utility or an LNG plant a choice of supply that a single-route pipeline cannot.[20] That position showed up in the numbers. Adjusted EBITDA rose from $7,946 million in 2021 to $8,391 million in 2025 and to $9,189 million in the twelve months to September 2026, and net debt divided by that EBITDA fell from 4.2 times at the end of 2023 to 3.5 times.[14][7][1][12]

The credit rating agencies agree on the quality. At the end of 2025 the company was rated BBB by S&P Global (upgraded to BBB+ on January 13, 2026), Baa2 by Moody's (upgraded to Baa1 in March 2026) and BBB+ by Fitch.[8][2] The company also has $3.5 billion of bank credit that runs to May 2031, with about $3,490 million unused and no commercial paper outstanding at September 30, 2026.[18][2] This matters because pipeline builders live on cheap long-term money, and a stronger rating lowers the cost of the next $4 billion of projects.

Where the strength comes from

The first source is demand for natural gas. The U.S. Energy Information Administration expects LNG exports of 15.1 billion cubic feet a day in 2025, 17.6 in 2026 and 18.6 in 2027, and total gas use of 92.37 billion cubic feet a day in 2026 and 93.83 in 2027.[21] Management adds that large-load requests from power users in the Southeast now exceed 200 gigawatts, which it translates to about 8 billion cubic feet a day of gas, and that about 70% of the data-center power demand in development sits in states its pipes serve.[20][7] The Energy Information Administration also expects electricity demand from the commercial sector, which includes data centers, to grow 2.8% in 2027.[22]

The second source is contracts and the backlog described in chapter 1. The third is cost of capital: a BBB+/Baa1 borrower funds long projects at a lower rate than a weaker peer. The fourth is leadership continuity. Richard Kinder, 81, is executive chairman and owns 11.60% of the shares (258,086,579), and Kimberly Dang, 56, is chief executive; the 21 directors and officers as a group own 12.72%.[16] That is real alignment: the founder's own wealth rises and falls with the stock.

Where the weakness is

The first weakness is growth. Adjusted EBITDA grew only 1.4% a year from 2021 to 2025 (our calculation), and revenue moved between $15.1 and $19.2 billion with no trend (the 2022 peak came when commodity prices were high, and revenue includes gas the company buys and resells).[15][14][7] The dividend grew 2.0% a year over the same period, from $1.08 to $1.17.[14][7] The 2026 jump is therefore a change in pace, and part of it is borrowed from weather and commodity prices that may reverse.

The second weakness is the return on the money invested. The company carries $20,075 million of goodwill from past takeovers, so its return on invested capital is only 6.5% on operating income and 7.7% with joint-venture income (chapter 3).[1] A pipeline can earn a thin return and still be valuable, because the asset lasts decades. But it means each new dollar must earn well above the cost of capital to create value, and the 5.7 times multiple on new projects is the test.

The third weakness is that some risks sit outside the contracts. The SFPP pipeline on the U.S. West Coast faces a California Public Utilities Commission rate case, where an administrative law judge issued an interim proposed decision on July 10, 2026, and the company took a $138 million legal, environmental and other accrual in the quarter, treated as a one-time item.[2][1] A lawsuit over Winter Storm Uri gas supply to Freeport LNG is also pending.[2] The fourth is price: at 21.4 times adjusted earnings the shares are no longer cheap, and a pipeline stock with a 3.5% yield needs steady execution to keep that multiple.

Customers, competitors, regulators and people

The customers are mostly utilities, power producers, LNG plants and large producers, many with investment-grade credit. The Permian Link buyer is described as an A-rated hyperscaler, and the Southeast Connector shippers are described as creditworthy parties with long-term take-or-pay agreements.[1][20] The competition is other large pipeline companies, and they are growing too. In the latest quarter each reported: ONEOK adjusted EBITDA up 6%, Enterprise Products up 16%, Williams up 6% (second quarter), Targa up 38% (second quarter) and Energy Transfer up 31% (second quarter).[23][24][25][26][27] Kinder Morgan's 9% in the third quarter sits in the middle of that range.

On governance, shareholders re-elected Mr. Kinder with 1,622,287,287 votes for and 66,013,694 against at the May 13, 2026 meeting, and approved executive pay with 1,605,994,160 votes for and 77,788,484 against, which is 95.4% support among votes cast for or against (our calculation).[17] The company's disclosure controls were judged effective in the latest quarterly filing.[2] One note on leadership: several directors, including the chairman, are in their late seventies or eighties, so succession is a long-run question even though the day-to-day team is stable.[16] What this means for an owner: the business is protected, funded and well run, and the open questions are how much of 2026 repeats and how much the price already assumes. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk asks whether management did what it said it would do. Each year the company publishes a budget in December or January, and it ties pay to it. We compared budgets for 2023 through 2026 with what followed. On the dividend the record is clean: it was raised every year, and 2026 is the ninth consecutive increase.[19] On earnings the record is mixed. The company beat its 2025 budget and is beating 2026, but it missed the 2023 and 2024 earnings budgets by about 2% to 6%, in years when commodity prices came in below the budget assumptions.[10][12][14]

On leverage the record is also mixed. The company reported 4.2 times at the end of 2023 against a 4.0 budget, because it closed a $1.8 billion acquisition days before year end and spent about $522 million on unbudgeted share buybacks.[12][14] It then met the 2025 target of 3.8 times and is now at 3.5. One transparency note: in January 2025 the company stopped reporting distributable cash flow, which it said drew "declining investor interest" as a main performance measure, and it now leads with adjusted earnings per share instead.[10] A fair reader would say the company is reliable on dividends and leverage, slightly optimistic on earnings in down-price years, and conservative when prices rise.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter attacks our own estimates. For each estimate we name the weakest assumption, the evidence that would show it is wrong, and the date when that evidence arrives. The weakest assumption behind the 2027 estimate of $1.51 is that the $175 million of commodity gains fades while new project earnings of about $0.08 a share arrive. If the gains persist, 2027 is closer to our bull case of $1.62; if they overlap the $225 million of nonrecurring items, the figure is $1.57. The evidence is the 2027 budget, which the company published for 2026 on December 8, 2025 and which should come in early December this year (the date is not confirmed), and then the first-quarter 2027 report in April.[19][20]

The weakest assumption behind the $34.40 central value is the multiple. We assume the market keeps paying 21.4 times earnings, the median of three years in which the stock doubled. If the multiple reverts to 19 times, the value is $30.59 with no change in earnings. The evidence is the multiple itself, which is visible every day, and the first test is how the stock behaves around the 2027 budget. The weakest assumption behind the leverage estimate is that expansion spending stays near $4 billion a year. Management called that a run rate for "the next few years" and said more projects are coming, so the figure could rise.[20]

Red-team bear case: the stock is a fully priced utility-like asset after a 30% rise in 52 weeks (+30.25%), earnings are inflated in 2026 by weather and commodity prices, the company is committing about $4 billion a year to projects that earn about 17.5% before depreciation and tax but arrive in 2030 and later, and leverage rises as it builds. In that case the stock reverts to a lower multiple, and $28.50 would be the price a buyer pays.[28]

Fatal flaw 1: the one-time gains of 2026 do not repeat and growth stalls until 2028

In this scenario 2027 earnings fall to $1.45 or below. The company itself said 2026 contained $225 million of nonrecurring items and $175 million from commodity prices that may not last, and the U.S. Energy Information Administration expects the Henry Hub gas price to fall 9% in 2027.[20][21] Analysts already model a 1.3% decline for 2027 ($1.54 against $1.57), and our own central estimate of $1.51 is lower still.[29] The risk is that the market, which now pays 21.9 times 2026 earnings, applies that multiple to a lower 2027 number and then marks the stock down to 19 times. That is the bear value of $28.50, a 16% fall from the close.

We would see this coming in three ways: the December budget showing 2027 adjusted earnings below $1.45, the first-quarter 2027 report missing the 2026 base without a clear cause, or the stock closing below $29.51. Any of these would end the idea.

Fatal flaw 2: the building program outruns cash flow and leverage drifts toward the 4.0 times red line

The company plans about $4 billion a year of expansion spending for the next few years, plus about $1 billion of maintenance spending, against cash from operations of $6,733 million and dividends of $2,638 million over the last twelve months.[20][8][1] That leaves a gap of roughly $0.7 billion to $0.9 billion a year before any project cost grows. Leverage of 3.5 times leaves room, and 4.0 times is about $4.6 billion of extra debt, so a few years of gaps are fundable. But the 2030 projects are large, and a 10% overrun on $4.7 billion is $0.47 billion. Our own reject trigger is 3.9 times, one tenth below the 4.0 times hard red line, so that we act before the line is reached.

Interest cost is the second part. The average rate on borrowing was 3.85% at the end of 2025 and 3.92% in June 2026, while the August 2026 notes cost 5.55% for 2036 and 6.15% for 2056.[4][1] Debt of $1,726 million is due within a year, including $500 million of 1.75% notes due in November 2026 and $567 million of 2.25% notes due in March 2027, which would refinance at rates nearer the 5.55% paid in August.[2] Each percentage point on $33 billion is about $0.12 a share after tax. The hard red line, 4.0 times with interest cover under 2.0, is far away, but the premium multiple would not survive a leverage move toward 4.5 times.

Fatal flaw 3: demand, policy or a legal outcome breaks the contract story

The backlog rests on a few large customers, including one A-rated hyperscaler on the Permian Link and Southeast utilities on the Southeast Connector, and on permits. Both projects are subject to the timely receipt of permits and approvals before their 2030 dates. Management says it is "very comfortable" with the current permitting regime and points to FERC permits for the MSX and South System 4 projects within a year, but a permit delay on a 2030 project would still move earnings later.[1][20] If data-center demand slowed, or if a customer's plans changed, take-or-pay contracts would protect revenue, but renewals and new projects would suffer. The company says large-load requests in the Southeast exceed 200 gigawatts, but a request is not a contract.[20]

A legal event is the smaller but real piece. The California Public Utilities Commission case on SFPP could require refunds or lower rates, although the 10-Q says the outcome is not expected to have a material impact, and the company accrued $138 million in the third quarter for legal, environmental and other items.[2][1] A lawsuit filed on September 13, 2021 over Winter Storm Uri gas supply to Freeport LNG is also open.[2] We treat these as headline risks that we cannot size from the filings, and not as thesis breakers, but they add to the case for a lower entry price.
