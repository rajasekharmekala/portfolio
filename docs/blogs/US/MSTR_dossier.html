1. What the company does

Strategy runs two businesses under one roof. The first is enterprise analytics software: dashboards, reporting and an artificial-intelligence layer that large companies license or rent by subscription. It had 1,539 employees on June 30, 2026 and booked $477.2 million of revenue in 2025.[6][7] The second is a bitcoin treasury. As of October 18, 2026 the company held 848,517 bitcoin that it bought for $64.02 billion, an average of $75,446 per coin.[1] Since the second quarter of 2026 Strategy reports the two as separate segments, and all of the revenue comes from software.[6]

The software does not pay for the bitcoin. In the second quarter of 2026 the software segment earned $3.7 million of net income, and the whole company produced only $9.9 million of operating cash flow in the first half.[6] The bitcoin is funded by selling things to investors: common shares through an at-the-market program (called an ATM, a way of selling new shares gradually at the market price), convertible notes, and five series of perpetual preferred stock that pay fixed dividends. In 2025 the company raised $25.3 billion this way, and in the 2026 year to date it had raised $17.06 billion by July.[2][8]

A common shareholder owns what is left after those senior claims are paid. At the October 23 bitcoin price of $80,672.82 the pile is worth $68.45 billion, while the convertible notes and preferred stock add up to about $20.27 billion at face value.[9][1][10] So the stock behaves like a bitcoin position with built-in borrowing. When bitcoin rises, the residual rises faster; when it falls, the residual falls faster. The market also pays a premium or discount on top, which is the mNAV measure explained in the glossary below.

The accounting adds a twist. Since 2025 Strategy records bitcoin at fair value, so every move in the bitcoin price flows through net income. In 2025 that meant a $5.40 billion unrealized loss, and in the second quarter of 2026 an $8.32 billion unrealized loss on a $122.4 million revenue quarter.[7][8] Reported profit and earnings per share (EPS, the profit divided across all shares) are therefore mostly a bitcoin price gauge. This report reads them that way and anchors value on bitcoin and the capital structure instead.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • mNAV is the multiple of the bitcoin pile that the market pays for the whole company, measured as enterprise value divided by bitcoin value. A reading of 1.12 means you pay $1.12 for each $1.00 of bitcoin the company holds.
  • BTC Yield is the percentage change in bitcoin held per diluted share over a period. It rises only when the company adds coins faster than it adds shares and share-like claims.
  • BTC Hurdle is the company's own estimate of its yearly cost of credit, 10.8% in the second quarter release. Bitcoin has to beat that rate for common shareholders to gain on a per-share basis.
  • USD Reserve is cash and short-term investments set aside only to pay preferred dividends and interest. The board's policy requires at least 12 months of cover.
  • Convertible note is a loan that can turn into shares at a set price. If the stock stays below that price, the lender can demand cash at the put date.
  • Perpetual preferred stock is a share class that pays a fixed dividend with no maturity date and ranks ahead of common stock. STRC, STRF, STRK, STRD and STRE are the five series.
  • Senior claims are the convertible notes plus the preferred stock at face value, the amounts that rank ahead of common shareholders.
  • Fair-value accounting means the bitcoin is marked to the market price each quarter, with the gain or loss counted in net income.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position rests on scale and access to capital, not on software. Strategy calls itself the largest institutional holder of bitcoin, and it kept raising money through the bitcoin decline of early 2026: $25.3 billion in 2025 and $17.06 billion by late July 2026.[2][8] In the week to October 18 its common-stock program still had $18.79 billion of capacity and its STRC program $17.51 billion.[1] That access is the real asset, because it lets the company buy bitcoin with other people's money when the stock trades above the value of the coins.

The same engine is also the weak point. The cheapest funding stopped working in the summer. STRC, the flagship preferred, traded below its $100 face value, so management raised its dividend from 11.00% in January to 11.50% by March and 12.00% in July, and began buying it back.[11][8][3] In the week to October 18 it sold no preferred stock and spent $193.1 million repurchasing 1,938,780 STRC shares, an average of about $99.60 a share. The repurchase program was lifted from $2.0 billion to $3.0 billion, with $1.16 billion left.[1] Money spent on repurchases is money not spent on bitcoin, so the growth of the pile now leans on common-stock sales.

The software moat is modest. Revenue was $510.8 million in 2021 and $477.2 million in 2025, a decline of about 1.7% a year over four years, and gross margin, the share of revenue left after the direct cost of delivering the product, slid from 82.0% to 68.7% as sales shifted from licenses to cloud subscriptions.[12][7] Subscription revenue grew 54% in the second quarter of 2026 while product-support revenue kept shrinking, and gross margin was 66.6%.[8] Signed software contracts not yet recognized as revenue, called remaining performance obligations (RPO), were $546.9 million on June 30, 2026, with $320.1 million due within 12 months.[6] The business is real, profitable at the margin and stable, but at about $0.5 billion of yearly revenue it is too small to carry the obligations on its own.

Peers show how crowded the model has become. Metaplanet trades at 0.79 times its bitcoin, Twenty One Capital at 1.10 and Strive at 1.41, while Strategy sits at 1.17 on the same headline measure those sites use, or 0.96 on a simple ratio of market value to bitcoin value. Chapter 3 explains the difference.[13][14][15][10] Anyone who wants bitcoin alone can buy it directly. Owning MSTR adds a premium, dividend claims ranking ahead of you, and a software business, so the premium needs a reason: the company's ability to raise capital and add coins per share faster than a plain holder could.

Where the edge holds and where it does not

The edge holds in scale, liquidity and a clear playbook. A reserve of $4.83 billion gives about three years of cover for the dividends and interest, and the company has never missed a preferred dividend, with 18 months of consecutive payments reported in July.[1][8] The edge does not hold in cost of capital. A 12.00% dividend on the largest preferred series sits above the company's own 10.8% hurdle, so each new dollar of STRC needs bitcoin to rise more than 10.8% a year just to break even per share.[3][8] What this means for an owner: the structure can work for years, but it works best when bitcoin rises and the stock trades at a premium, and it asks the owner to accept a wide band of outcomes. The next chapter lists the five measures that track whether it is still working.

The senior claims are fixed in dollars, so a drop in bitcoin hits the common stock harder than it hits the coins.
