Before you read

Amazon runs the biggest online store, the AWS cloud business and a large advertising arm, and its second quarter was strong: sales rose 20% to $200.6 billion and AWS grew 37%.[1] We still reject the stock for new money: over the last year it earned $161.4 billion from operations but spent $169.0 billion on buildings and equipment, so cash flow was minus $7.6 billion, and the possible gain is only 2.81 times the possible loss against a required 3.0.[1][25] We own no shares, our only exposure is two long-dated call options (LEAPS), and this verdict says nothing about selling them.[27] This rejection would be wrong if AWS keeps growing above 30% and the new data centers pay back quickly, and we look again after the October 29 results.[15] Status: PUBLISHED_WITH_GATE_EXCEPTIONS.

1. What the company does

Amazon sells goods, rents out computers and sells advertising. It reports three parts. North America and International cover its online and physical stores and the ads and subscriptions tied to them. Amazon Web Services (AWS) sells computing, storage and artificial intelligence services to businesses, governments and universities.[7] In 2025 sales were $716.9 billion. The two store segments brought in $588.2 billion of that and AWS $128.7 billion.[7]

Customers pay in five main ways. Shoppers buy goods that Amazon sells itself ($269.3 billion of online store sales in 2025), or they pay commissions and shipping fees when outside sellers use the Amazon warehouses and delivery network ($172.2 billion).[7] In the second quarter of 2026, outside sellers supplied 61% of the paid units, so the marketplace ships more items than Amazon's own retail does.[1] Brands and sellers pay for advertising ($68.6 billion in 2025), households pay for Prime and other subscriptions ($49.6 billion), and AWS customers pay for computing by usage or under multi-year contracts ($128.7 billion).[7]

The profit pool is uneven. Retail is a thin-margin business. The operating margin is the share of sales left after all running costs, and in 2025 it was 6.9% in North America and 2.9% in International, against 35.4% at AWS.[7] AWS was 18.0% of sales but earned $45.6 billion of the $80.0 billion of total operating income, which is 57%.[7] Advertising sits inside the store segments and is also high margin, but the filings we read do not report a separate profit for it.[7]

The 2026 story is spending. AI demand has pushed Amazon to build data centers, buy chips and servers, and sign power contracts at a scale it has not tried before. Capital expenditure (capex) is money spent on buildings and equipment that last for years. Cash capex was $128.3 billion in 2025, and management now expects about $220 billion in 2026, up from about $200 billion planned in February.[7][14][13] Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after the business pays for its operations and its equipment. Amazon measures it as cash flow from operations, the cash the business generates before financing, minus net capex. Over the twelve months to June 30, 2026, that measure was minus $7.6 billion on operating cash flow of $161.4 billion.[1]

Two customers that are also investments

Two of the biggest AI customers on AWS are companies Amazon has also put money into. Amazon holds non-voting preferred stock in Anthropic and preferred stock in OpenAI. These private stakes were carried at $122.3 billion on June 30, 2026, up from $16.2 billion at the end of 2025.[2] In 2026 both labs agreed to bigger AWS contracts: an expansion of $100 billion over 8 years for OpenAI and more than $100 billion over 10 years for Anthropic.[2] That link matters twice. It supports the backlog, and it also produced most of the second-quarter profit, as chapters 3 and 6 explain.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Backlog: signed contracts for future services that Amazon has not yet counted as revenue. At June 30, 2026 it was about $496 billion, mostly AWS.
  • Run rate: the latest quarter multiplied by four. AWS sales of $42.2 billion in the second quarter give about $169 billion a year.
  • Mark-up: a gain recorded because the price of a private holding was raised, with no cash received.
  • LEAPS: long-dated call options that give the right to buy 100 shares each at a fixed strike price until a date a year or more away.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of the business is scale, and the filings give the evidence. AWS reached a $169 billion annual run rate, grew 37% in the second quarter after 28% in the first, and holds about $496 billion of signed future commitments with a weighted-average remaining life of 6.4 years.[1][2] Its operating margin was 39.4% in the quarter and 36.8% over twelve months.[1] Management says its AI and chips businesses each passed a $25 billion run rate, and that Anthropic and OpenAI have made multi-year commitments to its Trainium chips.[1] On the call, the CEO said that even at $220 billion of capex Amazon will not have enough capacity for all the demand it sees in 2026.[13]

The stores add a second source of strength. Worldwide paid units grew 17% in the second quarter, and Amazon says it set delivery-speed records for Prime members in the first half.[1] Advertising grew 26% to $19.8 billion in the quarter and about $76.1 billion over four quarters, which turns shopper intent into high-margin revenue.[1] Subscription services, mainly Prime, grew 12% to $13.7 billion.[1] A rival would need warehouses, trucks, a seller marketplace and an ad system at the same time, and that mix is hard to copy.

The weak points are cost, capital and concentration. Retail margins are thin: North America earned 7.4% over twelve months and International 3.1%.[1] Shipping costs rose 19% to $27.9 billion in the quarter, almost as fast as sales.[1] The 10-K itself calls the marketplace intensely competitive, and the filings describe antitrust cases by the Federal Trade Commission and state attorneys general that seek billions of dollars of damages and structural relief.[7][2]

Spending is the price of staying ahead

Amazon is not alone in spending. Microsoft added $115.9 billion of property and equipment in its fiscal year to June 2026, and Alphabet spent $91.4 billion in 2025.[20][21] In June 2026 Alphabet also raised $49.6 billion of new equity and mandatory convertible preferred stock to help pay for AI capacity.[22] Amazon chose debt instead: face value of long-term debt rose from $68.8 billion at the end of 2025 to $133.0 billion at June 30, 2026, and after quarter end it issued $25.0 billion of dollar notes plus sterling notes with an offering price of 4.242 billion pounds.[2][17] The race rewards the biggest builders, but it also means every large player now needs outside money, which is a different business from the cash-rich one investors owned in 2023.

The profit line is flattered by gains on private stakes

Reported profit overstates what the business earns. In the second quarter Amazon booked $53.4 billion of pre-tax non-operating income, primarily from its Anthropic investments, and net income was $62.6 billion, or $5.75 a share.[1] The gain came from raising the carrying price of the stake, with no cash received.[2] Operating income, which excludes these items, was $27.5 billion. We therefore judge the business on operating income and cash flow, and we treat the gains as a risk as much as a benefit, because a lower price for the stakes would reverse them.

Governance reads clean on the evidence we reviewed. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Sales grew from $469.8 billion in 2021 to $716.9 billion in 2025, a compound rate of 11.1% a year, and reached $775.7 billion over the twelve months to June 30, 2026.[9][7][1] The operating margin rose from 5.3% to 11.2% over the same four years and to 12.1% in the latest twelve months.[9][7][1] Net income looks even better at $135.3 billion, but about $81.8 billion of pre-tax non-operating income sits inside it, mostly mark-ups on the Anthropic stake, so we read operating income and cash flow first.[1]

Six terms come before the tables. earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. EBITDA, short for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, is a rough measure of operating cash profit, which we compute as operating income plus depreciation and amortization. Net debt is interest-bearing debt minus cash and marketable securities, and debt-to-equity is total debt divided by shareholders' money. Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money invested in the business, so it shows what each dollar put to work earns. The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share.

Several terms about the chart and the trade plan also need plain definitions before the tests. The Weinstein stage scale names the phase of a stock: Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend, Stage 3 is a topping or pullback phase, and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend. A base is a flat stretch of trading before a move, the pivot is the top of that stretch, and a breakout is a move above the pivot on heavy volume. The relative strength index (RSI) is a gauge from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has moved, where under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing in dollars, and we use it as a ruler for distance.

Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, or (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut. The entry zone is the band of prices where we would buy. A measured move projects the height of a base above its pivot as a target.

The number

Return on invested capital for the twelve months to June 30, 2026 is 13.7%, and it was 16.8% in fiscal 2025.

How it was calculated

We took operating income, taxed it at 2025's effective rate of 19.6%, and divided it by the average money invested in the business. Invested capital is shareholders' equity plus debt plus long-term lease liabilities, minus cash and marketable securities.

The inputs

Operating income for the twelve months was $93,712 million, which gives after-tax operating profit of $75.3 billion.[1] The 2025 effective tax rate was 19.6%.[7] Invested capital was $321.0 billion at December 2024, $444.2 billion at December 2025 and $656.0 billion at June 2026, built from the balance sheets and debt notes.[7][1][2] Fiscal 2025 after-tax operating profit was $64.3 billion.

The range

Taking out the Anthropic and OpenAI stakes, which are not operating assets ($16.2 billion at December 2025 and $122.3 billion at June 2026), lifts the figure to 15.7%.[2] A 21% tax rate lowers it to 13.5%.

If the number is off

A figure below 12% would fail the gate. Invested capital grew $211.8 billion in six months, so the figure keeps falling unless operating profit catches up, and that needs AWS to hold a margin near 39% while its asset base roughly doubles.

The number

On a stricter measure of owner cash, today's price assumes 11.5% growth a year for ten years, not the 7.0% shown in our ledger.

How it was calculated

The ledger takes 75% of operating cash flow, $121.1 billion, as owner cash flow. Depreciation and amortization was $75.2 billion, which is the best public stand-in for the equipment that wears out each year. Subtracting it from operating cash flow leaves $86.2 billion. We then solved for the ten-year growth rate at which these cash flows, plus an 18 times exit value, discounted at 10%, equal today's market value of $2,696.6 billion.

The inputs

Operating cash flow of $161.4 billion and depreciation and amortization of $75.2 billion are twelve-month figures.[1] Market value and the 7.0% result on the ledger basis come from the session ledger.[25] The 10% discount rate and the 18 times exit multiple are the portfolio rules.[28]

The range

Also subtracting stock pay of $19.3 billion leaves $66.9 billion and implies 14.8% a year. Using the ledger's $121.1 billion gives 7.0%.

If the number is off

A higher implied growth rate means more is already in the price. At 11.5% the market is not pricing a cheap cloud company; it is pricing a cloud company whose heavy capex pays off on schedule.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 attacks our own estimates. The red-team bear case is short. Amazon plans to spend about $220 billion this year against $161.4 billion of operating cash flow over the last twelve months, it has borrowed to do it, and the profit it reports is flattered by non-cash gains on two private companies that also buy its cloud services. If AWS growth slips below 30%, or the stakes are marked down, the stock could fall to about $203, which is 19% below the close and well below our $236.78 stop, so a gap on an earnings night could cost more than the stop implies.[1][2][13]

Weakest assumption 1: new capacity will earn its cost

Our base case assumes the $496 billion backlog turns into revenue on schedule. The assets are growing far faster than sales. AWS property and equipment rose 72% in 2025, from $110.7 billion to $190.1 billion, while AWS sales grew 20%, and total property and equipment rose another 25% in the first half of 2026.[7][1] Management says data center capital is spent about two years before servers go in, that each data center is then monetized for 30-plus years, and that Amazon faces near-term free cash flow headwinds until the new sites are full.[13] We would see the first proof in the October 29 report if AWS growth holds above 30% and the margin stays above 37%.

Weakest assumption 2: the backlog and the profits are independent of Amazon's own money

At least $200 billion of the backlog comes from 2026 expansions by OpenAI and Anthropic, and Amazon holds stakes in both, carried at $122.3 billion.[2] Amazon also funded the remaining $21.3 billion of its OpenAI commitment after June 30 and set up a facility of up to $20.0 billion for Anthropic.[2] If those companies could not pay, or if their valuations fell, Amazon would lose both revenue and the gains that now lift EPS. Evidence of trouble would show up in the other income line of every quarterly report, starting October 29, and in any change to the commitment disclosures.

Weakest assumption 3: growth is as fast as the second quarter suggests

Prime Day was assumed to fall in the second quarter this year, so the quarter grew 20%, while the third-quarter forecast is for 9% to 12%. Amazon says third-quarter growth would be nearly 4 points higher if Prime Day were removed from both years.[3][1] A weak-looking headline is likely, and the market may not look through it. Regulators also remain a tail risk: the FTC and state attorneys general seek billions of dollars in damages and possible structural remedies in cases that began in 2020.[2]
