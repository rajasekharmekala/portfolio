Before you read

Amazon runs a very large online store, a cloud business called Amazon Web Services (AWS) that earns most of its operating profit, and a fast-growing advertising arm.[1] We conclude WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT on the shares. AWS sales grew 36.7% and signed backlog reached $496 billion, but cash after spending is negative, the price sits 1.66 average daily swings above its floor, and results arrive on October 29.[1][2][33][14] We would buy only between $243.38 and $248.00, with an exit level at $238.11 and a central target of $288.[33] The view is wrong if AWS growth drops below 33%, if the price closes below $238.11 first, or if two of our five operating measures fail.

1. What the company does

Amazon sells to four kinds of customers, and each pays in a different way. Shoppers and businesses buy goods from Amazon's online and physical stores, or pay monthly and yearly fees for Prime and other subscriptions. Outside sellers pay commissions and fees so that Amazon will list, store, pack and ship their goods. Advertisers pay to place products in front of shoppers who are already searching to buy. Companies and developers pay AWS for computing power, storage, databases and artificial intelligence services.[10]

The size and shape of the business are easiest to see in the twelve months to June 30, 2026. Net sales were $775.7 billion and operating income was $93.7 billion.[1] AWS was 19% of sales at $148.4 billion, but it earned $54.7 billion of operating income, which is 58% of the company total (our calculation from the same table).[1] That split matters because AWS growth and AWS profit now decide most of the earnings story, while the stores decide how much cash is left over.

Here is how the money is made in each part. In the stores, Amazon earns a thin profit on each sale: North America kept 7.4% of its sales as operating income over the last twelve months and International kept 3.1%.[1] Advertising and seller fees sit on top of the same shopper visits and carry much higher margins than the goods themselves, which is why the stores' profit has risen even while Amazon kept prices low. AWS rents out servers and software by the hour, so its profit grows when the same buildings and chips are used more heavily.

Two things changed in 2026. First, demand for AI computing pushed AWS growth to 36.7% in the June quarter, the fastest in 18 quarters, and the company says its AI business and its chips business each passed a $25 billion yearly sales pace.[1] Second, spending jumped. Management now expects to spend about $220 billion in cash on buildings, servers and equipment in 2026, up from about $200 billion planned in February.[13][5] Cash from running the business was $161.4 billion over twelve months, but spending on property and equipment was $169.0 billion after sales proceeds, so cash after spending was an outflow of $7.6 billion.[1] Amazon also owns stakes in two AI labs, Anthropic and OpenAI, carried at about $220 billion on the June 30 balance sheet, which flows into reported profit through large gains and is one reason headline earnings are hard to read.[2]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • RSI (relative strength index) is a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has been rising or falling; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in dollars; we use it to measure how far the price sits from a floor and to place stops a sensible distance away.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is operating profit with the large non-cash write-offs for buildings and equipment added back; we use it to size debt.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business, so it says what each invested dollar earns.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is cash from running the business minus the cash spent on property and equipment, net of proceeds from selling or being reimbursed for equipment; it is what is left for owners and lenders.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying employees in shares, which are new shares that spread ownership thinner.
  • RPO (remaining performance obligations) is the value of signed customer contracts not yet recognised as revenue; Amazon calls its AWS-heavy version the backlog.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates before buying assets or paying lenders.
  • capital expenditure is spending on buildings, servers, chips and other equipment that will be used for years.
  • gross margin is net sales minus the cost of goods and services sold, as a share of net sales.
  • operating margin is operating income, which is profit before interest, investment gains and tax, as a share of net sales.
  • net margin is net income as a share of net sales.
  • net debt is interest-bearing debt minus cash and marketable securities; a negative figure means the company holds more cash than debt.
  • price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share, which says how many dollars buyers pay for each dollar of yearly profit.
  • guidance is the company's own published forecast for the coming quarter or year.
  • consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow the stock.
  • dilution is the rise in the share count when a company issues new shares, which shrinks each existing share's slice of profit.
  • buyback is a company's purchase of its own shares, which lowers the share count.
  • working capital is the cash tied up in running the business day to day, such as inventory and customer bills, minus bills owed to suppliers.
  • limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, so it does not chase a rising stock.
  • stop loss is the price at which the reason for owning the stock has failed and the position is cut.
  • entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, measured from the entry price; 2.0 means two dollars of planned gain for each dollar at risk.
  • base is a sideways stretch of trading that builds the platform for the next move; a pivot is the top of that stretch, and a breakout is a move above it on rising volume, while a retest is a pullback that checks the level from above.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend in the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend; Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase between them.
  • measured move is a price target found by adding the height of a base to its breakout level.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Amazon's strength is that its businesses share assets and pay for each other. AWS is the clearest case. It reached $42.2 billion of sales in the June quarter, a $169 billion yearly pace, with $16.6 billion of operating income, and its signed backlog was $496 billion at June 30, up from $364 billion at March 31.[1][2][4] Amazon designs its own chips: the chips business passed a $25 billion yearly pace, and the company says Graviton, its general-purpose chip, is used by 98% of its 1,000 largest EC2 customers.[1] Own-design chips matter because they let AWS sell the same computing at a lower cost than buying every chip from outside suppliers, although AWS still buys Nvidia chips for customers who want them.[13]

The stores and advertising businesses give Amazon a second engine. Paid units grew 17% in the June quarter, the company delivered more than 40% more items same day or overnight in the first half than a year earlier, and advertising revenue rose 26% to $19.8 billion.[1][13] Advertising grows on top of shoppers who already arrive with a purchase in mind, which is why the North America segment's operating income rose to $9.1 billion on a 7.9% margin even though the company kept prices low.[1][13] Management also says Prime membership is growing at a double-digit rate, and the Prime Day sale moved into the June quarter this year, which flatters June and will depress the reported September growth rate by nearly 4 points.[13]

The weak points are cash, concentration and a crowded field. Spending has outrun cash from operations, so over twelve months Amazon produced $161.4 billion of operating cash but spent $169.0 billion on property and equipment, and it borrowed to bridge: long-term debt proceeds were $81.9 billion in the twelve months to June.[1] Backlog is also concentrated. The 10-Q describes three AWS commitments with Amazon's own investees: an earlier $38.0 billion OpenAI commitment, a $100.0 billion expansion over 8 years, and an Anthropic expansion of more than $100.0 billion over 10 years, which add up to at least $238 billion, or 48% of the $496 billion backlog if all three sit inside it (our sum; the filing does not say how much of each is counted).[2] Both labs are also Amazon's investees, so Amazon is financing some of the demand it later books.[2][23]

The competition has not stood still. In the quarter that ended September 30, published after the close on October 28, Google Cloud revenue rose 103% to $30.7 billion and Microsoft said Azure grew 48%, against AWS growth of 36.7% in the June quarter, which is the latest AWS figure until October 29.[27][25][1] Different quarters and different definitions limit the comparison, and Alphabet said sales of its TPU chip systems added to cloud growth.[27] The fair reading is that the whole cloud group is accelerating on AI demand, that AWS is growing slower than both rivals on the latest numbers, and that the October 29 figure for AWS is the one that tells us whether it is gaining or losing share.

Legal and governance checks

The largest legal item is the Federal Trade Commission's monopoly case. On October 9, 2026, the judge denied Amazon's request to delay the trial and wrote that the court had been preparing for a March 29, 2027 trial date.[24] The 10-Q lists the case among several antitrust and consumer-protection suits that seek billions of dollars of damages and structural remedies.[2] A separate Federal Trade Commission matter already cost $2.5 billion in the third quarter of 2025.[10][6] We found no accounting restatement, going-concern language or auditor warning in the filings we read.

Governance looks stable and founder-led. Jeffrey Bezos is executive chair and holds 950,434,581 shares, or 8.8% of the company, with Vanguard at 7.2% and BlackRock at 5.9%.[19] On September 8, 2026 the board added Kevin Mandia, who joined the audit and security committees; the filing discloses that his sister-in-law is an employee on a $185,000 salary under standard terms, which we treat as immaterial.[20] Management has repurchased no shares in the first half of 2025 or 2026, so owners are not being paid back in cash while the spending runs.[2]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's near-term forecasts have been cautious and beaten. In each of the last six quarters the company published a range for net sales and operating income, and actual sales beat the top of the range every time.[9][8][7][6][5][3][1] Operating income beat the top of the range in four of the six quarters and landed inside the range in the other two, just under the midpoint in the third quarter of 2025 and in the upper half in the fourth, and those two quarters carried disclosed charges of $4.3 billion and $2.4 billion.[6][10] The June 2026 quarter included $1.2 billion of one-time benefits from tariff refunds and an energy contract gain, so even without them operating income would have been $26.3 billion, above the $24.0 billion top of the range (our subtraction).[13][1]

Spending forecasts are the soft spot. The company's own 2025 estimate was about $125 billion and cash capital spending came in at $128.3 billion, 2.6% higher.[18][10] For 2026 it said about $200 billion in February and about $220 billion in July, a 10% increase blamed on the higher cost of memory chips.[5][13] The promise on power capacity, to double 2025 capacity by the end of 2027, is described by management as on track, but it cannot be checked until 2027.[13] Management also said in February that it expects 'strong long-term return on invested capital' from the spending, a claim the reported numbers do not yet show: our twelve-month figure is 11.5% on the reported capital base.[5]

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter puts five years of Amazon's results in front of you and then asks the Layer 1 question: does the company meet the fixed quality floors today, and is it moving toward or away from them. Revenue has grown at an even 11% to 12% a year since 2022, which gives a three-year compound rate of 11.7% from FY2022 to FY2025 (our calculation), and the twelve months to June 2026 grew 15.8%, the fastest of the period.[12][10][1] Operating income is the number that changed most: it rose from $12.2 billion in FY2022 to $93.7 billion over the latest twelve months, and the operating margin moved from 2.4% to 12.1% as AWS, advertising and fulfilment efficiency lifted profits.[11][1]

Two cautions apply to the profit lines. Net income of $135.3 billion over twelve months includes large gains on Amazon's stakes in AI labs: second-quarter net income of $62.6 billion includes $53.4 billion of non-operating income, primarily from Anthropic, and the first quarter included $16.8 billion of gains.[1][3] A further gain of $9.5 billion was booked in the third quarter of 2025, so the diluted earnings per share of $12.44 for twelve months is about 77% above what operating profit alone supports.[6][1] Our own estimate of earnings from operations alone is about $7.0 per share for the same twelve months (see Chapter 5), which is why the 20.5 price-to-earnings ratio in the ledger overstates how cheap the stock is: on that operating basis it is about 36 times.[33]

Cash tells the other half of the story. Cash from operations nearly doubled from $84.9 billion in FY2023 to $161.4 billion for twelve months, but purchases of property and equipment rose faster, from $52.7 billion to $173.0 billion, so the company's own free cash flow measure fell from $36.8 billion to an outflow of $7.6 billion.[11][1] That is a choice rather than a failure of the business: management says servers pay back in a little less than three years and data centers last 30 years or more.[13] The risk is timing, because the cash goes out before the revenue comes in, and the balance sheet shows how that has been financed.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 has a weakest assumption. This chapter names each one, says what evidence would break it and says when we will see that evidence. For the Reverse DCF, it is that after-tax operating profit becomes cash owners can take. For the 2027 earnings, it is the AWS operating margin. For the twelve-month value, it is the multiple. For the gains, it is that they are a one-off. We then give the red-team bear case in three parts, each of which could end the thesis on its own. We finish with the one claim the whole case hinges on.

Bear case 1: the backlog is real but concentrated, and Amazon is helping to fund it

The red-team argument starts with the $496 billion backlog. The 10-Q describes an earlier $38.0 billion OpenAI commitment. It adds a $100.0 billion OpenAI expansion over 8 years and an Anthropic expansion of more than $100.0 billion over 10 years. Together these are at least $238 billion, or 48% of the backlog if all of them are inside it.[2] Amazon also holds equity in both labs, carried at $220.2 billion at June 30. It bought a further $21.3 billion of OpenAI stock after quarter-end. It has a facility of up to $20.0 billion for Anthropic, of which $15.0 billion remained available after the June investments.[2][23] In the first half of 2026, other income of $69.1 billion was larger than operating income of $51.3 billion. Reported profit already leans on the lab valuations.[1]

Here is how it breaks. Suppose either lab cannot raise the money to pay for the compute it has promised. The signed backlog then turns out softer than it looks. The stakes are marked down through other income. Amazon has built capacity for a customer that cannot pay. A 10% mark-down of the $220.2 billion stakes is about $22 billion before tax. We would see the first evidence in the October 29 backlog figure. We would also see it in any change to the 6.4-year average life of contracts, and in the lab funding rounds that Amazon discloses in its 10-Q.[2] The weakest assumption this attacks is the one behind our central case: that backlog converts into revenue on schedule.

Bear case 2: spending outruns cash for longer than the market expects

Amazon plans about $220 billion of cash spending in 2026. Operating cash flow was $161.4 billion over the last twelve months, and depreciation was $75.2 billion. So cash after spending is negative. The company borrowed $81.9 billion of long-term debt in twelve months. Since June 30 it has added $25.0 billion of dollar notes and £4.24 billion of sterling notes.[13][1][2][21] Management argues that servers pay back in a little less than three years and that data centers last 30 years or more. It also changed the useful life of a subset of servers from six years to five in 2025, which raises depreciation. And it said memory prices lifted the 2026 plan by $20 billion.[13][10]

Here is how it breaks. If AWS growth slips toward 24% while depreciation keeps rising, the AWS margin could fall toward 31%. Earnings for 2027 would fall toward $8.00 per share, which is our bear case. The company also has $137.2 billion of leases not yet started and $130.1 billion of purchase obligations. These commitments are already made.[2] Leverage itself is not a threat for years. Debt is 0.93 times EBITDA after the new notes, by our calculation. The cost is that the stock gets no cash-flow cushion. We would see this in the 2026 spending number and the first view of 2027 spending on October 29. We would see more in the February 2027 report.

Bear case 3: share loss, then a legal overhang, when growth is the whole case

The latest competitor numbers show faster growth. Google Cloud grew 103% to $30.7 billion and Azure grew 48% in the September quarter. Oracle's cloud infrastructure revenue grew 121% in the August quarter. AWS grew 36.7% in June.[27][25][28][1] Part of Alphabet's growth is TPU system sales, and the quarters differ, so the comparison is imperfect.[27] Still, a stock priced on AWS acceleration cannot afford to be the slowest of the group two quarters running. Street previews expect AWS growth between about 35% and 37%, so even a good number is already in the price.[31][32]

On top of that sits the Federal Trade Commission's monopoly case. It is headed for trial on March 29, 2027, after the court refused a delay request on October 9. Consumer-protection claims and structural remedies are in play.[24][2] A ruling would come after the trial, so it is a 2027 risk rather than a 2026 one. It sets a ceiling on the multiple. We would see the share-loss signal on October 29 and in February 2027. We would see the legal signal on and after March 29, 2027.

7. What we would do, and when

Six terms need plain definitions before the numbers. A call option is a contract that gives its holder the right, but not the duty, to buy a stock at a fixed price, called the strike, up to an expiry date; one contract covers 100 shares. Delta says how much one share of option moves when the stock moves: a delta of 0.37 means the option gains about 37 cents for each dollar the stock gains, so one contract acts like 37 shares. Implied volatility is the size of price swings that an option's price assumes; a higher figure means a dearer option. Breakeven is the stock price at expiry at which the option's payoff just equals what was paid for it. A basis point is one hundredth of one percent, so 50 basis points is 0.50% of net worth. A risk tier, such as TIER_3, is our label for conviction: it sets the share of net worth we are willing to lose at the stop and the largest position size.
