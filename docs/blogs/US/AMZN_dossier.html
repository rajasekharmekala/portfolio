Before you read

Amazon runs a very large online store, the cloud business Amazon Web Services (AWS) and a fast-growing advertising business.[1] We conclude WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT on the shares, and we would cut the account's three call options to one. The business is strong, with AWS sales up 36.7% and a $496 billion backlog, but free cash flow is negative and earnings arrive on October 29.[1][2][8] We would buy only between $229.50 and $238.00, with a stop at $219.50 and a base target of $285. The view is wrong if AWS keeps speeding up and the price never pulls back, or if the spending hurts more than we expect.

1. What the company does

Amazon is three businesses sharing one logistics and technology base. The first is the stores: the Amazon website and app, third-party sellers who list goods on it, and a small chain of physical stores. The second is advertising, which sells promoted placement to the sellers and brands that already trade on the site. The third is AWS, which rents computing power, storage and artificial intelligence services to companies and governments. In the twelve months to June 2026 the company booked $775.7 billion of net sales, and AWS was 19.1% of that total by our calculation.[1]

Who pays, and for what, differs for each engine. Shoppers pay the product price and, if they join Prime, a yearly or monthly fee; the filings do not give a Prime member count, so we do not state one. Sellers pay commissions plus fees for storage, packing and shipping, and they supplied 61% of the paid units sold in June 2026.[1] Advertisers pay per click or per view: advertising services brought in $19.8 billion in the June quarter, up 26%, and $76.1 billion over the last four quarters by our addition.[1] AWS customers pay for what they use, and the largest ones sign multi-year commitments that sit in the backlog.

The money-making profile is lopsided. AWS earned a 36.8% operating margin over the last twelve months and produced $54.7 billion of operating income, which is 58% of the company total by our calculation.[1] North America stores earned a 7.4% margin and International stores 3.1% over the same period.[1] In plain terms, the stores bring the customers and the cash turnover, and AWS and advertising bring most of the profit. That mix is why the AWS growth rate matters more to the share price than any retail number.

The cost of staying ahead is large and rising. Amazon bought $173.0 billion of property and equipment in the last twelve months, mostly technology infrastructure for AWS and added fulfilment capacity, and management now expects about $220 billion of cash capital spending for 2026, up from $200 billion in February.[1][2][12] The company also holds a stake in the AI lab Anthropic, which produced $53.4 billion of pre-tax non-operating income in the June quarter, so reported net income of $62.6 billion is far above the $27.5 billion of operating income.[1] Smaller bets are still early: the March 2026 guide included about $1 billion of higher year-over-year Leo satellite costs, and the Zoox robotaxi service has only just won approval to charge for rides.[4][1]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • AWS (Amazon Web Services): Amazon's cloud unit, which rents servers, storage, chips and AI models over the internet.
  • Backlog: signed customer contracts for future services that have not yet been billed as revenue. The filing calls it performance obligations, and the glossary term RPO means remaining performance obligations.
  • Capital expenditure — cash spent on long-term plant and equipment — (capex): money spent on buildings, servers and equipment that last several years.
  • Free cash flow: cash from operations minus the cash spent on property and equipment, so it shows what is left after the business keeps up and expands its assets.
  • Trainium and Graviton: chips Amazon designs itself. Trainium is for AI training and inference, and Graviton is a general-purpose processor.
  • Non-operating income: gains or losses outside the core business, here mostly the rise in value of Amazon's stake in Anthropic. It is not cash.
  • Third-party seller services: fees Amazon earns when independent sellers use its marketplace and fulfilment network.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

AWS has the clearest advantage. Sales grew 36.7% in the June quarter, the fastest in 18 quarters, after growing 17%, 17%, 20%, 24% and 28% in the five quarters before it.[1][11] The backlog tells the same story: $244 billion at December 31, 2025, $364 billion at March 31, 2026 and $496 billion at June 30, 2026, with the average remaining life of the contracts rising from 4.1 to 6.4 years.[5][7][2] Management says AWS still cannot supply all the demand it has: the CEO said that even at $220 billion of capital spending, the company will not have enough capacity in 2026, and that he expects the same in 2027.[12] That is a strong position, because customers are waiting for the product rather than the product waiting for customers.

The moat has three layers. Scale comes first: AWS is a $169 billion annualized business.[13] Custom chips are the second layer: Amazon says its chips business and its AI business each passed a $25 billion yearly run rate, growing triple digits, and that Anthropic and OpenAI have each made multi-year, multi-gigawatt commitments to Trainium.[1] The third layer is the commitment contracts themselves. The 10-Q says AWS and OpenAI expanded an existing $38.0 billion commitment by $100.0 billion over 8.0 years, and AWS and Anthropic expanded their agreement by more than $100.0 billion over 10.0 years.[2] Long contracts make revenue visible, but they also tie AWS to two young AI labs, which is the main concentration risk in this report.

The stores and advertising businesses add a second, quieter advantage. North America operating margin was 7.9% in the June quarter and 7.4% over twelve months, against 6.9% for the twelve months to December 2025.[1] Advertising grew 26% to $19.8 billion.[1] Management says delivery speeds hit records, with over 40% more items delivered same-day or overnight in the first half.[1] The caution is cost: worldwide shipping costs rose 19% in the quarter while paid units rose 17%, so each unit cost slightly more to ship.[1] The CFO also pointed to higher fuel and trucking costs.[13]

Where the moat is thinner

Competitors are not standing still. Alphabet reported that Google Cloud revenue rose 82% to $24.8 billion in its June quarter, and Microsoft reported Microsoft Cloud revenue of $59.3 billion, up 27%.[24][25] AWS is still larger than Google Cloud, at $42.2 billion, but it is growing at less than half of Google's rate, so some share shift is possible.[1] Walmart, the largest store rival, grew global eCommerce 23% and advertising 38% in its latest quarter, which shows retail rivals are building the same advertising model.[26]

The regulatory moat also has holes. The Federal Trade Commission's marketplace monopoly case is set for trial on March 29, 2027, after a judge refused to delay it on October 9, 2026; the case says Amazon's anti-discounting rules and Prime fulfilment rules illegally protect its online store position.[19][20] A separate Prime enrollment case ended in a $2.5 billion settlement, with $845 million of refunds issued by September 2026.[22][21] Amazon has disputed the government's framing from the start.[19] A loss at trial could change fee structures, and that would be felt in the highest-margin part of retail.

Last, the quality of reported profit is a weakness. In the first half of 2026 the company booked $62.8 billion of upward value adjustments on private-company investments, primarily Anthropic, and $50.5 billion of that came in the June quarter alone.[2] Anthropic itself said in late May that a funding round valued it at $965 billion.[23] Those gains inflate earnings per share without adding cash, and they can reverse. Our work therefore uses operating income and cash flow, not net income, to judge the business.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management guidance — management's official forecast — for profit has been cautious and beaten. For the March 2026 quarter, Amazon guided to net sales of $173.5 billion to $178.5 billion and operating income of $16.5 billion to $21.5 billion; it reported $181.5 billion and $23.9 billion.[4][3] For the June 2026 quarter, it guided to $194.0 billion to $199.0 billion and $20.0 billion to $24.0 billion; it reported $200.6 billion and $27.5 billion.[3][1] The call summary says June operating income included a $1.2 billion benefit from tariff refunds and energy contract accounting; without it, income of $26.3 billion by our subtraction would still be above the top of the range.[13]

Spending guidance is different. In February the company said 2026 capital spending would be about $200 billion, and it held that number in April.[12] In July it raised the figure to about $220 billion, and the CEO blamed higher memory chip prices and more capacity needs.[12][13] The promise on power was kept so far: management said it expects to have double the power capacity at the end of 2027 that it had in 2025, and that it remains on that track.[13] On cash returned to owners, the company repurchased no shares in the first half of 2025 or 2026, and $6.1 billion remains on a $10.0 billion authorization from 2022.[2]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Over five years Amazon grew sales from $469.8 billion in 2021 to $716.9 billion in 2025, and to $775.7 billion over the twelve months to June 2026, a 15.8% rise on the prior twelve months.[6][5][1] Operating income grew faster than sales because AWS, advertising and logistics efficiency added profit: operating margin went from 5.3% in 2021 to 12.1% over the last year. The three-year sales growth rate from 2022 to 2025 was 11.7% a year by our calculation, and three-year profit growth is not meaningful because 2022 was a net loss.

Cash tells a harder story. Cash from operations rose from $46.3 billion in 2021 to $139.5 billion in 2025 and $161.4 billion over the last twelve months.[6][5][1] Net capital spending rose even faster, from $48.1 billion in 2023 to $169.0 billion over the last twelve months, so free cash flow, which is cash from operations minus that spending, fell from $38.2 billion in 2024 to minus $7.6 billion.[1] Management says the spending is for AI customers who have signed long contracts, and that servers on average pay back in a little less than three years.[13]

Earnings quality needs a warning. Net income was $135.3 billion over the last twelve months, but the cash flow statement subtracts $79.8 billion of non-operating income to reach cash from operations, and most of that is the Anthropic gain.[1][3][2] The session ledger, which comes from a data vendor, shows free cash flow of $3.2 billion and a trailing price-to-earnings ratio of 19.95; we do not use either, because the company's own filing shows free cash flow of minus $7.6 billion and the earnings include investment gains.[28][1]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is weaker than the rest. This chapter names each weak point, says what evidence would show it wrong and when that evidence arrives. Fatal flaw 1: AI demand digests before the spending pays back

Amazon plans about $220 billion of cash capital spending in 2026.[12] Part of the demand story is two large AI-lab commitments. The 10-Q names an expansion of $100.0 billion over 8.0 years with OpenAI and one of more than $100.0 billion over 10.0 years with Anthropic.[2] The filing does not say how much of either sits in the $496 billion backlog, so we cannot size the concentration, only flag it. Both customers are young companies that need outside funding, and Amazon holds a stake in one of them. If funding tightens, contracts can be renegotiated, capacity can sit idle and the $190 bear price becomes likely. Fatal flaw 2: the investment gains reverse and the cash gap lasts longer than expected

Net income of $62.6 billion in the June quarter included $53.4 billion of non-operating income, mostly Anthropic mark-ups that are not cash.[1] Anthropic reportedly filed confidentially for an initial public offering in June, so a market price will soon test the $965 billion valuation behind those gains.[23] A lower value would cut reported earnings without touching cash. The cash side is the second part: management itself expects free cash flow pressure until the data centers under construction earn money, and debt already doubled to $133.0 billion at face value.[13][2] If the market stops lending at current terms, spending would slow and growth would follow. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days: the SMA (simple moving average) weights every day equally, and the EMA (exponential moving average) weights recent days more. CMP means current market price, here $248.00, and a basis point (bps) is one hundredth of one percent, so 50 bps is 0.50%.
