1. What the company does

Unum Group sells income protection to employees through their employers. If a worker cannot work because of illness or injury, a Unum policy pays part of their salary. The company earns money in two ways: it collects premiums (about $10.8 billion in 2025) and it invests the premiums while it waits to pay claims (about $2.0 billion of net investment income in 2025).[1]

The four segments

Unum US is the largest, covering group disability, group life and supplemental products. Colonial Life sells voluntary benefits at the workplace. Unum International is mostly the United Kingdom group income protection business. The Closed Block holds older individual disability and long-term care policies that are no longer sold.[1] In 2025 adjusted operating income by segment was Unum US $1,271.9 million, Colonial Life $463.6 million, International $152.3 million and the Closed Block $63.5 million.[1]

Why long-term care matters to the whole company

Long-term care pays for nursing homes and home care years after a policy was sold. Prices were set decades ago on assumptions that proved too optimistic. In the third quarter of 2025 Unum cut its reserve assumptions, a pretax charge of $478.5 million, which dragged 2025 net income down to $738.5 million from $1,779.1 million a year earlier.[1] In July 2026 the company agreed to pass the rest of its individual long-term care block (about $3.8 billion of statutory reserves, about 50,000 policies) to Fortitude Re, with closing expected in 2026 pending regulatory approval.[6][7]

Glossary

Adjusted operating income is profit before investment gains and one-off items, and it is the number management guides to. A benefit ratio is claims paid as a share of premiums, so a higher ratio means less profit. RBC ratio (risk-based capital) compares an insurer's capital to the minimum regulators require, and Unum reported about 480% at mid-2026.[4] Reinsurance is when one insurer pays another to take over a block of risk. A Weinstein stage is a four-step reading of the price trend, where Stage 2 is an advance and Stage 3 is a stall near the highs.

What this means for an owner: you own a slow-growing group benefits business whose profit is predictable, plus a long-term care block whose cost can jump. The next chapter asks whether the good business outweighs the bad one.

6. Why estimates might be wrong

Three fatal-flaw scenarios would break our rejection, or our caution, depending on direction.

Red team: the fatal flaws

  1. The median multiple is too low. The stock re-rated from about 5.9x to about 10.5x as the long-term care risk shrank. If the old block is truly gone after the Fortitude Re deal, the right multiple may be nearer 10x, which would erase the negative base reward. Hinge claim: the 8.65x anchor.

Takeaway: the largest risk to our reject call is the multiple. Even if it is wrong, the Stage 3 and NO_MOMENTUM failures are independent of it.
