Chapter 1

Cheniere's modern identity was not present at its founding. The company began in 1996 as an oil-and-gas exploration business. It moved into LNG infrastructure when it developed the Sabine Pass terminal in Louisiana as an import and regasification facility, which entered service in 2008. Regasification is the reverse of liquefaction: a terminal receives LNG shipped from abroad, warms it back into gas and sends that gas into pipelines. The asset was designed for a United States that expected to import more natural gas. [1][2]

The strategic reversal followed the rapid growth of domestic shale gas supply and a change in global gas-market conditions. In 2010 Cheniere announced a plan to add liquefaction and export capability at Sabine Pass. The turn was consequential because a receiving terminal is not automatically an export plant: the company needed federal authorization, liquefaction trains, feed-gas access, marine facilities, long-term buyers and a financing structure capable of carrying construction before the new equipment generated operating cash. Cheniere's existing site, storage, docks and pipeline connections offered a base to build from, but the export conversion still required a multi-year capital program. [1][2][4]

The commercial design was as important as the concrete. In 2011 Cheniere signed its first LNG sale and purchase agreement indexed to the U.S. Henry Hub gas benchmark, with a flexible contract structure. In 2012 it became the first company in the contiguous United States to receive federal authorization to begin construction of liquefaction services, made a positive final investment decision for the first two Sabine Pass trains and started construction. More trains followed, and a second, greenfield export site began development near Corpus Christi in South Texas. The change converted Cheniere from a developer centered on import capacity into an integrated LNG exporter that connects North American gas supply to overseas demand. [1][2]

The financial cycle is a construction-to-cash-flow cycle

The audited ten-year record makes the operating transition visible. In 2016 revenue was $1.283 billion, net income attributable to Cheniere was a loss of $610 million, operating cash flow was negative $404 million and capital expenditure was $4.356 billion. By 2021, revenue had grown to $15.864 billion, but the company still reported a $2.343 billion attributable net loss while operating cash flow was $2.469 billion and capital spending had fallen to $966 million. These are different signals: construction can turn into physical capacity before accounting earnings settle, and derivative marks, financing costs, commissioning and noncontrolling interests can separate net income from cash generated. [12][2]

In the 2022–2025 period, the completed platform produced a different cash pattern. Revenue rose to $33.428 billion in 2022 and then moved to $20.394 billion in 2023, $15.703 billion in 2024 and $19.976 billion in 2025. Operating cash flow was $10.523 billion, $8.418 billion, $5.394 billion and $5.539 billion in those respective years. Capital spending was much lower than in the initial buildout, but it did not disappear: it ranged from $1.830 billion in 2022 to $3.078 billion in 2025 as Cheniere returned to expansion. The visible change is not that the company stopped being cyclical; it is that a large installed base began throwing off operating cash while new trains again absorbed growth capital. [12]

Revenue is not a clean proxy for physical throughput in every year. LNG volumes, contract mix, the timing of cargo recognition, feed-gas costs, delivered versus FOB obligations and noncash derivative fair-value movements can all move reported results. The 2023 earnings peak, for example, included $8.0 billion of derivative fair-value gains disclosed in the 2025 Form 10-K's segment note. In 2026, the direction reversed: management attributed most of the nine-month decline in attributable net income versus 2025 to $3.6 billion of unfavorable derivative fair-value changes, predominantly from long-term Integrated Production Marketing agreements, even as recognized delivery volumes and Adjusted EBITDA increased. You should therefore read net income, cash flow and shipped cargoes together, not treat a single annual figure as the whole operating story. [2][3]

A pivot does not erase the old risk

Cheniere's first major success was transforming an import-oriented terminal into export infrastructure. That conversion gave the company a head start in permitting, site access and connections, but it did not make subsequent trains inexpensive or automatic. Sabine Pass and Corpus Christi still depend on permits, contractors, reliable feed gas, customer commitments, financing and multi-year construction schedules. A repeatable platform can reduce some cost and execution burdens; it cannot eliminate project-cycle risk. [1][2][7]

The development did not remain confined to one site. In 2015 Cheniere made a positive FID for the first Corpus Christi trains while it continued expanding Sabine Pass. That decision spread the operating platform along the Gulf Coast but also created overlapping construction, permitting and financing needs. The two locations ultimately offered separate marine and pipeline systems, but during the build they competed for management attention, contractors and capital. A two-terminal footprint is therefore both a source of operating breadth and a record of accumulated project risk. [1][2]

Cargo milestones help you separate physical adoption from accounting performance. Cheniere reported its 1,000th cargo in January 2020, less than four years after its first shipment, and passed 3,000 cumulative cargoes by 2023. In August 2026 it announced its 5,000th cargo and substantial completion of CCL Stage 3. These milestones say that the platform repeatedly produced, loaded and exported LNG; they do not show margin per cargo, customer payment timing or the return earned on each train. [1][10][11]

The production cycle has several sources of lumpiness even after a train enters service. Maintenance takes equipment offline; ambient temperature affects LNG output; new trains ramp through commissioning; and the number of cargoes delivered and recognized in any quarter depends on scheduling and contract transfer points. The 10-K warns that operating profit may not be generated evenly through the year because of production levels and maintenance timing, with weather affecting output. A high annual nameplate figure should therefore be treated as potential throughput, not a guaranteed sales line. [2]

The 2022–2025 period shows the second version of the cycle: the installed base throws off cash, then new construction takes a larger share of that cash. CFO was $10.523 billion in 2022 and $8.418 billion in 2023, while capital spending remained near $2 billion in each year. By 2025 CFO was $5.539 billion and capex $3.078 billion. The annual figures are affected by derivative accounting and working-capital movements, but CFO less capex stayed positive through these four years. This is a more useful description of an operating company funding another build phase than a claim that results have become smooth. [12][2]

The output milestones underline how much of the pivot was execution rather than a single announcement. Trains 1 and 2 at Sabine Pass entered service during 2016; the site reached all six trains over several years. Corpus Christi introduced a second export location and then added Stage 3, whose first train began producing in December 2024 and final train reached substantial completion in 2026. The company has moved from an import-terminal conversion to a paired platform that can support overlapping maintenance, shipments and expansion projects. [1][3][4][5][10]

Cheniere's capacity figures must also be read by development stage. The 2026 Q3 release described approximately 56 mtpa operating, approximately 5 mtpa under construction or commissioning and more than 40 mtpa in the permitting process. These numbers mark a transition from the completed first build to a new investment queue, but the latter categories are not operating capacity. CCL Stage 4 remained in regulatory review, and the first phase at Sabine Pass remained conditional on permits, commercial arrangements and financing. The business is again balancing cash from mature trains against capital required to make later trains real. [3][7]

The early export ramp and construction-cycle figures are grounded in Cheniere's original 2016–2021 annual Form 10-K filings, rather than a prior published dossier. [24][25][26][27][28][29]

Chapter 2

The most important segment fact is the one companies sometimes bury beneath a list of facilities: Cheniere reports one operating and reportable segment. Its CEO receives consolidated operating and financial information, and the company describes its activities as an integrated LNG service. Sabine Pass, Corpus Christi, their connected pipelines, commercial marketing, and delivery support are not separately reported business segments. Do not divide company revenue or operating profit across these physical assets as if Cheniere disclosed a plant-by-plant profit statement; it does not. [2]

The operating segment combines several linked tasks. Cheniere and affiliates acquire natural gas, secure pipeline transportation, liquefy the feed gas, store LNG, load cargoes and sell LNG under long-term sale and purchase agreements, Integrated Production Marketing agreements, or short-, medium- and long-term marketing arrangements. Some cargoes are sold free-on-board at the terminal; for delivered sales, Cheniere arranges shipping and delivery to a receiving terminal. These are activities in one integrated business, not evidence of separately disclosed revenue segments. [2][3]

Two terminals, a single operating system

Sabine Pass in Cameron Parish, Louisiana has six operating trains, approximately 30 mtpa of production capacity, more than 4.7 Bcf/d of gas-processing capability, five storage tanks, three marine berths and multiple pipeline connections. Its network gives the facility access to gas supplies from a broad set of continental regions east of the Rocky Mountains. Corpus Christi in Texas has ten operating trains after completion of Stage 3, more than 25 mtpa of nominal production capacity, two docks, three storage tanks and pipeline access to supplies including the Permian Basin. Facility scale and physical access are part of the operating advantage, but disclosed site capacity is not a revenue or profit split. [4][5][6][10]

A third-party estimate would still be needed to assign a current revenue share to either facility. Cheniere's consolidated external-customer revenue includes LNG sales and other activity, while the filing's segment note says management evaluates a single integrated business. The same caution applies to the marketing affiliate. It can sell uncontracted production, purchase third-party LNG and market it onward; that is a commercial channel within the segment, not a separately reported profit pool. [2]

A customer journey: Petrobras and a free-on-board SPA

Petrobras provides a concrete example of the only reported segment's output. Cheniere Marketing, LLC signed an agreement under which Petrobras will purchase approximately 0.8 million tonnes of LNG per year on a free-on-board basis for 22 years. Neither the Cheniere announcement nor Petrobras's release discloses the contract's price, so no per-unit charge should be attached to this example. The general long-term SPA architecture disclosed in the 10-K includes a fixed fee on contracted volume and a variable component, but the precise Petrobras formula is unavailable and should not be inferred from other contracts. Petrobras says long-term volumes are intended to reduce exposure to short-term LNG-market volatility, strengthen gas-portfolio risk management and improve flexibility and security in serving its contracts. Petrobras operates a regasification terminal at Guanabara Bay in Rio de Janeiro, a relevant receiving point for the customer's downstream system. [2][3][13]

The route from resource to buyer begins with Cheniere Marketing's commercial agreement and Cheniere's gas procurement from U.S. producers. Firm transportation moves feed gas through pipeline networks to a liquefaction site. Gas treatment and the refrigeration process cool the gas to liquid form, reducing its volume to roughly one-six-hundredth of its gaseous volume. Storage tanks hold the LNG between production and loading. Cheniere schedules a cargo at the terminal and loads a vessel. Under FOB terms, Petrobras takes delivery at the export terminal and arranges the vessel and the ocean voyage; Petrobras then manages transport to its import terminal and regasification system. The resulting gas can be integrated into its wider supply portfolio. Each stage depends on specifications, vessel scheduling, export authorizations and receiving capacity. [2][3][4][5][13]

The commercial distinction is important. In a delivered-at-place sale, Cheniere arranges chartering and delivery to one or more designated terminals. That shifts vessel procurement and voyage execution toward Cheniere and the accounting transfer point follows the contract's delivery conditions. With FOB, the buyer arranges transport after loading and retains destination flexibility subject to law and the agreement. Petrobras's SPA is explicitly FOB; its contract price is not publicly disclosed in the announcement. [2][3]

Do not turn facilities into fictional segments

You can learn a great deal about the two terminals from their capacities, tanks, docks and gas networks. You cannot infer a facility's revenue share, segment margin or stand-alone profit from those operating facts. Cheniere's audited filing explicitly presents one operating and reportable segment; absent a reported allocation, any exact split would be invented. [2][4][5]

The group structure explains why segment profit and economic ownership are not identical. Cheniere consolidates Cheniere Partners, which owns the Sabine Pass liquefaction business. At year-end 2025, the parent owned 100% of the general partner and 48.6% of the limited-partner interests, together with incentive distribution rights. Consolidation places the controlled subsidiary's revenues and expenses into group statements; noncontrolling interests then identify the earnings and equity attributable to outside owners. Treating all consolidated profit as if it belonged to the parent would overstate the economics available to Cheniere shareholders. [2]

The filing does disclose revenue by customer geography, based on the principal place of business of the counterparty, but geography does not identify where a cargo was produced or which facility earned a margin. The customer-location table therefore answers a different question from segment reporting. A cargo can originate at Sabine Pass, be sold through a marketing affiliate to a counterparty in one jurisdiction, and eventually reach a receiving terminal elsewhere. The source's accounting geography follows the agreement counterparty, not an operational profit center. [2]

Keep nameplate capacity separate from sold volume. CCL's ten trains and more than 25 mtpa are operating-site capacity facts after Stage 3; the EIA described the project in Bcf/d terms and distinguished nominal capacity from peak capacity. A company can load fewer tonnes than nominal capacity because of commissioning, planned maintenance, weather, operating performance, pipeline supply or cargo schedules. For the same reason, the reported 2026 cargo and TBtu totals are throughput indicators, not an allocation of revenue among trains. [3][5][6]

The Petrobras deal also illustrates the difference between a commercial product and an inferred price. The buyer is Petrobras, not a retail customer; the product is LNG, not an open-ended terminal reservation; the disclosed obligation is approximately 0.8 mtpa FOB for 22 years. Petrobras identifies portfolio flexibility and lower dependence on short-term LNG purchases as the reason for buying long-term volumes. Cheniere's platform supplies the gas chain through procurement, pipeline access and liquefaction, but the 2026 announcement does not disclose the term's index, fixed fee or total value. The company's generic SPA description cannot be used to reverse-engineer Petrobras's exact confidential pricing formula. [2][3][13]

One segment does not mean the financial statements lack detail. The 2026 third-quarter release separately reported LNG revenue, regasification revenue and other revenue. Those categories help describe what customers paid for during that quarter, but they do not disclose operating profit by product, terminal or sales channel. The filing does not tell us what net income, EBITDA or free cash flow each revenue category produced. [2][3]

The go-to-market model is built around negotiated commercial relationships rather than a downstream reseller network. Cheniere Marketing markets available volumes directly through SPAs and shorter-term sales, while the terminal subsidiaries perform liquefaction services and coordinate operational delivery. If the company buys third-party LNG, its marketing affiliate can place those molecules into the broader portfolio. The channel is specialized and institutional, so credit evaluation, destination terms, cargo windows and shipping choices replace broad retail acquisition metrics. [2][3]

The October 2026 QatarEnergy agreements show another form of customer acquisition: long-dated offtake used to underpin future brownfield capacity. SPL and CCL signed agreements for up to 4 mtpa on an FOB basis, beginning in the early 2030s and running through 2054; 1 mtpa depends on an SPL Expansion FID and other conditions. The 2026 Q3 release does not publish the contract prices. This is evidence that Cheniere is extending the commercial book, not a new reportable segment and not an operating-capacity addition today. [3]

The 2025 segment disclosure also defines the profit boundary. The CEO is the CODM, the executive team is organized by function rather than by facility, and financial and operating information is assessed on a single basis consistent with the integrated offering. Net income attributable to Cheniere is the profit measure most consistent with GAAP that the CODM regularly receives. It is therefore inappropriate to reverse-allocate that number across trains using capacity as a proxy. [2]

The company also gives a consolidated customer-concentration test: no external customer accounted for 10% or more of 2025 revenue from contracts with external customers. That is meaningful evidence about annual revenue dependence, but it is not a count of SPAs or a facility mix. The filing separately says customer balances can be more concentrated in receivables and contract assets. Revenue diversity describes who bought during the period; it does not show where the cargo was produced or whether every receivable is equally collectible. [2]

Read the segment note as a description of what management monitors, not as a claim that the operation is simple. The CODM reviews consolidated results, total assets and significant cost categories while making resource-allocation decisions. Train-level engineering data and capacity tables remain useful for assessing execution, but the filing does not transform them into standalone segment accounts. For a reader, the right synthesis is one integrated LNG segment, product revenue categories where reported, one GAAP profit measure and no published profit allocation among Sabine Pass, Corpus Christi and marketing. [2][3]

Chapter 3

Cheniere does not sell LNG through retail outlets, dealers or a software-style reseller channel. Its go-to-market is direct, contract-led B2B selling through Cheniere Marketing and the terminal subsidiaries. The core customer instruments are long-term SPAs; spot and other short- or medium-term sales handle uncontracted production and trading opportunities. Some agreements are arranged with creditworthy utilities, portfolio companies, national energy firms and other LNG buyers. The seller's commercial team negotiates volume, delivery window, duration, cost allocation, fees, transfer point, cancellation rights, destination flexibility and credit protection. Contract terms differ; the 2025 Form 10-K describes the overall structures rather than publishing every confidential SPA. [2]

The supply chain starts upstream of the terminal. Cheniere buys natural gas from a network of producers and holds pipeline transportation capacity to move that gas to Sabine Pass and Corpus Christi. Sabine Pass has multiple interstate and intrastate connections and access to supply east of the Rocky Mountains; Corpus Christi's system connects to supply regions including the Permian. The company also has long-term transportation agreements. Pipeline access is a strategic input, not a decorative convenience: a liquefaction plant cannot run at design rates if gas is unavailable, a connection is constrained or transport capacity fails. [2][4][5]

At the terminal, gas is conditioned and passed through a train's refrigeration system. Cooling turns gas into a liquid at cryogenic temperature and compresses its volume to about one-six-hundredth of the gaseous state. Liquefaction consumes fuel and power; equipment must be maintained; and LNG is buffered in tanks before marine loading. Berths, loading arms, channel access, storage and pipeline redundancy each impose their own operating limits. The shipment calendar must match production, vessel arrival windows, export approvals and customer nominations. [2][4][5]

Contract economics: distinguish the gas molecule from the liquefaction service

Cheniere's 2025 Form 10-K describes long-term SPAs with fixed fees on contracted volumes, generally payable even if a buyer elects not to lift or suspends a cargo, subject to the contract. It also describes a variable fee that is primarily indexed to Henry Hub and is generally structured to cover gas purchases, transportation and liquefaction fuel. This combination gives the seller a fixed-fee component and a feed-gas pass-through, while leaving important contract, fuel, transport, basis, credit and operating details to the actual agreement. Do not transfer one SPA's terms to another. [2]

IPM agreements work differently. In the Form 10-K description, a gas producer sells gas to Cheniere on a global LNG or natural-gas index basis, less fixed liquefaction, shipping and other costs; the linked sale of LNG and purchase of gas can expose Cheniere to timing and index movements. Derivatives may hedge economic exposures, but accounting can recognize fair-value changes before physical delivery. This explains why a long-term contract book can stabilize a portion of expected commercial cash flow while reported earnings remain volatile. [2][3]

Revenue recognition and working-capital timing

FOB and delivered contracts can create different timing between loading, control transfer, delivery, invoicing and cash collection. For a delivered cargo, the ship may still be at sea at period end; Cheniere reported 61 TBtu of LNG sold on a delivered basis in transit at September 30, 2026. Its 10-K also reports contract assets arising primarily from SPAs with tiered payment structures: the company may have transferred goods or services and recognized a right to consideration before that consideration is due. Contract assets were $424 million at December 31, 2025, compared with $331 million a year earlier. [2][3]

The company did not publish a verified days-sales-outstanding figure in the evidence used here, so I will not manufacture one from annual revenue and a period-end receivable balance. Nor does the absence of a customer representing 10% of annual revenue mean collection exposure is evenly distributed: at December 31, 2025, one customer represented 15% of combined trade receivables and contract assets. The practical monitoring question is whether invoiced balances, contract assets, collateral postings, deferred revenue and cash receipts move in line with cargo delivery and the contracted payment schedule. The revenue-to-cash path is not simply invoice, then collection. Under a tiered SPA payment schedule, Cheniere can recognize a contract asset when the customer consideration is not yet due; deferred revenue captures cash or consideration received before the related performance obligation is satisfied. Margin deposits and derivative balances can also move with market exposure and contract timing. These are not all routine trade receivables, so a simple receivable-to-revenue ratio would combine different economic items. The 2025 Form 10-K describes the categories and the tiered-payment origin of contract assets but does not publish a DSO series. [2]

At the portfolio level, Cheniere says it aims to contract about 90% of current and planned liquefaction capacity under long-term SPAs and IPM agreements. Contracting future output may help support financing and de-risk the demand side, but a contracted tonne is still a promise to perform against a physical supply chain. Pipeline reliability, plant availability, export authorization and customer credit determine how well the contract converts into cash. The 2026 QatarEnergy SPA also makes the conditional nature of some growth support visible: 1 mtpa of the up-to-4 mtpa agreement depends on an SPL Expansion FID and other conditions. [2][3]

The linked purchase and sale positions are one reason the commercial platform can show derivative balances that look larger or move more sharply than the cash economics of a delivered cargo. A fair-value change can enter reported cost of sales before the associated LNG is delivered; later, the physical gas and LNG sale settle under their contract terms. Cheniere's own non-GAAP reconciliations remove qualifying fair-value changes when calculating Adjusted EBITDA, but the GAAP statement still records them. For a unit-economics review, separate the timing of mark-to-market accounting from the eventual physical purchase, liquefaction and customer delivery. [2][3]

Maintenance and weather are operational inputs into the delivery schedule, not afterthoughts. The company reports that production levels and maintenance timing can make operating profit uneven across quarters and that temperature affects facility output. Each train must be balanced with planned downtime, pipeline nominations, storage inventory and marine berth availability. Cheniere's tanks and multiple docks provide useful scheduling flexibility, but no public metric in the cited materials quantifies how much buffer each cargo schedule has in a normal operating quarter. [2][4][5]

Chapter 4

Cheniere is in a hard infrastructure business. A new export plant needs years of permits, design, equipment, construction and financing. It also needs gas supply, pipelines, export rights, buyers and an experienced crew. Gulf Coast sites give Cheniere a head start. It already has two terminals, docks, tanks, pipeline links and a record of cargoes. Yet LNG is a global product with many sellers. Buyers can compare price, contract terms, reliability, emissions data and delivery options. Cheniere's 10-K says sellers compete for new and renewed SPAs on price and commercial terms. [2][4][5]

The hard-to-copy asset is not a single train. It is the accumulated operating system around the trains: two coastal locations, feed-gas procurement across several producing regions, firm transport arrangements, storage, marine access, marketing, portfolio optimization and long-term customer relationships. Sabine Pass has six trains, five tanks, three berths and multiple pipeline links; Corpus Christi operates ten trains after Stage 3 and accesses Permian and other regional gas through its pipeline connections. Those networks can lower the incremental burden of expansions and give Cheniere a choice of routes and cargo execution. The moat is scale plus operating know-how, rather than consumer-brand loyalty. [2][4][5][10]

The asset base does not make expansion risk-free. Cheniere's Q3 2026 release described approximately 56 mtpa in operation, approximately 5 mtpa under construction or commissioning, and more than 40 mtpa in the regulatory permitting process. Those are separate project stages, not one committed total. For CCL Stage 4, Cheniere reported that FERC issued a draft environmental impact statement in September; a draft EIS is not a final authorization, and commercial support, financing and a positive FID still stand between a filing and an operating train. [3][20]

Competition is also rising from within the United States and from established LNG exporters. Cheniere's 2025 Form 10-K describes competition for new and renewed SPAs across projects around the world. EIA reported that U.S. LNG exports averaged 17.4 Bcf/d in the first half of 2026, 23% above the prior-year period, and forecast 18.7 Bcf/d in the first half of 2027 as new export capacity ramps. That increase supports the scale of the U.S. platform while giving buyers more alternatives; it is a supply-growth signal, not proof by itself of a global glut. [2][18]

A competitive edge also has to survive changing buyer requirements. Cheniere reports methane-measurement and emissions-transparency initiatives, including its 2025 recognition under the Oil and Gas Methane Partnership 2.0 Gold Standard. Such practices may help document supply-chain attributes for customers and regulators, but they do not eliminate emissions regulation, climate-policy risk or the need to control operational performance. The 10-K describes changing disclosure and methane requirements in importing markets as a live operating consideration. [2][11]

A second capacity comparison matters: Cheniere is both a scale leader and a participant in a sector adding competing capacity. EIA identified Corpus Christi as the second-largest U.S. LNG facility after Stage 3 completion, while its 2026 data show rising U.S. export throughput as new trains ramp. Scale can improve contracting reach and scheduling flexibility; a larger pool of supply can give buyers more alternatives when negotiating future SPAs. The moat is strongest when scale is paired with reliable operation and disciplined project economics, not when nameplate capacity alone is treated as bargaining power. [2][6][18]

Earnings quality: the derivative bridge is too large to ignore

The 10-K disclosed net gains from changes in derivative fair values of $8.0 billion in 2023, $1.3 billion in 2024 and $3.6 billion in 2025. These are noncash changes recognized before the related physical delivery or termination; they are part of GAAP earnings but can reverse or settle in different periods. The company's Adjusted EBITDA calculation removes qualifying derivative fair-value changes to make operating trends more comparable, and DCF then makes further adjustments for interest, maintenance capital expenditures, cash taxes, other items and noncontrolling interests. Both are company-defined non-GAAP measures, not substitutes for GAAP net income or audited CFO. [2][3]

The 2026 interim record is an unusually clear illustration. Third-quarter revenue was $6.173 billion and net income attributable to Cheniere was $1.170 billion; nine-month revenue was $17.773 billion and attributable net income was $736 million. Nine-month Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA was $6.037 billion and Cheniere DCF was $4.07 billion. Management attributed the nine-month year-over-year GAAP net-income decline chiefly to $3.6 billion of unfavorable derivative fair-value variances tied mainly to IPM agreements, partly offset by higher volumes and margins and a nonrecurring excise-tax credit. That earnings divergence is an accounting-timing warning, not evidence that the cash-flow statement can be ignored. [3][20]

Cheniere is capital intensive and uses subsidiary project-financing structures. At December 31, 2025 the comparative balance sheet showed approximately $22.8 billion of current and long-term debt and roughly $2.7 billion of operating lease liabilities. Cheniere owned 100% of the general partner and 48.6% of the limited-partner interest in Cheniere Partners, with incentive distribution rights; consolidated statements therefore include activity attributable to outside owners. Noncontrolling interests were $5.027 billion in stockholders' equity at year-end 2025. A consolidated dollar of revenue or cash flow is not automatically a dollar available to Cheniere common shareholders. [2][3]

The capital return record mixes debt repayment, dividends, buybacks and growth capital. In the first nine months of 2026 Cheniere repurchased 7.7 million shares for $1.8 billion, paid $347 million of dividends, invested $2.8 billion in growth capital and repaid $307 million of consolidated long-term debt. The company records receivables, contract assets, margin deposits, inventories, deferred revenue and derivative balances.
