Cheniere Energy (NYSE: LNG) turns US natural gas into liquefied natural gas at two Gulf Coast plants and sells most of it under contracts with about 15 years left.[2] The shares closed at $298.67 on 2026-10-23, and our verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT because our central value of $285 is lower.[3] We would buy only between $256.00 and $262.50, where the expected gain is about twice the loss if the price fell to $251.56, and only after a fresh check of support, with any fill before the 2026-10-29 report capped at a starter size.[21][22] We would be wrong if world gas prices keep lifting cash flow above the raised guide of $5.30 billion to $5.80 billion.[3] We would also be wrong if the two expansions win approval on strong terms.

1. What the company does

Cheniere buys natural gas in the United States, cools it into a liquid called liquefied natural gas (LNG), and ships it to buyers in other countries. The company describes itself as the leading producer and exporter of LNG in the United States.[3] It runs two plants: Sabine Pass in Louisiana and Corpus Christi in Texas. About 55 million tonnes per year (mtpa) of capacity is running, and more than 6 mtpa is under construction or in commissioning.[3] Over 40 mtpa more is in the regulatory permitting process, which means it is a plan, not a plant.[3]

Customers sign long sale-and-purchase agreements (SPAs). Under these, the customer pays a fixed fee on the contracted volume even if it chooses not to take a cargo, plus a variable fee linked to the Henry Hub gas price that covers the cost of the gas and the fuel used to make the LNG.[1] Gas producers can also sell Cheniere gas under integrated production marketing (IPM) agreements, where the price follows a global LNG or gas index less a fixed liquefaction fee.[1] Through these contracts Cheniere has sold 90% or more of the output of both plants through the mid-2030s, with about 15 years of average life left as of 30 June 2026.[2] The same figure was reported at 31 March 2026.[13]

The fixed fees are the core of the business. As of 31 December 2025, executed SPAs carried $290.6 billion of future LNG revenue, of which $107.7 billion was fixed fees and $182.9 billion was variable fees.[1] Fixed fees were therefore 37.1% of contracted revenue (our calculation from those two cited figures). The 2026 slice alone was $6.6 billion of fixed fees out of $16.4 billion.[1] Cheniere also sells uncontracted LNG itself. In the first half of 2026 that short-term business brought in $3,206 million of revenue on 233 TBtu, against $8,346 million on 1,070 TBtu for long-term contracts.[2] Dividing, short-term cargoes averaged about $13.76 per MMBtu and long-term cargoes about $7.80 (our calculation; both figures include the cost of gas).

The legal structure matters for an owner. Cheniere owns 100% of the general partner of Cheniere Energy Partners (CQP), which owns Sabine Pass, plus 100% of its incentive distribution rights and a 48.6% limited partner stake.[27] CQP is consolidated, so its whole income statement and debt appear in Cheniere's accounts, and the other owners' share shows up as non-controlling interests (NCI). Corpus Christi is 100% owned.[1] That is why we judge the parent on Cheniere's own distributable cash flow, which is after interest, maintenance spending, cash taxes and the NCI share, and not on consolidated cash flow.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • LNG is natural gas cooled to a liquid so that ships can carry it. mtpa means million tonnes per annum, a plant's yearly output capacity. MMBtu is one million British thermal units of energy, the price unit for gas, and TBtu is one trillion of them, so one TBtu equals one million MMBtu.
  • SPA is a sale and purchase agreement for LNG; IPM is an integrated production marketing agreement with a gas producer; Henry Hub is the benchmark US gas price. FID is a final investment decision, the board vote that commits money to build. FERC is the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, which permits terminals, and DOE is the Department of Energy, which permits exports.
  • Distributable cash flow (DCF) is Cheniere's own measure of cash that could go to owners, debt paydown or growth after interest, upkeep spending and taxes. NCI means non-controlling interests, the share of consolidated profit owned by outsiders. Other technical terms are explained where they first appear.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Our process judges every company in three layers. Layer 1 tests the financial levels and the direction they are moving (Chapter 3). Layer 2 scores the operating measures that fit this business and checks whether management kept its promises (Chapter 2B). Layer 3 sets how close the price is to a floor, how much to buy and when to stop (Chapter 7). This chapter covers the competitive position that feeds all three.

The strength is the contract book. A plant that has sold 90% or more of its output for about 15 years behaves like a toll road more than like a gas producer.[2] Rating agencies agree with the direction: S&P raised Cheniere and CQP from BBB to BBB+ in November 2025, and Moody's raised Cheniere's senior unsecured notes from Baa3 to Baa2 in February 2026.[4][5] The second strength is the pipeline of growth that sits on land the company already owns. Corpus Christi Stage 3 (the plant expansion, not the chart stages used in Chapter 4) is 98.4% complete, and the Sabine Pass expansion has a signed lump-sum construction contract with Bechtel for its first phase.[3] The company says expansions may be designed to share existing infrastructure, which would lower the extra cost of each new train.[1]

The weaknesses are real. First, earnings reported under US accounting rules swing with the paper value of the long-term gas supply contracts. The first half of 2026 shows it: the company reported a net loss of $434 million, yet its adjusted net income was $1,638 million, because net losses of about $2.2 billion from changes in the value of long-term supply contracts hit the first half, before the accounting treatment of most of them changed in June.[3] Second, about a tenth of output and the IPM contracts are tied to global prices, so a fall in world LNG prices would hurt. The company's own 10-K says global LNG supply grew by just under 20 mtpa in 2025 and expects a more ample supply that should loosen the market over the next few years.[1] Third, debt is large: $24,043 million of consolidated debt at 30 June 2026, of which CQP's $14,577 million is inside that figure.[3][27]

A fourth weakness is location. Both plants sit on the US Gulf Coast. The 10-K lists major hurricanes and winter storms among events that have already caused interruptions or minor damage, and says contract terms and insurance only limit the loss.[1] A fifth is regulation: the Sabine Pass expansion and the further Corpus Christi expansion each still wait for FERC and DOE non-FTA export approvals, so their start dates are not in the company's control.[2] On governance, directors and executive officers together own less than 1% of the shares[20], and the board added a tenth member in July 2026.[18] For an owner this adds up to a high-quality, contract-backed business whose profit per share still depends on gas spreads, on spending discipline and on the price paid for the shares, which is the subject of the next chapters.

Cheniere's accounting profit is hard to read. The number

We estimate Cheniere's ROIC at 13.6% for 2025 and 14.6% for the twelve months to June 2026.

How it was calculated

We took the company's adjusted EBITDA, subtracted depreciation to get adjusted operating profit, taxed it at 21%, and divided by average invested capital, which is debt plus total equity (including non-controlling interests) minus unrestricted cash. This is our calculation, not a company figure.

The inputs

For 2025: adjusted EBITDA $6,943 million[4], depreciation $1,329 million[1], debt $22,813 million, equity $12,942 million, redeemable non-controlling interest $136 million and cash $1,099 million at year-end[1], and $30,327 million of invested capital a year earlier[1]. The tax rate of 21% is our assumption; the reported 2025 effective rate was 18.0% (income tax of $1,488 million on $8,282 million of pre-tax income)[1].

The range

Using the lower actual tax rate lifts 2025 ROIC to 14.1%. Using year-end capital instead of the average lowers it to 12.7%. Using reported income from operations, which includes $3,844 million of non-cash contract gains, gives 21.5%, which we reject as a measure of what the plants earn.

If the number is off

If true ROIC were 12% or lower, the Layer 1 floor would fail. The cushion is only 1.6 points, and capital still includes Corpus Christi Stage 3 trains that were not yet earning, so the true run-rate return is probably a little higher than this number.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule is simple. The stock is in an uptrend, but it is 2.45 ATR above the 20-day average that the ledger uses as its nearest floor, and our price ladder puts everything above 1.6 ATR in a no-buy band, which means we do not chase at market and we place a limit order, a standing order that fills only at our price or lower. The reward-to-risk ratio, which is the gain we expect divided by the loss we would take if the exit price is hit, is -0.29 to 1 at the $298.67 close, and a core holding needs at least 2.0 to 1. That is negative because our central value of $285 is below the price, so we state it as it is: buying here has a negative expected gain on our central case.

Three terms need plain definitions. CMP means the current market price. A basis point (bps) is one hundredth of a percent, so a 50 bps risk budget means we accept losing 0.5% of net worth if the exit price is hit. Portfolio heat is the total of those accepted losses across all holdings, and our cap is 12%. Risk-unit sizing sets the position so that hitting the exit price costs exactly the risk budget, subject to a ceiling for the tier. Tiers run from TIER_1 (7% to 10% of net worth, 100 bps) to TIER_4 (1% to 2%, 35 bps), and TIER_0 means zero size. Our yardstick is TIER_3 (2% to 3.5%, 50 bps): good business, thin cushions, fair price.

A structural invalidation level is the price at which the chart pattern that justified buying has failed.
