1. What the company does

Microsoft makes the software that most office workers, developers and IT departments already use. A company pays it a monthly fee for each person who uses Microsoft 365, which bundles Word, Excel, Outlook and Teams. It also pays by usage for Azure, the computing power and storage that Microsoft rents out from its own datacenters. In fiscal 2026, the year ended June 30, 2026, the three reporting segments earned $140.0 billion, $137.8 billion and $54.1 billion of revenue. The whole company turned $331.8 billion of sales into $155.2 billion of operating income, a margin of 46.8%.[1][2]

The part that matters most to an investor is the Microsoft Cloud, the label Microsoft gives to its subscription and consumption businesses. It brought in $214.4 billion in fiscal 2026, up 27%, which is about 65% of all revenue, and the finance chief said nearly 90% of it came from customers other than the frontier AI labs.[2][9] Azure and other cloud services grew 41% for the year and 43% in the June quarter. Under the new reporting definitions that Microsoft published in September 2026, Azure alone was $101.9 billion in fiscal 2026.[2][8][17] Because customers sign multi-year contracts, Microsoft also reports remaining performance obligations (RPO), the dollar value of signed work it has not yet delivered, much like an order book. Commercial RPO stood at $678 billion on June 30, 2026, up 84%. Its average life is about 2.3 years, and roughly 30% should become revenue within 12 months.[4][9]

The newest and riskiest part of the story is artificial intelligence. Microsoft owns about 25% of OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT, on an as-converted basis, and in fiscal 2026 OpenAI paid Microsoft $24.1 billion for services and revenue sharing, which is 7.3% of Microsoft's sales.[5] Microsoft also sells its own AI products. Microsoft 365 Copilot reached more than 30 million paid seats in the June quarter, double the 15 million reported for the December quarter.[8][11] Microsoft has not published Copilot revenue in dollars, so the seat count is the only scale measure we can verify.

Who pays Microsoft, and what the contracts lock in

A backlog is only as safe as the customers behind it, and here one customer matters a great deal. In January 2026 Microsoft said about 45% of its $625 billion commercial RPO came from OpenAI.[11] That share has not been repeated since. On the June call the finance chief said all of the sequential rise in RPO came from customers outside the frontier model companies, and that RPO grew 25% when OpenAI was excluded.[9] The 25% is solid, but it sits far below the 84% headline, and the gap shows how much of the backlog is one customer.

Our own arithmetic gives a rough size for the exposure. If OpenAI's dollars were unchanged since December, 45% of $625 billion is about $281 billion, or 41% of the $678 billion total. That is an estimate built from two company statements, not a company figure, and the June 2026 share is not published.[11][9] OpenAI agreed in October 2025 to buy an extra $250 billion of Azure services, and Microsoft gave up its right of first refusal to be OpenAI's compute provider.[19] In April 2026 the two companies amended the deal: OpenAI may serve its products from any cloud, Microsoft's licence runs through 2032 but is now non-exclusive, and OpenAI's revenue-share payments continue through 2030 subject to a total cap.[20] For an owner, the revenue is contractual but no longer exclusive.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operations minus the cash spent on property and equipment; it is what is left for owners after the company pays to keep growing. Cash flow from operations is the cash a business generates from its normal activities before investing.
  • Capital expenditure is money spent on long-lived assets such as datacenters and servers. A finance lease is a way to get the same assets by paying over many years, and Microsoft counts the full value of a new finance lease as capital spending in the period it starts.[10]
  • Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given in company shares. It costs owners through dilution, which is the shrinking of each existing share's slice of the company.
  • Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is operating profit with the depreciation charge added back.
  • Gross margin is the share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of providing the product. Operating margin is the share left after all running costs. Net debt is borrowings minus cash; a negative number means the company holds more cash than debt.
  • Guidance is the range of results a company says it expects for the next period; we compare it with what arrives to judge whether management keeps its word. A consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow the stock. A buyback is a purchase of its own shares by the company.
  • Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share; it tells you how many dollars investors pay for one dollar of yearly profit. A measured move is a price target found by repeating the size of an earlier price range.
  • The 50-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 50 trading days, and the 20-day moving average and 200-day moving average work the same way over 20 and 200 days. Stage 2 is the markup phase of a stock's life cycle: the price is above a rising 50-day average, which is above a rising 200-day average. Stage 4 is the markdown phase: the price is below a falling 50-day average, which is below a falling 200-day average.
  • Average true range (ATR) is the typical size of one day's price move. RSI, the relative strength index, is a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has risen or fallen; above 70 is called overbought and below 30 oversold.
  • Asymmetry (also called reward-to-risk) is the potential gain to the target divided by the potential loss to the stop. An entry zone is the price range in which we would buy, a limit order is an order to buy only at a price you name or lower, and a stop loss is the price at which you sell to end a losing trade.
  • The base case is the middle of our three scenarios. A pivot, a breakout and a retest are chart events: a pivot is the price level that marks the top of a rest period, a breakout is a close above it, and a retest is a pullback to check that the old ceiling now holds as a floor.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Microsoft's strength is that its products reach the same buyers through one contract, one security system and one bill. A chief information officer who runs Microsoft 365 already has Entra for logins, Defender for security and Azure for hosting, so adding Copilot or an AI agent platform is a feature purchase, not a new vendor project. Microsoft sells a bundle called E7 that combines Copilot, security and agent controls, and it said hundreds of enterprise customers bought millions of seats in the first two months, with EY deploying it to 400,000 employees.[9] That bundling is the moat: a rival can match one product, but few can match the whole stack at once.

The evidence is in the margins and the growth rates. Operating margin rose to 46.8% in fiscal 2026 from 45.6% a year earlier and 42.1% in fiscal 2022, the best of the five years in this report, even as the company spent record sums on datacenters.[6][1] Azure growth stepped up from 39% in the December 2025 quarter to 40% and then 43% in June 2026, and management said demand still exceeds the capacity it can bring online.[11][10][9] The jump in commercial RPO, from $392 billion in September 2025 to $678 billion in June 2026, shows large customers signing long contracts before the capacity exists.[12][9]

The weaknesses are about cost and concentration, not about demand. Company gross margin fell to 67.9% in fiscal 2026 from 69.8% two years earlier, and the Microsoft Cloud's own gross margin fell to 66% for the year and 65% in the June quarter, because AI servers cost more to run than older cloud services.[6][1][2][9] Capital spending is the second weakness: cash paid for property and equipment reached $115.9 billion, 34.9% of revenue, up from 12.0% in fiscal 2022.[6][1] The third weakness is OpenAI, covered in Chapter 1 and again in Chapter 6.

How Microsoft compares with the other large cloud builders

Google Cloud and Oracle are growing faster from smaller bases, and AWS is growing more slowly from a larger one. Alphabet said Google Cloud revenue rose 82% to $24.8 billion in the June 2026 quarter, and Oracle said its cloud infrastructure revenue rose 121% in the August quarter. Amazon said AWS grew 37% to $42.2 billion.[32][34][33] Azure's 43% is slower than Google's pace but sits on a base above $100 billion a year, and over the last 12 months Microsoft generated $67.0 billion of free cash flow, against $53.3 billion at Alphabet and cash outflows of $7.6 billion at Amazon and $28.7 billion at Oracle.[1][35][33][37] Alphabet's cash flow from operations of $39.1 billion in the June quarter was smaller than its $44.9 billion of capital spending, so its free cash flow was negative for that quarter.[32] Heavy spending with thin cash is the shared risk of this cycle, and Microsoft has the largest cash cushion of the group.

Why the moat is wide but not fixed

In October 2025 Microsoft said OpenAI would stay its frontier model partner, but in April 2026 it agreed that OpenAI could serve customers from any cloud and that Microsoft's licence would be non-exclusive.[19][20] Microsoft's answer is model choice: more than 11,000 models in its catalog, including models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Mistral and xAI plus its own MAI family, and it says customers building with models from several providers rose fivefold since the start of the year.[9] If that works, Microsoft earns from whichever model wins. If it does not, Azure loses its most valuable customer's exclusivity without a replacement.

The consumer side is weaker. XBOX content and services revenue fell 10% in the June quarter and Windows OEM and Devices revenue fell 7%. Management guided Windows OEM and Devices to fall in the low twenties in percent for the September quarter and in the high teens for the full fiscal year, citing higher component prices and a prior year lifted by the end of Windows 10 support.[8][9] These businesses are small next to the cloud, but they are the reason More Personal Computing earned only a 26.6% margin.

What would show the moat narrowing

Three signs would tell us the moat is narrowing. The first is Azure growth falling below the 39% to 40% range of the three quarters before June before capacity is fully built. The second is paid Microsoft 365 commercial seat growth, which was 6% in the June quarter, slowing further. The third is Copilot seat growth stalling after the doubling in net seat adds.[9] The next two sections turn these signs into scored operating measures and check whether management has done what it said it would do.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A company's guidance is only as useful as its record. We compared what management said on each call from July 2025 to July 2026 with what arrived. On operating results the record is good: Azure growth beat its guided range in each of the last three quarters, and revenue and operating margin finished above the guides.[12][11][10][9] The weak spot is capital spending. In July 2025 the finance chief said fiscal 2026 spending growth would moderate; three months later she said it would be higher than in fiscal 2025, and cash spending grew 79.6% against 45.1% a year earlier.[13][12][1][6]

The capacity promise has also slipped. In October 2025 management said Azure would stay capacity constrained through at least June 2026. By April 2026 the date was calendar 2026, and in July 2026 demand still exceeded supply.[12][10][9] Constraints are not a failure of demand, but they show that the build is running behind the contracts. Management also tends to beat the quarter it guides, so the guide of about 45% Azure growth for September is more likely a floor than a ceiling, although the quarter that beats it is not the one that decides the stock.

5. What could happen next

A price is a bet on the future, and a Reverse DCF is a way to read the bet. DCF stands for discounted cash flow. Instead of forecasting cash flows and finding a value, it starts from today's price and solves for the yearly growth in cash the market must expect for the next ten years. We use the rules in our scoring file: a 10% discount rate, a terminal multiple of 18 times the year-10 cash flow, and ten years.[39] If the growth the price needs is lower than what the company can plausibly deliver, the stock is priced for less than it can do. If it is higher, the stock needs everything to go right.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A good report names the ways it could be wrong. Chapter 5 gave four estimates, and each rests on one weak assumption. This chapter states each assumption, describes the evidence that would show it is wrong, and says when we will know. It then gives the red-team bear case, which is the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion, as three scenarios in which the thesis fails.

Fatal flaw 1: the OpenAI backlog proves less solid than it looks

OpenAI was about 45% of commercial RPO in December 2025, which is roughly $281 billion, and its share today is not published. OpenAI's ability to pay depends on its own fundraising, and Microsoft's 25% stake means the two companies' fortunes are linked.[11][5] If OpenAI renegotiated, delayed or cut its Azure commitments, reported backlog could fall by up to roughly 40%, the 84% growth headline would disappear, and the market would treat Azure capacity built for OpenAI as stranded. Microsoft's own promises would remain: $743.8 billion of commitments, of which $329.1 billion are leases not yet started.[2][3]

The Aaa rating means Microsoft would survive such a shock. The stock multiple might not. In this scenario earnings estimates would also be cut, and the shares would trade on the 20 times multiple of June 2026 or lower, which puts them at or near our bear price of $441. Evidence that this is starting would be a commercial RPO below $678 billion without a clear reason, growth excluding OpenAI below 20%, a disclosed OpenAI share above 45%, or any new amendment to the agreement. We would see the first evidence in the report of October 28 and the next in January 2027.

Fatal flaw 2: capital spending outruns revenue and the cash never comes back

Management guided capital spending, including finance leases, to over $50 billion for the September 2026 quarter and said fiscal 2027 spending will grow.[9] At $50 billion a quarter, spending would be about $200 billion a year, or roughly half of the $391.58 billion of revenue analysts expect. In fiscal 2026 the four quarterly totals were $34.9 billion, $37.5 billion, $31.9 billion and $41 billion, which is $145.3 billion or 43.8% of revenue.[12][11][10][9][25] The consensus fiscal 2027 free cash flow of $33.19 billion is a fall of about half from $67.0 billion.[26] The only free cash flow promise management has made is that it will stay positive in fiscal 2027, which is a low bar.

In this scenario Azure growth slows to the low 30s once capacity catches up, prices for AI compute fall, and the new assets earn less than the old ones. The Reverse DCF in Chapter 5 shows what that does: the price already needs a cash margin near 35% by 2036. Evidence would be a quarterly free cash flow near zero, capital spending above $55 billion in the September quarter, or a rise in the calendar 2026 plan above about $175 billion. The first of these arrives on October 28 and the full-year answer in the annual report in July 2027.

Fatal flaw 3: per-seat software shrinks and the multiple resets

A large part of Microsoft's profit still comes from charging per person who uses Office and Teams. Paid commercial seat growth was 6% in the June quarter, and the company itself is cutting jobs and offering buyouts.[9][21] If AI agents reduce the number of people customers hire, seat growth could fall below 5%, and Copilot would have to make up the difference through usage pricing that is still new. Investors have already squeezed software stocks in 2026 on fears of AI disruption, and the Dynamics CRM business is already slowing with longer sales cycles.[22][9]

This scenario does not need any cloud failure. It needs the market to decide that the most profitable part of Microsoft, the 59.9% margin Productivity segment, is worth less than it was. Evidence would be paid commercial seat growth below 5%, a flat Microsoft 365 commercial cloud growth rate at or below the 15% to 16% guided for September, or Copilot seats slowing from the pace of the last two quarters. We will know in the October 28 report and the three that follow.
