1. What the company does

Microsoft makes the software that most of the world's office workers, developers and IT departments already use every day. A company pays it a monthly fee for each person who uses Microsoft 365, which bundles Word, Excel, Outlook and Teams. It also pays by usage for Azure, the rented computing power and storage that Microsoft runs in its own datacenters. In fiscal 2026 the three segments earned $140.0 billion, $137.8 billion and $54.1 billion of revenue, and the whole company turned $331.8 billion of sales into $155.2 billion of operating income, a margin of 46.8%.[12][1]

The part that matters most to an investor is the Microsoft Cloud, the label Microsoft puts on its subscription and consumption businesses. It brought in $214.4 billion in fiscal 2026, up 27%, which is about 65% of all revenue.[3] Azure and other cloud services grew 41% for the year and passed $100 billion of revenue for the first time; in the June quarter alone Azure grew 43%.[19][27] Because customers sign multi-year contracts, Microsoft also reports remaining performance obligations (RPO), the dollar value of signed work it has not yet delivered. Commercial RPO stood at $678 billion on June 30, 2026, up 84%, with an average life of about 2.3 years, and Microsoft expects to recognise roughly 30% of it within 12 months.[5]

The newest and riskiest part of the story is artificial intelligence. Microsoft holds an equity interest of about 25% in OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT, and in fiscal 2026 OpenAI paid Microsoft $24.1 billion for services and revenue sharing, which is 7.3% of Microsoft's sales.[6] Microsoft also sells its own AI products: Microsoft 365 Copilot passed 30 million paid seats in the June quarter, double the 15 million it reported in the December quarter.[19][21] The company said its AI business had passed a $37 billion annual run rate in the March quarter, up 123%, and the June call did not update that figure.[20][19]

Who pays Microsoft, and what the contracts lock in

Nearly nine of every ten dollars of Microsoft Cloud revenue come from customers other than the frontier AI labs, according to the finance chief, so the company is not simply an OpenAI supplier.[19] The same call also said that all of the sequential rise in commercial RPO came from customers outside those labs, and that RPO grew 25% when OpenAI was excluded.[19] That growth rate is solid, but it is far below the 84% headline, and the gap shows how much of the backlog is one customer. In January 2026 the company said about 45% of commercial RPO came from OpenAI.[21] Microsoft has not repeated that share since, so the June 2026 share is not published.[19]

OpenAI agreed in October 2025 to buy an extra $250 billion of Azure services, and Microsoft gave up its right of first refusal to be OpenAI's compute provider.[28] In April 2026 the two companies amended the deal: OpenAI can now sell its products on any cloud, Microsoft's licence to OpenAI technology became non-exclusive but runs through 2032, and OpenAI's revenue-share payments continue through 2030 subject to a total cap.[29] For an owner, the message is that the OpenAI revenue is contractual but no longer exclusive.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • RPO (remaining performance obligations) is the dollar value of customer contracts that Microsoft has signed but not yet delivered; it works like an order book.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operations minus the cash spent on property and equipment; it is what is left for owners after the company pays to keep growing.
  • Capital expenditure is money spent on long-lived assets such as datacenters and servers; a finance lease is a way to get the same assets by paying over many years.
  • Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given in company shares; it costs owners through dilution, which is the shrinking of each existing share's slice of the company.
  • Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business.
  • Average true range (ATR) is the typical size of one day's price move; RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast a price has risen or fallen.
  • Limit order is an order to buy only at a price you name or lower; stop loss is the price at which you sell to end a losing trade.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Microsoft's strength is that its products reach the same buyers through one contract, one security system and one bill. A chief information officer who runs Microsoft 365 already has Entra for logins, Defender for security and Azure for hosting, so adding Copilot or an AI agent platform is a feature purchase, not a new vendor project. The company sells a bundle called E7 that combines Copilot, security and agent controls, and it said hundreds of enterprise customers bought millions of seats in the first two months, with EY deploying it to 400,000 employees.[19] That bundling is the moat: rivals can match one product but not the whole stack.

The evidence is in the margins and the growth rates. Operating margin rose to 46.8% in fiscal 2026 from 45.6% a year earlier, the best of the five years in this report, even as the company poured record sums into datacenters.[1][10] Azure growth accelerated from 39% in the June 2025 quarter to 43% in June 2026, and management said customer demand still exceeds available capacity.[26][19] The 84% jump in commercial RPO shows that large customers are signing long contracts before the capacity exists.[5]

The weaknesses are about cost and concentration, not about demand. Gross margin, which is revenue left after the direct cost of providing the service, fell to 67.9% from 69.8% two years earlier, and the Microsoft Cloud's own gross margin fell from 69% in fiscal 2025 to 66% in fiscal 2026 and 65% in the June quarter, because AI servers cost more to run than older cloud services.[1][26] Capital spending is the second weakness: cash paid for property and equipment reached $115.9 billion, 34.9% of revenue, up from 12.0% in fiscal 2022.[2][10] The third is OpenAI, covered in Chapter 1 and again in Chapter 6.

Google Cloud and Oracle are growing faster from smaller bases, while AWS is growing more slowly from a larger one. Alphabet said Google Cloud revenue rose 82% to $24.8 billion in the June 2026 quarter, and Oracle said its cloud infrastructure revenue rose 121% in the August quarter. Amazon said AWS grew 37% to $42.2 billion, which it described as a $169 billion annualized run rate.[34][35][36] Azure's 43% is slower than Google's pace but sits on a base above $100 billion a year. Amazon and Oracle report negative free cash flow, and Alphabet's free cash flow turned negative in the June 2026 quarter, so heavy spending with thin cash is the shared risk of this cycle.[34][35][36]

Why the moat is wide but not fixed

In October 2025 Microsoft said OpenAI would stay its frontier model partner, but in April 2026 it agreed that OpenAI could serve customers from any cloud and that Microsoft's licence would be non-exclusive.[28][29] Microsoft's answer is to offer many models: more than 11,000 in its catalog, including models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Mistral and xAI, plus its own MAI family, and it says customers using models from several providers rose fivefold since the start of the year.[19] If that works, Microsoft earns from whichever model wins; if it does not, Azure loses its most valuable customer's exclusivity without a replacement.

The consumer side is weaker. XBOX content and services revenue fell 10% in the June quarter, Windows OEM and Devices revenue fell 7%, and management guided Windows OEM and Devices to fall in the low twenties in percent terms for the September quarter.[12][19] These businesses are small next to the cloud, but they are the reason More Personal Computing earned only a 26.6% margin.

What would show the moat narrowing

Three signs would tell us the moat is narrowing: Azure growth falling below the 39% to 40% range of the last four quarters before capacity is fully built; paid Microsoft 365 commercial seats, which grew 6% year over year in each of the last five quarters shown on the company's metrics page, slowing further; and Copilot seat growth stalling after the doubling in two quarters.[26][19][21] The next two sections turn these signs into scored operating measures and check whether management has done what it said.

5. What could happen next

A stock price is a bet about the future, and a reverse discounted cash flow model (reverse DCF) turns the bet into a number. It asks what yearly growth in cash flow the current price needs for ten years, if we discount that cash at 10% a year and value the business at 18 times cash flow at the end. If the answer is far above what the business can plausibly do, the price is too high; if it is below, the price has room.

For Microsoft the answer depends on which cash flow you start from. The session ledger started from a trailing free cash flow of $16.5 billion and found that the price needs 39.4% a year. We could not reproduce that base: the annual report shows $67.0 billion on the usual definition, and the company's own June quarter free cash flow was $19.6 billion, so the ledger figure looks like a data error.[2][19] The run notes explain the basis we chose and why. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter tries to break the case. A good estimate is one whose weakest link is known, so for each estimate in Chapters 3 and 5 we name the assumption most likely to fail, what would show it has failed, and when we would know. The strongest bear argument is simple: Microsoft is spending at a pace the cash flow cannot yet support, and one customer holds a large share of the work that justifies the spending.

The table that follows lists the weakest link behind each estimate. Three fatal-flaw scenarios come first, because each one alone would push the shares toward the bear price of $430.00 and through our stop of $490.94.

Fatal flaw 1: The OpenAI contracts are renegotiated or delayed

Microsoft owns about 25% of OpenAI, earned $24.1 billion from it in fiscal 2026, and had a $6.0 billion receivable from it on June 30, 2026.[6] About 45% of commercial RPO came from OpenAI as of the December 2025 quarter, and Microsoft has not said what the share was in June 2026.[21][19] Since April 2026 OpenAI has been free to buy compute from any cloud, and Microsoft's right of first refusal was removed in October 2025, so the $250 billion Azure commitment is a contract, not a guarantee of future behaviour.[28][29] If OpenAI cut back, Microsoft would hold unfinished datacenters and a gap in Azure growth at the same time.

What would reveal it: a drop in commercial RPO, a rise in the OpenAI receivable, or a change in how Microsoft describes the partnership on the October 28 call. The 10-Q filed after that call is the first filing that will show the new receivable and the related-party revenue.[25]

Fatal flaw 2: Spending outruns revenue and the cash margin falls below the floor

Cash capital spending was 34.9% of revenue in fiscal 2026, signed datacenter leases that have not started rose to $329.1 billion from $92.7 billion, and the company expects fiscal 2027 spending to grow again, with over $50 billion in the September quarter.[2][4][9][19] The consensus expects free cash flow to fall about half to $35.2 billion in fiscal 2027, which is 9.0% of revenue and below our 12% floor.[30] The finance chief said spending could be slowed because two-thirds of it is short-lived chips and servers, but a signed lease is a commitment of up to 20 years, and the 10-K lists $443.5 billion of lease payments and $194.1 billion of purchase commitments.[19][3][4] The 10-K also warns that electrical power and component supply could limit expansion.[7] If demand slows after the leases start, the cost stays and the revenue does not.

What would reveal it: a September-quarter free cash flow that is negative or far below $10 billion, a gross margin below the 67% of the June quarter, or capital spending guidance above $50 billion with Azure growth below 40%.[19]

Fatal flaw 3: A higher-rate market lowers the multiple on all AI spending

The 10-year US Treasury yield was 4.988% on the quote page on October 23, 2026, which is high for a stock priced at 26.5 times this year's expected earnings.[31][30] The year-end multiple was 20.1 times in June 2026 and the stock traded as low as $349.20 within the last 12 months, so a return to 20 times earnings is not remote: 20 times $23.72 is $474.40, below our stop.[30] Other AI spenders are raising outside money, which tells us the funding market is being tested: Alphabet raised $49.6 billion of equity in June 2026 and Oracle sold $20 billion of stock in the August quarter.[34][36] Microsoft does not need that funding today, but a market that stops paying for AI spending would mark down all of these shares together.
