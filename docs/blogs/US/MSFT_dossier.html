Before you read

Microsoft sells the software and the cloud computing that most large organisations run on. In the year to June 2026 it earned $331.8 billion of revenue and $133.7 billion of profit.[1] The shares closed at $529.05 on 2026-10-16, which is 4.5% under the 52-week high and 21.6% over the 200-day average price.[38] Our verdict is REJECTED, and it is a verdict on price and risk, not on quality, because the business clears every financial floor but the gain to our $640 target is only 1.05 times the loss to our $423.24 exit price, and the portfolio rule asks for 3.0 times.[39] We would be wrong if the October 28 results lift profit forecasts far above the Street, and a fall toward $477 would prompt a fresh look only once the chart has rebuilt its footing.

1. What the company does

Microsoft sells the tools that most large organisations use to run their work, and it makes its money in three main ways. First, it rents computing power by the hour through Azure, its cloud platform. Second, it sells monthly or yearly subscriptions to Word, Excel, Teams, Outlook and security software, now with an artificial intelligence assistant called Copilot added on top. Third, it sells things people use at home or on a games console: Windows, Xbox games and Bing search advertising. In the year to June 30, 2026, revenue was $331.8 billion, up 18%, and operating income was $155.2 billion.[1][7]

The company reports three segments, and the next table shows their size. Productivity and Business Processes (Microsoft 365, LinkedIn, Dynamics business software) brought in $140.0 billion at a 59.9% operating margin (the share of each revenue dollar left after the costs of running the business). Intelligent Cloud (Azure, server software and support services) brought in $137.8 billion, up 30%, at a 41.3% margin. More Personal Computing (Windows, Xbox, search ads) brought in $54.1 billion, down 1%.[7] The first two segments are the engine. The third is mature and shrinking at the edges.

How the money comes in: seats, usage and long contracts

Most revenue arrives in one of two forms. A subscription charges a fixed fee per user per month, so a company with 10,000 staff pays for 10,000 seats. A consumption contract charges for what is used, much like an electricity bill, and Azure works this way. Microsoft is now mixing the two: management says Copilot and Dynamics are moving from seats to seats plus usage.[21] Microsoft Cloud revenue, which adds Azure, Microsoft 365 commercial cloud, the commercial part of LinkedIn and Dynamics 365, was $214.4 billion in fiscal 2026, up 27%.[8]

The contracts are long. Commercial remaining performance obligations, meaning signed customer commitments that have not yet turned into revenue, stood at $678 billion at June 30, 2026, with a weighted average length of 2.3 years. About 30% of that should turn into revenue within 12 months.[5] Azure alone passed $100 billion of annual revenue for the first time.[15] After the September 2026 restatement, Azure's fiscal 2026 revenue is $101.9 billion against $72.6 billion a year earlier.[23]

Who pays, and how much depends on OpenAI

Customers are mostly businesses, governments and developers. Management says nearly 90% of Microsoft Cloud revenue in fiscal 2026 came from customers outside the frontier model companies, which means the large AI labs.[18] The largest of those labs is OpenAI, in which Microsoft holds an equity stake of about 25% on an as-converted basis, which is the 10-K figure after OpenAI's later funding rounds. The stake was about 27% at the October 2025 recapitalisation. OpenAI was also a customer: Microsoft booked $24.1 billion of revenue from its commercial arrangements with OpenAI in fiscal 2026, which is 7.3% of total revenue, and was owed $6.0 billion by OpenAI at year end.[6] The share of the backlog that belongs to OpenAI is larger than its share of revenue, and Chapter 6 returns to that.

What changes in fiscal 2027: two segments instead of three

From fiscal 2027 Microsoft will report two segments, Agents and Infra and Devices and Consumer. Restated for fiscal 2026, Agents and Infra had revenue of $268.1 billion and Devices and Consumer had $63.7 billion.[23] Azure becomes a cleaner measure of pure cloud infrastructure because GitHub cloud and Security Copilot move into Microsoft 365 cloud, and the first fiscal 2027 quarter will be reported in the new layout.[22] The change matters because the old growth figures will not line up exactly with the new ones, so we quote the restated Azure figure where it is available.

The cost side: chips, buildings and power

Serving AI customers needs graphics chips, ordinary server chips, datacenter buildings and electricity, and Microsoft pays for them in three ways. It pays cash for equipment, which the cash flow statement calls additions to property and equipment. It signs finance leases for large datacenter sites, which are in substance loans, and the lease asset is recorded in full when the lease starts. It also signs operating leases, which are rent. In fiscal 2026 cash additions to property and equipment were $115.9 billion, and a further $24.6 billion of assets came in through finance leases.[2][4] Roughly two thirds of fourth-quarter capital spending went on short-lived chips.[17] How these costs are counted decides which free cash flow figure you see. Free cash flow is the cash left after paying for the equipment the business needs. Chapter 3 shows three versions.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Azure is Microsoft's cloud platform, where customers rent computing, storage and AI services by usage.
  • Copilot is the family of AI assistants Microsoft sells inside Microsoft 365, GitHub, Dynamics and security products.
  • Commercial RPO (remaining performance obligations) is the dollar value of signed business contracts not yet recognised as revenue.
  • Finance lease is a long lease that works like a loan. The full asset and a matching debt-like liability appear on the balance sheet at the start.
  • Free cash flow is cash from operations minus cash spent on equipment. This report shows three versions because the treatment of leases changes the answer.
  • Return on invested capital measures profit after tax as a share of the money tied up in the business; Chapter 3 shows the working.
  • Reverse DCF (reverse discounted cash flow) solves for the yearly growth in cash flow that today's price already assumes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Microsoft's strength is that its customers are already inside its products. A company that runs email, documents, meetings, identity and security on Microsoft 365 does not swap that suite lightly, and Microsoft keeps adding value to the same seat. Commercial Microsoft 365 cloud revenue grew 17% in fiscal 2026 while paid seats grew only 6%, so most of the growth came from higher revenue per seat as customers moved to the higher-priced E5 suite and added Copilot.[7] More than 30 million seats now pay for Microsoft 365 Copilot, and management says net seat additions more than doubled from the third quarter to the fourth.[15][21]

The second strength is scale in the cloud. Microsoft added 88 datacenters in fiscal 2026, expects to roughly double its overall capacity in two years, and says demand still exceeds supply.[21] Azure grew 41% in fiscal 2026 and 43% in the fourth quarter, and management guided the first fiscal 2027 quarter to about 45% in constant currency, meaning growth with exchange-rate moves removed.[8][16][20] The company also builds its own chip, Maia 200, which it says delivers 30% better performance per dollar than the latest hardware in its fleet, and it offers over 11,000 models from many vendors.[21] Both facts point the same way: Microsoft is trying to be the neutral place where AI runs, whichever model wins.

The third strength is financial. Operating margin was 46.8%, up from 45.6%, while the company absorbed the cost of the build-out.[1] Debt is small beside cash and profit, which gives room to keep spending in a downturn. The auditor gave an unqualified opinion and management reported effective internal control over financial reporting at June 30, 2026.[14]

Where the moat is thinner

The moat is weaker in two places. The first is the link to OpenAI. In April 2026 the two companies amended their deal: Microsoft's licence to OpenAI technology became non-exclusive, OpenAI may now serve its products on any cloud, and Microsoft stopped paying OpenAI a revenue share. In return, revenue-share payments from OpenAI to Microsoft continue through 2030 but are subject to a total cap.[30] Microsoft remains a major shareholder, valued at roughly $135 billion for about 27% at the October 2025 recapitalisation (about 25% in the 10-K after OpenAI's later funding rounds), and OpenAI contracted to buy an incremental $250 billion of Azure services.[29] A partner that can now shop elsewhere is a partner whose spending is a promise, not a lock.

The second is the consumer side. Windows OEM and Devices revenue is guided to fall in the low twenties percent in the September 2026 quarter because of weak PC demand and high component prices, and Xbox content and services is guided to fall in the mid-single digits even though management says it expects the business to return to growth in fiscal 2027.[20] These units are small beside the cloud, but they show that not every Microsoft product has a moat.

The third is capital intensity. A moat is only as good as the return on the money spent to hold it, and Microsoft's spending has jumped. Cash additions to property and equipment rose from $64.6 billion to $115.9 billion in one year, and the company has $743.8 billion of contractual commitments, of which $443.5 billion are leases and $194.1 billion purchase commitments.[2][11] Competitors are spending at the same pace, which means the capital does not buy an advantage by itself. It buys the right to stay in the race. Chapter 3 compares Microsoft with four peers on this point.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management earns trust by doing what it said. We read the last two years of calls and filings and compared each dated promise with the result. The record on revenue, margin and Azure is strong: growth, operating margin and Azure growth all landed at or above guidance in the quarters we checked, and the fourth-quarter revenue of $90.0 billion beat the $86.7 billion to $87.8 billion range.[27][16] Two items need care. Discrete items in the fourth quarter, including a $3.2 billion gain on an Anthropic investment, added a net $0.27 to earnings per share, and management itself said that after adjusting for those items it still exceeded expectations.[17]

The weak spot is capital spending. In July 2025 the CFO said capital expenditure growth would moderate against fiscal 2025. Capital expenditure is cash spent on datacenters, chips and other long-lived equipment. Three months later she said the fiscal 2026 growth rate would be higher than fiscal 2025.[24][25] The numbers confirm the second statement: cash additions to property and equipment rose 80% against 45% a year earlier, and total capex including finance leases rose 65% against 52%.[2][4] Management explained the change as demand running ahead of supply. That may be a good reason, but it was a promise broken, and the investor takeaway is that capex guidance has been revised up once and is now guided higher again for fiscal 2027.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks two things. Are the static levels good enough today? And is the direction of travel right? The first tests are the hard red lines, the facts that end the discussion. One is a distressed balance sheet, measured by net debt (debt minus cash) over earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA). Another is a Stage 4 breakdown, the Weinstein stage of a confirmed downtrend, with no base, which is a sideways resting zone where a stock builds strength. The third is accounting fraud. Microsoft passes all three. Next come the floors for a compounder, a company that earns high returns and keeps reinvesting. It passes those too. The technical floor asks for Stage 2, the confirmed uptrend phase, or a breakout, a move above the top of a base on heavy volume. Microsoft is in Stage 2. The last floor is asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk: the possible gain divided by the possible loss. The target behind it must be anchored in forward earnings per share (EPS) times a sourced median multiple, a cash flow model, or a measured move, which is the height of a base projected upward. Ours is anchored. It fails the test anyway. The 12-month central target of $640 is only 1.05 times the distance from the $529.05 close down to the $423.24 exit price. The rule asks for 3.0 times.[39][38]

How it was calculated

Return on invested capital (ROIC) is profit after tax divided by invested capital. We took operating income of $155.237 billion times one minus the tax rate of 19.4% ($32.185 billion of tax on $165.934 billion of pre-tax income) to get $125.1 billion of after-tax operating profit. Invested capital is debt plus finance-lease liabilities plus equity minus all cash and short-term investments: $40.294 + $66.594 + $442.387 - $76.843 = $472.4 billion at June 30, 2026, and $338.2 billion a year earlier. The average is $405.3 billion, so 125.1 / 405.3 = 30.9%. The new-capital figure is the rise in after-tax profit ($19.3 billion) over the rise in invested capital ($134.2 billion).[1][3][4]
