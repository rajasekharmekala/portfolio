Before you read

Perrigo sells self-care products, and its 2025 operating result was a loss of $1.12 billion after a large goodwill write-down. This report concludes that the stock is REJECTED, because it fails three of four financial tests. The tests are ROIC (return on invested capital, the profit earned on money tied up in the business), the free cash flow margin (cash left after equipment spending, as a share of sales), and net debt (borrowing minus cash) measured against EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization). The view would change only if these measures, and the chart, reached the required levels in a later filing. Status: PUBLISHED_WITH_GATE_EXCEPTIONS and BEST_EFFORT_AFTER_REVIEW_LIMIT.

How to read this report

The report tests the company in layers. Layer 1 is the first set of hard tests: the company must pass every financial floor and the technical floor, and one failure rejects it. Layer 2 is an operating scorecard, and it is used only for names that pass Layer 1, so it is not used here.

The momentum gate is a separate price test, computed from closing prices. It has two routes. Test A asks whether the stock is already in a rising trend and not stretched. Test B asks whether it is turning up from a base, which is a sideways period in which a stock stops falling and builds a floor. A stock that fails both routes receives no allocation. Relative strength compares the stock's return with the market's return over the same period, and SPY, an exchange-traded fund that tracks the S&P 500, stands in for the market.

Company figures come from the SEC, the US securities regulator. Its public database, EDGAR, holds the filings: the annual report on Form 10-K, the quarterly report on Form 10-Q, and the current report on Form 8-K. The structured figures behind them are published in XBRL, a standard machine-readable format. The notes list each source.

Shareholders' equity is the company's net worth on paper, meaning assets minus liabilities. Operating cash flow is the cash the business produces from its normal work, before spending on equipment. NO_MOMENTUM is the gate's label for a stock that fails both routes.

What the company does

Perrigo sells self-care products, the medicines and health items that people buy without a prescription. The company's own filing calls it "a leading pure-play self-care company" [1]. The filing also says the company is one of the originators of the over-the-counter self-care market [1]. For a reader, the model is simple: the company makes the products, sells them into retail channels, and carries the cost of its factories, ingredients and staff until the product is sold.

That model has two consequences for an investor. First, the filings in this report do not describe the customer base, so this report draws no conclusion about customer concentration. Second, the company needs high volumes and careful cost control to earn a profit, because the factories and staff costs are fixed in the short run. When sales fall even a little, profit can fall much further, because the factories and staff costs do not shrink at the same speed. The 2025 results show both effects. Net sales fell to $4,253.1 million from $4,373.4 million, a decline of $120.3 million or 2.8 percent [1]. The filing says sales fell in several areas, including by $51.5 million in Digestive Health [1].

The first half of 2026 shows the pressure continuing. Net sales for the six months to 27 June 2026 were $1,992.0 million, against $2,100.2 million a year earlier [2]. That is a fall of 5.2 percent, which is larger than the decline in 2025. The company also sold businesses during the period; net proceeds from sales of businesses were $362.9 million in the first half [2]. Asset sales can reduce debt, but they also remove the revenue and cash flow of the business that was sold, so the reader should ask what remains after each sale.

What this means for an owner: the business is a large, mature consumer-health operation whose earnings fell sharply in 2025. The question for the underwriting is not whether the brands are known. It is whether the business still earns a return above its cost of capital, and whether it produces cash after it pays for its factories. The next two chapters answer those questions with the filed numbers.

Glossary for this report

Terms used in this report, in the order they first appear.

EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is a rough measure of operating cash earnings before the cost of debt and tax.

Capital expenditure is money spent on buildings, machines and other long-lived assets.

Free cash flow means cash from operating activities minus capital expenditure: the money left after the company pays for the equipment it needs to keep running.

Net debt is total borrowing minus cash on hand.

Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given to staff in shares.

Dilution means existing owners hold a smaller share of the company because new shares have been issued.

Goodwill impairment is a write-down of the premium paid for a past acquisition when that premium no longer looks justified. With these terms in hand, chapter 2 asks whether the franchise earns a return that justifies its capital.

Why the business is strong or weak

A competitive position is the reason a business keeps its customers and its margins. For Perrigo, the filing says the company is one of the originators of the over-the-counter self-care market [1]. That history can be an asset, but this report cannot measure it from the research record, so it is not used to offset a failed financial floor. Layer 2 of the underwriting, which scores three to five operating measures, is not evaluated for this name. The rule is that a company failing a Layer 1 financial floor is rejected at once, and the operating scorecard is not used to rescue it.

The reason is in the 2025 income statement. Operating income was a loss of $1,122.2 million, against a profit of $112.9 million in 2024 [1]. The loss is more than explained by goodwill impairment: the company recorded a charge of $1,325.3 million in 2025, against $27.5 million in 2024 [3]. Working from the filed numbers: operating income of negative $1,122.2 million plus the goodwill charge of $1,325.3 million gives about $203.1 million before that charge. That is a derived figure, not a filed one, and it is shown only to explain the loss. The goodwill charge means the company judged that the businesses it bought in the past are worth less than it paid, and that judgement is itself evidence about the strength of those businesses.

The 2026 figures suggest the charges did not stop [3]. The six-month goodwill impairment figure in the company's filing is $330.8 million [3], and the six-month operating loss is $348.9 million [4]. Without that impairment, the six-month operating result would be a loss of about $18.1 million, which is close to break-even. For a reader, the message is simple: the core operations are roughly at break-even on this measure, and the write-downs are the main reason the profit line looks so poor.

What this means for an owner: the strength of the franchise cannot be judged from the brand list alone. It has to show up as a return on capital and as cash, and both measures are weak in the latest filings. Chapter 3 shows the numbers that make this clear, and chapter 6 explains why the two-year pattern of charges matters for the verdict.

What the price is doing

The momentum gate asks a plain question: is the stock behaving like a stock that is rising, and is it close enough to a sensible buying level to be worth acting on? The result is calculated from the closing prices on 9 October 2026, not judged by eye. The result is the one the scripts produce. Stage 2 is the phase of a rising trend, in which the price stays above its 50-day average and that average stays above the 200-day average (the ledger's label). The falling 200-day test is separate, and it is the test that fails here. Stage 4 is the phase after a trend has broken, when the price falls below a falling long-term average. The stock is in Stage 2 on the ledger's test, which is a fair description of the price pattern over the past months. A base is a sideways period in which a stock stops falling and builds a floor. Estimate 1: the free cash flow needed to pass the gate

This estimate asks how far the company's cash generation sits from the 12 percent floor. Cash is the test the owner can check most easily, because it is what is left after the business pays for its equipment. The answer is that the gap is about $365 million a year, which is more than the company generated in 2025.

The number

To pass the 12 percent free cash flow floor on the 2025 sales base, the company would need about $510 million of free cash flow in a year, compared with $145 million in 2025, a gap of about $365 million.

How it was calculated

Twelve percent of net sales of $4,253.1 million is $510.4 million. The 2025 free cash flow was $145.1 million, which is operating cash flow of $238.5 million minus capital expenditure of $93.4 million. The gap is $510.4 million minus $145.1 million.

The inputs

Net sales and operating cash flow are from the 2025 annual report [1][5][6]. Capital expenditure is from the same structured data [7].

The range

In a bull case, the company would cut capital spending and improve operating cash flow at the same time. Each $100 million of extra operating cash flow closes about a quarter of the gap. In a bear case, another year like 2025 would widen the gap. The record does not show a company forecast for 2026 operating cash flow, so the range is shown as arithmetic, not as a projection.

If the number is off

If the true figure is closer to the 2024 level of $244.6 million, the gap narrows to about $266 million. That is still a long way from the floor, so the verdict does not turn on a small error in this estimate.

Why these estimates might be wrong

The verdict rests on the 2025 operating loss, and the weakest assumption behind it is that the goodwill charges are one-time. If that were true, the operating line would look close to break-even, and the business would be a candidate again. The evidence that would show this assumption wrong is already partly in the record. The six-month 2026 goodwill charge is $330.8 million [3], which means write-downs were still being booked in 2026, after the large 2025 charge. A business that keeps writing down its acquisitions is telling the investor that the acquisitions are not earning what was paid for them.

The second weak point is the cash line. The six-month 2026 operating cash flow is a $31.0 million outflow [2], compared with an $11.4 million inflow a year earlier. That reverses the trend of the previous years. Seasonality can affect a half-year figure, so the number alone is not conclusive, but it is the opposite of what the gate needs.

The third point is the sales line. Net sales for the six months fell 5.2 percent [2]. Part of that fall may reflect the businesses sold during the period, which produced $362.9 million of net proceeds [2]. The research record does not separate the effect of those sales from the effect on the businesses that remain, so this report does not try.

When the evidence arrives: the next periodic report, the Form 10-Q for the quarter ending in September 2026, is the first filing that will show whether the 2026 impairments and cash outflow continue. The research record does not give the filing date for the next report, so this report expects it in the autumn and does not rely on a specific date. The report should not be revisited before then unless the company announces a material event.
