1. What the company does

RenaissanceRe Holdings Ltd. is a holding company based in Pembroke, Bermuda, and its shares trade on the New York Stock Exchange.[3] It was founded in 1993 and sells reinsurance and some insurance solutions, mostly through brokers.[1] Reinsurance is insurance for insurance companies: a primary insurer pays RenaissanceRe a premium, and RenaissanceRe pays a share of the insurer's biggest losses.[1] The company writes two kinds of cover. The Property segment covers losses from hurricanes, earthquakes and other catastrophes, and the Casualty and Specialty segment covers liability, professional, credit and other specialty risks.[1] Management says it earns money from three drivers of profit: underwriting, fees from managing other people's capital, and investment income.[5]

An insurer collects premiums first and pays claims later, and the money it holds in between is called float. RenaissanceRe invests that float, and its total investments were $36.2 billion at September 30, 2026, earning a weighted average yield to maturity of 5.8 percent.[1] The yardstick for underwriting is the combined ratio, which is claims plus expenses divided by premiums earned; below 100 percent means the insurer made an underwriting profit.[1] The third-quarter combined ratio was 79.7 percent, so the company kept about 20 cents of each premium dollar before investment income.[1] That number was helped by 12.7 points of favorable reserve releases. Without them the current-year ratio was 92.4 percent, which is the sum of a 61.6 percent claims ratio and a 30.8 percent expense ratio (our arithmetic).[1]

The second driver is fees. RenaissanceRe manages capital for outside investors in joint ventures and funds named DaVinci, Fontana, Vermeer and Upsilon, and it earns management and performance fees for choosing the risks they take.[1] Management said in October 2025 that these partners fund roughly one-third of its property catastrophe risk.[10] Fees were $328.9 million in 2025 and $81.7 million in the third quarter of 2026, of which $48.4 million were management fees and $33.3 million were performance fees.[8][1] Because outside investors own part of these vehicles, the accounts show $7.4 billion of redeemable noncontrolling interests, which is the outside investors' share, and $150.7 million of third-quarter profit that belongs to them rather than to RenaissanceRe shareholders.[1]

The latest quarter looks odd until you split it in two. Operating income, which leaves out investment price swings, was $512.6 million, but the reported result was a net loss of $161.4 million, because $914.8 million of mark-to-market losses were added back to get from one to the other.[1] The cause was a rise of about 80 basis points in the ten-year Treasury yield during the quarter, which lowers the market value of bonds already owned.[5] Management put the unrealized loss in its retained bond portfolio at $654 million, or $16 a share, and expects most of it to reverse as the bonds approach maturity.[5] Tangible book value per share, the owner's scoreboard, fell 1.9 percent in the quarter to $242.51.[1]

Who pays, and for what

Customers are insurance companies, and they buy through brokers. For property, a Florida homeowners insurer might buy a layer of cover that pays only after it has lost a large amount to one storm, and RenaissanceRe is one of the companies that takes that layer. About 26.9 percent of 2025 gross premiums came from property cover in the United States and the Caribbean, the region most exposed to hurricanes.[6] For casualty and specialty, insurers buy a share of their liability and specialty books, and the company's largest classes are other specialty and general casualty.[1] More than two-thirds of the business comes from clients that buy both property and casualty or specialty cover, which lets the underwriters trade margin between lines for the same customer.[5]

What the company does with its profit

The company pays a small dividend of $0.41 a quarter, about $1.64 a year, which is a yield near 0.5 percent at the $332.19 close.[23][15] The main use of profit is share repurchases. In the first nine months of 2026 it bought back 3.3 million shares for about $1.0 billion at an average price of $303.14, and it bought a further $143.8 million between October 1 and October 23.[3][1] A buyback is when a company buys its own shares, which shrinks the share count and raises each remaining share's claim on book value. Shares outstanding fell from 43.96 million at December 31, 2025 to 40.84 million at September 30, 2026, a drop of 7.1 percent (our arithmetic).[6][1] The board renewed the $750 million repurchase authorization for the third time in 2026 on July 29, and $553.2 million was left at September 30.[23][3]

What changed in 2026

Four things changed. First, property catastrophe prices fell: management said its rates were down in the low teens at January 1 and in the high teens at mid-year, and the Guy Carpenter index fell 14.7 percent and then 16 percent.[9][11][23] Second, the company cut and shared more casualty risk: it shared about 35 percent of casualty and specialty gross premiums, through reinsurance it buys and outside capital, against 25 percent a year earlier, and that is why net premiums written fell 20.5 percent in that segment in the third quarter.[11][1] Third, bond yields rose, lifting the portfolio yield to 5.8 percent from 4.8 percent at the end of 2025 but cutting the market value of bonds already held.[1] Fourth, leadership is changing: Chief Financial Officer Robert Qutub retires on December 31, 2026, and Matthew Neuber takes the job on January 1, 2027.[13]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio: claims plus expenses divided by premiums earned; below 100 percent is an underwriting profit.
  • Operating return on equity: operating income divided by average common equity; operating income leaves out investment price swings, currency swings and purchase-accounting items.
  • Tangible book value per share: shareholders' equity less goodwill and intangibles, divided by shares; it is the main scoreboard for a reinsurer.
  • Catastrophe loss and large-loss events: claims from hurricanes, earthquakes and other single events that cost many insurers money at once.
  • Prior-year development: changes to estimates of old claims; favorable means the earlier estimates were too high and the extra is released as profit.
  • Float: premiums collected now that will be paid out as claims later, and invested in the meantime.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is profit earned on each dollar invested in the business; for an insurer we use return on equity instead.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before financing and accounting charges; insurers do not publish it.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying employees in shares.
  • Net debt is interest-bearing debt minus cash; for an insurer we use debt to total capital instead.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying assets; for an insurer it is mostly premiums waiting to be paid out, so we mark it N/A.
  • Operating margin is the share of revenue left after running costs; an insurer's equivalent is the combined ratio.
  • Guidance is what management says it expects for coming quarters; we compare it with what happened.
  • Dilution is the drop in each shareholder's slice of the company when new shares are issued.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast the price has moved; above 70 is hot and below 30 is cold.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in dollars.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend phase on the Weinstein scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend; Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase.
  • A base is a sideways stretch the price builds before a move, and a pivot is the top of that stretch.
  • A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the potential gain divided by the potential loss: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).
  • TTM (trailing twelve months) means the latest four quarters added together.
  • Discount rate is the yearly return you demand for waiting, so a future dollar is worth less than a dollar today; the terminal multiple is the number of years of earnings that the business is assumed to sell for at the end of year ten.
  • Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share; price-to-book is the price divided by book value per share.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

RenaissanceRe is a strong business facing a falling price cycle and a bond market that now matters a lot to reported results. The strength shows in the long record. Book value per share rose from $132.17 at the end of 2021 to $247.00 at the end of 2025, about 17 percent a year (our arithmetic). The measure management uses most, tangible book value per share plus dividends, grew 30.8 percent in 2025 and 26.0 percent in 2024.[6][8] Operating return on equity was 23.5 percent in 2024, 18.2 percent in 2025 and 20.3 percent for the first nine months of 2026. All three are above the 12 percent floor we use.[8][1] Management describes the company as one of the five largest global property and casualty reinsurers. That is its own claim, but the scale shows in $11.7 billion of gross premiums in 2025 and $36.2 billion of investments.[12][6][1]

The weakness is the cycle. Prices jumped about 50 percent in 2023. Management says its US property catastrophe rates are now down about 20 percent over two years. It expects another decline at January 1, 2027.[11][5] In the third quarter, gross premiums written fell 9.5 percent and net premiums written fell 19.1 percent. The company is writing less casualty and passing more of it to other reinsurers.[1] Earned premium, which drives profit, lags written premium, so the smaller 2026 writings will show up in 2027 results.[3]

Where the competitive position is strong

The first strength is the property catastrophe franchise. Property earned $1,797.2 million of underwriting income in the first nine months of 2026, at a 31.2 percent combined ratio. Management still calls the business rate adequate, meaning that prices earn more than the cost of the capital behind them.[1][11] The second is the tool kit for shaping risk. The company keeps about half of its property business and shares the rest through reinsurance it buys and through outside capital. It says it retained about two-thirds of casualty and specialty premiums this year, against 75 percent a year ago.[5] The third is a pricing system built in-house: the 10-K says the company developed a proprietary pricing and exposure management system, called REMS, that helps it judge the risk and return of each contract against its overall portfolio.[6]

The fourth strength is earnings that do not depend only on underwriting. Management said fees plus investment income contribute 15 points of annualized return on equity. That is a steady layer under the underwriting result.[5] Net investment income was $440.9 million in the third quarter, against $438.4 million a year earlier. The yield to maturity on the portfolio rose 1.0 point to 5.8 percent.[1] Management expects retained investment income of about $325 million in the fourth quarter and more than $1.25 billion for 2026. It expects further growth in 2027.[5]

Where the competitive position is thin

Property prices are the first thin spot. The CEO said it is still early, but he expects catastrophe rates to fall again at January 1. The underwriting chief said supply will exceed demand. He expects demand growth in the single-digit billions of limit, after $10 billion last year.[5] He expects the decline to be smaller than the mid-teens fall of 2026, and expects terms and retentions to hold. That is a forecast. The next test is the January 1 renewal.[5][22]

Casualty is the second thin spot. In US general liability, management sees claims costs rising 10 to 12 percent a year. Prices are rising 8 to 10 percent, so margins are being squeezed.[5] The Casualty and Specialty segment ran a 104.7 percent combined ratio in the first nine months of 2026, the same as a year earlier. It lost $183.2 million on underwriting alone.[1] The company accepts this because the segment generates float, invested at a portfolio yield of 5.8 percent. It says it will cut more if margins do not hold.[5]

Bond-market sensitivity is the third. Rising yields cost $879.8 million of mark-to-market losses in one quarter. The company has also lengthened the retained portfolio's duration, a measure of price sensitivity to rates, from 3.0 years at the start of 2026 to 4.2 years.[1][5] That locks in higher yields for longer, which is good for income. It also means the next rise in rates would hurt book value by more. The leadership change is the fourth thin spot: the chief financial officer and the chief portfolio officer retire at year-end.[13][22]

Reserves: how honest are the numbers?

Reserves are the money an insurer sets aside for claims it has not yet paid. If they were set too high, the insurer later releases the extra as profit, which is called favorable development. RenaissanceRe released $267.1 million in the third quarter, worth 12.7 points off the combined ratio. It released $628.6 million in the first nine months, worth 9.7 points.[1] All of that came from Property, which was 26.1 points favorable over nine months. Casualty and Specialty was 1.4 points adverse.[1] The second-quarter adverse figure included 4.1 points from moving Baltimore Bridge losses from Property to Specialty, and the CFO said nothing like that happened in the third quarter.[11][5]

This is a reasonable cushion, but it is part of reported profit. It is also smaller than last year's: $628.6 million against $845.6 million over nine months, which is 25.7 percent less (our arithmetic).[1] Management says it has kept casualty loss picks elevated, which means it books casualty at cautious loss ratios. It will not reflect better rates in reserves until the improvement proves lasting.[5][10] We treat reserve strength as real in property and untested in casualty, and we do not count on releases of this size continuing.

Catastrophe and large-loss reality

The 2026 Atlantic hurricane season has been unusually quiet, which management attributes to a strong El Nino. The first hurricane of the season, Idris, made landfall in October as a Category 2 storm. Management expects no significant reinsurance loss from it.[5] The losses that did arrive were not hurricanes. The Middle East war cost $69.7 million of underwriting income. The Ras Laffan gas facility explosion in Qatar cost $65.2 million. The Colombian earthquake cost $56.6 million. The total was $191.4 million, or 9.3 points on the combined ratio, and it fell to $111.5 million after outside investors' share and tax.[1] For Ras Laffan the company said it reserved its share of a full-limit industry loss of $1.7 billion.[5]

A quiet storm season flatters 2026. In 2025 the company absorbed the California wildfires, a $1.1 billion underwriting loss, and management cited a $786 million net negative impact on 2025 operating income.[9] The company does not publish a one-in-100-year loss as a share of capital in the release, 10-Q or financial supplement we read. That measure is N/A for this report, and we use the large-loss burden on earnings instead.[1][3] The season is not over, and any late storm would show in the fourth-quarter results. The more important test for 2027 is how many points of large losses the new price level can absorb.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter takes the weakest assumption behind each estimate, builds the strongest honest case against our conclusion, names the one claim the verdict depends on, and says when we will know.

The red-team case starts with quality of earnings. The 79.7 percent combined ratio includes 12.7 points of reserve releases, so the current-year ratio was 92.4 percent, and 18.9 percent operating return leaves out $914.8 million of investment losses that the reported return of (6.0) percent includes.[1] Operating income fell 30.1 percent, fees fell 19.7 percent, net premiums written fell 19.1 percent, and analysts already expect 2027 operating earnings to be as much as 28 percent below 2026.[1][24] The Casualty and Specialty segment ran above 100 percent combined in the first nine months of both 2025 and 2026, the company is retaining less of it, and the tax credits that cut its expenses depend on Bermuda law.[1][5] If earnings land at the ledger's $36.37 and the market pays the three-year median 7.8 times forward earnings, the stock is worth about $284. In a bad year with book value near $268 and a price equal to book, it is worth about $268.[15]

Flaw 1: January 1 prices fall more than management expects

Our central case assumes that property catastrophe rates at January 1, 2027 fall by less than the mid-teens of 2026, and that terms and retentions hold. Management said so on October 28, and the trade press headline says rate declines will slow, but a forecast is not a renewal.[5][22] Supply from catastrophe bonds and traditional reinsurers is growing faster than demand, and demand growth is slowing to single-digit billions of limit after $10 billion last year.[5] Property earned all of the underwriting profit in 2026, so a second year of mid-teens declines on a smaller premium base would cut 2027 operating EPS below $36.37 and pull our $284 tangible book value per share down. Evidence would be broker reports of rate declines of 15 percent or more in early January, and our own Layer 2 premium measure turning to FAIL.

Flaw 2: bond yields keep rising and the mark-to-market loss repeats

The third-quarter ten-year yield rise of about 80 basis points cost $763 million of retained mark-to-market losses, or about $18.7 a share, which is 7.7 percent of tangible book value (our arithmetic).[5][1] The company then extended its retained duration to 4.2 years, so a repeat would hurt more, not less. Management's case is that the losses pull back to face value, higher yields lift income, and the new-money yield is above the book yield, which is why we count $4.5 a share of pull-to-par in the central case.[5] Evidence against us would be a further rise in the ten-year yield during the fourth quarter, shown in the tangible book value per share reported with fourth-quarter results. We have no view on rates, which is why the bear case carries an $8 a share loss, not a forecast.

Flaw 3: casualty reserves or large losses surprise

The reserve cushion is real in property, but the 1.4 points of adverse development in Casualty and Specialty and a 104.7 percent nine-month combined ratio show where the soft spot is. Management itself says US general liability claims costs are rising 10 to 12 percent against price increases of 8 to 10 percent.[1][5] A charge on that book like the 8.5-point reserve charge Everest took this quarter would lower book value per share directly, and the market would pay less for it: Everest trades at 1.02 times book.[20] Large losses are the other path. 2026's were Middle East war, Ras Laffan and a Colombian earthquake, $191.4 million before outside investors and tax, but a single major hurricane would be several times that, and the company publishes no one-in-100-year loss as a share of capital for us to size it.[1][3] Evidence would be a reserve charge in the fourth-quarter results, a combined ratio above 100 percent in Property, or a second quarter of large specialty losses.

Flaw 4: the market values earnings, not book

Our central target multiplies book value per share by 1.20, the three-year median of year-end price to book. That median was earned while operating return on equity was about 18 to 23 percent, and our 2027 central case has it near 13 percent (our arithmetic), so a lower multiple is the weakest part of the number.[18][8] The one-factor stress cuts the multiple to 1.10 with book value unchanged: $302.83 times 1.10 is $333, which gives 0.03 to 1 at the close and 2.12 to 1 at $316. That is why we lowered the top of the zone from $326, where only the central case passes, to $316. The cross-check on earnings points the same way. The price-to-earnings ratio is the price paid for each dollar of earnings, and the three-year median forward ratio is 7.80 times; applied to $36.37, $39.42 and $42.06 it gives $284, $307 and $328, all below the close.[15][17] If the market uses earnings, our $363 is too high and the verdict would become REJECTED.
