1. What the company does

Blackstone is a company that other people pay to invest their money. A pension fund, an insurance company or a wealthy family hands Blackstone cash. Blackstone buys things with it: office towers, warehouses, data centers, whole companies, loans to companies. In return it charges a yearly fee, and it keeps a share of the profit when an investment is sold at a gain. At 30 September 2026 it managed $1,368.3 billion, which it calls assets under management, and $989.7 billion of that earns regular fees[1].

These are called alternative assets because they are not ordinary stocks and bonds that anyone can buy on an exchange. They are private, they are harder to sell quickly, and the buyers expect a higher return in exchange. Blackstone runs four businesses: real estate, private equity (which includes infrastructure and secondaries, the purchase of existing fund stakes), credit and insurance, and multi-asset investing, a set of hedge fund style strategies[1].

The company earns money in four ways, and the difference between them is the key to reading its results. First, management fees: a percentage of the money it oversees, paid every quarter whether the investments rise or fall. They were $2,159.5 million in the third quarter of 2026. Second, fee-related performance revenues of $540.1 million, which are profit shares that the company earns on a regular schedule from funds that never close. Third, realized performance revenues, usually called carried interest or carry, of $464.4 million: a share of the profit paid when a fund sells an asset. Fourth, a small amount of income from the company's own investments, $52.6 million[1].

Fee-related earnings, or FRE, is the profit left after paying the people and the other costs of earning the fees. It was $1,572.1 million in the quarter, up 6% from a year earlier, on a margin of 58.2%. We calculate that margin as fee-related earnings divided by management fees plus fee-related performance revenues. It means that for every $100 of fee revenue the company kept about $58[1]. Distributable earnings, or DE, adds the carry and the company's own investment income and then subtracts taxes. DE was $1,715.7 million, or $1.32 a share, down 9% from $1,889.5 million a year earlier because asset sales were slower[1]. Fee-related earnings were 83% of total segment earnings in the quarter, up from 75% a year earlier[33].

A feature that makes Blackstone different from a bank is that it owns very little of what it manages. The balance sheet holds $13.2 billion of cash, treasury holdings and other liquid investments against $13.1 billion of debt at face value, so the company is close to net cash, and it carries A+ ratings from two agencies[1]. Most of the assets belong to clients. That is why managers call the model brand-heavy and capital-light: the brand brings in client money and the clients bear most of the investment risk.

Who pays Blackstone, and why

Three groups of customers supply the money. Institutions, such as pension funds and sovereign wealth funds, commit capital to funds that run for ten years or so. Insurance companies hand over large accounts, because private loans pay more than public bonds. Individuals, reached through banks and wealth advisers, put money into funds that let them withdraw money on a schedule. Private wealth assets under management were $330 billion, up 15% in a year, and insurance assets were above $300 billion, up 14%[11].

The individual-investor funds matter for this report. They are called semi-liquid: an owner may ask to sell shares back to the fund every month or quarter, but the fund may limit how much it pays out, normally to 5% of its value in a quarter. Blackstone's own filing says redemption requests in these vehicles must be met only at the discretion of the firm or the vehicle's board, with notice periods of 2 to 95 days[6]. When too many owners ask at once, the cap applies, and that is what has been happening at the BCRED private credit fund.

What the money is invested in

Blackstone chooses themes it believes will grow. Management says about 7% of its $1.4 trillion is tied to artificial intelligence, mainly data centers on 15 to 20 year leases where 96% of the tenants have investment-grade credit ratings[11]. Energy, logistics warehouses, private credit, life sciences and India make up other large themes. The infrastructure business grew 40% in a year to $97 billion[11].

Returns in the third quarter were uneven. Infrastructure funds rose 6.4% in the quarter and 28.9% over twelve months. Corporate private equity rose 2.8% and 14.7%. The opportunistic real estate funds fell 3.2% in the quarter, because Blackstone raised the yield it uses to value buildings when interest rates went up[1][11]. Because carried interest depends on those marks, real estate returns feed straight into the carry line.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Assets under management (AUM): the total money and assets a manager oversees for clients; fee-earning AUM is the part that pays fees.
  • Perpetual capital: money with no end date, so it need not be returned at a fixed time; it was $562.3 billion, or 41% of total AUM[1].
  • Fee-related earnings (FRE): the profit from fees after the costs of earning them; it is the steady part of Blackstone's profit.
  • Distributable earnings (DE): the profit available to pay dividends, including carry, after taxes; Blackstone pays out about 85% of it[1].
  • Carried interest (carry): the share of a fund's profit that goes to the manager when an investment is sold at a gain.
  • Net accrued performance revenues: carry that has been earned on paper but not yet paid; it stood at $7.3 billion, or $5.84 a share[1].
  • Semi-liquid fund: a fund that lets owners ask to sell shares on a schedule but may cap how much it pays out.
  • Realizations: sales or payoffs of investments, which return cash to fund owners and trigger carry.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Blackstone's strength is its size, its record and its reach. It raised $63.3 billion in the latest quarter and $271.6 billion over twelve months[1]. A pension fund choosing where to put a new $1 billion tends to pick a name it already trusts, so each year's fundraising is partly a result of last year's reputation. That is a real advantage, and it shows in the numbers: fee-earning AUM grew 9% in a year[1].

The second strength is the quality of the revenue. Perpetual capital, the money that has no fixed end date, reached $562.3 billion, up 12%, and $469.5 billion of it earns fees, or 47% of all fee-earning assets[1]. This money cannot simply be withdrawn at a fund's end, so the fee stream is steadier than a fund that must return capital after ten years. Management points out that nearly half of the assets that can earn carry now sit in these vehicles, so some performance income arrives without any asset sale[11].

The third strength is the cost structure. Fee-related earnings margin was 58.2% in the third quarter, and it has stayed between 57.5% and 59.3% for five quarters[1]. One likely reason is that the same teams and brand can manage a larger pool of money without hiring in proportion. The firm also keeps a strong balance sheet, with $13.2 billion of cash and liquid investments against $13.1 billion of debt, and A+ ratings from two agencies[1]. On 27 October 2026 it priced $1.1 billion of new notes at 5.650% and 5.900%, which shows that it can still borrow in the bond market[3].

The weaknesses are just as specific. First, a large part of the profit still depends on selling things. Net realizations, the firm's own profit from sales after paying staff, fell 34% in the quarter to $331.5 million, and distributable earnings fell 9%[1]. Second, real estate is shrinking: total AUM fell 4%, fee-earning AUM fell 2%, and fee-related earnings fell 9%[1]. Third, the individual-investor credit fund has been under strain for three quarters, covered below. Fourth, a company whose product is trust is exposed when headlines question private markets, and management itself blames private credit worries, AI disruption fears and higher interest rates for the share price pressure[11]. The stock closed 29.1% below its 52-week high on 28 October 2026[37].

The moat: why competitors find it hard to copy

A moat is a lasting advantage that protects profits from rivals, the way water protects a castle. Blackstone has three. The first is scale in sourcing: it is among the few buyers that can write a multi-billion-dollar cheque for a data center campus, a power plant or an entire company. The second is distribution: it has built channels to individuals, insurers and institutions on every continent, and analyst research estimates that Blackstone earns about 50% of all private wealth revenue among the major alternative managers[13]. The third is brand and track record, which lets it charge fees that smaller managers cannot.

Competitors are large and well funded. KKR, Apollo, Ares and Carlyle all sell similar products. Our peer table in chapter 3 shows that the market values Blackstone's forward earnings above KKR, Apollo and Carlyle but below Ares and BlackRock, which leaves less margin for error than a deeper discount would[28][29][30][31][32]. The moat is wide but not unlimited. Rivals sell similar products, so Blackstone has to keep winning on performance and service to keep its place.

The private credit fund that is testing the model

BCRED, the Blackstone Private Credit Fund, is a private credit fund for individual investors with a portfolio of about $78 billion. It holds loans to more than 650 companies, 97% of the debt senior secured, with an average loan-to-value of 41% when the loans were made[15]. Its net asset value was $41.1 billion at 30 September 2026[1].

Investors asked to sell back about 7.9% of the fund in the first quarter, about 10% in the second ($4.5 billion) and about 10% in the third ($4.3 billion)[35][18]. The fund pays out only up to its 5% quarterly cap, so about half of the second- and third-quarter requests were met[18][12][11]. The first-quarter requests were met in full only after the cap was raised temporarily and Blackstone and its employees put in $400 million[18]. The fund's value per share fell from $24.79 at the end of 2025 to $23.65 at 30 June 2026, and the monthly payout was trimmed twice, to $0.18 per share[18].

Credit quality is the counter-argument. Loans on which the fund has stopped accruing interest were 2.2% of cost and 1.1% of fair value at 30 June 2026, down from 2.4% and 1.4% in March, and the fund held more than $17 billion of cash and undrawn borrowing against 0.8 times debt-to-equity (total borrowings divided by shareholder equity)[15][18][16]. So the stress so far is about investor confidence and liquidity timing, not about heavy losses. That distinction is the main judgement of this report, and we return to it in chapters 2B and 6.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Before the numbers, a word on method. Our standard yardsticks come from industrial companies. Free cash flow (FCF) margin asks how much cash is left after the factory is paid for. Return on invested capital (ROIC) asks how well the money put into the factory is used. Blackstone has no factory. Its cash flow statement includes money moving through the funds it consolidates. Its accounting equity is small, because most of the assets belong to clients. So we use the measures that Blackstone's own investors use: fee-related earnings, distributable earnings and returns on equity. We still show the standard measures where a vendor publishes them, and we say what each one means.

The five-year record shows why. GAAP revenue swung from $22,176 million in 2021 to $8,016 million in 2022. It was $14,213 million in 2025. Accounting rules make Blackstone book the rise and fall of its funds' investments as they happen[19]. Fee-related earnings did not swing. They rose from $4,051 million to $5,738 million over the same four years, with a small dip in 2023[8][7]. Distributable earnings fell to $5,061 million in 2023, when asset sales stopped. They recovered to $7,111 million in 2025[7]. The business has a steady fee core and a volatile carry layer. A sensible buyer prices the two parts differently.

Cash and debt tell a similar story. Operating cash flow, as the vendor reports it, ranged from $3,482 million to $6,336 million. The free cash flow margin ranged from 17.7% to 76.1%. That band is too wide to value a company on[19][20]. Total debt as the vendor counts it was $14,194 million at the latest date. That figure includes leases and borrowing inside consolidated vehicles[19]. The company's own measure is debt at face value: $13,076 million, against $13,193 million of cash and liquid investments[1]. On that measure the firm is slightly net cash.

Stock-based compensation, or SBC, is the cost of paying staff in shares. It is a real cost to owners. It was $1,603 million over the last twelve months. That is 10.0% of GAAP revenue and about 21% of distributable earnings[1]. Our rule says SBC should be below 8% of revenue. An exception allows up to 15% when the firm still earns plenty after the pay and the share count grows slowly. Blackstone qualifies under the exception, as the Layer 1 table shows.

Growth rates tell the same two-speed story. From 2022 to 2025, GAAP revenue grew 21.0% a year. Net income attributable to Blackstone grew 20.0% a year. Fee-related earnings grew 9.1% a year. Distributable earnings grew only 2.4% a year, because 2022 was a strong carry year. These are our calculations from the cited figures[19][8][7].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in the last chapter rests on a few assumptions. A good report says which one is weakest and what would prove it wrong. We name three below, plus one further risk. After that come the red-team bear case, which is the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion, and the single claim on which the verdict turns, called the hinge.

Weakest assumption 1: that 2027 carry rebounds to the level consensus needs

Consensus 2027 distributable earnings of $7.37 per common share equal about $9.5 billion across all shares and units. We get that from the ratio of the last twelve months: $7,702.6 million of earnings and $5.95 per share, or about 1,295 million share-equivalents. Fee-related earnings growing 8% to 12% give about $7.0 to $7.2 billion. About 10% leaks away to taxes and net interest. The rest has to come from net realizations of roughly $3.4 to $3.7 billion[1][24]. Over the last twelve months net realizations were $2,151 million. In the latest quarter they were $331.5 million[1]. So consensus needs carry to run at about 1.6 times its recent level.

Management points to evidence. Fourth-quarter carry under contract should alone exceed all of third-quarter net realizations. The initial public offering pipeline is large. Accrued carry stands at $7.3 billion[11][1]. Against that stands the record of the year so far. On 22 September the company said realized revenue for the quarter to date was just over $350 million. The full quarter ended at $517.0 million, made of $464.4 million of performance revenues and $52.6 million of principal investment income. Net realizations still fell 34%[5][1]. Sales have been slow all year. The evidence that would prove us wrong is fourth-quarter net realizations below about $500 million. We will know when the fourth-quarter results arrive, expected around 28 January 2027. That date is an estimate from the dates of the last three releases, not a confirmed date[9][10][2].

Weakest assumption 2: that BCRED is a liquidity problem, not a credit problem

Our Layer 2 score treats BCRED as a WATCH and not a FAIL because the loans look sound. Non-accruals are 1.1% at fair value. Liquidity is above $17 billion. Of the debt, 97% is senior secured. Interest coverage in the portfolio is 2.3 times[15]. But there are warning signs. Net unrealized depreciation was $1.85 billion in the first half of 2026. That is more than three times the $522.9 million for all of 2025. Value per share fell 4.6%, from $24.79 to $23.65[18]. Software is 26% of the portfolio, and the fund has already marked down some of those loans for company-specific reasons[15].

BCRED's net asset value of $41.1 billion is about 3% of Blackstone's $1,368.3 billion of AUM[1]. But it is a large part of the firm's reputation in the individual-investor market. A fourth straight quarter of requests near 10% would harden the market's view. The evidence that would prove us wrong is non-accruals above 3% of fair value, or a further fall of 3% or more in value per share. We will see part of it in the 10-Q for 30 September, which management says it will file in a few weeks. We will see the rest in the fourth-quarter tender result, expected around early December if the pattern of 3 September holds. That timing is an estimate[11][18]. BREIT is the reassurance. It went through the same test in 2022 and 2023. In the second quarter of 2026 it posted its first quarter of positive net flows in nearly four years, and a 10.3% twelve-month return[17][12].

Weakest assumption 3: that a 19 times multiple is fair

Our 19 times multiple is a judgement. It is below the 27.55 times median of year-end forward multiples for the last three years, so it is not aggressive. On reported earnings the median is 30.4 times. It is above the 15.9 times at which the stock trades on 2027 earnings, so it assumes the market pays more for the same earnings by next October[22][24][26]. Suppose it does not, and the stock stays at 15 times. The base case price then falls from $140 to $110.55, which is 7.37 times 15. On the call, management said that bond yields had risen to the highest level in decades. It also said the higher cost of capital had hurt investor sentiment and transaction activity[11]. High rates squeeze the multiple of any company whose earnings depend on asset sales.

The evidence that would prove us wrong is a forward multiple that stays below 16 after the fourth-quarter results, even though earnings arrive as forecast. We would know within a week of those results. In that case the discount is a lasting feature of the sector, not a passing mood. We would cut our target toward $110 to $118. At 15 times the base target of $110.55 would be 2.17% below the $113.00 top of our zone, so the multiple alone could remove the upside.

A further risk: concentration in AI infrastructure and real estate marks

About 7% of the $1.4 trillion, or roughly $96 billion, is tied to artificial intelligence through data centers, power and lending. Management says 96% of the data center tenants are investment grade, on leases of 15 to 20 years[11]. Data centers are also 27% of BREIT's portfolio[12]. If AI spending slows or financing becomes scarce, valuations of these assets would fall, and carry would follow. In real estate, Blackstone raised the yield it uses to value buildings in the third quarter. Its opportunistic funds fell 3.2% in the quarter and 4.0% over twelve months[1][11]. The chairman said there will be situations where investors lack capital and projects cannot get power or approvals[11].

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, and the rule behind it is the reward-to-risk floor. At $117.06 the upside to our $140 base target is $22.94 a share. The risk down to our stop of $101.47 is $15.59. That is a ratio of 1.47 to 1, under the 2.0 required of a Compounder. At $113.00 the ratio is 2.34 to 1, and at $109.75 it is 3.65 to 1. The distance from support, 1.13 ATR, is inside the standard band of our ladder on its own. But the Stage 4 label and the failed ratio at the market price override it. This is a timing verdict, not a rejection. Blackstone passes Layer 1 and scores 4.0 out of 5.0 in Layer 2. The verdict becomes a buy only when a limit order fills and the momentum gate passes on a live re-check, as the paragraphs below set out.

The entry zone is $106.50 to $113.00, but it is a conditional limit, not an automatic fill. The top is just above the 20-day average of $112.56, the nearest floor under the price. The bottom is a quarter of an ATR above the 20-day swing low and 63-day low of $105.44. Our Stage 4 routing rests on Test C, and Test C wants the close at or above the 20-day average. Most of the zone sits below that average, so a fill there is a reason to re-check, not permission to hold. The stress tests in chapter 5 point the same way. At $113.00 the ratio is under 2.0 on either stress alone, and the earnings-only stress clears 2.0 only at or below $111.35. So we split the plan into three equal parts of about 1.2% of net worth each, at $113.00, $110.00 and $106.50. We treat the part at $113.00 as the least protected. One mechanical point: the $1.12 dividend goes ex-dividend on 2 November 2026 and is paid on 9 November. The share price will fall by about that amount on 2 November without any change in value[1][23]. Our zone is not adjusted for it. The effect is under 1%. The event that would change this zone is a close above $124.52, which the first trigger below covers.

The fill-date re-check works like this. Before an order is placed, and again at the close of any session in which it fills, we re-run the four Test C conditions on fresh data. First, the close is more than 1% above the 63-day low, and the 20-day swing low is no more than 2% below it. Second, the close is at or above the 20-day average, with positive 28-day relative strength or a positive two-week gain. Third, the close is within 2.0 ATR of active support. Fourth, the RSI is at least 32. We also recompute support, the stop and the reward-to-risk ratio from live numbers. We proceed only if the ratio is at least 2.0 on our base target. If Test C fails at the fill-date close, we cancel every remaining resting order and we do not add. Shares already filled are then judged under the hard-red-line rule, which treats Stage 4 without Test C passing as a failure, and under the $101.47 stop. If the re-check shows that hard-red-line failure, we exit the filled shares at the next open. We never buy a Stage 4 stock only because the price is lower. Plainly, parts filled at $110.00 or $106.50 close below the 20-day average of $112.56 unless that average falls, so they will normally fail the short-term-turn condition of Test C at the fill date. In practice only the part at $113.00 is likely to pass the re-check at once. The lower parts are mainly a plan to re-check, and to exit at the next open when the check fails.[37]

The stop is $101.47. That is the 63-day low of $105.44 minus one ATR of $3.97, as our rule for a core holding on a turnaround base requires. It sits just under the 52-week low of $101.73[37]. We judge it on the closing price, not on intraday touches. Before a fill, we cancel the orders if the stock closes below $105.44. The floor has then failed, Test C no longer passes, and a Stage 4 stock without a base is a red line. After a fill, we exit on a close below $101.47, or at the next open if the fill-date re-check shows the hard-red-line failure described above.

The targets are $140 for the base case, $172 for the bull and $90 for the bear, from the estimates in chapter 5. The size follows our risk-unit rule. We assign conviction tier 3, because the stock is in a downtrend and the BCRED question is open. That tier risks 50 basis points of net worth. At a stop 10.2% below $113.00 that would allow 4.9% of net worth. The tier cap is 3.5%, which is lower, so the cap binds and the full plan is 3.5% of net worth, filled in three parts. The risk budget would bind only if the stop were wider than about 14%. If the thesis works, the let-winners-run rule applies. We do not trim only because the RSI passes 70 or the weight passes 15%. We raise the stop to the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average while the trend and the fundamentals hold. A winning position may grow to at most 22% of net worth. The size changes if the BCRED fourth-quarter result improves, as the third trigger below explains.
