American International Group (NYSE: AIG) sells insurance to companies and wealthy families in more than 200 countries, and it earned $4.5 billion of adjusted profit in the last year.[1][4] We rate the business sound and the action WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. 1. What the company does

AIG is the holding company for a group of insurers that includes National Union Fire Insurance Company of Pittsburgh, American Home Assurance, Lexington Insurance (its surplus lines arm), AIG Europe, Talbot Underwriting at Lloyd's of London, Western World and Glatfelter Insurance Group.[10] It sells insurance to companies of every size, from small firms to multinationals, and to wealthy households. About 22,100 people work there, in about 45 countries, and the company booked $26.8 billion of revenue in 2025.[10] AIG says it serves clients in more than 200 countries and jurisdictions, which it reaches through its own operations and licenses and through network partners, so the reach is wider than the 45 countries where its staff sit.[1] A reader should drop one old idea at the door. AIG used to own a large life insurance and retirement business, and the watchlist note that seeded this report still called it a diversified commercial and life insurer. That is out of date. AIG sold its last Corebridge shares in May 2026 for about $710 million, and management now calls the company a focused global property and casualty insurer.[1][21]

Premium is the price a customer pays for protection. Net premiums written (NPW) is the premium AIG keeps after paying other insurers to share part of the risk, a practice called reinsurance. In 2025 AIG wrote $23.7 billion of it, split almost evenly between three segments: North America Commercial at $8.8 billion, International Commercial at $8.7 billion and Global Personal at $6.3 billion.[10][4] North America Commercial covers the United States, Canada and Bermuda and sells property, casualty and financial lines cover. International Commercial runs through the AIG brand abroad and through Talbot at Lloyd's. Global Personal sells accident and health cover, high net worth cover, and auto and home cover to individuals.[1][21][16]

How AIG makes money

An insurer earns money twice. First it collects premium and pays claims and costs. The gap is underwriting income, and the combined ratio measures it: claims plus costs divided by premium earned, so 89.0 means AIG kept 11 cents of every premium dollar before investment income.[1] Second, it invests the premium it holds until claims are paid. AIG's investment portfolio was $91.7 billion at June 30, 2026, and the core fixed-income portfolio earned an annualized yield of 4.72 percent, 30 basis points better than a year earlier.[2][21] In the second quarter General Insurance earned $686 million of underwriting income and $871 million of net investment income, which is why General Insurance adjusted pre-tax income reached $1,546 million (the two lines add to $1,557 million before small other items).[1]

Between the two profit sources and the shareholders sits a small cost centre called Other Operations. It holds parent company interest expense ($99 million in the quarter), corporate overhead and the parent's cash portfolio, and it lost $142 million before tax.[1] After tax, AIG earned $1,069 million of adjusted after-tax income, or $2.00 a diluted share, in the second quarter; the figure is also called AATI and removes market swings in Corebridge and other investments.[1] This is the number to watch, because reported net income of $948 million still moves with the value of securities AIG holds.[1]

How AIG got here: a five-year clean-up

In 2021 AIG was still a conglomerate. Its general insurance arm earned a combined ratio of 95.8, and it also carried the life and retirement unit that listed as Corebridge in 2022 and was deconsolidated in June 2024.[13][4] Since then management has sold Validus Re and the travel business, cut costs, tightened underwriting and sold down Corebridge, finishing in May 2026.[5][1] The results were a general insurance combined ratio that fell from 95.8 in 2021 to 90.1 in 2025, more than $7 billion of cumulative underwriting income from 2021 to 2024, and a share count that dropped 36 percent, from 818.7 million shares to 524.7 million.[13][4][11][8][2]

The turnaround has a cast change attached. Peter Zaffino, who led it, handed the chief executive role to Eric Andersen, a former president of Aon, on June 1, 2026, and then stepped down as Executive Chair on September 15, 2026 to become a senior advisor.[17][18] On September 16 AIG also said Jon Hancock, who runs General Insurance, will retire on December 31, 2026.[19] Chapter 6 treats this as a main risk, because the plan that produced the improvement now has new owners.

The recent deals: growth with less balance-sheet strain

Late in 2025 and early in 2026 AIG announced a set of capital-light partnerships: a Lloyd's syndicate (Syndicate 2479) formed with Blackstone and Amwins, a 35 percent stake in Convex Group and a 9.9 percent stake in its majority owner Onex, a place in CVC's new private equity secondaries platform, and the conversion of Everest's global retail insurance portfolio.[4][3] Management said on the February 2026 call that these transactions should be more accretive in 2026 and 2027 than share repurchases.[22] In the second quarter AIG also agreed to buy Everest's insurance operations in Colombia.[21] These deals explain why first-half 2026 NPW grew 13 percent while the market is softening: part of the growth is bought or earned in from partners, not won by price increases alone.[21]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio: claims and costs as a share of premium earned. Under 100 is an underwriting profit.
  • Accident-year combined ratio (AYCR): the combined ratio with catastrophe losses and the effect of past-year reserve changes taken out, so it shows the margin on the year's own business.
  • Prior-year development (PYD): the gain or loss when an insurer revises what it expects to pay on claims from earlier years. Favorable means earlier years cost less than expected.
  • Reserves: money set aside to pay claims that have happened but are not yet settled. Thin reserves flatter profit today and hurt it later.
  • Catastrophe losses: losses from large events such as storms, quakes or war, counted separately because they come in lumps.
  • Adjusted after-tax income (AATI): AIG's own measure of profit after tax that leaves out swings in the value of investments and one-off items.
  • Book value per share: shareholders' equity divided by shares. For an insurer it is the closest thing to a measure of what the company owns.
  • Core operating return on equity: adjusted after-tax income as a share of equity after removing unrealized investment gains, some tax assets and the Corebridge stake.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest argument for AIG is that the business is now measurably better than it was. The underwriting record is consistent: the general insurance combined ratio fell from 95.8 in 2021 to 91.9 in 2022, 90.6 in 2023 and 91.8 in 2024, then to 90.1 in 2025 and 89.0 in the second quarter of 2026.[13][12][5][4][1] Across the same years the accident-year ratio ran at 91.0, 88.7, 87.7, 88.2 and 88.3, so the improvement is in the underlying margin rather than in a run of mild storms or reserve releases.[13][12][5][4] Three rating agencies agreed: S&P raised AIG's insurer financial strength to AA- from A+ in May 2025, Moody's raised it to A1 from A2 in June 2025, and Fitch followed in November 2025.[10]

The balance sheet backs the claim. Debt is 18.1 percent of total capital, and debt has fallen from $23.7 billion in 2021 to $9.0 billion today, partly because Corebridge left the group.[1][13][2] Interest cover on the last twelve months is about 10 times (our arithmetic: pre-tax income of $3,626 million plus interest of $404 million, divided by interest).[1][2][10] Capital return is large too: $6.8 billion in 2025. A buyback is when a company buys its own shares, and $2.75 billion of authority to do so remained at June 30, 2026, which is about 6.6 percent of the $41.9 billion market value.[4][2] The shares sit at about 1.03 times book value, and management called buying them back at a modest premium to tangible book value a very attractive use of capital. The Board authorized up to $7.5 billion of repurchases in March 2025, effective April 1.[1][21][15] On September 24, 2026 AIG also closed the sale of 625 million euros of 4.250 percent notes due 2031 and 500 million euros of 4.750 percent notes due 2036, so debt will be above the June figure; the filing does not say what the money is for.[20]

Against that, three things hold the business back. The first is return on equity. AIG's core operating return is 11.1 percent, inside its own 10 to 13 percent target but well below Chubb at 14.9 percent, Arch at 16.0 percent, The Hartford at 17.9 percent and Travelers at 22.1 percent.[1][16][31][34][33][32] A Barclays analyst put the gap to management directly on the August call: expense and combined ratios now look like the peers', but return on equity is still lower, mostly because of the premium leverage in the business, which we read as AIG writing less premium per dollar of capital.[21] Management's answer was to grow into its existing capital and keep buying shares.[21]

The second is the price cycle. Management described the market as moving from an extended phase of broad price increases into a more selective one, and the evidence is in the lines: North America retail casualty prices rose 10 percent and excess casualty 14 percent, but International Commercial renewals fell 6 percent, global energy fell 15 percent, and AIG cut its Lexington property book so far that retention in that book dropped 9 percentage points in one quarter.[21] The third is reserves and cat losses: in the second quarter AIG added $74 million to U.S. excess casualty reserves, mostly in accident years 2016 and 2023, even as it released $177 million in workers' compensation.[21] Excess casualty is the kind of long-tail liability cover where court awards (social inflation) can surprise, so we score reserve development as a watch item rather than a pass.

Is there a moat?

We call it a moderate competitive position, not a wide moat. AIG has three real edges. It can write multinational programs that need local licenses in many countries, which few insurers can do. It has a specialist surplus-lines arm in Lexington and a Lloyd's platform in Talbot that win complex risks. And it has a brand that, in management's words, made recent partnership deals available to it exclusively.[9] These edges show up in the pricing: retail casualty rates are up double digits and above loss-cost trend.[21]

But an insurance moat is thin because the product is a commodity whenever capacity is plentiful. Management itself said capacity has increased significantly, through surplus-lines carriers, managing general agents, delegated authority, insurance-linked securities and sidecars, and that it is walking away from property business it considers underpriced.[21] The test of a moat is whether margins hold when prices turn, and that test has barely started. Commercial accident-year ratios already rose in both North America (86.7 against 86.2) and International (87.3 against 85.0) in the second quarter, and only the Global Personal repair and a lower expense ratio kept the group figure flat to better.[1][21]

Where the business is weakest

Investment income is the quiet weak spot. General Insurance net investment income was flat at $871 million in the second quarter, because income from alternative investments (private equity and similar assets) fell to $13 million from $48 million a year earlier while fixed-income income grew 4 percent.[1][21] AIG also gave up its Corebridge dividend, which was $27 million in the year-ago quarter, when it sold the stake.[1] Earnings quality in the first half is also flattered by a light first quarter for catastrophes: the first-quarter combined ratio was 87.3 and the second 89.0, while Chubb has already reported $705 million of third-quarter catastrophe losses against $285 million a year earlier.[3][1][31]

What this means for an owner: the business is good enough to own and cheap enough to hold at the right price, but it does not have the margin of safety of the leaders. Chapter 2B scores the five numbers that run the business, and Chapter 3 puts the levels and the trend side by side.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Read the five-year table with one warning: AIG's accounts changed shape in the middle of it. Until June 2024 AIG consolidated Corebridge, its life and retirement arm, so 2021 and 2022 revenue of $52.1 billion and $56.4 billion includes a business that has since left. In the 2025 annual report the revenue, cash and debt rows for 2023, 2024 and 2025 show continuing operations only, which is why revenue falls to $27.9 billion in 2023 and $26.8 billion in 2025; the net income rows are for the whole company in each year.[13][10] The 2021 and 2022 per-share figures are as first reported, and 2023 adjusted profit per share is the recast figure from the next year's release, so the columns are a good guide to direction but not a perfect like-for-like series.[8][7][5]

Three stories sit in the numbers. The first is shrinkage by design: revenue is down by more than half, but adjusted after-tax income per diluted share rose from $4.55 in 2022 to $7.09 in 2025 and $8.24 over the last twelve months, because the business that was kept earns more per dollar of revenue.[7][4][1] The second is noise in reported profit. Net income swung from $10.2 billion in 2022 to a $1.4 billion loss in 2024, and the filings tie the swings to non-insurance items. In 2022 realized gains of $8,991 million included a $7,481 million gain on a Fortitude Re embedded derivative, and AIG explains the 2025 recovery as the absence of the loss on the June 2024 Corebridge deconsolidation. We therefore lean on adjusted profit and the combined ratio instead.[13][4] The third is capital shrinking into the share count: shares outstanding fell from 818.7 million to 524.7 million, and the 2025 buybacks alone retired about 73 million shares.[8][2][4]

Insurers are read differently from other companies, and three of our usual tests change. EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by shares, and we use the adjusted figure. Free cash flow margin is not applicable, because cash from operations for an insurer is mostly premium received before claims are paid; AIG's last-twelve-months cash from operations was $3,850 million (our addition: $3,314 million in 2025 less $1,335 million in the first half of 2025 plus $1,871 million in the first half of 2026), which is 85 percent of adjusted profit.[10][2] The usual leverage test is replaced by debt to capital and interest cover. And SBC (stock-based compensation, the cost of paying staff in shares) is measured against revenue: $207 million, or 0.77 percent of 2025 revenue, flat against $199 million in 2023 and $211 million in 2024.[10] The effect of share pay on the share count is tiny next to the 36 percent fall from buybacks.

Layer 1 uses a few terms that need defining before the tables. Net debt is what a company owes after subtracting its cash. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a rough measure of operating cash profit. Both fit an insurer poorly, so we use debt to capital and interest cover. A Stage 4 chart is a stock in a markdown phase, with the price under a falling 50-day average that sits under a falling 200-day average. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the gain from the entry price to the target divided by the loss from the entry price to the stop.[37]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A forecast is only as good as its weakest assumption, so this chapter attacks ours. It opens with the red-team bear case, which is the strongest honest argument against our own verdict, then takes each risk in turn, then names the single claim the verdict depends on and the date on which we will start to learn whether it holds. The three estimates in Chapter 5 have one weakest assumption each. For the reverse DCF it is the 18 times terminal multiple, which probably overstates the pessimism. For the $88 base value it is that margins hold while prices soften. For the probability weights it is the 30 percent bear weight, which is a judgment and not a measurement.

The red-team bear case. The profit surge is a cyclical high that the market is right to distrust. First-half profit per share of $4.12 came with a first quarter of unusually light catastrophe losses, and the third quarter is forecast at $1.87, down 15 percent.[1][3][27] Margins held in 2026 only because Global Personal and expenses improved while both commercial segments got worse on the underlying measure, and that offset cannot repeat forever.[1][21] The stock is not cheap on the measure that matters: per point of return on equity AIG is priced like Arch, and the 'no growth priced in' reading depends on an 18 times multiple that is too generous for an insurer.[31][34][37] Finally, the team that made the promises has left, the General Insurance chief retires in two months, and a reserve strengthening has just appeared in the line where insurers are most often surprised.[18][19][21] If all of that is right, $65 is a fair value and the stock has 19 percent to fall from the close.

Prices fall faster than costs

Prices carry the weakest assumption behind the base value: that margins hold while prices soften. The evidence for worry is specific. International Commercial renewal prices fell 6 percent and global energy fell 15 percent, North America property is under pressure so that Lexington property retention fell 9 percentage points, and commercial loss ratios have risen in five of the last six quarters, as the chief financial officer acknowledged.[21] The international acquisition ratio also rose 130 basis points to 32.1 percent, which makes the sub-30 expense ratio target harder.[21] The EDGAR filing index shows no third-quarter report yet.[35] We will know more on the November 4 call, when management comments on renewal pricing, and again at the January 1, 2027 reinsurance and commercial renewals. The fourth-quarter report date is not confirmed; AIG reported the fourth quarter of 2025 on February 10, 2026, so we expect early to mid February 2027.[24][4]

Reserves turn out too thin

Reserves are the weakest assumption in the verdict itself, and they are the hinge in the last paragraph of this chapter. In the second quarter AIG added $74 million to U.S. excess casualty reserves, mostly for accident years 2016 and 2023, while releasing $177 million from workers' compensation.[21] Net excess casualty reserves are $3,163 million, so the addition is 2.3 percent of that pool: small on its own, but a signal if it repeats.[2] An analyst asked whether earlier accident years such as 2022 and 2021 hold similar shortfalls, and management answered with its process (limit management, reinsurance, a review every 90 days) and said 2023 was moved up within its expected range, with no real deterioration in frequency or severity; it did not address 2022 and 2021 by name.[21] We will see the next data point in the third-quarter 10-Q and the year-end review in the 2026 annual report, whose date is not confirmed. Because aggregate favorable development can hide a line-by-line problem, we test reserves with one rule used everywhere in this report: the reserve gate, which passes only if the third-quarter release and 10-Q show no repeat strengthening of U.S. excess casualty reserves in any amount and no strengthening in any line or accident year not already named in the second quarter (excess casualty, accident years 2016 and 2023). A failure cancels the order and rejects the verdict, and rejection overrides arming.

A new leadership team owns the unfinished targets

Eric Andersen became chief executive on June 1, 2026 after joining in February from Aon. Peter Zaffino, who set the Investor Day targets, left the Board on September 15, 2026 and is now a senior advisor, and Jon Hancock, who runs General Insurance, retires on December 31, 2026.[17][18][19][14] Mr. Andersen said on the August call that AIG remains on track for its Investor Day objectives, but the two open targets, the expense ratio below 30 percent and growth of 20 percent a year in profit per share, are exactly the ones a new team could reset.[21][16] We will know at the November 4 call and at the next strategy update, whose date is not announced.

Earnings quality: catastrophes and investment income

Chubb reported $705 million of third-quarter catastrophe losses against $285 million a year earlier, and Arch reported $234 million of current-year catastrophe losses for the quarter.[31][34] AIG's own second quarter already included $210 million, or 3.4 points of loss ratio, with $75 million from the Middle East conflict, and the company said it is watching energy and aviation pricing that does not reflect that exposure.[1][21] On the other side of the ledger, alternative investment income fell to $13 million from $48 million, and the Corebridge dividend of $27 million is gone.[21][1] Consensus already expects third-quarter profit of $1.87 against $2.20, so some of this is priced; the risk is that the miss is larger than 15 percent. We find out on November 3.[27][9][24]

Part of the growth is bought

Net premiums written rose 24 percent in the first quarter (18 percent at constant dollars) and 13 percent in the first half, helped by the Everest renewal-rights deal, a 7.5 percent quota share of Convex's business for 2026 (rising to 10 percent in 2027 and 12.5 percent in 2028) and other transactions.[3][21][4][22] Growth acquired from partners can carry higher acquisition costs and unknown loss experience, and management itself said that it is getting harder to tell organic growth from the deals.[21] The 9 percent second-quarter growth with 11 percent excluding North America property is good, but we cannot separate how much of it was won on price and quality. We will learn that from the accident-year loss ratio in the third quarter, which was 57.3 percent in the second.[1]

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule behind it is reward-to-risk. At the $79.88 close the target is $88 and the stop is $71.23. The gain is $8.12 for a risk of $8.65, which is 0.94 to 1. That is below the 2.5 floor for a turnaround idea.[36][37] Everything else would allow a purchase. The chart is in Stage 2 and the RSI is 56.5. The close is 0.91 ATR above support, which is in the standard band of our ladder (up to 1.2 ATR). The only blocker is the price paid.[36][37] The strategy bucket is CORE_ALPHA, not the TACTICAL_MOMENTUM bucket the watchlist seed suggested. Test A (leadership) and Test B (entering from a flat base) both fail. The case rests on a 12 to 24 month story of rising return on equity and book value, not on a momentum move.[36]

The entry zone is the price range in which we would buy. We set it at $74.40 to $76.00 and anchor it to verified support. The low is just above the 20-day swing low of $74.33. The high is the price at which reward-to-risk reaches 2.5, which is $76.00 and gives 2.52 to 1. That high is also just under the 50-day average of $76.83.[36][37] The stop is $71.23. It is the 63-day base low of $72.63 less one ATR of $1.40, as our rule requires for a turnaround base.[36][37] At the zone high the stop is $4.77 below the entry, or 6.28 percent. At the zone low the gain-to-risk ratio is 4.3 to 1. We state both ratios: 0.94 at the close and 2.52 at the zone high.[36]

Support sets how the zone is split. Below the 50-day average the nearest support at or under the price is the $74.33 swing low. Within 0.6 ATR of it, which is $0.84 or up to about $75.17, our ladder allows full size, so the lower part of the zone ($74.40 to $75.17) is an IMMEDIATE_BUY. From $75.17 up to $76.00 the price is within 1.2 ATR, so that part is an ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP at standard size. The position cap is the same 3.5 percent in both parts.[36][37]

We would not place the order now. AIG reports after the close on November 3 and holds its call on November 4. All five risks in Chapter 6 are first tested then. The sensible plan is to wait for the numbers, see where the price settles, and re-run the arithmetic.[24][25] The re-run is not optional. The $88 target rests on consensus 2027 profit of $8.81 at 10 times, and either a 9.0 times multiple or a 10 percent profit cut alone gives $79.29. That target would give only 0.69 to 1 at $76.00 and 1.54 to 1 at $74.40. So after the release we rebuild the target from the revised estimate, compute the entry ceiling as (target + 2.5 x $71.23) / 3.5, and set the upper limit of the order at the lower of $76.00 and that ceiling. If the ceiling is below $74.40, there is no order. A fill in the old zone that fails 2.5 to 1 is not allowed.[27][28][37] Size comes from risk. TIER_3 allows 50 basis points of portfolio loss at the stop. Divided by a 6.28 percent stop distance, that would be about 8 percent of net worth. The tier caps the position at 3.5 percent, so the cap binds.[37] We rate conviction TIER_3, not higher. Layer 2 is 3.5 of 5.0 with only two passes, reserves are unresolved, and the new chief executive is five months in.

A fill at the $71.23 stop would lose 6.28 percent, about 0.22 percent of the portfolio. A gap through the stop would land between the two. These are scenario losses and not guaranteed bounds.[36][37]

If a position is built and wins, the let-winners-run rule applies. We would not sell only because the RSI passes 70 or the weight passes 15 percent. We would trail the stop to the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average while the trend and the fundamentals hold, up to the 22 percent ceiling in our policy.[37] We would revisit the target if the price reaches $88 with estimates still rising, because the $100 bull value would then be in play.
