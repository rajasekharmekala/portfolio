1. What the company does

Keurig Dr Pepper sells drinks, and the machines and capsules that make coffee. The company says it owns, licenses or partners on more than 150 brands, among them Dr Pepper, Canada Dry, Mott's, 7UP, Snapple, GHOST and Core Hydration, and that it sells in more than 100 markets with more than 50,000 employees.[1] In the second quarter of 2026 the two U.S. segments made $3,843 million of $7,309 million of sales, or about 53%, by our calculation from the segment table.[1]

The company is paid in two ways. In drinks, it sells to stores and also relies on independent bottlers and distributors for a significant part of its business, and those partners can in most cases leave without cause.[6] In coffee, it sells K-Cup pods, single-serve brewers and accessories to partners and retailers and directly to shoppers on Keurig.com.[5] The pod is the repeat purchase, so pod volume tells you more about the coffee business than brewer sales do.

On 1 April 2026 the company bought JDE Peet's, a global coffee and tea group with brands such as Jacobs, L'OR and Peet's.[2] KDP paid $17,430 million of net cash, and total consideration was $17,930 million including a $402 million liability to holders who had not yet tendered.[2] By 15 April it held 97.75% of the shares and had started a statutory buy-out of the rest.[2] The deal added a fourth segment, so the Q2 table below shows $2,802 million of JDE Peet's sales that did not exist a year earlier.[1]

The financing used no new common shares. KDP issued bonds and a delayed-draw term loan, sold $4,500 million of Series A convertible preferred stock, and sold a 49% interest in a new pod manufacturing joint venture for $4 billion in cash.[2][5] The preferred pays 4.75% a year and converts at an initial $37.25 a share.[2] Our calculation: converting at that price would create about 120.8 million shares, which is 8.9% of the 1,360.8 million shares outstanding today.[1] The plan is a tax-free spin-off of the coffee business as Global Coffee Co., leaving KDP as Beverage Co.[14] The 10-Q says this is expected in early 2027 and still needs board approval, SEC filings, tax opinions and an exchange listing.[2] Russ Torres starts on 3 November 2026 as chief executive of the coffee unit and future Global Coffee Co.[19] The company's EDGAR filing list shows no Form 10 registration statement for Global Coffee Co. as of 24 October 2026, which is our own check and is described in the run notes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business has one clear strength and one clear weakness. The strength is U.S. Refreshment Beverages: sales rose 10.0% in the second quarter on 6.5 points of volume and 3.5 points of price, and the segment earned a 29.9% adjusted operating margin. Operating margin is operating profit divided by sales.[1] Management says Dr Pepper gained share, that Dr Pepper Zero Sugar retail sales grew nearly 30%, and that its energy brands crossed a 9% market share.[3] Those are company statements, not independent share data, but the segment numbers behind them are filed.

The weakness is coffee and the cost of carrying the deal. U.S. Coffee sales fell 3.2% and adjusted operating income fell 24.7% as pod shipments dropped 11.6% as reported, or 8.3% after a reporting shift for Peet's pods.[1][3] Management blamed green coffee cost, tariffs, a weaker coffee category and a move toward private label, and it expects only a subdued 2026 for the segment.[3] Gross margin is the share of sales left after the direct cost of making the product. The group gross margin fell from 55.0% to 46.5% on an adjusted basis, and 210 basis points of that fall came from cost pressure rather than from adding JDE Peet's.[1][3]

Pricing versus volume

Price and volume both helped legacy KDP in the second quarter: net price added 4.2 points and volume and mix added 3.1 points, for 7.3% growth.[1] That is a better mix than the first quarter, when price added 5.5 points and volume and mix added only 2.6 points.[4] A company that can raise price and still sell more units has some pricing power, which is the quality that matters most when the cost of coffee beans and aluminum moves. The caution is that U.S. Coffee shows the other pattern: price added 5.0 points but volume and mix took away 8.2 points.[1]

Coffee: pods, tariffs and green coffee cost

Pods are the weak spot. Pod shipments fell 11.6% as reported and 8.3% excluding the Peet's reporting shift, while brewer shipments rose 2.1%.[3] Management says trade inventory moves in pods hurt the quarter and expects better volume in the second half.[3] The 10-Q adds a tariff point. In February 2026 the Supreme Court invalidated certain tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, KDP has begun to file refund claims, and it had recorded no receivable for pending claims at 30 June.[2] Guidance, the company's own outlook for the year, now includes a one-time cash benefit from refunds that management says offsets a 2% non-cash depreciation headwind from the JDE Peet's purchase accounting.[3] A refund is cash that does not repeat, so it should not be capitalized into the multiple.

Energy and hydration: GHOST, Bloom, C4 and Core Hydration

GHOST, which the February 2025 outlook called a recent acquisition, added 6.2 points of volume and mix to U.S. Refreshment Beverages growth for the full year 2025.[11][10] In the second quarter management named Bloom and GHOST as two of the top-performing trademarks in the energy category and said the C4 packaging refresh produced a double-digit sales lift where it is sold.[3] The company does not publish stand-alone sales for GHOST or for Core Hydration, so we cannot size either brand from filings; the Core Hydration search is recorded as not found in the run notes.

Risks that sit outside the income statement

Two legal and structural risks matter. The first is the long-running antitrust litigation over Keurig brewers and pods. The 10-Q says the company has not accrued a loss and cannot predict the outcome, and a plaintiff's paid press release says a federal judge in the Southern District of New York denied Keurig's request to end the case in a 139-page opinion dated 18 September 2026.[2][30] We treat that release as the plaintiff's account, not as a court record, and we have not found a published trial date. The second is the pending split, which the company says will bring dis-synergies in Beverage Co. that it is working to offset.[3]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of filed numbers show a slow-growing, cash-producing drinks company that changed shape in 2026. Sales rose from $12,683 million in 2021 to $16,603 million in 2025, which is 7.0% a year over four years and 5.7% a year over the last three, by our calculation.[8][6] The twelve months to June 2026 show $20,090 million of sales, but that figure holds JDE Peet's for only one quarter, so it understates the full-year size of the new group.[1]

Profit tells a more careful story. Gross margin is gross profit divided by sales; it was 54.2% in 2025 and 49.4% over the twelve months to June 2026 because JDE Peet's carries lower margins and because $314 million of inventory write-up passed through the income statement in the second quarter.[6][1] Operating margin is operating income divided by sales; it fell from 21.5% to 16.2% for the same reasons plus deal costs.[1] Trailing GAAP earnings per share is $0.99 against adjusted earnings per share of $2.11, so the 35 times trailing price-to-earnings ratio, which is the share price divided by a year of earnings, is a deal-cost effect and not a valuation signal.[24]

Cash flow is the weaker part of the record. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business collects from its trading, and capital expenditure is money spent on plant and equipment. Free cash flow (FCF) is the first less the second. FCF was 19.3% of sales in 2021 and 17.7% in 2022, fell to 6.1% in 2023, and has since run between 9% and 11% of sales; the company's own 2025 figure was $1,519 million.[8][6][10] Dividends of $1,250 million in 2025 took about 83% of our $1,505 million free cash flow, and repurchases fell from $1,110 million in 2024 to $9 million in 2025.[6] Stock-based compensation (SBC) is the cost of paying staff in shares. SBC is only 0.6% of sales, so it is not the problem here. Debt is.

The balance sheet changed most. Debt rose from $16,141 million at the end of 2025 to $29,980 million at 30 June 2026, and net debt, which is debt less cash, over GAAP EBITDA moved from 3.5 times to a company-measured 4.4 times pro forma.[6][1] Goodwill and intangible assets now total $67,873 million, or 77.5% of $87,619 million of total assets and 2.7 times common equity, by our calculation from the balance sheet.[1] The company has taken impairments before: $477 million of intangible assets in 2022, $306 million of goodwill and $412 million of intangibles in 2024, and $78 million of intangibles in 2025.[8][6][7] A coffee business whose profit is falling is the kind of business that tests those values.

Some terms matter for the table below. Portfolio heat is the share of net worth we would lose if every stop were hit at once; our cap is 12%. Risk-unit sizing sets the position so that a stop-out costs a fixed number of basis points (bps, hundredths of a percent) of net worth. A conviction tier sets that budget: TIER_1 is 100 bps, TIER_2 is 75, TIER_3 is 50, TIER_4 is 35 and TIER_0 is zero, which means no position. The yardstick for a re-opened KDP would be TIER_4 (35 bps), because Layer 2 and the archetype tests would still be weak; at the $32.03 zone top and the $29.66 stop that is a 7.40% stop distance, so 35 bps over 7.40% gives 4.73%, capped at the TIER_4 limit of 2.0%. CMP means current market price, which is $34.68.

Three more terms. An EMA (exponential moving average) is a moving average that weights recent days more, and an SMA weights all days equally. Structural invalidation is the price at which the reason for owning the stock is broken, not just a random percentage. The let-winners-run rule says we do not trim a Stage 2 winner just because RSI passes 70 or the weight passes 15%; we trail the stop to the rising 21-day EMA or 50-day SMA until trend or fundamentals break. This rule applies only after a position exists. One more point keeps price and business apart. A lower price can fix the payoff ratio but cannot fix the pod, gross margin and leverage tests or the 12% return and cash flow floors, and a good leverage print does not fix the return floor either. Each has to be cured on its own evidence.
