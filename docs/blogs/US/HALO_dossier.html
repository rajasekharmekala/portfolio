1. What the company does

Many modern cancer and immune-disease drugs are antibodies first approved as intravenous infusions, which take hours in a clinic. Halozyme sells a way around that. Its enzyme, called rHuPH20 and marketed as ENHANZE, breaks down a gel-like substance under the skin so a large dose can go in as a quick injection instead. Halozyme does not make or sell those cancer and immune drugs. It licenses ENHANZE to drug makers, and each partner pays a royalty that averages a mid-single-digit percent of the partner's sales.[3] In the quarter ended June 30, 2026, royalties were $307.7 million, 64.0% of the company's $481.0 million of revenue, and they grew 49.6% from a year earlier.[1]

Because partners run the clinical trials, pay for the manufacturing of the drug and sell it, most of each royalty dollar reaches profit. That is why the second-quarter operating margin was 59.8% (operating income of $287.7 million on revenue of $481.0 million).[1] The largest royalty streams come from DARZALEX Faspro (Janssen), VYVGART Hytrulo (argenx), Phesgo (Roche) and a group of newer launches such as Ocrevus SC, Tecentriq SC, Rybrevant SC and Opdivo Qvantig.[2] The company says ten partner products are on the market and that it has signed five new ENHANZE and Hypercon agreements in 2026 against a goal of three for the year, including Vertex, Oruka, GSK and Incyte.[1]

The other 36% of revenue comes from four sources. Halozyme sells its own products (Hylenex, which helps other injected drugs spread and absorb, and XYOSTED, a weekly at-home testosterone injection bought with Antares). It sells bulk enzyme to partners, which grew 165% in the first half of 2026 to $133.1 million and follows partner orders rather than a fixed schedule. It supplies auto-injector devices, which was $29.6 million in the first half. And it books upfront fees and milestone payments when partners sign or reach regulatory steps, which arrive unevenly.[2] The product lines carry much lower margins than royalties: in the June quarter cost of sales was $79.2 million against $129.6 million of product sales, which is a 38.9% gross profit on that slice (our calculation).[1]

Halozyme is building a second and third platform because the core enzyme patents expire in 2027 in the United States and 2029 in Europe.[3] In November 2025 it bought Elektrofi, whose Hypercon microparticle technology reduces injection volume for the same dose, and in December 2025 it bought Surf Bio, whose polymer-based technology does something similar and has since been renamed Aerocon.[2][7][23] Management says both technologies have intellectual property running into the mid-2040s, and it points to a line of sight to more than $2 billion of total revenue by 2028.[10][7] These platforms are the company's answer to the largest question in this report: what protects the royalty stream once the original enzyme patents lapse.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ENHANZE and rHuPH20: Halozyme's licensed enzyme technology, and the enzyme itself, that lets a large drug dose be injected under the skin.
  • Subcutaneous (SC) and intravenous (IV): under the skin versus into a vein. SC versions of IV drugs are what Halozyme enables.
  • Royalty: a percentage of a partner's sales paid to Halozyme for the right to use its technology.
  • MDASE: a separate family of modified enzyme patents that Halozyme owns and has asserted against Merck's subcutaneous Keytruda. It is not part of the ENHANZE license program.
  • Hypercon and Aerocon: the two concentration technologies bought in late 2025 (Aerocon is the new name for the Surf Bio technology) that let more drug fit in a smaller injection.
  • Convertible note and capped call: a bond that can be settled in cash or shares at a set conversion price, and a purchased option contract that offsets part of the share dilution — the increase in share count that shrinks each share's slice — up to a cap price.
  • IPR&D: acquired in-process research and development, an expense booked when a company buys unapproved technology that has no other use.
  • Adjusted EBITDA: the company's own version of operating earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, with some one-time items removed. The acquired IPR&D charge is not removed.
  • PTAB and PGR: the US Patent Trial and Appeal Board and its post-grant review process, in which a challenger asks the patent office to cancel an issued patent.
  • FCF is short for free cash flow: the cash a company generates from operations after it pays for capital spending.
  • Pivot is the price level, usually the top of a resting range, that a stock must clear to confirm a breakout.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The moat is real but narrower than the headline margins suggest. Ten commercial products already use ENHANZE, which means a drug maker that wants a subcutaneous version of an antibody has a proven, approved route that regulators and doctors already know.[1] The proof is in the growth: royalties rose from $203.9 million in 2021 to $867.8 million in 2025 and to $1,042.4 million over the trailing twelve months to June 2026 (our calculation).[5][3][1][2] Partners have also come back for more: argenx widened its license from six to eight targets on October 6, 2026, and Halozyme signed five new agreements through July against its goal of three for the year.[22][1]

The weakness is concentration. The 10-K does not name partners in its customer table, but it shows one partner at 41% of 2025 revenue, a second at 17%, a third at 16% and a fourth at 6%, and it says 69% of receivables were owed by Janssen, Roche and argenx.[3] The royalty table is more specific: in the June 2026 quarter DARZALEX royalties from Janssen were 49.5% of all royalties and VYVGART Hytrulo royalties from argenx were 23.6%, so two products produced about 73% of the royalty line (our calculation).[2] A price cut, a label problem or a competing drug on either product would show up directly in Halozyme's earnings.

Royalties by product: how concentrated is the stream

The filing breaks royalties into three named products and an "other" bucket. The bucket holds newer launches (Ocrevus SC and Tecentriq SC from Roche, Rybrevant SC from Janssen, Opdivo Qvantig from Bristol Myers Squibb) and the older Herceptin and MabThera products, where the company reports modest price erosion. Halozyme does not publish separate figures for Ocrevus, Opdivo Qvantig or Rybrevant, so their individual size is not published.[2]

DARZALEX was $152.2 million of $307.7 million in the June 2026 quarter (49.5%), down from 58.3% a year earlier. VYVGART Hytrulo rose 143% to $72.6 million (23.6%), Phesgo rose 8% to $28.4 million (9.2%), and the "other" bucket rose 85% to $54.4 million (17.7%), with shares as our calculation from the filed table.[2] Diversification is improving, but the stream still leans on two products and two partners. For an owner this means the next quarterly reports from Janssen and argenx matter more than any new license announcement.

How long the patents last, and what the Merck dispute means

The 10-K lists four patent groups. The core enzyme has an issued US patent that expires in 2027, an issued European patent that expires in 2029, and other patents valid into 2029. A second family called MDASE, covering modified enzymes, has US patents expiring from 2032 to 2034 and European patents expiring in 2032. The newer concentration technologies have intellectual property into the mid-2040s.[3][10] The contract language matters as much as the dates: a partner's royalty term ends at the later of a set period or the last valid patent claim, and where no valid claim remains in a country the royalty rate is reduced after the rHuPH20 patents expire. Janssen's own DARZALEX SC patents do not change the timing of that reduction.[2] The size of the reduction by product is not published, and we searched for it without success, so it is the largest unknown in the stock.

Halozyme's fight with Merck is about MDASE, not ENHANZE. The US patent office has issued final written decisions finding challenged claims unpatentable in at least nine Merck challenges: four earlier decisions that Halozyme has appealed to the Federal Circuit (case numbers 26-2299, 26-2300, 26-2302 and 26-2303), one on the '520 patent, and four more issued between September 25 and October 2, 2026. The board found the claims unpatentable for lack of written description and enablement.[25][26] On the other side, a German court granted Halozyme a preliminary injunction against Keytruda SC in December 2025, and the October 7, 2026 Dutch ruling rejected Merck's argument that the European patent is invalid and covers Belgium, Denmark, France, Ireland, Italy, Sweden, Switzerland and the Netherlands.[23][24]

Halozyme itself states that the MDASE patents are not part of its ENHANZE license program, so the outcome will not change any partner's right to use ENHANZE or the fees for it.[23] We read the dispute as upside that could be large but is uncertain, mostly lost in the United States so far and partly won in Europe, not as a threat to current royalties. It has a cost: the company added back $6.9 million of litigation expense to adjusted EBITDA in the June quarter alone.[1] It also has a size, and the size has grown: Keytruda Qlex generated about $40 million of sales in 2025, including about $35 million in the fourth quarter, and subcutaneous Keytruda was $463 million of Merck's $8.4 billion of Keytruda sales in the second quarter of 2026, about 5.5% of the total (our calculation).[26][24] A settlement or damages case would therefore rest on a product that is already large and still growing, which is why the market has started to price one. We still keep it out of our numbers, because most of the US patents at issue have been ruled unpatentable, the appeals have no date, and neither the size nor the timing of any payment is known.[26] The market has started to price a settlement: H.C. Wainwright and TD Cowen each lifted their price target to $130 after the European rulings, and TD Cowen warned that expectations are now high.[27]

The auto-injector business, the acquisitions and how they were paid for

Halozyme's device business came from the May 2022 Antares purchase. A margin for this business is not published, because the company reports one segment and our searches found none.[3] The cash flow statement shows what the 2025 deals cost: $726.0 million paid for Elektrofi and $287.8 million for Surf Bio, both net of cash acquired. Surf Bio was booked as acquired research and development expense of $284.9 million because the technology had no other use, which is why 2025 net income fell.[3] A $48.7 million intangible was also written off in the fourth quarter of 2025.[7]

The financing did not stop with those two deals. On September 22, 2026 Halozyme closed a further $1,500.0 million of 1.50% convertible notes due 2033, received about $1,471.1 million after fees, spent $187.5 million on capped calls, and used cash to buy back $151.7 million of 2027 notes and $220.0 million of 2028 notes for $217.0 million and $435.5 million including accrued interest. It plans to use the rest for general purposes, including acquisitions.[11] Paying $652.5 million for $371.7 million of face value shows how far the share price had risen above those notes' conversion prices of $77.17 and $56.02 (our calculation).[2][11] Chapter 3 follows what this does to leverage.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management guidance is the company's own forecast. It has been conservative on revenue and royalties, and management has repeatedly raised the numbers. For 2025, the January 2025 guidance was revenue of $1,150 million to $1,225 million and royalties of $725 million to $750 million. The November 2025 guidance was $1,300 million to $1,375 million and $850 million to $880 million, and the company delivered $1,396.6 million and $867.8 million.[9][8][3] The 2026 guidance followed the same pattern: revenue guidance started in January at $1,710 million to $1,810 million, was left unchanged in May, and was raised in August to $1,835 million to $1,910 million. Royalty guidance moved from $1,130 million to $1,170 million to $1,220 million to $1,245 million.[10][6][1]

The profit guidance needs a careful read. The November 2025 guidance for adjusted EBITDA was $885 million to $935 million and for non-GAAP earnings per share $6.10 to $6.50. Reported results were $657.6 million and $4.15, because the company did not remove the $284.9 million Surf Bio research charge, a deal completed in December 2025, after the guidance, from those measures.[8][7] Adding the charge back (our calculation) gives about $942.5 million of adjusted EBITDA, above the range. We count this as a met promise with a presentation cost: the reported figures look like a miss, and a reader who only sees the headline would think so.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue grew from $443.3 million in 2021 to $1,396.6 million in 2025, a 28.4% compound annual rate over the last three years (2022 to 2025), and to $1,663.7 million over the trailing twelve months to June 2026.[4][3][1][2] Royalties drove it: they were 46% of 2021 revenue and 64% of the latest quarter's. Operating income did not rise in a straight line, because 2022 carried the Antares amortization and 2025 carried the $284.9 million Surf Bio charge and the $48.7 million write-off. Before those two 2025 items the operating margin would have been 57.5% (our calculation).[3] Net income of $402.7 million in 2021 included a $154.2 million income tax benefit, so it flatters that year.[4] Three-year net income growth (2022 to 2025) is a slower 16.2% a year for the same reasons (our calculation).

Cash is the cleanest part of the story. Free cash flow was 45.0% to 46.2% of revenue in 2023, 2024 and 2025 and 48.5% over the trailing twelve months, because the business needs almost no capital spending (under $16 million a year) and earns royalties that arrive as cash.[4][3][2] Our free cash flow figure is cash from operations minus capital expenditure. It excludes the $726.0 million paid for Elektrofi and the $287.8 million paid for Surf Bio, both net of cash acquired; a reader who wants cash left after acquisitions should subtract both.[3] A second data vendor shows the same $806.2 million of trailing free cash flow, so the figure reconciles.[15]

The balance sheet changed shape in 2025 and again in September 2026. Debt was $1.5 billion from 2022 to 2024, rose to $2.1 billion after the November 2025 note sales and the Elektrofi purchase, and stood at $2,179.6 million of face value on June 30, 2026 against $231.0 million of cash and securities.[4][3][2] The September 22 financing then added $1,500.0 million of notes and retired $371.7 million of older ones (Chapter 2).[11] Equity is small ($143.5 million) because buybacks reduce it, so debt-to-equity looks extreme at about 15 times and tells us little. The ratio that matters is net debt to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA), covered in the estimate below.

Shareholders have been paid back steadily. The company bought $1,544.8 million of stock from 2021 through 2025 and $332.7 million more in the first half of 2026 (our sums from the cited cash flow statements), and the diluted share count fell from 146.8 million in 2021 to 121.2 million in the June 2026 quarter.[4][3][2][1] One thing counts against that picture. The earnings per share figures that analysts quote are non-GAAP, which means they exclude stock-based compensation, the amortization of acquired intangibles and the extra shares the convertible notes could create. In the June 2026 quarter GAAP diluted earnings per share were $1.90 against $2.28 non-GAAP.[1]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter attacks our own numbers. Each estimate in Chapter 5 has one assumption that carries most of its weight, and we name it here with the evidence that would prove it wrong and the date we would know. The bear case in one paragraph: Halozyme is a very good business that has moved from about 9 times earnings at the end of 2025 to about 15 times today, whose second-half royalty ramp is steeper than the first half, whose two biggest products make up nearly three quarters of royalties, whose core enzyme patents expire in 2027 and 2029 with an unpublished royalty step-down, and whose debt rose to about $3.3 billion of face value in September 2026 with the 2027, 2028, 2031 and 2032 notes all convertible at prices below today's share price.[16][3][2][11]

The weakest assumption in the reverse DCF (estimate 1) is that trailing free cash flow of $806.2 million is a clean starting point: it includes a large swing in receivables and is struck before the $1.0 billion spent on acquisitions in late 2025.[3] The weakest assumption in the base value (estimate 2) is that the multiple falls only partway to its history; if investors return to 9.77 times, the value is $104.3, which is below the whole entry zone, so a fill only counts if the post-report rerun still supports 12.0 times. The weakest assumption in the bull value (estimate 3) is that the multiple stays at 14.71 times while earnings rise, which is a re-rating of the kind that has already happened once. The weakest assumption in the bear value (estimate 4) is that 8.85 times is the floor, when the multiple could go lower on an adverse patent outcome. The weakest assumption in the probability-weighted value (estimate 5) is the 25%, 45% and 30% split itself, which is our judgment and not a measured frequency.[16]

Fatal flaw 1: the royalty rate steps down after the core patents expire

The core enzyme patents expire in 2027 (United States) and 2029 (Europe), and the filings say that when no valid patent claim covers a product in a country the royalty rate is reduced after the rHuPH20 patents expire.[3][2] The size of the reduction and the timing by product are not published, and the 10-Q adds that Janssen's patents covering DARZALEX SC do not affect the timing, which leaves the position of the largest product unclear.[2] If the step-down is large and early, royalties could stop growing in 2027 and the 18 times terminal multiple in our DCF would be wrong.

Evidence that would show it: guidance for 2027 royalty growth well below the 2026 pace, or a partner filing that cites reduced royalty rates. We would know when the company gives 2027 guidance with its fourth-quarter report in early 2027 (the date is not announced), and again each quarter through 2029. Management's own answer is that new agreements and co-formulation patents carry royalties into the 2040s, but that is a statement about future deals, not about the rate on today's products.[10][7]

Fatal flaw 2: two products and one partner turn

DARZALEX and VYVGART Hytrulo produced 73% of June-quarter royalties.[2] A price cut, a safety or label change, or a competing product on either drug would cut Halozyme's royalties directly. The 10-Q also discloses that a partner is deciding whether to return a drug target, with a decision now expected by February 2027. If it does, and no replacement target is named, Halozyme may have to test the $450.6 million acquired customer-relationship intangible and related goodwill for a material impairment charge.[2] An impairment would not cost cash, but it would cut reported earnings and raise questions about what Halozyme paid for Elektrofi.

Evidence that would show it: a sequential decline in DARZALEX or VYVGART royalties in the third-quarter report on November 5, 2026, or an impairment note in the annual report.[12] We would know on November 5 for the first and by February 2027 for the second.

Fatal flaw 3: the financing and the convertible notes

Halozyme's June 2026 notes would convert into about 28.3 million shares at their stated conversion prices, worth about $3.68 billion at $130.03 against $2.18 billion of face value (our calculation from the cited conversion prices). The excess, about $1.50 billion or 11.5 million shares (10.2% of the 113.5 million outstanding), is partly offset by capped calls: the 2031 and 2032 capped calls run to a $136.78 cap, just above today's price, while the 2028 capped call cap of $75.41 is far below it.[2] The September 2026 sale of $1.5 billion more notes at 1.50%, convertible at $139.84 with a $208.39 cap, raised face value to about $3.3 billion (our calculation).[11] The non-GAAP earnings per share that analysts quote use a share count that excludes part of this potential dilution (the June-quarter adjustment removed only 1.5 million shares for the 2028 notes), which flatters the per-share figure.[1]

Evidence that would show it: a diluted share count that rises, a debt-to-EBITDA ratio above 3.0 times on the company's own definition, or another large acquisition before the debt is reduced. We would know at each quarterly report, first on November 5, 2026, when the balance sheet shows the September financing and the note repurchases.[11][12]
