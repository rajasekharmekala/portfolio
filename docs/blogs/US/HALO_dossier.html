1. What the company does

Many modern cancer and immune-disease drugs are antibodies that were first approved as intravenous infusions, which take hours in a clinic. Halozyme sells a way around that. Its enzyme, called rHuPH20 and marketed as ENHANZE, breaks down a gel-like substance under the skin so a large dose can be injected there. The company's own filing compares a five-minute subcutaneous injection with a one-and-a-half to four-hour infusion.[1] Halozyme does not make or sell those drugs. It licenses ENHANZE to drug makers, and the partner pays a royalty that averages a mid-single-digit percent of the partner's sales.[1]

Ten partner products currently pay royalties, five of them from Roche, two from Janssen, and one each from Takeda, argenx and Bristol Myers Squibb.[1] In the quarter ended June 30, 2026, royalties were $307.7 million, 64.0% of the company's $481.0 million of revenue, and they grew 49.6% from a year earlier.[1] Partners lead the development of the drugs, so most of each royalty dollar reaches profit, which is why the quarterly operating profit was 59.8% of revenue.[1]

The other 36% of revenue comes from four sources. Halozyme sells its own products (Hylenex, which helps other injected drugs disperse and be absorbed, and XYOSTED, a once-weekly at-home testosterone injection acquired with Antares). It sells bulk enzyme to partners, which jumped 156% in the quarter on partner demand and follows partner orders rather than a fixed schedule. It supplies auto-injector devices to partners, a line that came with the Antares business and was 3.0% of revenue. It also books upfront fees and milestone payments when new deals are signed or partner drugs reach regulatory steps, which arrive unevenly from quarter to quarter.[1]

Halozyme is also building a second and third platform. In November 2025 it bought Elektrofi, whose Hypercon microparticle technology reduces the injection volume for the same dose, and in December 2025 it bought Surf Bio, whose polymer-based technology does something similar.[1] Management expects Hypercon royalties to begin in 2030 and new-deal royalties to run from the 2030s to at least the mid-2040s, and says the patent protection for both technologies runs into the mid-2040s.[7][9][11] These deals are the company's answer to the biggest question in this report: what protects the royalty stream after the core enzyme patents expire in 2027 and 2029.[2]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ENHANZE and rHuPH20: Halozyme's licensed enzyme technology, and the enzyme itself, that lets a large drug dose be injected under the skin.
  • Subcutaneous (SC) and intravenous (IV): under the skin versus into a vein. SC versions of IV drugs are what Halozyme enables.
  • Royalty: a percentage of a partner's sales paid to Halozyme for the right to use its technology.
  • MDASE: a separate family of modified enzyme patents that Halozyme owns and has asserted against Merck's subcutaneous Keytruda. It is not part of the ENHANZE license program.
  • Hypercon and Surf Bio: the two concentration technologies bought in late 2025 that let more drug fit in a smaller injection.
  • Convertible note and capped call: a bond that can be settled in cash or shares at a set conversion price, and a purchased option contract that offsets part of the share dilution — the increase in share count that shrinks each share's slice — up to a cap price.
  • IPR&D: acquired in-process research and development, an expense booked when a company buys unapproved technology that has no other use.
  • Adjusted EBITDA: the company's own version of operating earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, with some one-time items removed. The acquired IPR&D charge is not removed.
  • PTAB and PGR: the US Patent Trial and Appeal Board and its post-grant review process, in which a challenger asks the patent office to cancel an issued patent.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The moat is real but narrower than it looks. Ten commercial products already use ENHANZE, and the filing notes that certain drugs co-formulated with it have received extra exclusivity that extends the product's patent life beyond the expiry of the plain intravenous drug.[1] That is a reason for a partner to stay: by our reading of the filing, a partner that moved to a rival enzyme would give up that extra exclusivity. The proof is in the growth, because royalties rose from $203.9 million in 2021 to $867.8 million in 2025 and to $1,042.4 million over the trailing twelve months (our calculation).[3][2][1]

The weakness is concentration. The 10-K does not name partners in its customer table, but it shows one partner at 41% of 2025 revenue, a second at 17%, a third at 16% and a fourth at 6%, and it says 69% of receivables were owed by Janssen, Roche and argenx.[2] The royalty table is more specific. In the quarter ended June 30, 2026, DARZALEX royalties from Janssen were half of all royalties, and VYVGART Hytrulo royalties from argenx were nearly a quarter, so two products produced about 73% of the royalty line (our calculation).[1] A price cut, a label problem or a competing drug on either product would show up directly in Halozyme's earnings.

Royalties by product: how concentrated is the stream

The filing breaks royalties into three named products and an 'other' bucket. The bucket holds the newer launches (Ocrevus SC and Tecentriq SC from Roche, Rybrevant SC from Janssen, Opdivo Qvantig from Bristol Myers Squibb) and the older Herceptin and MabThera products, where the company says modest price erosion is under way. Halozyme does not publish separate figures for Ocrevus, Opdivo Qvantig or Rybrevant, so their individual size is not published, and the filing names them only as the expected drivers of growth in the bucket.[1]

How long the patents last, and what the Merck dispute means

The filing lists four patent groups. The core enzyme (rHuPH20) has an issued US patent that expires in 2027, an issued European patent that expires in 2029, and other patents valid into 2029. A second family called MDASE, covering modified enzymes, has US patents expiring between 2032 and 2034 and European patents expiring in 2032. The Hypercon and Surf Bio patents run into the mid-2040s.[2] The contract language matters as much as the dates: a partner's royalty term ends at the later of a set period or the last valid patent claim, and where no valid claim remains in a country the royalty rate is reduced after the rHuPH20 patents expire.[1] The size of that reduction and the product-by-product timing are not published, and we searched for them without success, so they are the largest unknown in the stock.

Halozyme's fight with Merck is about MDASE, not ENHANZE. Halozyme sued Merck in New Jersey in April 2025, alleging that Merck's subcutaneous Keytruda (Keytruda Qlex) infringes 15 MDASE patents.[20][21] Merck challenged 15 Halozyme patents at the US patent office in all, and the office opened 14 of those reviews and declined one in May 2026.[20] Final decisions have gone against Halozyme in at least nine of them: four earlier decisions that Halozyme has appealed, a September 1 decision on the '520 patent, and four more issued between September 25 and October 2, 2026. The board found the challenged claims unpatentable for lack of written description and enablement. Where the decisions address obviousness, the board did not find it.[17][18][19] Halozyme has appealed the earlier decisions to the Federal Circuit, and three more reviews of older patents were opened in September.[19][18] On the other side, a German court granted Halozyme a preliminary injunction against Keytruda SC in December 2025, and in October 2026 a Dutch court granted an injunction covering eight more European countries and rejected Merck's argument that the European patent is invalid.[8][21]

Halozyme itself states that the MDASE patents are not part of its ENHANZE license program, so the outcome will not change any partner's right to use ENHANZE or the fees for it.[21] We read the dispute as optional upside that is now mostly lost in the US and partly won in Europe, not as a threat to current royalties. It does carry a cost: litigation expense that the company adds back to adjusted EBITDA was $6.9 million in the June quarter alone, and a poor appeal result would remove the main argument for a second royalty stream from the largest cancer drug franchise.[6]

The auto-injector business, the acquisitions and how they were paid for

Halozyme's device business came from the May 2022 Antares purchase.[4] The auto-injector technology carries $402.0 million of acquisition cost on the books and XYOSTED another $136.2 million, and in the quarter proprietary product sales were $55.4 million (up 19%) and device partnered sales $14.6 million (up 24%).[1] A margin for this business is not published, because the company reports one segment, and our searches found none. As a rough guide, cost of sales was 61% of product sales in the June quarter, which implies about 39% gross profit on all product lines together, against 83.5% on total revenue (our calculation).[1] The device pipeline also had a setback: the company wrote off its ATRS-1902 development asset in the fourth quarter of 2025 after deciding to stop the program.[2]

Elektrofi cost $810.4 million in total consideration, of which $787.4 million was cash paid to stockholders and option holders at the November 18, 2025 closing and $23.0 million was the estimated fair value of future milestone payments. The filing says it was funded by cash on hand and rollover equity.[1][2] Surf Bio cost $305.0 million of consideration, with up to $100.0 million more in milestones, and was booked as acquired research and development expense because the technology had no other use, which is why 2025 net income fell.[1][2] In November 2025 the company also raised $1.5 billion by selling two series of convertible notes (0% due 2031 and 0.875% due 2032) and used part of the money to buy back older notes.[8][1]

The financing did not stop there. On September 22, 2026 Halozyme closed a further $1,500.0 million of 1.50% convertible notes due 2033, received about $1,471.1 million after fees, spent $187.5 million on capped calls and about $652.5 million to buy back $151.7 million of 2027 notes and $220.0 million of 2028 notes, and plans to use the rest for general purposes including acquisitions.[15] Paying $217.0 million for $151.7 million of face value, and $435.5 million for $220.0 million, shows how far the share price had risen above those notes' conversion prices of $77.17 and $56.02.[15][1] Chapter 3 follows what this does to leverage.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue grew from $443.3 million in 2021 to $1,396.6 million in 2025, a 28.4% compound annual rate over the last three years (2022 to 2025), and to $1,663.7 million over the trailing twelve months.[3][2][1] Royalties drove it: they were 46% of 2021 revenue and 64% of the latest quarter's. Operating income did not rise in a straight line, because 2022 carried the Antares amortization and 2025 carried the $284.9 million Surf Bio charge and the $48.7 million ATRS-1902 write-off. Before those two 2025 items the operating margin would have been 57.5% (our calculation).[2] Net income of $402.7 million in 2021 included a $154.2 million income tax benefit, so it flatters that year.[3]

Cash is the cleanest part of the story. Free cash flow was 45.0% to 46.2% of revenue in 2023, 2024 and 2025 and 48.5% over the trailing twelve months, because the business needs almost no capital spending (under $16 million a year) and earns royalties that arrive as cash.[3][2][1] Our free cash flow figure is cash from operations minus capital expenditure. It excludes two investing-activity payments: $726.0 million for Elektrofi and $287.8 million for Surf Bio, both net of cash acquired.[2] The $726.0 million is the $810.4 million total consideration in Chapter 2 less $84.4 million of cash acquired.[1] The $287.8 million is the Surf Bio payment as the cash flow statement reports it. It is lower than the $305.0 million of consideration, which includes $10.2 million of transaction expenses, because the filing deducts $10.8 million of accelerated stock-based compensation to reach a $294.2 million acquisition cost and then deducts $6.4 million of cash acquired.[2][1] A reader who wants cash left after acquisitions should subtract both. The trailing free cash flow of $0.337 billion in the portfolio's market snapshot could not be reproduced from the filings, so we use our own figure.[29]

The balance sheet has changed shape. Debt was $1.5 billion from 2022 to 2024, rose to $2.1 billion after the November 2025 note sales and the Elektrofi purchase, and stood at $2,179.6 million of face value on June 30, 2026 against $231.0 million of cash and securities.[4][3][2][1] Equity is small ($143.5 million) because buybacks reduce it, so debt-to-equity looks extreme at about 15 times and tells us little. The ratio that matters is net debt to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA), covered below.

Shareholders have been paid back steadily. The company bought $1,544.8 million of stock from 2021 through 2025 and $332.7 million more in the first half of 2026 (our sums from the cited cash flow statements), and the diluted share count fell from 146.8 million in 2021 to 121.2 million in the June 2026 quarter.[3][2][1][6] There is one thing to hold against that picture. The earnings per share figures that analysts quote are non-GAAP, which means they exclude stock-based compensation, the amortization of acquired intangibles and, for the share count, the extra shares that the convertible notes could create. In the June 2026 quarter, GAAP diluted earnings per share were $1.90 against $2.28 non-GAAP.[6]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter attacks our own numbers. Each estimate in Chapter 5 has one assumption that carries most of its weight, and we name it here with the evidence that would prove it wrong and the date we would know. The bear case in one paragraph: Halozyme is a very good business that has moved from about 9 times earnings at the end of 2025 to about 14 times today, whose second-half royalty ramp is steeper than the first half, whose two biggest products make up nearly three quarters of royalties, whose core enzyme patents expire in 2027 and 2029 with an unpublished royalty step-down, and whose debt rose to about $3.3 billion of face value in September 2026 with the 2027, 2028, 2031 and 2032 notes all convertible at prices below today's share price.[22][1][2][15]

The core enzyme patents expire in 2027 (United States) and 2029 (Europe), and the filings say that when no valid patent claim covers a product in a country the royalty rate is reduced after the rHuPH20 patents expire.[2][1] The size of the reduction and the timing by product are not published, and the 10-Q adds that Janssen's patents covering DARZALEX SC do not affect the timing, which leaves the position of the largest product unclear.[1] If the step-down is large and early, royalties could stop growing in 2027 and the 18 times terminal multiple in our DCF would be wrong.

Evidence that would show it: guidance for 2027 royalty growth well below the 2026 pace, or a partner filing that cites reduced royalty rates. Management's own answer is that new agreements and co-formulation patents carry royalties into the 2040s, but that is a statement about future deals, not about the rate on today's products.[9][7]

Fatal flaw 2: two products and one partner turn

DARZALEX and VYVGART Hytrulo produced 73% of June-quarter royalties.[1] A price cut, a safety or label change, or a competing product on either drug would cut Halozyme's royalties directly. The 10-Q also discloses that a partner is deciding whether to return a drug target, with a decision now expected by February 2027. Evidence that would show it: sequential decline in DARZALEX or VYVGART royalties in the third-quarter report on November 5, 2026, or an impairment note in the annual report.[16] We would know on November 5 for the first and by February 2027 for the second.

Fatal flaw 3: the financing and the convertible notes

Halozyme's June 2026 notes would convert into about 28.3 million shares at their stated conversion prices, worth about $3.56 billion at $125.63 against $2.18 billion of face value (our calculation from the cited conversion prices).
