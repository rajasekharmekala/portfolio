1. What the company does

Alphabet is a holding company, and Google is its largest business. It earns money from three kinds of customers. Advertisers paid $294.7 billion in 2025, which was 73.2% of its $402.8 billion of revenue. Most of that came from Google Search & other ($224.5 billion, 55.7%). Next came YouTube ads ($40.4 billion). The rest was ads that Google places on other companies' sites and apps, called Google Network ($29.8 billion).[1] Consumers and device buyers paid $48.0 billion for subscriptions, apps and phones (11.9%), and companies paid $58.7 billion to Google Cloud (14.6%).[1] Other Bets, the group that includes the Waymo robotaxi service, added only $1.5 billion (0.4%).[1] The shares we hold, GOOG, are Class C: they carry no vote. Class A shares (GOOGL) carry one vote each and Class B shares carry ten. The founders Larry Page and Sergey Brin owned about 89.3% of Class B. That gave them about 52.7% of the voting power at the end of 2025.[1][14]

The advertising model is an auction. When a person types a query, advertisers bid for the space beside the answer, and Google is paid when the ad is clicked or when the sale it led to is recorded. Google shares part of the money with the companies that send it traffic. These payments are called traffic acquisition costs (TAC), and they go to browser makers, phone makers and the sites in Google Network. TAC was $16.2 billion in the June quarter, or 19.8% of advertising revenue. A year earlier it was 20.6%. The rate falls because Search, the faster-growing part, pays out a smaller share than the slower Network part.[4][9][1] Subscriptions are the second leg: YouTube Premium and Music, YouTube TV, Google One storage and the new Google AI plans. Management said in April that paid subscriptions across consumer services reached 350 million.[5]

Google Cloud sells computing power, data tools, security, productivity software (Workspace) and now AI models to companies. Its revenue was $24.8 billion in the June quarter, up 82% from $13.6 billion a year earlier,[8] and its operating margin (the share of revenue left after running costs) was 35.6%, up from 20.7%.[4][9] Multiplying the quarter by four gives a run rate of $99.1 billion. That is our arithmetic, because Alphabet does not report annual recurring revenue (ARR, the yearly value of subscriptions that renew). What it does report is revenue backlog, also called remaining performance obligations (RPO): signed contracts not yet recognised as revenue. Backlog was $519.5 billion at June 30, of which $513.9 billion was Cloud, and the company expects to recognise just over 50% of it within 24 months.[2] Two new pieces arrived in 2026. Alphabet closed its purchase of the security firm Wiz in March. In the second quarter it also delivered its own Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) chips, as complete systems, to customers' data centers for the first time. Cloud now sells hardware as well as services.[10][9]

Everything else is investment. Other Bets had revenue of $382 million and an operating loss of $1.8 billion in the June quarter. The loss for the first half of 2026 was $3.9 billion against $2.5 billion a year earlier, up 58%.[4][5] Waymo is the best known: it was giving more than 400,000 paid rides a week in December and passed 500,000 a week by late March.[11][10][20] A separate line, Alphabet-level activities, lost $5.8 billion in the quarter, mostly shared research on Gemini, the company's family of AI models.[4] About 51% of June-quarter revenue came from the United States, and the company employed 198,933 people.[2][4] Finally, 2026 changed how the business is funded. Alphabet issued $49.6 billion of new stock and mandatory convertible preferred stock in June, plus $20.3 billion of senior notes in the quarter. It has not bought back any shares this year.[4][2] Chapter 3 shows why.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Class C share: Alphabet stock with no vote, listed as GOOG; Class A (GOOGL) has one vote and Class B has ten.
  • Traffic acquisition costs (TAC): money Google pays to partners that send it search traffic or host its ads.
  • Free cash flow (FCF): operating cash flow less purchases of property and equipment, the cash left after building data centers.
  • Revenue backlog (RPO): signed customer contracts that have not yet been recognised as revenue.
  • Tensor Processing Unit (TPU): Google's own AI chip, now sold inside complete systems to outside customers.
  • Gemini: Google's family of AI models, sold to companies through Google Cloud and used inside Search and YouTube.
  • Other Bets: Alphabet's early-stage ventures, mainly Waymo, with small revenue and large losses.
  • Mandatory convertible preferred stock: a share class that pays a fixed dividend and converts into common stock on a set date (May 2029).
  • At-the-market (ATM) program: a standing permission to sell new shares gradually at market prices; Alphabet's is up to $40.0 billion.[2]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Alphabet's position is strong because it owns every layer of the AI business at once: the chips (TPUs), the models (Gemini), the cloud that rents both out, and the products that hundreds of millions of people already use. Search revenue grew 17% in the June quarter even though people now get AI-written answers, while Meta's total revenue grew 28%.[4][31] Management says AI Mode has passed one billion monthly users, the Gemini app has 950 million, and its model interfaces now handle about 22 billion tokens a minute, up from 16 billion a quarter earlier.[9] These are company-reported claims. We treat them as signs of usage, not as audited facts. The audited signs are the revenue lines and the 41.8% operating margin of Google Services.[4]

The evidence on the Cloud side is the backlog and the margin. Backlog grew from $155 billion at the end of September 2025 to $240 billion in December, more than $460 billion in March (the 10-Q gives $467.6 billion for the whole company) and $514 billion in June.[12][11][10][3][9] Margin rose at the same time, from 17.5% in the fourth quarter of 2024 to 35.6% now, which suggests Google is not buying growth with discounts.[11][9] The CEO says Cloud customers now exceed their commitments by more than 50% and that nearly 90% of the Fortune 100 use Gemini Enterprise.[9] Management says the majority of the backlog is typical Google Cloud contracts from a broad mix of customers, but it does not say how much depends on a few AI labs or how much is TPU hardware, so we cannot size the concentration.[9]

Competition is real and also well funded. Microsoft's Azure grew 43% in the quarter ended June 30, passed $100 billion of yearly revenue for the first time and carries $678 billion of commercial backlog.[28][29] Amazon Web Services grew 37% to $42.2 billion for the quarter, a $169 billion yearly run rate, and earned $16.6 billion of operating profit.[30] Google Cloud's $24.8 billion quarter is smaller than both and is growing faster from that lower starting point. In advertising, Meta grew revenue 28% to $60.8 billion and is Google's closest rival for performance ad budgets.[31] Google Search also faces a different threat than a rival search engine: AI chat products that answer questions without a list of links. So far the revenue says that threat has not shown up, and Google's CEO says queries are at an all-time high.[9][5]

Where the business is weak

The weakness is capital intensity and what it did to cash. Capital spending, also called capital expenditure, was $132.4 billion over the last twelve months, 29.7% of revenue, against 9.6% in 2021, and the company now guides $195 billion to $205 billion for 2026, more than the $185.7 billion of operating cash it earned in the past year.[4][9] To pay for it Alphabet raised $20.0 billion and $31.8 billion of dollar and foreign-currency notes in 2026, sold $30.5 billion of common stock (including $10.0 billion to an affiliate of Berkshire Hathaway) and $19.1 billion of preferred stock in June, and stopped buying back shares.[2][4][15][16] Long-term debt rose from $10.9 billion at the end of 2024 to $98.2 billion, and the share count rose 1.2% in six months, from 12,088 million to 12,230 million.[1][4] The balance sheet remains strong with $140.1 billion of net cash, but the direction is now toward external funding.

Two other weak points deserve a plain statement. First, the legal overhang is large. A federal judge entered a final judgment in the Search case in December 2025 that limits how Google distributes its services and requires it to share certain search data with competitors; both sides appealed.[2][19] In the ad-tech case the judge declined on September 2, 2026 to force a sale of the AdX exchange and ordered behavioural remedies instead, to run six years under a monitor.[17][18] Europe added fines: Google paid $5.2 billion in July 2026 on the final Android decision and was ordered to pay about $2.1 billion in damages to the price-comparison site PriceRunner.[2] Second, Gemini is not leading on every measure. The CEO told analysts that coding is an area where Google needs to improve and that the next model, Gemini 4, needs a much larger underlying model to compete at the frontier.[9]

What this means for an owner: the franchise is stronger than it was a year ago, because Search is growing, Cloud has doubled its backlog and the margins are rising. The change is in the price of that strength. The company is spending more than it earns in cash, is selling stock to do it, and is exposed to a court system that has now ruled against it in two separate monopoly cases. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record on what it said it would do is good on operations and mixed on money. It delivered the Cloud acceleration, the Waymo scale-up and the first TPU system deliveries on the schedule it gave, and it warned correctly that depreciation and cash flow would come under pressure. The weak point is the spending guide itself: the 2025 figure was raised once and the 2026 figure twice, so the original numbers understated the build.

The capital-return history is the other thing to read carefully. Alphabet bought back $169.4 billion of its stock from 2023 through 2025, then stopped in 2026 and sold stock instead, including $10.0 billion to an affiliate of Berkshire Hathaway at about $348 a share for Class C stock.[1][15] Management had no stated promise to keep buying shares, so we do not call this a broken promise. It does change what an owner can count on: share count is now rising, not falling.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 follows the money in four steps: what was earned, what was collected, what is owed and what the stock is assumed to be worth. Over five years Alphabet's revenue rose from $257.6 billion to $402.8 billion, and over the latest twelve months it reached $445.9 billion. Operating margin dipped to 26.5% in 2022, climbed to 32% in 2024 and 2025, and reached 33.1% over the twelve months to June.[13][1][4] Earnings per share (EPS, profit divided by shares) rose from $5.61 to $10.81 between 2021 and 2025. Net income looks far larger, at $244.1 billion for the trailing year, but that figure includes a $99.0 billion gain on equity securities in the June quarter alone, so we value the business on operating income and on cash, not on reported net income.[4][2]

Cash is where the story changes. Operating cash flow rose in every year, from $91.7 billion in 2021 to $185.7 billion over the latest twelve months. Capital spending rose faster: $24.6 billion in 2021, $91.4 billion in 2025 and $132.4 billion in the latest twelve months. The difference is free cash flow, which was $73.3 billion in 2025 and $53.3 billion now. In the June quarter it was negative. A company that earns 33% operating margins but converts only 12 cents of each revenue dollar into free cash is in the middle of an investment cycle, and the question for an owner is how long the cycle lasts.[1][4][13]

The balance sheet is large and has changed shape. Cash and marketable securities were $242.5 billion on June 30 against $101.1 billion of notes and $1.3 billion drawn on credit lines, so net cash was $140.1 billion. That is $55 billion below the $195 billion on the watchlist seed, which we could not support. Long-term debt was $10.9 billion at the end of 2024 and is $98.2 billion now. Moody's assigned an Aa2 rating (stable outlook) and S&P an AA+ issue rating to Alphabet's new notes in May 2026, as reported by a secondary source.[2][4][21]

We tested the COMPOUNDER archetype first, which asks for ROIC of at least 12%, free cash flow margin of at least 12%, net debt (borrowings minus cash) under 3 times EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) and stock-based pay under 5% of revenue. It fails on two of those four financial tests, and on the technical and reward-to-risk tests.

Two terms need explaining before the table. Our technical test uses the Weinstein stage scale: a stock in Stage 1 is building a base, a flat price range that follows a decline; Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend that starts when the price breaks out of that range; and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. The pivot is the top of the base, and a breakout is a close above the pivot on rising volume. A measured move is a price target found by adding the height of the base to the pivot. Asymmetry, which we also call reward-to-risk, is the potential gain divided by the potential loss: the distance from the price to our target divided by the distance from the price down to our stop. Core names need at least 3.0, which means three dollars of expected gain for each dollar at risk. We then tested the turnaround and tactical archetypes honestly; neither qualifies.

5. What could happen next

A reverse DCF (discounted cash flow) works backwards. Instead of forecasting cash flows and deriving a price, it takes the price and asks how fast cash flow must grow, for ten years, to justify it, using a 10% discount rate and a final value of 18 times the tenth-year cash flow. The shared ledger answered +35.1% a year, using a free cash flow of $22.665 billion. That input does not match Alphabet's own filings, which show $53.273 billion over the trailing twelve months, so we replaced it and the answer becomes +23.1% a year.[4][33][34]

What does +23.1% a year mean? Free cash flow would have to rise from $53 billion to about $424 billion in ten years. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one weak assumption. We name it, say what evidence would prove it wrong and say when that evidence arrives. The next dated event is Alphabet's third-quarter report on Wednesday, October 28, 2026, with a call at 1:30 p.m. Pacific time.[22][23] That report will show whether free cash flow turned positive again, what Cloud margin did under the third-party capacity bridge and whether the capital spending guide moved again.

The reverse DCF assumes trailing cash flow is a fair starting point

The weakest assumption behind +23.1% is that $53.3 billion is a clean base. It is not. It contains two strong quarters from late 2025 ($24.5 billion and $24.6 billion) that will drop out as the next two reports arrive, and two weak ones ($10.1 billion and -$5.9 billion).[4] Analysts' 2026 figure of $7.07 billion says the trailing number is on its way down.[24] On that figure the same price needs +52.5% a year. In the other direction, inventory rose to $10.0 billion from $2.4 billion in six months as Alphabet built TPU systems for customers, and that cash returns when the systems are delivered, mostly in 2027.[4][9] Here is the one test, used everywhere in this dossier. Third-quarter free cash flow below $10 billion (the first-quarter 2026 level was $10.1 billion) would confirm our view that $53.3 billion is too high a base, because the trailing figure would then fall toward the analysts' $7 billion for the year. Third-quarter free cash flow of $10 billion or more would show the base is fairer than we think and would count against our caution. We will know on October 28.

The earnings estimate assumes depreciation and rented capacity do not crush margin

The $15.29 estimate for 2027 assumes operating margin keeps expanding while depreciation grows 42% a year and Cloud runs part of its load on third-party capacity. Management itself says the bridge will 'create modest margin pressure' and that Wiz will pressure Cloud margin by a low single-digit number of points.[9][10] A Cloud margin below 30% in the third quarter, from 35.6% now, would be the first sign that growth is being bought. Capital spending of about $59.7 billion a quarter in the second half implies another leap in depreciation in 2027. The evidence would be a Cloud margin below 30% on October 28 or a 2027 capex guide, due with the February 2027 results, above $250 billion.

The price target assumes the multiple stays near its three-year median

Our central price multiplies $15.29 by 23.79, the median of three year-end readings. Three points are a thin sample, and the ratio on 2026 earnings is 25.64x, above the median. The stress that matters is the multiple: at the five-year low of 19.09 and unchanged earnings, the price is 15.29 x 19.09 = $291.89, which is 13.4% below the close, and the reward-to-risk ratio is negative. Cutting earnings alone by 10% to $13.76 at the median multiple gives 13.76 x 23.79 = $327.35, 2.8% below the close; the two together give the bear price of $262.68, 22.0% below. Most of the downside therefore comes from the multiple, which can fall with no bad news at all. The evidence would be a close below $323.97 or earnings estimates that stop rising; revisions can be read every week on the consensus pages, and the next big update comes after the October 28 report.[25][24]

The paragraphs below give the strongest case against us, three ways the thesis could fail outright and the one claim everything rests on. Red-team bear case: a few large AI customers re-time their commitments, the $514 billion backlog converts more slowly, capital spending runs near $200 billion for another year, the court of appeals upholds the Search judgment and the multiple drops to 19.09 on earnings cut 10% to $13.76. Fatal-flaw scenario one, an AI demand air pocket: management says most of the backlog is ordinary Google Cloud contracts from a broad mix of customers, but it does not publish how much comes from a few AI labs, many of them funded by outside capital, or how much is TPU hardware, and some of the demand is being served on rented capacity. Fatal-flaw scenario two, an adverse remedy on Search: Search and other was 55.7% of 2025 revenue.
