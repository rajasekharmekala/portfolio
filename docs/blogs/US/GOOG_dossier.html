1. What the company does

Alphabet is the holding company for Google. It earns money from three kinds of customers: advertisers, businesses that rent computing power, and consumers who pay for subscriptions and devices. In 2025 its revenue was $402.8 billion.[2] In the third quarter of 2026, which ended 30 September, revenue was $127.6 billion, up 25 percent. Search and other ads brought in $63.35 billion, YouTube ads $11.2 billion, partner websites in the Google Network $7.5 billion, subscriptions, platforms and devices $14.3 billion, and Google Cloud $30.7 billion.[1] Advertisers pay for the first three, consumers pay for the fourth, and companies pay for the last.

The mix is changing fast. Google Cloud rents computing power, data tools, AI models and workplace software to businesses, and since the second quarter it also sells complete TPU systems, which are Alphabet's own AI chips.[1] Cloud revenue doubled from $15.2 billion to $30.7 billion in a year. By our arithmetic, Cloud supplied $15.55 billion of the $25.23 billion revenue increase, about 62 percent of all the growth (30,709 minus 15,157, divided by 127,577 minus 102,346, in millions).[1] Advertising still pays for most of the profit, though. Google Services earned $39.2 billion of operating income in the quarter against $11.1 billion for Cloud.[1]

Three costs sit outside those two engines. Alphabet-level activities, mostly shared AI research, cost $7.7 billion in the quarter, up from $4.5 billion a year earlier. Other Bets, which includes the Waymo driverless ride service, lost $2.0 billion. And traffic acquisition costs, the fees Alphabet pays phone makers and browsers to route searches to Google, were $16.4 billion.[1] The quarterly profit therefore depends on advertising staying healthy while the AI bill grows.

The shares come in three classes with the same economic rights but different votes. Class B shares carry ten votes, Class A one vote, and Class C, the GOOG shares in this book, none.[2][10] Larry Page and Sergey Brin owned about 89.3 percent of Class B at the end of 2025, which was about 52.7 percent of the total voting power.[2] A Class C owner has price and dividend rights but no vote, so the founders can decide any shareholder vote alone. In June 2026 Alphabet sold new Class A and Class C shares to the public and to an affiliate of Berkshire Hathaway at about $351 to $355 a share, well above today's price.[9][5]

How the cash engine pays for the AI build

Think of Alphabet as a farm with one very productive field (advertising) and a new, much larger field being planted (AI infrastructure). The old field produced $164.7 billion of cash flow from operations, which is the cash a business generates from its normal operations, in 2025, and the planting cost $91.4 billion of capital expenditure, which means money spent on data centers, chips and equipment that last years.[2] In the twelve months to 30 September 2026, operating cash was $179.6 billion and capital expenditure was $159.8 billion, so only $19.7 billion was left.[1]

In the third quarter alone, operating cash was $42.3 billion and capital expenditure was $51.4 billion.[1] The gap was covered by cash on hand and by new money: $28.7 billion of notes and $12.4 billion of new shares sold through an at-the-market program, a program that sells shares gradually at market prices.[1] That funding choice, not the revenue, is the main reason the share price fell after the report.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The core of the moat is reach. Google held 89.94 percent of worldwide search in September 2026, against 5.29 percent for Bing.[20] That reach feeds a flywheel: more queries give more data to improve results and ads, and the ads fund the research. The third-quarter release says the year-over-year growth rate of Search queries was the highest in five years, and Alphabet credits its AI answers for that.[1] The number that tests the flywheel is revenue per query, and there the news was softer: Search and other revenue grew 12 percent to $63.35 billion, below the $64.96 billion analysts expected, after growing 19 percent in the first quarter and 17 percent in the second.[11][7][6]

The second source of strength is the full AI stack. Alphabet designs its own chips (TPUs), trains its own models (Gemini), and sells both through Google Cloud. The Gemini app reached one billion users, and the company's model interfaces process nearly 32 billion tokens a minute, more than four times a year ago.[1] Customers are buying: Cloud revenue grew 103 percent, ahead of Azure at 48 percent and Amazon Web Services at 37 percent in its latest filed quarter.[1][24][27] Cloud's segment operating margin, which is operating income divided by revenue, rose to 36.2 percent from 23.7 percent a year earlier (11,121 over 30,709, against 3,594 over 15,157).[1] Signed backlog stood at $519.5 billion at 30 June, $513.9 billion of it in Cloud, up from $155 billion in October 2025.[5][16]

The weaknesses are about money and control, not demand. First, spending now exceeds internal cash. Capital expenditure of $51.4 billion in one quarter was 21 percent above cash from operations of $42.3 billion, and management expects spending to rise significantly again in 2027.[1][11] Second, the earnings line is noisy. Alphabet booked a $99.0 billion gain on its stakes in other companies in the second quarter and an $8.7 billion loss in the third, which is why reported EPS fell from $2.87 to $2.12 while operating income rose 30 percent.[6][1] Third, AI chatbots are a new rival for the search habit: ChatGPT held 80.48 percent of chatbot use in September against 10.91 percent for Gemini.[21]

The fourth weakness is the legal overhang. A final judgment in the US Search case, entered in December 2025, limits how Google distributes its services and requires data sharing with rivals; Google appealed in January 2026 and the Justice Department appealed in February.[5] In the ad technology case a federal court rejected a forced sale of the AdX exchange in September 2026 but imposed behavioral limits for six years.[22][23] The European Commission fined Alphabet 3.0 billion euros in September 2025 over ad technology, and that appeal is pending.[5] None of these is a solvency threat for a company with $265.1 billion of cash and securities, but together they cap how much Alphabet can use its distribution power.[1]

Why founder control matters to a Class C owner

Class C shares carry no vote, and Class B shares carry ten.[10] The consequence for you is concrete. If management chose to fund the AI build with more shares, as it did in June and again in the third quarter, no Class C owner could vote against it. The company sold 24 million Class C shares in the third quarter for $8.1 billion through the at-the-market program, and $27.6 billion of that program remained available at quarter end.[1] That is a governance fact, not a legal red flag, and it is one reason the share count in chapter 2B is scored as a watch item.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A walk-the-talk audit asks a plain question: when management said what it would do, did it? On spending, the answer is that it did what it said and then more. The 2025 capital expenditure forecast started at $75 billion, was raised to $85 billion, then to $91 billion to $93 billion in October 2025, and the year closed at $91.4 billion.[16][2] The 2026 forecast has the same shape: $175 billion to $185 billion in February, $180 billion to $190 billion in April, and $195 billion to $205 billion in July.[14][15][13]

The record on funding is weaker. In July the company said the new at-the-market program was primarily meant to cover tax on employee equity grants, and that none had been sold by 30 June.[6] In the third quarter it sold $12.4 billion of stock through the program, while its net payments on stock awards were $5.4 billion, so the proceeds were about 2.3 times the stated need.[1] Share buybacks went the other way: $45.7 billion in 2025, and none in the first nine months of 2026.[2][1] Management did not promise buybacks, but a company that spends $39.8 billion on them in nine months of 2025 and then sells stock is asking owners to read a changed policy.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter attacks our own estimates. The bear case in one paragraph: Alphabet is turning a cash machine into a capital-hungry builder at the top of a spending cycle. Fatal flaw 1: spending outruns what Cloud can earn

The weakest assumption in our base value is that the 2027 build earns Cloud-like returns.
