1. What the company does

Alphabet is the holding company for Google. Almost all of its $402.8 billion of 2025 revenue came from three kinds of customers: advertisers, businesses that rent computing power, and consumers who pay for subscriptions and devices.[1] Advertisers pay for ads next to search results ($224.5 billion in 2025), on YouTube ($40.4 billion) and on partner websites through the Google Network ($29.8 billion).[1] Businesses pay Google Cloud, which earned $58.7 billion, for computing power, data tools, workplace software and AI services.[1] Consumers and device buyers added $48.0 billion through paid subscriptions, app purchases and devices.[1]

Google Services is the cash engine. It earned $139.4 billion of operating income in 2025 on $342.7 billion of revenue.[1] Search sells ads against questions typed or spoken into Google, and it pays partners, such as phone makers, for sending those questions its way; these payments are called traffic acquisition costs and were $16.2 billion in the second quarter of 2026.[5] Google Cloud earns usage fees and subscriptions, and since the second quarter of 2026 it also books product revenue from selling TPU systems, which are Alphabet's own AI chips.[4][5] Other Bets holds Waymo, the driverless ride service; its revenue was $1.5 billion in 2025 against an operating loss of $7.5 billion.[1]

AI is now both the product and the cost. The Gemini app has 950 million monthly active users, and Alphabet's models process 22 billion tokens per minute through its developer interfaces.[5] The cost shows up in two places. The shared AI research that is not charged to any segment cost $5.8 billion in the second quarter of 2026, up from $3.4 billion a year earlier.[5] And capital expenditure, which means spending on data centers, chips and equipment, was $44.9 billion in that quarter alone, about double the $22.4 billion of a year earlier.[5]

Alphabet has three share classes with identical economic rights but different votes. Class B shares carry ten votes, Class A one vote, and Class C, the GOOG shares in this book, none.[1] Larry Page and Sergey Brin held about 89.3 percent of the Class B shares at the end of 2025, which was about 52.7 percent of the total voting power.[1] A Class C owner therefore has price and dividend rights but no vote, and the founders can decide shareholder votes alone. On 23 October 2026 the Class A shares closed at $320.21 while the Class C price used throughout this book was $317.28.[17][19]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of this business is that three growth engines run on one set of assets. Search and YouTube produce advertising cash. Google Cloud turns Alphabet's chips (TPUs), its Gemini models and its data centers into a service that large customers sign multi-year contracts for. The signed Cloud backlog was $513.9 billion at the end of June 2026, more than double the $242.8 billion at the end of 2025, and the company expects to recognise just over half of the total backlog as revenue within 24 months.[1][4] Few competitors own the chips, the models and the distribution at once.

The weakness is that every one of those engines needs more spending than last year. The company raised its 2026 capital expenditure forecast from a range of $180 billion to $190 billion to a new range of $195 billion to $205 billion, and said spending would rise again in 2027.[9][14] The chief financial officer said the increase was driven mainly by faster delivery of capacity to meet demand.[9] Spending that follows signed contracts is better than spending on hope, but it still has to be paid for before the revenue arrives.

Search and YouTube: still growing, with AI as the open question

Search and other revenue grew 17 percent in the second quarter of 2026 to $63.3 billion, after 19 percent in the first quarter; YouTube ads grew 13 percent.[5][6] Google held 89.94 percent of worldwide search in September 2026, against 5.29 percent for Bing.[21] The same source shows the threat: in the AI chatbot measure, ChatGPT had 80.48 percent and Google Gemini 10.91 percent.[22] That measure counts web visits only, not app use, so it understates Gemini's reach, but it says the question of who answers people's questions first is open. For now the money still arrives, because advertising revenue keeps growing at double-digit rates.[1][5]

A product delay shows the competition is real. In July 2026 Alphabet shares fell about 4 percent on a report that the flagship Gemini 3.5 Pro model was months behind schedule, with weak coding ability named as a reason.[13] We could not verify the model's release status after July from a primary source, so we treat it as an open risk and not as a fact.

Google Cloud: growth and margin both rising

Google Cloud revenue rose 82 percent to $24.8 billion in the second quarter of 2026, after 63 percent in the first quarter and 48 percent in the fourth quarter of 2025.[5][6][7] Operating income was $8.8 billion, a 35.6 percent margin, against 20.7 percent a year earlier (our calculation from the segment table).[5] Each extra dollar of Cloud revenue brought about 54 cents of extra operating income over the year (our calculation: $6.0 billion more profit on $11.1 billion more revenue).[5] Part of the second-quarter revenue came from selling TPU systems for the first time, which can be lumpier than usage fees, and management said growth was higher even without them.[4][14]

Antitrust and governance: the risks that are not about demand

Search: the US court issued a final judgment in December 2025 that limits how Google distributes its services and requires it to share some search data with, and offer syndication to, certain competitors. Google appealed in January 2026 and the Justice Department and states cross-appealed in February 2026.[4] Advertising technology: on 2 September 2026 the Virginia court refused the Justice Department's request to break up the ad technology business and ordered behavioral limits instead, which stay in place for six years and take effect 60 days after the final judgment is entered.[23][24] In Europe, the Android fine became final in July 2026 and Alphabet paid $5.2 billion; a separate €3.0 billion ad technology fine is under appeal and the Digital Markets Act investigations remain open.[4]

These cases matter for a Class C owner in two ways. The cost is mostly behavioral limits and fines that are small next to $147.6 billion of trailing operating income (our calculation), so they do not threaten solvency. The larger risk is slower growth if distribution deals shrink, and a governance structure in which the founders hold 52.7 percent of votes and Class C holders have none.[1] We treat both as reasons to cap the position size, not as red lines.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Over three years from 2022 to 2025 revenue grew at 12.5 percent a year and operating income at 19.9 percent a year (our calculation from the filings).[3][1] Net income grew faster, 30.1 percent a year, but it is flattered by gains on investments: other income was $29.8 billion in 2025 and $98.0 billion in the second quarter of 2026 alone, mostly unrealised gains on stakes in other companies.[1][5] That is why reported earnings per share (EPS), the profit divided by shares, jumped to $9.11 in the second quarter against about $2.88 expected; operating income is the cleaner guide.[5][14]

Cash tells a different story. Cash from operations grew from $91.7 billion in 2021 to $185.7 billion in the twelve months to June 2026, but capital expenditure grew from $24.6 billion to $132.4 billion, so free cash flow (FCF), the cash left after buying equipment, went from $67.0 billion to $53.3 billion.[3][5] On Alphabet's own definition, FCF was 18.2 percent of 2025 revenue and 11.95 percent of revenue in the twelve months to June 2026, which is the number that misses our 12 percent floor by five hundredths of a point.[1][5]

The balance sheet is strong but changing. Cash and securities were $242.5 billion at the end of June 2026 against $98.2 billion of long-term debt, so Alphabet has net cash, meaning cash exceeds debt, of about $142 billion after counting the $2.0 billion due within a year.[5][4] Most of the June cash came from selling $49.6 billion of equity and $20.3 billion of notes during the quarter, and long-term debt has more than doubled since December.[5] Property and equipment, which is the asset side of the spending, grew from $246.6 billion to $321.2 billion in six months.[5]

5. What could happen next

A Reverse DCF works backwards: it asks what yearly growth in cash flow, for ten years, the share price already assumes. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above has a weakest assumption. The third-quarter cash flow estimate assumes operating cash flow stays near the second-quarter level, which the company does not guide. The earnings estimate assumes consensus figures are free of investment gains, but the 2026 figure of $13.33 is lower than the $14.24 reported for the first half alone, so the estimates mix definitions.[20][5] The base target assumes no re-rating while the ten-year Treasury yield is about 5 percent, and a higher yield usually lowers multiples.[16] The probabilities are judgment, and the evidence that would show them wrong is the 28 October report.

The bear case, as strongly as it can be made: Alphabet has become a heavy-spending infrastructure company that is paying for growth by selling shares and borrowing, and the stock is already below the $351.80 per share that public buyers of Class C stock paid in June and the $348.20 that an affiliate of Berkshire Hathaway paid.[8][4] If the backlog turns out to be slower to deliver than expected, capital spending would run ahead of revenue for longer, free cash flow would stay near zero, and the multiple would fall toward 18 times.

Fatal flaw 1: spending runs ahead of payback

Backlog is signed contracts, not cash, and the filing notes that it excludes cancellable contracts and depends on delivering capacity on time.[4] Part of Cloud growth is now TPU system sales, a product line that can be lumpy.[4][5] If Cloud growth slows from 82 percent toward 40 percent while second-half spending reaches about $119 billion, Cloud margin would fall and free cash flow would stay negative into 2027. The sign of this flaw would be a third-quarter Cloud margin under 30 percent or a backlog addition much below the $50 billion-plus of the second quarter.[14]

Fatal flaw 2: funding and dilution

The company sold $49.6 billion of equity in June and set up an at-the-market program of up to $40.0 billion, which the company says is mainly to meet tax obligations on employee share grants.[5] The preferred stock pays 6.25 percent and converts into shares.[4] Interest expense rose from $0.26 billion to $1.28 billion a quarter in a year.[5] If more equity is sold at prices under the June offering, Class C owners are diluted and per-share growth falls short of company growth. The sign would be a visible share count above 12.4 billion on the 28 October report.

Fatal flaw 3: search erosion and remedies together

The chatbot measure shows ChatGPT at 80.48 percent against Gemini at 10.91 percent, and the US search judgment under appeal requires data sharing and syndication to competitors.[22][4] If AI answers reduce the number of paid searches while the remedy helps rivals, the cash engine that pays for the build-out slows. The sign would be Search growth under 10 percent for two quarters; it was 17 percent in the second quarter.

7. What we would do, and when

Four ideas drive the decision, and each is plain arithmetic. Layer 1 is the static and trend test in chapter 3, Layer 2 is the five-KPI scorecard in chapter 2B, and Layer 3 is the entry and sizing rule in this chapter. A conviction tier is our judgment of how much the evidence deserves, and each tier has a maximum weight: TIER_1 is 7 to 10 percent of net worth, TIER_2 is 4 to 6.5 percent, and TIER_0 is zero size. Risk-unit sizing, also called portfolio heat, sets the weight so that a loss to the stop costs a fixed share of net worth, measured in basis points (bps), where one bps is one hundredth of a percent; TIER_2 allows 75 bps. Current market price (CMP) is the $317.28 close.

A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days. The simple version (SMA) weights all days equally, and the exponential version (EMA) weights recent days more. A structural invalidation stop is a price that, if the stock closes under it, shows that the support the thesis relied on has failed; here that is $308.57, one ATR below the 63-day low of $316.99. The let-winners-run rule says never to trim a rising Stage 2 stock only because RSI is high or the weight passed 15 percent, and to let the weight run up to 22 percent while the stop trails the rising 21-day EMA or 50-day SMA.
