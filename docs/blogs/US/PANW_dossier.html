1. What the company does

Palo Alto Networks is based in Santa Clara, California. It sells security tools that protect company networks, cloud systems and employee devices. Since February 2026 it also protects the logins that people, machines and software programs use. More than 70,000 customers buy from it [2]. In the fiscal year to July 31, 2026, subscription and support was 80.1% of revenue. That is $9.2 billion, up 24% [1]. Product, mainly firewall hardware and software licenses, was 19.9% at $2.3 billion, up 27% [1]. A customer that buys a firewall typically also buys support. Many add subscriptions for threat prevention, cloud security or security operations. The company sells through two steps. It sells to distributors, who sell to resellers, who sell to the end customer [1].

Revenue grew 24% to $11,480 million, and two purchases supplied part of the rise. Chronosphere and CyberArk together added $930 million of revenue after the dates they were bought. That is about 41% of the $2,259 million increase (our calculation). They also added an operating loss of $797 million [1]. Management now reports three platforms. Network and AI Security earned $8.35 billion, up 17%. Cortex, which covers security operations and observability, earned $1.92 billion, up 25%. Idira, the identity security platform built on CyberArk, earned $1.26 billion on a pro forma basis, which means as if owned all year, up 21% [16]. These platform figures do not add up to reported revenue. Idira is shown as if owned all year, and professional services sit in a separate line. We use the platform figures only to compare growth rates.

The business model is called platformization. The company wants a customer to replace many small security products with one connected set of its own. Management says customers that did this showed net revenue retention (NRR) above 120% in the fourth quarter. NRR is the share of last year's revenue that is still there this year, plus any growth [16]. The goal is more than 4,000 platformizations and $20 billion of next-generation security ARR (annual recurring revenue) by fiscal 2030 [2][16]. A transcript of the fourth-quarter call prints the net new platformizations for that quarter as approximately 22. An analyst's question on the same call prints 200 year-to-date at the third quarter and $4.50 for the full year. The printed figures do not reconcile, so we do not rely on a count [16].

Cash arrives faster than profit. Customers often pay for several years in advance. Deferred revenue, which is cash received for service not yet delivered, was $14.76 billion at July 31, 2026. Of that, $7.75 billion is due within a year and $7.01 billion later [1]. Management says annual billing, which spreads cash over the contract, rose from 6% of bookings in fiscal 2020 to 27% in fiscal 2025 and about 30% in fiscal 2026 [16]. That shift means less cash is collected up front on a given contract. Free cash flow, the cash left after running the business and buying equipment, can then grow slower than revenue even when the business is healthy. Of the $11,480 million total, the Americas supplied 66.9%, EMEA 21.1% and Asia Pacific 12.0% [1].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ARR (annual recurring revenue) is the yearly value of subscriptions at a date, assuming contracts renew on the same terms. The company reports a narrower line called next-generation security ARR, which leaves out hardware and older attached services.
  • RPO (remaining performance obligations) is the value of signed contracts not yet recognised as revenue. It is the company's contracted backlog.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is cash from operations minus spending on equipment and similar assets. The company also publishes an adjusted version that adds back some one-off items.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying staff in shares. It is a real cost, because new shares dilute the existing owners.
  • GAAP (generally accepted accounting principles) is the official accounting standard. Non-GAAP figures leave out items such as SBC, amortisation of acquired intangibles and acquisition costs, so they look more profitable.
  • Platformization is the company's term for a customer replacing many point products with its connected platforms. The company also reports a count of such customers.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. That money is equity plus debt minus surplus cash and investments.
  • Reverse DCF works backwards from today's price to find the yearly growth in cash flow that the price needs. It uses a 10% discount rate and an 18-times exit multiple.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. The stage label here is Stage 3, a pullback or top.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence for the business is its cash generation and its breadth. Free cash flow was $4,113 million, which is 35.8% of revenue. Cash from operations was $4,553 million. So the company can pay for acquisitions in cash and run a buyback, which means repurchasing its own shares, out of its own cash generation [1]. At year end it held $7.9 billion of cash and investments. Against that sat $1.77 billion of convertible notes carried at fair value. The net cash position is about $6.1 billion by our calculation [1]. Management reported three product signals on its call. The security-operations product XSIAM grew its ARR 70% in the fourth quarter. The AI-security product Prisma AIRS passed $100 million of ARR in its first year. Software firewall ARR grew 29% [16]. Those are company statements on a call, not audited numbers, so we weigh them below the filings.

The weakest part is the quality of growth. Reported revenue rose 24%. Removing the $930 million that Chronosphere and CyberArk added leaves about $10,550 million. That is roughly 14.4% growth over the prior year's $9,221 million (our calculation) [1]. The company's own pro forma view treats the deals as owned for both years. It shows revenue of $12,312 million against $10,486 million, up 17.4% [1]. The fiscal 2027 revenue outlook is $14.10 to $14.20 billion. That is about 14.9% above the pro forma 2026 figure (our calculation) [2]. So the 23% to 24% reported growth guided for next year is mostly the deals being owned for a full year. The like-for-like pace is mid-teens and slowing.

Profit on the official accounting basis is thin and fell. Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of delivery, as a share of revenue. It was 70.4% against 73.4% a year earlier. Operating margin is profit after all running costs, as a share of revenue. It was 6.1% against 13.5% [1]. The subscription gross margin fell from 72.5% to 69.2%, on amortisation of acquired intangibles and higher cloud costs [1]. The fourth quarter showed a GAAP net loss of $282 million. It included a $524 million non-cash loss on the change in value of the convertible notes and related hedges that came with CyberArk [2]. Management also warned that memory and storage costs are rising in the hardware business, which is about 10% of the company [16]. Operations in Israel expanded sharply with CyberArk, which added 4,223 staff. The 10-K lists the region's conflicts as a risk to business continuity [1].

Governance gives no sign of a hard problem. The auditor's opinion on internal control over financial reporting at July 31, 2026 is clean [1]. Our search of the filing text for 'going concern' and 'substantial doubt' found neither phrase. The 2025 proxy statement reports a board that is 82% independent. It says the pay committee changed its equity programme after feedback from the 2023 and 2024 shareholder votes on pay [25]. In August 2026 the board approved an executive severance policy. It pays the chief executive 200% of salary and 200% of target cash bonus on a qualifying termination after a change in control [26]. We read these as ordinary items, not red lines.

How the CyberArk and Chronosphere deals changed the picture

The deals changed the balance sheet more than they changed the income statement. Goodwill and intangible assets rose from $5.33 billion to $29.03 billion. Equity rose from $7.82 billion to $27.49 billion because shares were issued. Share count rose from 668 million to 815 million [4][1]. About $18.9 billion of equity was issued for the deals. The cash flow statement shows $18,862 million of equity consideration. That is the $18,488 million of CyberArk stock plus $374 million of replacement awards recorded in equity (our reconciliation) [1]. So owners of the old company were diluted in one step rather than gradually [1]. The company also took on CyberArk's $1.25 billion of 0.00% convertible notes due June 15, 2030. During the year $153 million were converted and settled for $160 million in cash, which left $1.1 billion of principal [1]. The notes can be exchanged for shares and cash. The company chose to carry them at fair value, which was $1,774 million at year end. Swings in that value flow through GAAP profit [1].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest one, the evidence that would show it, and the date we would know. Our verdict is a rejection, so the bear case here is the case against our own conclusion. The strongest argument is that we are too cautious about a company that is executing very well.

Weakest assumption 1: the multiple returns to its three-year level

Our central value assumes the price-to-earnings ratio falls from 91.1 to 54.4. That proxy comes from a period when revenue grew about 15% to 17% in fiscal 2024 and 2025, plus 24.5% reported in fiscal 2026, mostly acquired. Reported growth is now 24%, and the guided ARR growth is 22%. The timing and the odds of a fall in the multiple are uncertain. Our own bull multiple of 85 times on the unchanged $4.88 EPS gives $414.80, which is 9.0% above the close. The three-year proxy does not prove that a fall will happen within 12 months. A market that is paying for a platform company could hold a higher multiple for years. The analysts' mean target is above the close [17]. The evidence that would prove us wrong is a Q1 FY27 report, estimated for November 18, 2026, that raises the fiscal 2027 outlook for ARR above $11.175 billion with the price holding. A second report, estimated for February 2027, would need to do the same [18][19]. If that happens, the bull case of $446 is the better guide. We would rerun the numbers the next day.

Weakest assumption 2: consensus fiscal 2028 EPS of $4.88 is about right
