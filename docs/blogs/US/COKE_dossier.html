How to read this report

The report tests the company in layers. Layer 1 is the first set of hard tests: the company must pass every financial floor and the technical floor, and one failure rejects it. Layer 2 is an operating scorecard, and it is used only for names that pass Layer 1, so it is not used here.

The momentum gate is a separate price test, computed from closing prices. It has two routes. Test A asks whether the stock is already in a rising trend and not stretched. Test B asks whether it is turning up from a base, which is a sideways period in which a stock stops falling and builds a floor. A stock that fails both routes receives no allocation. Relative strength compares the stock's return with the market's return over the same period, and SPY, an exchange-traded fund that tracks the S&P 500, stands in for the market.

Company figures come from the SEC, the US securities regulator. Its public database, EDGAR, holds the filings: the annual report on Form 10-K, the quarterly report on Form 10-Q, and the current report on Form 8-K. The structured figures behind them are published in XBRL, a standard machine-readable format. The notes list each source.

Shareholders' equity is the company's net worth on paper, meaning assets minus liabilities. Operating cash flow is the cash the business produces from its normal work, before spending on equipment. NO_MOMENTUM is the gate's label for a stock that fails both routes.

What the company does

Coca-Cola Consolidated is a bottler. A bottler does not make the drink's brand; it buys concentrate and syrup from the brand owner, mixes, bottles and packs the drink, and delivers it to shops, restaurants and vending points within a licensed territory. The company's filing says it serves approximately 60 million consumers within its territories [1]. For a reader, the business is a route network: trucks, cold storage, bottling lines and a sales force that must reach a large number of small customers every week.

That model has consequences for an investor. In general, the brand owner sets the price of the concentrate, so the bottler's margin depends on how much it can charge retailers and how efficiently it runs its lines. Heavy equipment is needed constantly, and the cash spent on trucks and bottling lines is the main call on the company's money after operations. That spending is called capital expenditure, meaning money spent on long-lived assets such as trucks and bottling lines. The filing shows its scale: capital expenditure was $312.3 million in 2025 and $371.0 million in 2024 [2].

The growth figures show a company that is getting larger. Net sales were $7,228.1 million in 2025, up from $6,899.7 million in 2024 [3]. The first half of 2026 shows the same direction: net sales were $3,899.1 million, against $3,435.5 million a year earlier, which is a rise of 13.5 percent [4]. Sales growth is not in doubt. The question for the underwriting is what share of that growth turns into cash.

What this means for an owner: the business is large and growing, with a clear route to market. The gate that matters is whether its cash generation keeps pace with the growth and with its spending on equipment. Chapter 2 looks at that question through the strength of the franchise, and chapter 3 tests it with the filed numbers.

Glossary for this report

Terms used in this report, in the order they first appear.

EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is a rough measure of operating cash earnings before the cost of debt and tax.

Capital expenditure is money spent on trucks, buildings, bottling lines and other long-lived assets.

Free cash flow means cash from operating activities minus capital expenditure: the money left after the company pays for the equipment it needs to keep running.

Net debt is total borrowing minus cash on hand.

Buyback means the company buys back its own shares.

Dilution means existing owners hold a smaller share of the company because new shares have been issued.

Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given to staff in shares. With these terms in hand, chapter 2 asks whether the franchise, meaning the underlying strength of the business and its place in its market, earns a return that justifies its capital.

Why the business is strong or weak

A competitive position is the reason a business keeps its customers and its margins. For a bottler, the position usually rests on exclusive territories, close ties to the brand owner, and a route network that is costly to copy. The filing says the company has exclusive rights to distribute, promote, market and sell the authorized brands in its territories [1]. Those features make the business hard to displace in its region. They also tie it to the brand owner's pricing, which the company cannot set on its own.

The filing also shows a capital event that matters for the reader. In November 2025 the company repurchased 18,835,460 shares of its common stock from a single seller, and it paid for part of that purchase with a bridge loan, meaning a short-term loan taken to pay for something before longer-term funding is arranged [1][5]. The annual report records bridge loan proceeds of $1,200.0 million in 2025 [1]. A buyback funded partly by borrowing raises debt, and retiring the repurchased shares reduces equity. By the end of 2025, shareholders' equity was negative $739.7 million, against positive equity of $1,417.6 million at the end of 2024 [6].

Negative equity makes one of the measures look better than it is. The return on invested capital test, which divides operating profit by debt plus equity, comes out at about 46 percent. That number is high partly because the equity term is negative and so shrinks the denominator. It passes the test, but this report does not count it as evidence of strength. The cash test is the one that decides the verdict, and it is set out in chapter 3.

What this means for an owner: the franchise is real, but the balance sheet has moved from a conservative position toward a leveraged one in a single year. The owner should weigh the franchise against the debt that came with the buyback, and the free cash flow that must service that debt.

What the price is doing

The result is calculated from the closing prices on 9 October 2026, not judged by eye. The falling 200-day test is separate, and it is the test that fails here. Stage 4 is the phase after a trend has broken, when the price falls below a falling long-term average. On the ledger's test, COKE is in Stage 2.

Estimate 1: the free cash flow needed to pass the gate

This estimate asks how far the company's cash generation sits from the 12 percent floor. Cash is the test an owner can check most easily, because it is what is left after the business pays for its equipment. The gap is about $248 million a year on the 2025 sales base.

The number

To pass the 12 percent free cash flow floor on the 2025 sales base, the company would need about $867 million of free cash flow in a year, compared with $620 million in 2025, a gap of about $248 million.

How it was calculated

Twelve percent of net sales of $7,228.1 million is $867.4 million. The 2025 free cash flow was $619.6 million, which is operating cash flow of $931.9 million minus capital expenditure of $312.3 million. The gap is $867.4 million minus $619.6 million, or $247.8 million.

The inputs

Net sales, operating cash flow and capital expenditure are from the SEC structured data for fiscal 2025 [3][8][2], which agree with the annual report [1].

The range

In a bull case, the company holds capital spending near the 2025 level and grows operating cash flow by about $250 million. In a bear case, spending rises as it did in 2024, and the gap widens. The first-half 2026 free cash flow of $273.3 million on sales of $3,899.1 million is a margin of 7.0 percent [4]. Half-year figures are seasonal, so this is a reading, not a forecast.

If the number is off

If the true 2026 margin reached 10 percent, the company would still fall short of the floor. The verdict would turn on the 12 percent line, so the reader should watch whether the margin reaches it, not whether it improves.

Why these estimates might be wrong

The verdict rests on the 2025 free cash flow margin, and the weakest assumption behind it is that the 2025 margin is representative. The 2025 figure is 8.6 percent, but the margin was 4.1 percent in 2022, 7.9 percent in 2023 and 7.3 percent in 2024, so the 2025 figure is the highest of the four [8][2]. If the 2025 result was the high point, the gap is wider than shown. If capital spending falls sharply, the margin could rise above the floor. Either outcome would show up in the next annual report, so the assumption can be tested.

The second weak point is the debt. The leverage test passes today, but the debt rose by more than four times in two years. The first half of 2026 shows the non-current debt at $2,413.1 million at 3 July 2026, down from $2,686.0 million at the end of 2025 [4]. That fall may reflect a reclassification between current and non-current debt, and the record does not settle which. Until the debt schedule is read in full, the leverage pass should be treated as provisional.

The third point is the sales line. Net sales rose 13.5 percent in the first half of 2026 [4]. Growth of that size can come from acquisitions, price increases or volume, and the record does not separate them. Growth that is bought with debt does not improve the cash test on its own.
