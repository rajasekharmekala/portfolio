1. What the company does

Selective Insurance Group is a holding company for ten insurers that sell business and personal insurance in the United States. It was founded in 1926, is based in New Jersey, and sells only through independent agents and wholesale brokers. [4] An independent agent is a local firm that sells policies from several insurers, so Selective wins business by being easy to work with and by being financially strong. Its financial strength rating from the rating agency AM Best is A+, which AM Best labels Superior. [1]

An insurer makes money in two ways. The first is underwriting: it collects premium, pays claims and costs, and keeps what is left. The measure is the combined ratio, which is claims plus costs as a share of premium; below 100% the insurer made an underwriting profit. In the third quarter of 2026 Selective's combined ratio was 96.8%, so it kept about $3.20 of every $100 of premium earned. [1] The second way is investing. Premium arrives months or years before claims are paid, and the insurer invests that money, called the float. Selective held $11.5 billion of investments against $3.4 billion of total equity at September 30, 2026. [1] [2] Investing earned $160.5 million before tax in the quarter, about $13.60 for every $100 of premium earned. [2]

The split between the two matters more than usual here. In the third quarter, insurance operations added 3.5 points of operating return on equity and investment income added 15.2 points, and after holding-company interest and costs the operating return on equity was 16.9%. [1] For the full year 2025 the same split was 3.4 points from insurance operations and 13.3 points from investments, before holding-company costs. [4] A reader should therefore think of Selective as a bond portfolio with an insurance company attached. When interest rates are high and the portfolio rolls into higher yields, profit rises even if underwriting is only average. The yield on its portfolio reached 4.4% after tax in the quarter, up from 4.1% a year earlier. [1]

Most of the premium comes from one place. Standard Commercial Lines, which sells packages of property, liability, auto and workers' compensation cover to small and mid-sized businesses, was 79% of 2025 net premiums written, and contractors were 43% of that segment's premium. [4] Personal lines (mainly homeowners for wealthy households) were 8% and excess and surplus lines, called E&S, were 13%. [4] E&S covers risks the standard market will not take, so the insurer is free to set its own prices and terms. About 34% of 2025 premium came from three states: New Jersey 16%, Pennsylvania 10% and New York 8%. [4]

How one premium dollar is spent

Take $100 of premium earned in the third quarter of 2026. Claims and claim-handling cost took $64.60, running costs took $32.10 and dividends to policyholders took $0.10, which leaves $3.20 of underwriting profit. [1] That is a thinner cushion than it looks, because catastrophe losses (storms and fires) took 5.4 points of the premium in the same quarter. [1] In a quiet quarter the same business would show a combined ratio near 91%, and in a bad storm quarter it would be above 100%. The reserve behind future claims is large: Selective carried $8.0 billion of gross loss reserves at September 30, 2026 against $3.4 billion of equity. [2] A reserve is the insurer's own estimate of what it will still pay on claims already made or incurred, and a small error in it moves profit a lot. The next chapter explains why that is the heart of the debate on this stock.

The 2026 reset: shrinking on purpose

Premium fell in each of the first three quarters of 2026: net premiums written were down 1% in the first quarter, 5% in the second and 7% in the third. [10] [7] [1] Management says this is deliberate. It is lifting prices hard on the worst 10% of its commercial renewals and letting many of those customers leave: retention in that group fell to 52% from 79% a year earlier, while the price rise on the group that stayed was 17.2%. [3] Retention in the best-performing group stayed at 90%. [3] The company says those actions explain five points of the 8% fall in commercial premium. [3] This matters for the investment case because fewer policies mean the fixed cost of running the company is spread over less premium: the third-quarter expense ratio was 32.1% against 30.6% a year earlier. [1] [3] The shrinkage is the price paid for a cleaner book, and whether the cleaner book earns enough to justify it is the question the next chapter takes up, using the numbers in Chapters 2B and 3.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Net premiums written is the premium the insurer wrote in the period after paying reinsurers, which are insurers that share its risk.
  • Reserve development is the change in the estimate of claims from earlier years; adverse means the estimate rose, which cuts current profit.
  • Renewal pure price is the average price change on policies that renew, before changes in the amount of risk insured.
  • Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by the number of shares; adjusted book value removes unrealised gains and losses on bonds.
  • Operating return on equity is profit excluding investment gains and losses, divided by average common equity.
  • EPS stands for earnings per share, the company's profit divided across all shares.
  • RSI stands for relative strength index, a meter from 0 to 100 of how fast the price has been rising; above 70 is called overbought.
  • ATR stands for average true range, the average daily price swing in dollars; we measure distances in these units.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow, which is the cash left after running the business and buying assets; it is not meaningful for an insurer because float moves it.
  • SBC stands for stock-based compensation, the cost of paying employees in shares.
  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a profit measure used to judge debt; net debt is interest-bearing debt minus cash.
  • ROIC stands for return on invested capital, the profit earned on each dollar put into the business; for an insurer we use return on equity instead.
  • Stage 2 is the Weinstein uptrend stage, in which the price sits above its 50-day and 200-day averages and the 200-day average is flat or rising, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average are the average closing prices over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days.
  • Base is a sideways stretch the price builds before a move, a pivot is the top of that stretch, a breakout is a close above it on volume, and a retest is the pullback that tests the level from above.
  • Stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong and the position is cut; a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better; the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry — upside reward relative to downside risk — and reward-to-risk mean the same thing here: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).
  • Guidance is the company's own published forecast for the coming period; a consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for a number.
  • A buyback is the company buying its own shares, which shrinks the share count; dilution is the opposite, a rise in share count from issuing shares.
  • Price-to-earnings is the price paid for each dollar of earnings; cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates before financing.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Selective's strength is a distribution franchise, a balance sheet that analysts trust, and a pricing system that is getting more precise. Its weakness is that the business it sells most, commercial liability and commercial auto, is the part of insurance where claims have been costing more than anyone expected. The evidence for both halves is in the filings, and the stock's price depends on which half wins.

Where the edge is real

Selective is the 34th-largest property and casualty group in the United States by premium and holds about 1.5% of a highly fragmented commercial market. [4] [3] Fragmented means no one dominates, so a mid-sized insurer with good agents can keep winning customers one by one. It appointed 120 new agency locations in the first nine months of 2026 and is entering new states such as Montana and Wyoming. [3] Agents steer clients to insurers they trust to pay claims, so the ratings matter: AM Best A+ (stated in the October 2026 release), and S&P A, Moody's A2 and Fitch A+, each with a stable outlook in the 2025 annual report. [1] [4]

The pricing system is the second real edge. Management sorts renewals into cohorts by expected profit and prices each differently. The best cohorts renewed at 90% and the worst at 52%, and the worst were priced up 17.2%. [3] In general liability it has taken price increases in the 10% range for seven quarters while industry surveys showed mid-single-digit rises, and commercial auto liability price rose 11.6% in the third quarter. [11] [3] Rising price is what offsets rising claims cost, so this is the discipline that has to work.

The third edge is the balance sheet. Net premiums written were 1.25 times policyholders' surplus at September 30, 2026, a conservative level, and debt was 20.7% of total capital. [1] Pre-tax profit plus interest covered interest expense 12.3 times in the first nine months of 2026. [2] The company has raised its dividend for thirteen straight years, most recently by 12% to $0.48 a quarter, and has authorised a new $200 million share repurchase. [1] Those facts say a downturn would not threaten solvency, which is why the debate is about returns and not survival.

Where it is thinner than it looks

The first weak point is the underwriting record. Management states a long-term combined ratio target of 95%. [13] Over 2021 to 2025 the actual ratios were 92.8%, 95.1%, 96.5%, 103.0% and 97.2%, an average of 96.9%, and the first nine months of 2026 came in at 97.7%. [12] [4] [1] Only 2021 beat the target. Because investment income covers the gap, return on equity looks healthy while underwriting does not.

The second is reserves. In 2024 the company added $285.3 million to claims reserves for earlier years, and in 2025 another $77.5 million, including $120.4 million for commercial auto. [4] On the January 2026 call, management said it had strengthened commercial auto reserves by about $190 million in 2025 and raised its expected severity trend for commercial auto liability to about 10%. [6] There has been no net adverse development in the first three quarters of 2026, and general liability reserves have been stable for seven quarters. [3] That is progress, but three clean quarters do not cancel two years of charges, and commercial auto and general liability are, in the CFO's words, not yet at target profitability. [3]

The third is shareholder return. Selective's annualised total shareholder return over the three years to 2025 was -0.4%, against 18.2% for the S&P property and casualty index and 23.0% for the S&P 500. [13] The market has been telling the company for three years that the earnings were not good enough, and the October 2026 rally is the first sign that the market now sees improvement.

The market around it: soft in property, firm in casualty

Marsh's index of commercial insurance rates fell 5% globally in the third quarter of 2026, the ninth straight quarterly fall, and US rates fell 1%. [32] The split matters. US property rates fell 10% while US casualty rates rose 6%, or 9% excluding workers' compensation, with commercial auto up by high single digits. [32] Selective's own renewal price rise of 5.7% in Standard Commercial Lines is close to Marsh's casualty reading, and management said property pricing was tempering while liability pricing was holding up. [1] [3]

AM Best reported that downgrades of US property and casualty insurers halved in the first half of 2026 to 3.8% of rating actions, but that the majority of 2026 downgrades were commercial casualty and commercial auto carriers. [33] In plain terms the industry is paying for the same lines that Selective is trying to fix. Selective's CEO said casualty margins across the industry are under pressure and pricing is generally below the elevated severity trend. [3] That is why shrinking premium may be the right call and why a growth reading of -7% is not by itself a sign of failure. It is also why the next two quarters, not the last one, will say whether the repair is working.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

The red-team case is the strongest honest argument against our own call. Our call is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT on a business that passes its quality floors, so the red team has to argue in two directions. The first argument is that waiting is right but the entry zone is too high, because the quarter that lifted the stock was flattered and the underlying business is still shrinking. The second is that even the zone is too generous if one of three flaws is real. [1] [3]

The bear also has a record to point to. Selective's three-year annualised total return to the end of 2025 was -0.4% against 18.2% for the property and casualty index. [13] The stock then rose 18.8% in a month on a quarter whose earnings beat included about $0.18 to $0.25 a share of items that will not repeat, and it trades at 1.81 times a book value that fell 5% in the quarter. [1] [2] [19] The three flaws below could each be fatal to the thesis, and each can be tested with data in the next two quarters.

Fatal flaw 1: casualty reserves are still short

General liability and commercial auto hold 67% of Selective's net claim reserves: $2.9 billion (46%) and $1.3 billion (21%) at the end of 2025. [4] Management already raised its assumed severity growth for commercial auto liability to about 10% and added about $190 million to that line in 2025, after $285.3 million of charges across the company in 2024. [6] [4] If those two lines needed another 10%, the pre-tax cost would be $420 million, about $330 million after tax or $5.60 a share, which is 10% of book value. Our arithmetic. [4]

The company's own range of reasonable reserve estimates for net claims runs from $5,694 million to $6,960 million, a high end about 10% above the roughly $6.3 billion it carries (our arithmetic from the 46% share). [4] The weakest assumption in our base case is that the 2024 and 2025 charges were enough. It would be wrong if any quarter shows adverse development at line level, or if the fourth-quarter reserve review adds to commercial auto or general liability. We would know in late January 2027 when the fourth quarter is reported, and by February 2027 in the annual report.

Fatal flaw 2: shrinking premium becomes a loss of franchise

Net premiums written fell 1%, 5% and 7% in three consecutive quarters, and management said the headwind from its own underwriting actions was a little bigger in the third quarter than in the second. [10] [7] [1] [3] Five points of the 8% commercial decline come from letting the worst cohorts leave. The remaining three points come from lower new business, which management ties to its price discipline on casualty. [3] Meanwhile Marsh reports that US property rates fell 10% in the quarter, so the profitable property business is under price pressure at the same time. [32]

The risk is that agents, who are paid on volume, move good customers too. The expense ratio is the early warning: it rose to 32.1% from 30.6%, and each extra 3% fall in premium with unchanged costs adds about 1 point more. Our arithmetic. [1] The weakest assumption is that the customers lost are the unprofitable ones. Evidence against it would be retention in the best cohorts falling below 90%, or premium falling faster than 7% with an expense ratio above 33%. We would know from the fourth-quarter call and, more clearly, from the first-quarter 2027 results in April 2027.

Fatal flaw 3: the return is a bond-market return

Investment income supplied 15.2 of the 16.9 points of third-quarter operating return, and underwriting only 3.5. [1] The portfolio's yield was 4.4% after tax and the average duration 4.5 years, so income keeps rising only while bonds are replaced at higher yields than they earned. [1] [3] Selective bought $2.94 billion of fixed-income securities in nine months, about $3.9 billion at that pace. One point less yield on a year of such purchases would cost about $39 million before tax a year, about $0.52 a share after tax, and the loss would grow each year as more bonds roll. Our arithmetic. [2]

The same rates hurt book value when they rise. Net unrealised losses on bonds (accumulated other comprehensive loss) reached $491.8 million at September 30, 2026, up from $151.7 million at year-end, which is the reason book value per share fell 5% in the quarter while operating profit rose. [2] [1] The ten-year Treasury yield stood near 4.99% on the day of the ledger. [3] The weakest assumption is that yields stay high enough to hold investment income growth near 3% in 2027. We would know from the quarterly investment income line and from the 2027 outlook the company publishes with fourth-quarter results.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The first rule is the support-distance ladder in our scoring file: the $99.32 close is 4.03 ATR above the nearest verified support (the 20-day average at $89.68), and anything above 1.6 ATR is the no-buy band. [35] [36] The RSI of 77.36 also sits above the 72 level at which the framework calls a stock overextended. The second rule is reward-to-risk: Core needs 2.0 to 1 for new money, and at $99.32 the ratio is 0.47 to 1, because the upside to our $108.00 value is $8.68 and the risk to the $80.71 stop is $18.61. The company passes every quality gate, so this is not a rejection. It is a price decision, and the price that would change it is $89.50 or lower, where the ratio is 2.10 to 1. The price is too high for the risk, and the risk is not too high for the business.

The entry zone is $86.00 to $89.50, and each edge has a reason. The top ($89.50) is the cluster of the 20-day average ($89.68) and the 50-day average ($89.45), the first place where support is likely to hold and where the ratio just clears 2.0.
