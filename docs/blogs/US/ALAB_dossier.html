1. What the company does

Astera Labs sells chips, circuit boards and cable modules that move data inside AI and cloud servers. Think of a server as a city where processors, memory and network cards need fast roads between them. Astera makes the traffic hubs and the signal boosters on those roads. The company describes four product families: Aries retimers, which clean up weak high-speed signals and re-send them so data can travel farther; Taurus Ethernet cable modules; Leo controllers that let processors share memory over a standard called CXL; and Scorpio fabric switches, which connect many AI processors to each other.[1] Astera designs the chips but does not own factories. TSMC makes every one of its chips and the company has not qualified a second source.[1]

The customers are cloud giants and AI-system builders, but they mostly buy through distributors and manufacturing partners. The 10-K counts the end customer behind each order. In 2025 one end customer was more than 70% of revenue and the top three were about 86%. A year earlier no end customer was above 40% and the top three were about 80%, so concentration rose.[1][2] In the June 2026 quarter four direct customers each passed 10% of revenue, at 29%, 25%, 15% and 13%, which adds up to 82%. The filing labels them A to D and does not name them.[4] Amazon holds warrants to buy Astera shares that vest as Amazon and its affiliates make payments to Astera, including a February 2026 warrant for up to 3,262,299 shares at $142.82. The filings do not say where Amazon ranks among the lettered customers.[1]

Astera makes money by selling products. All 2025 revenue of $852.5 million came from product sales, with no engineering-services revenue.[1] Revenue by product family is not published, so nobody outside the company can measure the Aries retimer business alone. What management does say is that PCIe 6 products (the sixth generation of the PCIe link standard) were more than 50% of second-quarter revenue, up from one-third in the first quarter, and that Scorpio will become the largest product family in the third quarter, one quarter earlier than planned.[13] Revenue by location is counted by the customer's billing address, so $152.7 million of second-quarter revenue billed to China reflects where manufacturing partners and distributors sit, not where the finished AI systems are used.[4]

For an owner, the model has two sides. Gross margin (the share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of making the product) is high, at 73.3% in the June quarter, and management expects it to drift toward a long-term target of 70% as lower-margin boards and modules grow.[9][13] The company is also young as a public stock: the IPO prospectus is dated March 19, 2024, so the filings give us about four full years of statements, not five.[3] Next, Chapter 2 asks how strong the competitive position really is and tests the three claims in the watchlist note.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Retimer is a chip that receives a weak, distorted high-speed signal, rebuilds it, and sends a clean copy onward.
  • PCIe (Peripheral Component Interconnect Express) is the standard data link between processors, memory, storage and network cards; each new generation doubles speed.
  • CXL (Compute Express Link) is a standard built on PCIe that lets processors share memory with other devices.
  • Fabric switch is a chip that connects many processors, like a traffic hub that lets any AI processor talk to any other.
  • XPU is the industry word for any AI processor, whether a GPU or a custom accelerator.
  • GAAP is the official US accounting standard; non-GAAP figures leave out items such as stock-based pay, so they run higher.
  • Hyperscaler is a very large cloud operator that buys computing equipment by the hundreds of thousands of units.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence of a good competitive position is growth with high margins. Revenue rose 115% in 2025 and 104% in the June 2026 quarter, and GAAP gross margin was 75.8%, 75.6%, 76.3% and 73.3% in the four quarters from June 2025 to June 2026.[1][9][11][10] A fabless chip company that grows this fast at these margins is usually selling something its customers cannot easily replace in a design that is already shipping. Management's own wording is that Aries established itself as the gold standard across major XPU and CPU platforms for PCIe 5.0 and that this position has continued for PCIe 6.0.[13] That is a claim about position, not a measured share, and we treat it that way.

The moat has three parts that we can point to. First, each chip ships with COSMOS, the company's software for monitoring and tuning links. On the call management said COSMOS now takes part in orchestration, which it argues makes customers more likely to reuse the platform in the next generation.[13] Second, the products are designed into specific server and rack designs, so a rival must win the next design, not just match a price. Third, management says it works on the next standard before it arrives: UALink-enabled Scorpio X switches are planned for 2027, and custom designs, including NVLink Fusion-based ones, start shipping in 2027.[13] Our reading is that these are advantages in timing and trust, not legal barriers, so they fade if a rival ships a cheaper part that passes the same tests.

Competition is real and named. The 10-K lists Broadcom, Credo, Marvell, Microchip, Montage, Parade and Rambus as principal competitors.[1] Microchip announced PCIe 6.0 and CXL 3.1 retimers in June 2026, which puts a large supplier into Astera's core product.[27] The sharper risk is structural. Scale-up links inside AI racks may move to UALink, to NVIDIA's NVLink Fusion, or to connectivity that a hyperscaler builds into its own custom chip. Astera plays in each of those, which hedges the risk, but each also shifts bargaining power toward the chip buyer. Custom designs also bring lower-margin modules and boards: the CFO said gross margin should trend toward 70%.[13]

Weak points are concentrated. One end customer was more than 70% of 2025 revenue, TSMC is the only chip foundry, and customer agreements generally do not require any minimum purchase.[1] The best defence is breadth of design wins: Barchart reports more than 10 customers engaged on Scorpio X, but production at customers other than the lead one is not expected until around the end of this year or early next year.[20] Chapter 2B turns these points into scored KPIs, and Chapter 3 turns the profit statement into numbers.

Checking the three claims in the watchlist note

The watchlist seed said Astera has a monopoly-like share of about 85% in PCIe Gen 5 and Gen 6 retimers. We could not find that number in any source. The 10-K and the earnings call give no market share, and outside market-size estimates conflict: one 2025 report sizes the entire retimer market at $674.4 million, less than Astera's own 2025 product sales of $852.5 million, which include switches and modules.[28][1] We therefore record the 85% claim as unsourced, and the dossier does not rely on it. A narrower statement that we can support is that Astera is a leading Aries supplier with named rivals in each product.

The seed also said gross margin is 75% or higher. That is true over the last twelve months (75.1%) but not for the latest quarter, which was 73.3% on a GAAP basis, with about 72% guided for the next quarter and a long-term target of 70%.[14][9][13] The claim of more than 50% revenue growth is correct and understated: growth was 104% year over year in the June quarter.[9] Two other seed claims did not match our data either. The note said Stage 2, which is the rising phase of a trend, but the price is in Stage 3, a topping or pullback phase. It also quoted free cash flow (FCF), which is the cash left after paying for equipment, at 28.4% of revenue, while the twelve-month figure rebuilt from filings is 23.0%.[14]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue grew from $34.8 million in 2021 to $852.5 million in 2025 and reached $1,201.9 million over the last twelve months, a figure we rebuilt as 2025 revenue minus the first half of 2025 plus the first half of 2026.[4][1][9] From 2022 to 2025 that is a compound growth rate of about 120% a year. The company only went public in March 2024, so the filings give four full years of statements (2022 to 2025) plus 2021 revenue, not a full five-year record.[3][4] Operating margin (operating profit as a share of revenue) moved from a loss of 29.3% in 2024 to a profit of 20.3% in 2025 and 22.7% over the last twelve months.[1][14]

Reported profit and adjusted profit are far apart, and owners should look at both. In the June quarter, GAAP operating margin (the official accounting figure) was 22.7% and non-GAAP operating margin was 39.1%. The gap is stock-based compensation, or SBC, which is pay given in company shares instead of cash. SBC was $64.0 million, or 16.3% of revenue, in that quarter.[9] GAAP net income of $153.1 million was also lifted by an income tax benefit of $50.3 million, so it sits above the pre-tax profit of $102.8 million.[9] We therefore lean on operating income and cash flow, not on the headline $0.83 earnings per share (EPS, profit divided by shares).

The SBC story has a one-time distortion. SBC was $234.6 million in 2024, or 59.2% of revenue, because shares that had waited for an IPO to vest were booked when the March 2024 IPO took place.[1][2] It fell to 18.8% in 2025 and 16.2% over the last twelve months. The Q3 guide of $76 million in operating-expense SBC on about $550 million of revenue implies a figure near 14% next quarter, which is our arithmetic and not a company statement.[9] Free cash flow (the cash left after paying for equipment) was $276.7 million over the last twelve months, a 23.0% margin, but after subtracting SBC it is $81.7 million, or 6.8% of revenue.[14][1][9]

Insiders are sellers, and the stock count is rising. The IPO lock-up, a promise not to sell for 180 days after the March 19, 2024 prospectus, ended long ago, and sales now run through pre-set Rule 10b5-1 plans.[3][1] The 10-K lists plans for the CEO (up to 900,000 shares), the President and COO (960,000) and two directors, and the June 10-Q adds a 1,412,000-share plan for the Chair.[1][4] Quiver counted 295 open-market insider trades in six months, all sales and no purchases, led by the President and COO at about $238.2 million, the Chair at $132.5 million and the CEO at $83.6 million.[18] Shares outstanding rose from 162.0 million at the end of 2024 to 173.5 million on July 31, 2026.[1][4] Chapter 3 now sets these figures out in tables, then scores them against our Layer 1 rules.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

The Q3 jump of about 40% at the midpoint is driven mainly by Scorpio X in volume production at one lead customer. Fatal flaw 2: an AI funding shock compresses the multiple

On October 22 the Nasdaq fell 2.6% on fears about AI spending and capital scarcity, and Astera fell 11.1%.[16][17] The stock's five-year beta is near 4, which means it tends to move about four times the market.[19] If the funding fear continues, customers could delay purchases, and the multiple could fall to 25 to 30 times even with good results.
