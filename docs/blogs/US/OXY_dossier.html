1. What the company does

Occidental finds oil and natural gas, pumps it, and sells it at market prices. It reports two segments: oil and gas, and midstream and marketing. Midstream buys, markets, gathers, processes, transports and stores oil, gas and carbon dioxide, and it also houses the low-carbon projects.[1] Until the start of 2026 the company also owned OxyChem, a chemicals maker. Occidental agreed to sell OxyChem to Berkshire Hathaway on 1 October 2025 for $9.7 billion in cash, and the sale closed on 2 January 2026.[13][11] After closing adjustments the cash price was $9,472 million, and the company booked a gain of about $3.1 billion after tax.[2] Occidental kept the environmental liabilities tied to OxyChem's legacy sites and guaranteed its indemnity promises to Berkshire, so the sale removed a business but not every obligation attached to it.[2][13]

In the second quarter of 2026 the company sold 1,433 thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day (Mboed). A barrel of oil equivalent counts natural gas and natural gas liquids at their energy content so that every product adds up to one number. The Permian Basin in Texas and New Mexico gave 804 Mboed, the Rockies and other domestic fields 280, the Gulf of America 144, and the international fields 205.[3] Oil is about half of the volume (48.8% of third-quarter production), and the company ended 2025 with 4.6 billion barrels of oil equivalent of proved reserves.[4][9] Management also counts about 16.5 billion barrels of total resources, which it says gives a runway of more than 30 years.[5][25]

Because Occidental sells at market prices, its profit follows the oil price. Management says each $1 move in oil changes annual pre-tax cash flow by about $265 million: about $240 million comes from WTI (West Texas Intermediate, the main US benchmark) and about $25 million from Brent (the main international benchmark). Our model uses the $265 million and assumes Brent moves with WTI.[5] In 2026 that leverage has been large. WTI averaged $71.93 in the first quarter, $92.79 in the second and $85.79 in the third, and the company realized $69.91, $96.78 and $85.91 a barrel for its oil in those quarters.[3][4] Natural gas is the weak side. Domestic gas sold for a negative $1.48 per thousand cubic feet in the second quarter because of a temporary price gap between the Waha gas hub in West Texas and the Gulf Coast, and for $1.61 (54% of the NYMEX benchmark of $2.97) in the third.[3][4][25]

Oil and gas earned $4,586 million of pre-tax segment income in 2025 and midstream earned $252 million, so oil and gas is about 95% of segment profit.[1] Midstream holds the company's 36.4% stake in the limited-partner units of Western Midstream (WES) and Oxy Low Carbon Ventures (OLCV).[2] OLCV is building STRATOS, a direct air capture plant in Ector County, Texas, designed to pull up to 500,000 tons of carbon dioxide a year out of the air once all four units run. BlackRock has invested its full $550 million share of the joint venture that owns the plant.[1][2] Management now guides 2026 midstream pre-tax income to $1.3 to $1.5 billion, up from $0.2 to $0.4 billion in February.[5][7] The filings we cite do not use the name 1PointFive; they call the unit Oxy Low Carbon Ventures. Its capital, after partner contributions, was $450 million in 2025, and management says about $400 million of it rolls off from 2027 as STRATOS moves from construction to operation.[5][25] A 2025 tax law raised the credit value for qualified carbon dioxide projects, but the 10-K warns that such benefits remain subject to administrative action and possible repeal.[1]

The deals that shaped the balance sheet

Occidental closed its purchase of CrownRock, a Permian Basin producer, on 1 August 2024.[14] The total price was $11,141 million: $9,386 million in cash and 29.6 million Occidental shares.[1] After that deal the company carried $28.9 billion of principal debt. It repaid $8.5 billion in 2024 and 2025 and another $8.6 billion in 2026 so far, which leaves $11.8 billion of principal debt after the second quarter of 2026. Management's next milestone is $10 billion.[5] In March 2026 it raised the size of a cash tender offer for its notes from $700 million to $1.2 billion to speed the paydown.[16]

The OxyChem sale was the biggest single step in that repayment. OxyChem earned $262 million after tax in 2025, but it spent more on its plants than it took in from operations: $926 million of operating cash flow against $1,116 million of investing cash outflow, a gap of about minus $190 million by our calculation.[3] Using the proceeds to retire debt cuts annual interest by about $630 million compared with 2025, which management counts as a run-rate saving.[5][1] That saving is bigger than the profit OxyChem was earning, which is why the sale improves free cash flow even though it shrinks the company.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Mboed means thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day, the unit this report uses for volume.
  • Barrel of oil equivalent (boe) counts gas and natural gas liquids at their energy content so that all products can be added together.
  • NGL (natural gas liquids) are products such as ethane and propane that are separated from natural gas and sold on their own.
  • WTI is West Texas Intermediate, the US benchmark crude price; Brent is the international benchmark.
  • EOR (enhanced oil recovery) means injecting carbon dioxide or water into old fields to push out more oil.
  • Direct air capture (DAC) means machines that pull carbon dioxide out of the air so it can be stored underground.
  • Preferred stock is a share class that is paid before common shares; Berkshire Hathaway holds Occidental's, which pays 8% a year.
  • Warrants are long-dated rights to buy shares at a fixed price; they add shares when the stock trades above that price.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Occidental's edge is a large, long-life resource base and a cost position that looks good for a producer that sells at market prices. Management counts about 16.5 billion barrels of oil equivalent of resources, roughly 88% of them in the US, and says its drilling program breaks even near $38 a barrel on average.[5] That breakeven is management's own figure for new wells, so Chapter 2B adds our own cash breakeven that also covers the dividends. Proved reserves were 4.6 billion barrels at the end of 2025, and the company replaced 107% of its 2025 output from its own drilling, against 116% on a three-year average.[9] US lease operating cost, the daily cost of running wells, was $7.80 per barrel of oil equivalent in the second quarter, below the $8.10 full-year guide.[3][5]

The company also claims an operating edge. Its slides say Occidental's US wells produce more than 10% more oil in six months than the industry average in every basin it operates, citing Enverus data, and that waterfloods at the Horn Mountain platform in the Gulf of America could add about 20 Mboed with a 40% to 50% return at $60 WTI.[5] On the call, management said waterfloods in Oman have cut the field decline rate from 19% to about 7%.[25] These are company statements, not audited results, but the quarterly production beats in Chapter 2B show the volume side is real.

The weaknesses are just as clear. First, Occidental takes prices as given: roughly $265 million of annual pre-tax cash flow moves with each $1 of oil, with Brent assumed to move alongside WTI.[5] Second, its base wells decline about 25% a year, which forces about $5.4 billion a year of sustaining capital before any growth.[5] Third, the balance sheet still carries $13.7 billion of debt and finance leases and $8.5 billion of Berkshire preferred stock that pays 8% a year, or $679 million, ahead of common holders.[2][1] S&P rates the debt BB+, below investment grade, while Moody's rates it Baa3 and Fitch BBB, both investment grade.[2]

Fourth, operations are not immune to outages. In September the company cut some Gulf of America output because of a third-party gas pipeline limit, and third-quarter Gulf sales volumes were 110 Mboed against a guided 115 to 123.[4][5] International volume fell to 205 Mboed in the second quarter, with Al Hosn at 74 against 93 a year earlier, which management ties to Middle East disruptions.[3][25] The honest moat description is narrow: Occidental is good at getting oil out of the ground cheaply and keeping old fields alive, but it cannot set the price of what it sells.

Where the advantage ends: obligations left behind

Selling OxyChem did not clear every old obligation. At 30 June 2026 the balance sheet still showed $1,702 million of environmental remediation liabilities and $2,472 million of long-term tax liabilities.[2] One tax case matters most. Anadarko, a company Occidental owns, received a tentative refund of $881 million in 2016 tied to a $5.2 billion legal settlement, and the IRS disputes the deduction behind it. The Tax Court heard the case in 2023 and 2024 and may rule at any time. If Occidental loses, it would repay about $2.4 billion including interest, with no extra tax expense recorded in earnings.[2] That is about $2.40 a share by our calculation, which is $2.4 billion divided by 999.6 million shares. It is one of the three fatal-flaw scenarios in Chapter 6.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter states the bear case: the strongest honest argument that our estimates are wrong. Each estimate has a weakest assumption, evidence that would show it is wrong, and a date when we will know. The three fatal-flaw scenarios below are the ways the plan could lose money even though the chart and the cash flow look good today.

Fatal flaw 1: oil falls before the cash arrives

The weakest assumption in the base value is that WTI averages $72 in 2027. The company's own filing says second-quarter prices benefited in part from risk premiums tied to the conflict involving Iran, and it calls the situation in the Strait of Hormuz fragile.[2] The futures curve already says the premium fades: front-month WTI is $103.27, the December 2027 contract is $80.52 and the last listed contract is $52.31.[21] At $58 WTI our model gives a value of $39.57, which is 38% below the close.

The company cannot hedge its way out of this. The two-way collars cover 100 thousand barrels a day only through December 2026, with a $55 floor and a $75.89 ceiling, and they have already cost cash: $183 million in the first half and $94 million in the third quarter.[2][4][5] The filings we read disclose no collar protection beyond December 2026, so from January 2027 the cash flow carries the full exposure of about $265 million a year before tax for each $1 move in oil, with Brent assumed to move alongside WTI.[5]

Fatal flaw 2: a $2.4 billion tax bill and chemical liabilities left behind

Anadarko's tax case could end in a repayment of about $2.4 billion including interest, and the Tax Court may rule at any time. No extra tax expense would be booked, but the cash would leave, which is about $2.40 a share by our calculation.[2] The balance sheet could absorb it, with $4.15 billion of cash and an undrawn $4.2 billion credit line, but it would use cash that otherwise reduces debt.[2][5]

The sale of OxyChem also left long-dated promises. Occidental kept the environmental liabilities tied to OxyChem's legacy sites, carries $1,702 million of environmental remediation liabilities, and guaranteed its indemnity obligations to Berkshire Hathaway.[2][13] The cost of those promises is spread over many years, so it is hard to date, which is why we treat it as a drag on confidence rather than a numbered loss.

Fatal flaw 3: a new chief executive, a delayed project and a preferred stock that ranks ahead of you

Richard Jackson became chief executive on 1 June 2026, and on the August call the CFO said continuous buybacks are a lower priority until the preferred stock is redeemed.[15][25] The $8.5 billion of Berkshire preferred stock pays 8%, or $679 million a year, ahead of common holders, and cannot be redeemed voluntarily before August 2029 except through the mandatory match for large payouts.[1] Berkshire also holds a warrant for 83.9 million shares at $59.59, which is below the $64.17 price, and it can pay the exercise price with preferred stock instead of cash.[1][5] The common warrants add 30.4 million shares at $22 and expire on 3 August 2027.[1]

STRATOS, meanwhile, was expected to begin operating in 2026 and now has full commissioning pencilled in around year-end with repairs under way.[1][5] If it slips again, the $400 million of low-carbon capital that management expects to roll off from 2027 would roll off later, and the free cash flow gain would arrive later.[25]

Some terms first. CMP means current market price, here the $64.17 close. A simple moving average (SMA) is the plain average close over a set number of days, and an exponential moving average (EMA) is a version that gives recent days more weight. A stop loss is the price at which we sell because the idea is wrong. Structural invalidation means a price where the chart pattern behind the trade breaks, such as a close below the recent swing low. A basis point (bp) is one hundredth of a percent of net worth. Portfolio heat is the share of net worth that would be lost if every stop were hit together, and our ceiling for it is 12%. Risk-unit sizing sets each position so that hitting its stop costs a fixed number of basis points: weight equals the risk budget divided by the distance to the stop, capped at the tier maximum. Tiers rank conviction. TIER_1 is the highest (7% to 10% of net worth, a 100 bp budget), TIER_4 is the lowest (1% to 2%, a 35 bp budget) and TIER_0 means zero size. TIER_4 is the yardstick here, so the ceiling is 2.0% of net worth. We chose it because the probability-weighted value is only 7.8% above the price and the case depends on oil.
