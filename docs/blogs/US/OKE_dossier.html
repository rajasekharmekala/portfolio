1. What the company does

ONEOK is a midstream company. Midstream means the pipes, plants and storage between the place where oil and gas come out of the ground and the place where they are used. Think of it as a toll road and a car wash for energy. Producers pay ONEOK to collect their raw gas, clean it, split off the liquids and deliver everything to refineries, chemical plants, power plants and export docks. ONEOK does not drill wells. Its network spans about 60,000 miles and the company belongs to the S&P 500[2]. The shares trade as OKE on the New York Stock Exchange, and since September 10, 2026 they belong to a new holding company with the same name[16].

The word guidance is used throughout this report. Guidance is the range of results that management publishes in advance, so we can later check whether it was met. ONEOK guides to adjusted EBITDA, which is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization with some non-cash items removed, and it includes ONEOK's share of the earnings of joint ventures. It is a company measure and not an accounting standard. For 2025 it was $8,020 million, and the company guides to about $8.55 billion for 2026[6][2].

The customers are oil and gas producers, refiners, fuel marketers, power plants and chemical companies. About 90% of 2026 earnings are expected to come from fees and not from owning the commodity[1]. The fees sit in long contracts that often carry minimum volume commitments or take-or-pay terms. Take-or-pay means the customer pays for the space whether or not it uses it. One customer, spread across all four segments, supplied about 12% of 2025 revenue and 11% of 2023 revenue[11]. That is meaningful, and the filing does not name the customer.

The company reached this size by buying. It closed the Magellan deal on September 25, 2023, paying $9,062 million in shares plus cash, then took control of EnLink on October 15, 2024 and bought Medallion on October 31, 2024[8][11]. Cash paid for acquisitions was $5,015 million in 2023 and $5,829 million in 2024[11]. On October 6, 2026 it added Brazos Midland, a Permian Basin gas gathering and processing business, for about $4.6 billion in cash[1]. Each deal widened the chain of services from the wellhead to the water's edge, and each came with promised cost and sales savings, called synergies, that Chapter 2B checks.

How the fee model works, and where it does not

Most of the money is made in three ways. A producer pays a processing fee for each unit of gas that passes through a plant. An NGL customer pays a fee to move and split its liquids through a pipe and a fractionator, which is a plant that separates a mixed stream into purity products. A refiner or shipper pays a tariff to use a pipeline or terminal. Volumes drive all three, so ONEOK earns more when producers drill more.

The model also includes optimization and marketing, which means ONEOK uses its spare space and its wide network to buy at one place and sell at another, or to blend fuels, when price gaps open. This is not a fee. It pays well when price gaps are wide and little when they close. Chapter 2 shows how much of 2026's earnings growth came from it, because that changes how much of the growth an owner can count on.

What changed in September and October 2026

On September 10, 2026 ONEOK reorganized into a holding company. Every share became one share of the new ONEOK, Inc., with the same ticker, and all debt now sits one level down, in ONEOK, L.L.C., with a guarantee from the parent[16][1]. The reason was a financing: Apollo, a large asset manager, paid $9 billion in cash for 900,000,000 Class B Units of ONEOK Holdings, the company that owns the operating business[14][16]. ONEOK keeps control, because it appoints all three managers of Holdings, and it owns 87% of the units while Apollo holds 13%[1][14].

The Class B Units are neither debt nor ordinary stock. Apollo's return is capped at 7.01% a year for the first nine years, which management says is below the cost of its publicly traded equity[1]. Until the balance falls to $200 million, Apollo receives 15% of ONEOK's cash flow from operations, a measure the agreement calls CFFO, and ONEOK may raise this to 20%; it is 20% whenever ONEOK's leverage ratio as defined in the agreement is above 4.5 to 1[1]. Any payment above the 7.01% return pays down Apollo's balance, which is good for common shareholders over time. The units sit behind all debt and rank ahead of shareholders for cash[1]. We treat that as the single most important new fact about the stock.

ONEOK used $5 billion of the Apollo money to retire debt and the rest to buy Brazos Midland[16][1]. Net debt is borrowings minus cash, and it is the figure that measures how safe the balance sheet is. Total debt principal extinguished in September was $5,462 million, including $2.5 billion of notes bought back at a discount for $2.0 billion[1]. Standard & Poor's and Fitch upgraded the company to BBB+, and Moody's stays at Baa2, all with stable outlooks[1]. Brazos adds about 600,000 dedicated acres under fixed-fee contracts with more than 12 years left on average, 14 rigs and 1.2 billion cubic feet a day of processing capacity once its newest plant is built[15].

The capital plan in progress

ONEOK now expects about $3.5 billion of capital expenditure in 2026, up from the $2.7 billion to $3.2 billion range it gave in February[2][6]. Capital expenditure, or capex, is the cash spent on building and maintaining plants and pipes. The larger projects are the Medford fractionator rebuild at about $550 million, the Texas City export terminal at about $700 million, the 400 million cubic feet a day Bighorn plant in the Permian at about $380 million, and the Eiger Express gas pipeline to Katy, Texas at about $350 million for ONEOK's share[1]. Management expects the first phase of Medford in mid-December and the Cassidy I plant at year end[21][1]. Chapter 2B checks the record on delivery.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

ONEOK is strong because its assets are connected, and a connected system is hard to copy. A barrel of liquids can be gathered in the Bakken, processed near the well, moved on ONEOK's own pipe to Mont Belvieu in Texas, split at ONEOK's own fractionators and shipped from ONEOK's own dock, and ONEOK earns a fee at each step. A rival can match one step, but not the chain, and the chain is what producers pay for. The evidence is volume. NGL raw feed throughput, the barrels per day of raw liquids that ONEOK moves or splits, averaged 1,595 thousand barrels a day for nine months of 2026 against 1,466 a year earlier, which is 8.8% higher (our calculation)[2].

The contracts add protection. Brazos Midland's dedicated acreage runs under fixed-fee contracts with a weighted average of more than 12 years left[15]. Natural gas pipeline capacity was 92% contracted in the third quarter against 90% a year before[2]. About 90% of 2026 earnings are expected to be fee-based[1]. Credit rating agencies now agree: Moody's rates the company Baa2, and Standard & Poor's and Fitch raised theirs to BBB+ after the Apollo investment and the debt repayment[1].

The weakness is that the 2026 earnings jump rests more on spreads than the headline suggests. Nine-month adjusted EBITDA rose $481 million, from $5,875 million to $6,356 million. The company itself lists optimization and marketing gains of $82 million in NGLs, $151 million in refined products and crude, and $191 million in natural gas pipelines, which add to $424 million, or 88% of the rise (our calculation)[2]. The gas pipeline gain came from the price gap between the Waha hub in West Texas and Katy, Texas, and management says that gap narrowed in the third quarter and will narrow further in the fourth[2][21]. That does not make the earnings fake, because the same network is what earns the gap. It does mean that a large part of this year's growth is the kind that fades.

The second weakness is a new claim on cash. Apollo gets 15% of cash flow from operations before shareholders see anything beyond the dividend[1]. Chapter 3 puts a figure on it. The third is spending: capital expenditure guidance has moved from $2.7 billion to $3.2 billion in February to about $3.5 billion now, partly because Brazos adds $200 million[6][2][21]. The company discloses this openly, but the cash left for owners after dividends has been close to zero for two years.

Customers, competitors, regulators and people

ONEOK sells to producers, refiners and utilities that mostly carry investment-grade credit, under contracts that the company says include minimum volumes and take-or-pay terms[1]. Concentration is the point to watch: one customer was about 12% of 2025 revenue[11]. Competitors are growing as fast or faster. Kinder Morgan reported third-quarter adjusted EBITDA up 9%, Enterprise Products reported third-quarter adjusted EBITDA up 16%, Williams reported second-quarter adjusted EBITDA up 6% and Targa reported second-quarter adjusted EBITDA up 38%[29][32][30][31]. Williams and Targa have not yet reported the third quarter, so those two figures are a quarter older. ONEOK's 6% in the third quarter is at the low end of this group, level with Williams. It falls to about 4%, below every peer, if the one-off debt gains and costs are removed (our calculation from $2,238 million less $53 million, against $2,119 million less $22 million)[2].

Regulation is a modest factor here. The gas pipeline segment and the refined products and crude pipelines have regulated tariffs, and the company lists rate regulation and environmental rules among its risks[2]. The Holdings structure adds a small governance point: Apollo has consent rights over specified actions inside Holdings, and standstill terms stop it from seeking control of ONEOK[14].

Leadership has been stable. Pierce Norton is president and chief executive and was re-elected in May 2026 with 449,817,473 votes for and 4,129,359 against[2][19]. The advisory vote on executive pay passed with 428,986,336 votes for and 22,828,183 against, which is 95.0% support among votes cast for or against (our calculation)[19]. Two directors, Gerald Smith and Pattye Moore, retired on May 20, 2026, one by the age rule and one by choice[18]. Directors and executive officers as a group held 926,014 shares, less than 1% of the company[20], so the management team is not an owner-operator, which is common for companies of this size. We found no sign of a governance or accounting problem in the filings we read.

What this means for an owner: the business is a good one with a hard-to-copy chain of assets, and the 2026 results are strong. The worry is the quality of this year's growth and the new Apollo claim, not the survival of the company. Balance sheet, debt and interest cover

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter takes each estimate from Chapter 5 and states its weakest assumption, what evidence would show it wrong, and when that evidence arrives. It also gives our strongest honest bear case, the argument a skeptical analyst would make against our own conclusion, and the single claim everything depends on. Our verdict is already a wait, so the three flaws below show how far the price could fall if we are early. The case that waiting is too cautious is the bull scenario in Chapter 5.

The red-team bear case in one paragraph: ONEOK is being valued as a steady fee business, but its 2026 growth came mostly from price gaps that are already closing, and its new owner of $9 billion of capital sits ahead of common shareholders for 15% of cash flow. After Apollo's full 15% cash distribution, dividends and $3.5 billion of capital spending, it is about $0.7 billion short of cash flow, 10.3% of the total. It just bought a $4.6 billion business when commodity prices are strong, which management says is why private equity sellers are testing the market. The 2027 consensus is below 2026, management has missed its own 2026 growth outlook, and the stock is already up 12% in a month.

Fatal flaw 1: the price-gap gains reverse

Optimization and marketing gains were $424 million of the $481 million nine-month rise in adjusted EBITDA[2]. Management expects the Waha to Katy gap to narrow again in the fourth quarter and calls its guidance conservative, but it also says gains in blending and crude marketing are tied to wide spreads that follow Middle East events[21][1]. If half of the $424 million disappears in 2027, EBITDA falls $212 million, which is about $0.26 a share after a 22.9% tax rate and 630 million shares (our calculation). That takes 2027 earnings from $6.07 to about $5.81, and at 15.39 times the value would be about $89, close to the top of our buy range. That is a real loss for a buyer at today's price, and it needs no change in the business, only in how much of this year's growth repeats.

Fatal flaw 2: the Apollo claim and a second round of heavy spending

Apollo's cut is 15% of cash flow from operations during the initial period, which is about $0.9 billion to $1.0 billion a year, and it rises to 20% if ONEOK's leverage ratio under the agreement goes above 4.5 to 1[1][14]. ONEOK now says it has capacity for more projects, that capex will rise over the next few years, and that 3.5 times is the upper end of its range[21]. If spending runs at $3.5 billion or more a year while earnings growth slows, the cash left after dividends and Apollo stays negative. On the full-distribution basis the pro forma gap is $672 million, or 10.3% of cash flow. On a return-only basis it is $327 million, because $344 million of Apollo's payment repays its capital. If only 60% of Brazos EBITDA reaches operating cash flow, which is our assumption and not a filed figure, the full gap is $872 million. The choices are then slower dividend growth, more borrowing or new shares.[1][2] A fall to the bear value of $77.83 would be 7.0% below our $83.71 stop, so the stop would not hold the loss if the price gapped. That is the largest single risk to a buyer who enters near the top of our range.

Fatal flaw 3: execution, concentration and the commodity cycle

Several projects must finish on time: Medford phase 1 in mid-December, Cassidy I at year end, Cutter I in the fourth quarter, Bighorn in the second quarter of 2027 and the Brazos integration[21][1]. One customer supplied about 12% of 2025 revenue[11]. Producer activity responds to oil prices, and the February guidance assumed West Texas crude at about $55 to $60 a barrel[6]. Producer plans have improved since: management cites constructive development plans, and Williston Basin rigs up about 50% since March[21], so a return to those prices would remove part of the volume tailwind. A sharp fall in crude, a refinery outage cluster or a delay at Medford would hit the earnings that the fall of the price gaps leaves exposed. Management says private equity owners of Permian gathering assets see a window to sell while the market is strong[21], and a buyer that pays at the top of a window is the one that feels the fall.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, and the rule behind it is the reward-to-risk floor. Reward-to-risk compares the gain from an entry price to our target with the loss from that price to our stop loss, which is the price at which we would admit the idea is wrong. A CORE_ALPHA name needs at least 2.0 at the planned entry. With a central target of $97.55 and a stop of $83.71, ONEOK offers 0.14 at the current price of $95.82, 0.53 at the 50-day average of $92.78 and 2.00 at $88.32. The rule says buy only at or below $88.32, which is 7.8% below the price. The money floors in Chapter 3 do not decide the verdict alone. A pipeline-adapted version passes, so the price decides it.

Our entry ladder measures how far the price sits above the nearest verified support, in ATR units. FULL_SIZE means within 0.6 ATR of support, where a full-sized buy is allowed. STANDARD means 0.6 to 1.2 ATR, where a normal-sized buy is allowed. LIMIT_ORDER_REDUCE means 1.2 to 1.6 ATR, where only a starter or resting order is allowed. NO_BUY means beyond 1.6 ATR, where we do not chase and instead place an order lower or wait. The verdict labels then mean: IMMEDIATE_BUY, buy now at full size; ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP, buy at standard size on a pullback; WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, hold off and set an order at support; REJECTED, drop the idea.

Why not the other labels? IMMEDIATE_BUY needs the FULL_SIZE band and the floor, and the stock has neither: it is 1.31 ATR above the 50-day average, which is the LIMIT_ORDER_REDUCE band, and 4.22 ATR above the swing low, which is NO_BUY. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP needs the STANDARD band and the floor, and the floor fails at every price above $88.32. REJECTED needs a failed hard red line, a failed Layer 2, or no archetype clearing its floor at any verified support, and none of those happens: the red lines pass, Layer 2 scores 3.5 out of 5.0 with one failure, which the rule allows, and the floor is met at $88.32. That leaves WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. Our routing rule uses it for a stock that passes the gates and clears the floor at a lower support. A close above the 52-week high of $99.85 on heavy volume would not change this, because the central target would then sit below the price.

One more finding changes how we use that verdict. Our own stress test of the cash bridge asked whether growth and financing cover the dividend and the building program after Apollo's full cash distribution. The arithmetic, pro forma for Brazos, is: cash from operations $6,502 million, less 2026 capital spending of $3,500 million, less dividends of $2,698 million, less Apollo's 15% share of $975 million, leaves -$672 million, or 10.3% of cash flow. Counting only Apollo's 7.01% return of $631 million gives -$327 million, or 5.0%. The other $344 million repays Apollo's capital balance, so it is not a second expense, but it is real cash that does not reach shareholders or projects. If only 60% of Brazos EBITDA reaches operating cash flow, which is our assumption and not a filed figure, the full gap is -$872 million, or 13.9%[1][2][15]. Because the full gap is beyond the 10% limit in our funding measure, that measure now scores FAIL and Layer 2 falls to 3.5 out of 5.0. This does not change the verdict, which is already a wait at $95.82. It does tighten two things. Conviction falls from TIER_3 to TIER_4, which cuts the cap from 3.5% to 2.0% of net worth and the loss budget from 50 to 35 bps. And a price touch alone no longer makes an order executable, as the first trigger below says.

The archetype that fits is COMPOUNDER, adapted for a pipeline company in Chapter 3, and the bucket is CORE_ALPHA, because the return we are underwriting is a 4.5% dividend yield plus growth in earnings over several years, with timing the only tactical element. The watchlist seed said TACTICAL_MOMENTUM, and the chart does qualify as a Stage 2 leader, but a momentum trade needs a 2.5 floor and a move large enough to pay for it, and our central target is only 1.8% above the price.
