1. What the company does

Apple designs, makes and sells smartphones, personal computers, tablets, wearables and accessories, and it sells services that run on those devices.[1] In fiscal 2025, the year that ended September 27, 2025, revenue was $416.2 billion.[1] The iPhone brought in $209.6 billion of that, or 50.4%, and Services brought in $109.2 billion, or 26.2%.[7] The rest came from the Mac ($33.7 billion), the iPad ($28.0 billion) and Wearables, Home and Accessories ($35.7 billion).[7] Customers pay when they buy a device, and they pay again through App Store purchases, subscriptions, advertising-related licensing, cloud storage, payments and AppleCare plans.[1]

The two halves of the business earn very different profit. Products had a gross margin of 36.8% in fiscal 2025, which means that of each sales dollar about 37 cents were left after the direct cost of making the device. Services had a gross margin of 75.4%.[1] Services therefore produced $82.3 billion of the $195.2 billion of gross profit, or 42%, from 26% of revenue.[1] In the first nine months of fiscal 2026 the Services margin was 76.3% and the Products margin was 39.9%, although the Products figure includes tariff refunds that Chapter 2B removes.[3]

Apple's customers are spread across five reporting regions. In fiscal 2025 the Americas supplied $178.4 billion, Europe (which includes India, the Middle East and Africa) $111.0 billion, Greater China $64.4 billion, Japan $28.7 billion and the rest of Asia Pacific $33.7 billion.[7][1] Greater China is the region investors watch. Its sales fell from $67.0 billion in fiscal 2024 to $64.4 billion in fiscal 2025, and then rose 30% to $64.8 billion in the first nine months of fiscal 2026 alone.[7][3]

The base of existing customers is the real asset. Apple says its installed base, the count of active devices in use, passed 2.5 billion and reached a new high in every product category and region.[10] A device already in someone's pocket is a standing customer for Services. The iPhone also sets the replacement rhythm, and the iPhone 17 cycle has pushed iPhone revenue up 22% in the first nine months of fiscal 2026.[3][10]

Apple returns most of its cash to owners. In fiscal 2025 it spent $90.7 billion on buybacks, which means buying its own shares in the market, and paid $15.4 billion in dividends, against free cash flow of $98.8 billion.[1] Fewer shares means each remaining share owns a bigger slice of the profit. Diluted shares fell from 16.86 billion in fiscal 2021 to 15.00 billion in fiscal 2025, a drop of 11%.[2][1]

The fiscal year ends on the last Saturday of September, so fiscal 2026 ended on September 26, 2026, and Apple will report the fourth quarter on Monday, November 2, 2026, at 1:30 p.m. Pacific time.[1][15] Last year the annual report came one day after the results, on October 31, 2025, and we have not seen a published date for this year's filing, so we treat November 2 as the dated catalyst.[7][31] John Ternus, who ran hardware engineering, became chief executive on September 1, 2026, and Tim Cook became executive chair, so November 2 is also the first earnings call under the new chief executive.[8][15]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Gross margin is the share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of making the product or delivering the service.
  • Free cash flow is cash from operations minus the cash spent on equipment and buildings. It is the money an owner could take out of the business. Short form: FCF.
  • Buyback means the company buys its own shares, which shrinks the share count. An accelerated share repurchase (ASR) is a buyback paid up front to a bank that delivers the shares over a few months.
  • Installed base is the number of Apple devices in active use. It matters because each one can buy apps, subscriptions and accessories.
  • Reverse DCF works backwards from today's price to the growth in cash flow that the price assumes for ten years.
  • Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay in shares. It is a real cost to owners because it creates new shares.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is the after-tax operating profit earned on each dollar of debt and equity put into the business.
  • Tariff refund is money returned to Apple for import duties it paid under tariffs that the Supreme Court struck down in February 2026.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence of a durable business is how much cash it throws off while spending little on equipment. Over the twelve months to June 27, 2026, Apple's gross margin was 48.7%, its operating margin was 33.2% and its free cash flow was $136.7 billion, or 29.3% of revenue.[1][3][4] Capital expenditure, the cash spent on equipment and buildings, was only $10.0 billion, or 2.2% of revenue.[1][3][4] Microsoft spent $115.9 billion (35% of revenue) in its fiscal year to June 2026 and Alphabet spent $91.4 billion in 2025, and none of the four peers in Chapter 3 spent less than 18% of revenue on capital equipment.[23][24][25][26] Apple's large profits come without a matching bill for artificial intelligence computing, at least so far.

The moat, meaning the barrier that protects profit from competitors, rests on the installed base and on the fit between Apple's chips, software and devices. A customer who owns an iPhone, a Mac and a watch pays a price, in money and in hassle, to switch to a rival. That is our reading, because the filings do not measure switching costs. What Apple's reports do show is behavior: a June-quarter record for iPhone upgraders, and 22% iPhone growth in a quarter after Apple raised some prices to cover memory costs.[10][3] Apple also says, citing IDC, that it gained smartphone share worldwide in the quarter, which is a company statement we could not check against IDC's own data.[10]

The weak points are concentration and supply. The iPhone is half of revenue, so one product cycle sets the year.[7] The quarterly report says Apple obtains certain components from single or limited sources and relies on third-party cloud providers for computing capacity.[3] In the June quarter the company was held back by supply, mainly the advanced chip manufacturing capacity that makes its processors, and it expects the constraint to grow in the September quarter.[10] Tim Cook described it as a demand forecast problem, not a supplier problem: demand for the iPhone and Mac beat Apple's own plan.[10]

Costs are rising. Apple said it expects to pay even higher memory prices in the September quarter, and the chief executive called the memory market "a hundred-year flood" when explaining why Apple reluctantly raised prices.[10] The quarterly report adds that Apple "expects these trends to intensify".[3] Tariffs are a second cost swing. On February 20, 2026 the Supreme Court struck down tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, Apple applied for refunds and booked those received as a reduction of product cost, and the refunds lifted June-quarter gross margin by about 2 percentage points.[3][4] New tariffs under Section 301 were recently imposed and a Section 232 investigation covers semiconductors, so the tariff bill is not settled.[3]

Apple's position in artificial intelligence is the open question. Its approach is on-device models running on its own chips plus partnership: in January 2026 it said it was working with Google on the next generation of Apple Foundation Models, the base for a more personalized Siri.[12] The Siri AI upgrade was introduced at the June developer conference and began rolling out in beta, in English first, after the September 9 event.[3][14] Research and development spending rose 32% in the June quarter to $11.7 billion, mainly for AI infrastructure and staff.[3]

Regulation and legal exposure: App Store, search payments and antitrust

The European Union's Digital Markets Act (DMA) is a law that sets rules for the largest platform companies. On April 23, 2025 the European Commission fined Apple €500 million for restricting how developers tell users about offers outside the App Store.[22][3] In a second investigation the Commission issued preliminary findings the same day, and a final breach finding could bring a fine of up to 10% of annual worldwide net sales, which on fiscal 2025 sales of $416.2 billion would be $41.6 billion.[3][1] That is the legal ceiling, not our forecast. Apple has appealed the first decision and says it complies.

In the United States, a court order bars Apple from charging any commission on purchases made outside an app when the app links users there. The Ninth Circuit court said in December 2025 that Apple may charge some commission and sent the case back to be amended.[3] Apple asked the Supreme Court to review the case, and the Court granted review on June 30, 2026, limited to the legal standard for civil contempt.[3][20] Apple's brief was filed September 14, 2026, Epic's is due November 13, 2026, and oral argument is a 2027 event at the earliest, so the commission question is unlikely to be settled before 2027.[20][21] The chief financial officer said the App Store still set a June-quarter revenue record, and for the slowdown from 16% to 12% he named currency first, then the absence of last year's F1 theatrical release, weakness in mobile gaming and the link-out ruling.[10]

The Department of Justice sued Apple on March 21, 2024, alleging it monopolized smartphone markets, and Apple says it has substantial defenses.[3] The quarterly report gives no trial date. Separately, Apple earns revenue from licensing its search placement to Google. A court ordered remedies against Google on September 2, 2025, both sides appealed, and a reversal could stop Google from paying Apple for search distribution.[3] The filings do not give the dollar amount of the Google revenue, and we found no published figure in the documents we read, so we do not model it as a separate line. Services carries 42% of gross profit, so any cut to App Store commission or search payments hits the high-margin half of the business first.[1]

A new chief executive and what it changes

The Board named John Ternus chief executive on April 17, 2026, effective September 1, 2026, with Tim Cook becoming executive chair and Art Levinson becoming lead independent director.[8] Ternus's fiscal 2027 pay package is a $55 million equity award, 75% of it performance shares that vest on Apple's total shareholder return against the S&P 500, and Cook receives a $45 million award, 50% performance-based.[9] Pay tied to relative share performance rewards beating the market, not just growing sales. Cook also adopted a pre-arranged trading plan on May 28, 2026 that allows the sale of up to 16,250 shares through October 30, 2026.[3] What this means for an owner: the strategy is likely continuous, because Ternus built the hardware and silicon roadmap, but the first quarter under a new chief executive is when guidance habits, capital return and AI spending choices become visible. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk the talk means comparing what management promised with what it delivered. Apple publishes a range for revenue growth, gross margin, operating expenses and the tax rate one quarter ahead, which makes the record easy to check. In the last three quarters Apple landed above the top of its revenue range twice and inside it once, and above the top of its gross-margin range all three times, although in June only because of tariff refunds (about 48.1% without them, inside the range). The Yahoo Finance earnings history also shows four straight quarters of EPS (earnings per share) above the consensus estimate, by 4.5%, 6.3%, 3.5% and 6.7%.[13][12][11][6][5][4][16]

The record is weaker on three items. Services growth slowed more than the company had hinted in April. The personalized Siri promised for 2025 arrived a year later and still in beta. And the buyback pace slowed by 12% in the first nine months of fiscal 2026 while Apple removed its long-standing net cash neutral goal, so the cash pile is rising: net cash was $34 billion at September 27, 2025 and $62 billion at June 27, 2026.[13][11][3][4]

Reading the guidance rows with care matters, because the beats came with help. Tariff refunds added about 2 points to June-quarter gross margin, so the 50.1% is not a clean base. Revenue beats also came while Apple was supply constrained, which means the beats came from demand, not from shipping more units than planned. Management's habit of guiding conservatively is the reason we do not treat a beat as new information, and it is why the November 2 guidance for the December quarter matters more than the September number.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of audited results show a large business whose profit grew faster than its sales because it kept shrinking its share count. Revenue rose from $365.8 billion in fiscal 2021 to $416.2 billion in fiscal 2025, a compound growth rate of 3.3% a year.[2][1] Then it jumped: the twelve months to June 27, 2026 total $466.8 billion, 12.2% above fiscal 2025.[3][4] Diluted earnings per share (EPS), the profit divided across all shares, rose from $5.61 to $7.46 over the same four years, or 7.4% a year, and stands at $8.72 over the latest twelve months.[2][1][3][4]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption weaker than the rest. For the Reverse DCF, it is the starting point. The base year includes a working-capital gain and a tariff refund. For the base value, it is the multiple. Three year-end readings are a thin sample: the trailing multiple was 27.60, 36.95 and 33.85 at the last three year-ends, and today's 39.4 is above all three. For the bull value, it is that the Street's high EPS and the highest multiple arrive together. For the bear value, it is that memory costs rise as far as management warned. The scenarios below stress the base case from the bear side. One paragraph then argues the other side, so the rejection is tested both ways.

Fatal flaw 1: memory and chip supply costs break the margin

Apple paid more for memory in the March quarter than in December. It paid significantly more again in June. It said it expects even higher prices in September, with carry-in inventory helping less after that[10]. The chief executive called the memory market 'a hundred-year flood'. The quarterly report says the trends are expected to intensify[3]. If the memory bill grows faster than price rises can cover, margin could fall. Gross margin excluding refunds could drop from 48.1% to the mid-40s. That would turn the gross-margin KPI to FAIL. It would also take EPS toward the Street low of $8.50. The evidence to watch is the December-quarter margin guide on November 2. The fiscal 2027 margin commentary in January 2027 is the next check.

Fatal flaw 2: App Store and search rulings narrow Services

Services carries 42% of gross profit[1]. Three outcomes could cut revenue that has a margin above 75%. One is a final decision that lets developers steer users outside the App Store with no commission. A second is a fine under the European Union's second investigation. A third is a reversal of the Google search remedy that bars Google from paying Apple[3]. Services growth already slowed from 16.3% to 12.1% in one quarter. The Supreme Court review covers only the contempt standard. Briefs finish in November 2026, and argument is a 2027 event[20][21]. A fast resolution is unlikely. So the uncertainty stays in the price.

Fatal flaw 3: the iPhone cycle is pulled forward, then China and tariffs reverse it

iPhone revenue grew 22% over the first nine months of fiscal 2026. Apple guides to mid-teens growth in September[3][10]. A cycle this strong is often followed by a slower year. Greater China grew 30% in nine months after falling in fiscal 2025. A swing like that can reverse[3][7]. The Supreme Court ruling gave Apple refunds. But the 10-Q lists new Section 301 tariffs and an open Section 232 investigation on semiconductors[3]. Tariff costs could return and lift product cost. The new prices also test demand. The iPhone 18 Pro starts at $1,199 and the iPhone Duo at $1,999[14]. Yet the company says supply, not demand, is the limit[10].

Fatal flaw 4: AI spending and a first-year chief executive

Research and development spending rose 32% in the June quarter[3]. Operating expenses are guided to $19.1 billion to $19.4 billion for September[10]. Siri AI depends on server capacity and on a Google partnership for the underlying models[3][12]. If the compute bill keeps growing, operating margin could fall even if revenue holds. John Ternus is also a first-year chief executive, and November 2 is his first call. A guidance miss would hurt the multiple. So would a change in capital return policy. The multiple is the weakest link in our own base case.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED for now, under the rule that a name failing a Layer 1 gate is rejected. The failed gate is asymmetry, the reward-to-risk ratio: ($324.00 - $343.46) / ($343.46 - $285.76) = -0.34 to 1, against a floor of 3.0 to 1 for the Core bucket. Layer 2 also misses, with three of five KPIs at PASS against a minimum of four. The business itself clears every quality floor, so this is a verdict about price, not about whether Apple is a good company. A change in the verdict needs a lower price or a higher target, and the trigger lines below name both.[29][30]

Support places the shares in the STANDARD band of the support ladder. The nearest verified support is the 20-day average at $337.42, which is 0.9 average true range (ATR) units below the close of $343.46, inside the 0.6 to 1.2 band where a standard-sized purchase is allowed if every gate passes. The ladder would allow a limit order, meaning an order that fills only at your price or better, but the failed asymmetry gate overrides it, so no order is placed.[27][29]

The entry zone is $292.50 to $295.00. The top is the highest price at which the base target of $324 gives 3.0 to 1 against the $285.76 stop; the bottom is just above the 200-day average of $292.46, because a purchase below it would be buying into a broken floor. The stop is $285.76, which is the 200-day average minus one ATR of $6.70, as the Core rule requires: below the lowest of the swing low, the 50-day average and the 200-day average, minus one ATR. The zone is 14.1% below the close, so the idea depends on a pullback to the long-term average.[27][29]

Targets: the base is $324, the bull is $394 and the bear is $235, all derived in Chapter 5. Conviction today is TIER_0, which means zero weight because the verdict is REJECTED. If Apple re-qualified at the top of the zone, it would be TIER_2: a 75 basis point risk budget with a 3.13% stop distance gives a raw weight of 23.9%, capped at the 6.5% top of the tier range. A basis point is 0.01% of the portfolio, so 75 basis points is the share of the portfolio lost if the stop is hit.[29]

The let-winners-run rule applies after any purchase. A Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals is not trimmed only because its RSI passes 70 or its weight passes 15%; the weight may rise to 22%, and the stop trails the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average until the trend or the business breaks.[29] Apple is not a current holding, so there is no position to audit.

The table that follows is Layer 3 of our underwriting, and a plain reading of it goes like this. Layer 1 tests the business and Layer 2 tests its operating numbers. Layer 3 turns the verdict into action: where to buy, where the stop sits, how much to risk and how to treat a winner. Risk-unit sizing means we size a position by how much of the portfolio we would lose if the stop is hit, not by enthusiasm. That loss is counted in basis points, and portfolio heat is the total of those at-risk amounts across all positions. The conviction tier, from TIER_1 down to TIER_4, or TIER_0 for a rejected name, sets the risk budget and a cap on the weight. The let-winners-run rule says a rising Stage 2 winner is not sold just because it has grown large or strong.
