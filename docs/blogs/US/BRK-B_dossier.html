1. What the company does

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company, which means its main job is to own other companies. It owns insurers, a freight railroad, electric and gas utilities, and dozens of manufacturing, service and retail businesses, and it employed about 387,800 people at the end of 2025.[11] Its stock comes in two classes. A Class A share is the original. A Class B share, the one this report prices, is worth one-fifteen-hundredth of an A share, so every per-share figure in this report is per B share unless we say otherwise.[1] On June 30, 2026 there were 1,431,693 Class A equivalent shares, which is about 2.15 billion B equivalent shares (our arithmetic: 1,431,693 times 1,500).[1]

The company earns money in three ways. The first is insurance. Customers pay premiums up front and claims come later, and the gap is called float: money Berkshire holds and invests but does not own outright. Float was about $177.5 billion at June 30, 2026.[1] The 10-K says the combined insurance operations made an underwriting profit in each of 2023, 2024 and 2025, so the average cost of float was negative: Berkshire was paid to hold other people's money.[10] The second way is the operating businesses: the BNSF railroad, Berkshire Hathaway Energy and a large manufacturing, service and retailing group. The third is investing. At June 30, 2026 the company held $323.8 billion of listed shares and $365.5 billion of cash and U.S. Treasury bills (our sum of three balance-sheet lines: $35.1 billion, $324.9 billion and $5.5 billion).[2]

Operating earnings are the number to watch. Reported net earnings swing with the stock market, because accounting rules make Berkshire count every rise and fall in its listed shares as profit or loss. Net earnings were $96.2 billion in 2023 and $67.0 billion in 2025, but a loss of $22.8 billion in 2022.[8][12] Berkshire defines operating earnings as net earnings without investment gains and losses and without write-downs of investments. It reported $44.5 billion for 2025 and $24.3 billion for the first six months of 2026.[14][1]

Who pays, and for what

Drivers pay GEICO to cover accidents and theft. GEICO wrote $11.1 billion of premiums in the second quarter of 2026, and it sells mainly by direct response, which means customers apply to the company without an agent.[5] Commercial customers pay Berkshire's primary insurers, and other insurers pay the reinsurance group to take part of their risk. Shippers pay BNSF to move freight, and homes and businesses in several Western and Midwestern states pay the utilities for power and gas.[10][5] Customers of the manufacturing, service and retailing group pay for products and services of every kind, and that group had 7,669 of the 24,329 in operating earnings in the first six months.[1]

What the company does with its profit

Berkshire has not declared a cash dividend since 1967, and the 10-K says so in plain words.[11] CEO Greg Abel's first letter repeats the rule: no dividend while each dollar kept is reasonably likely to create more than a dollar of value for shareholders.[17] The company keeps its profit and uses it three ways. It buys businesses: OxyChem for about $9.4 billion in cash on January 2, 2026, and Taylor Morrison, a home builder, for $72.50 a share, about $6.8 billion in all, completed on July 24, 2026.[6][19] It invests in listed shares: in the first six months of 2026 it bought $39.4 billion and sold $27.8 billion.[4] And it can run a buyback, which is when a company buys its own shares, so that each remaining share owns a bigger slice of the company.

The buyback rule is strict. The program allows repurchases only when the CEO believes the price is below intrinsic value, conservatively determined, and not if cash, cash equivalents and Treasury bills would fall below $30 billion.[11] There were no buybacks from May 2024 through the fourth quarter of 2025. Berkshire then bought $235 million in the first quarter of 2026 and about $4.5 billion in the second, or about $4.8 billion for the half year.[17][16][1]

What changed in 2026

Six things changed. First, Greg Abel became CEO on January 1, 2026. Warren Buffett stays as chairman and owns about 13.7 percent of the economic interest and 30.0 percent of the votes.[11][18] Second, Todd Combs, who had managed part of the stock portfolio, left in December 2025, and Abel said responsibility for the stock portfolio rests with him as CEO.[17] Third, the company put $16.2 billion into two purchases (our sum of the OxyChem and Taylor Morrison prices).[6][19] Fourth, GEICO's profit margin fell sharply. Its combined ratio, which is claims plus costs divided by premiums earned, went from 83.5 percent in the second quarter of 2025 to 91.2 percent in the second quarter of 2026.[5] Fifth, buybacks restarted. Sixth, in 2026 Ajit Jain agreed to a $1.8 billion purchase of a 2.5 percent stake in Tokio Marine together with a quota share, which is a deal to take a fixed slice of its insurance business.[16]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares. For Berkshire we use operating earnings per Class B share.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is the profit earned on each dollar put into a business. For a holding company with a large investment book we use return on shareholders' equity instead, and Chapter 3 says why.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before financing and accounting charges. Berkshire does not report it for the whole group, so we use net cash and interest cover.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is the cash a business has left after paying to run itself and to buy equipment. For an insurer much of the operating cash is float, so the measure needs care.
  • Operating margin is the share of revenue left after the costs of running the business, and capital expenditure is money spent on track, plants, equipment and other long-lived assets.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying employees in shares.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast the price has moved. Above 70 is hot and below 30 is cold.
  • ATR (average true range) is the stock's typical one-day price swing. Support and stop distances are counted in ATRs.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend phase on the Weinstein scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • A base is a sideways stretch the price builds before a move, and a pivot is the top of that stretch.
  • A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the potential gain divided by the potential loss: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).
  • Net debt is borrowings minus cash. Berkshire has more cash than debt, so its net debt is negative, which we call net cash.
  • The price-to-earnings multiple is the price divided by a year of earnings per share, and price-to-book is the price divided by book value per share.
  • Guidance is what management says it expects to happen, and a consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow the stock.
  • Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by the number of shares, the accounting value of what one share owns. Operating earnings are net earnings without investment gains and losses.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Berkshire is a very strong business with a growth problem. The strength is financial. It holds $365.5 billion of cash and Treasury bills against $128.6 billion of borrowings, so it has net cash of about $236.9 billion (our arithmetic: $365.5 billion minus $128.6 billion, where the borrowings are $43.3 billion at insurance and other businesses plus $85.3 billion at the railroad, utilities and energy businesses).[2] Equity, the owners' stake in the books, was $747.9 billion, so debt is 17.2 percent of equity.[2] The problem is size and price. At $540.34 per B share the market value is about $1.16 trillion (our arithmetic: $540.34 times 2.1475 billion B equivalent shares), the stock trades at 1.55 times book value, and operating earnings without the Other line grew only 5.1 percent in the first half (our arithmetic).[30][1]

Where the competitive position is strong

The first strength is float that costs nothing. Float grew from $147 billion at the end of 2021 to $176 billion at the end of 2025 and $177.5 billion at June 30, 2026.[12][10][1] Pre-tax underwriting profit was $6.9 billion in 2023, $11.4 billion in 2024 and $9.5 billion in 2025, and $4.4 billion in the first half of 2026.[10][5] In 2022 the group lost $22 million before tax, which was the only loss in the five years.[12] Abel put the ten-year average combined ratio at 93 percent, which leaves about 7 cents of profit on each dollar of premium before investment income.[16]

The second strength is a balance sheet that lets Berkshire wait. It can move cash between subsidiaries in a highly tax-efficient way, Abel said, and BNSF sends large dividends to the parent, which can spend them on capital projects elsewhere, on listed shares, or on Treasury bills.[16] The third is assets that are hard to copy. BNSF has over 32,500 route miles of track, and Abel said BNSF is still carried on the books at its original purchase price, a fraction of its true value.[10][16] The fourth is alignment. Buffett was paid a $100,000 salary for 2025 and Abel $22 million, with almost nothing else, and Buffett owns about 13.7 percent of the economic interest.[18]

Where the competitive position is thin

GEICO is the weak spot. Its combined ratio was 84.7 percent in 2025 and 89.3 percent in the first six months of 2026, against 81.7 percent a year earlier. Pre-tax underwriting profit at GEICO fell 39.7 percent in the first half, from $3,994 million to $2,410 million (our arithmetic).[10][5] Claim frequency rose and so did the average cost of a claim: bodily injury severity was up in the ten to twelve percent range, and the expense ratio rose from 11.3 to 14.0 percent on commissions and advertising.[5] Abel also said GEICO's policies in force grew 2 percent in the first quarter while Progressive's grew 11 percent.[16] Progressive's combined ratio for its third quarter was 90.0 percent, close to GEICO's 91.2 percent for the second quarter.[25][5]

Insurance prices are also softening. Abel said a mild catastrophe period, with no major hurricane landfall in the United States for 19 months, has pulled more capital into the industry and that Berkshire will write less business if it cannot get the right price.[16] The numbers agree: Berkshire Hathaway Primary Group wrote 1.7 percent less premium in the first half, and RSUI, its property insurer, wrote 13.2 percent less.[5] The 10-K itself warns that underwriting earnings may decline from competition and rising claim costs.[10]

Three more weak points are specific to Berkshire. The stock portfolio is concentrated: Alphabet, American Express, Apple, Bank of America and Coca-Cola were 66 percent of the $323.8 billion of listed shares at June 30, 2026.[6][2] PacifiCorp, a Berkshire Hathaway Energy utility, faces wildfire lawsuits: juries have awarded about $1.25 billion in net damages to 201 plaintiffs so far, and 1,760 more class members filed mass complaints, all on appeal or pending.[6] And the company depends on a few people. The 10-K says it relies on a few key people for investment and capital allocation decisions, and that Abel now holds that role.[11]

Reserves and catastrophes: how much of the profit is a cushion?

Reserves are the money an insurer sets aside for claims it has not yet paid. If the estimates turn out too high, the insurer releases the excess as profit, and if too low, it takes a charge. In the first half of 2026 Berkshire's primary insurers cut their estimates for older claims by $444 million, mostly on property and to a lesser extent on casualty losses, and the reinsurance group cut its estimates by $869 million.[5][6] A year earlier the primary insurers had raised their estimates by $401 million, and the reinsurance group had cut its by $506 million. GEICO's change between the two years was described as relatively insignificant.[5][6] There were also no significant catastrophe events in the first half of 2026, against after-tax catastrophe losses of $850 million in the first half of 2025.[5]

Put together, these are real tailwinds. Net releases at the primary and reinsurance groups were $1,313 million before tax in 2026 against $105 million in 2025 (our arithmetic: 444 plus 869, against 506 minus 401). Without them, group pre-tax underwriting profit would have been about $3,132 million against $4,151 million, a fall of about 25 percent (our arithmetic, and it leaves out any releases at GEICO). So the 4.4 percent rise in group underwriting profit is better described as a flat-to-lower underlying result helped by a calm period and by cushion that was built earlier.[5][6] We treat the cushion as real but not as a source of future growth, and we weigh it in the Layer 2 score for underwriting.

Capital allocation: the record has misses

Good capital allocation is the reason to own Berkshire, so the misses matter. In 2025 the company took after-tax write-downs of $8.3 billion on its Kraft Heinz and Occidental holdings, which are holdings it accounts for under the equity method, where Berkshire records its share of their earnings.[10] The cash pile is the other question. It is $365.5 billion, or 48.9 percent of Berkshire's equity (our arithmetic), and it earns Treasury bill interest, which fell: insurance investment income was down 8.3 percent in the first half, on lower rates.[2] Management's answer is patience. It has spent $16.2 billion on two acquisitions in 2026, but the timing matters. By June 30 only OxyChem ($9.4 billion) had been paid, and the pile was down 2.1 percent (our arithmetic) from $373.3 billion at December 31, 2025. Taylor Morrison ($6.8 billion) closed on July 24 and comes out of the third quarter, which would put the pile near $358.7 billion, or 3.9 percent below December (our arithmetic).[7][6][19][17]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of results show two things at once: a steady earnings climb and a balance sheet that changed shape. Operating earnings rose from $27.6 billion in 2021 to $44.5 billion in 2025, a 61 percent gain or about 12.7 percent a year (our arithmetic), while revenue grew 34.5 percent. Operating earnings per Class B share rose from $12.18 to $20.62.[12][14][8] Net earnings tell a wilder story, from $89.9 billion in 2021 to a loss of $22.8 billion in 2022 and back to $96.2 billion in 2023, which is why we anchor on operating earnings.

The balance sheet changed more. Net cash, meaning cash and Treasury bills minus borrowings, was $5.8 billion at the end of 2022 and $244.2 billion at the end of 2025 (our arithmetic). Borrowings hardly moved, from $122.7 billion to $129.1 billion, so the whole change is cash.[12][7] Buybacks fell from $27.1 billion in 2021 to zero in 2025, and the Class B share count fell only 4.8 percent over the five years (average 2,265.3 million in 2021, 2,157.3 million in 2025).[12][9][8] Cash from operations minus capital expenditure did not grow: $26.2 billion in 2021 and $25.0 billion in 2025, because capital spending rose from $13.3 billion to $20.9 billion.[12][9]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter takes the weakest assumption behind each estimate, builds the strongest honest case against our conclusion, names the one claim the verdict depends on, and says when we will know. We give three flaws, each of which could by itself end the idea.

Flaw 1: the market values Berkshire on operating earnings, and not on book value

Our base target multiplies book value per share by 1.50, the median of the last three year-end price-to-book ratios. That ratio sat between 1.30 and 1.51 at the five year-ends, so it is a steady yardstick.[23] But a skeptic would say that Berkshire is a business that earns $22 a share and should be priced on that. On operating earnings the multiple at the last three year-ends was 20.8, 20.6 and 24.4 times, and today it is 24.3 times (our arithmetic).[23][12][8][14]

Apply the three-year median of 20.8 times to the 2027 consensus of $22.43 and the value is about $466 (our arithmetic). That is about 14 percent below the close, and below our stop of $489.60. At $503.50 the reward-to-risk would be negative, and no price in our zone would clear the 2.0 floor. If the market priced Berkshire this way, the verdict would be REJECTED.[20][23] There is a fair answer. Operating earnings leave out the retained gains on $323.8 billion of listed shares, which is why the earnings multiple has drifted up as the portfolio grew, and Abel's own measure of intrinsic value is net cash and Treasury bills, plus the listed shares at market, plus a ten-year view of the businesses.[2][16] The answer is not a proof, and the gap between $571 and $466 is the main risk to our number.

Flaw 2: a drawdown in a concentrated stock portfolio, with interest income already falling

Five holdings are 66 percent of $323.8 billion of listed shares: Alphabet, American Express, Apple, Bank of America and Coca-Cola.[6] Because accounting rules put every price change into reported earnings and into equity, a 25 percent fall in the portfolio removes $81.0 billion before tax and about $64.0 billion after a 21 percent tax assumption (our arithmetic). On 2.13 billion shares that is about $30 a share, and our base book value of $381 would fall to about $351 before any change in earnings.[2]

The same fall would probably pull the multiple down too. At 1.30 times, the 2021 level, the price would be $456 on that book value, below the stop. The cash pile adds a second exposure, because it earns Treasury bill interest. Insurance investment income already fell 8.3 percent in the first half on lower rates, and each one-point fall in bill yields would cut pre-tax interest by about $3.7 billion on $365.5 billion, or about 6 percent of operating earnings after tax (our arithmetic at a 21 percent tax assumption).[1][2]

Flaw 3: GEICO and the insurance cycle turn together, while a wildfire bill and leadership change arrive

GEICO's combined ratio was 89.3 percent in the first half and 91.2 percent in the second quarter. Each point on about $45 billion of annual premiums (GEICO's premiums written were $45.2 billion in 2025) is about $0.45 billion of pre-tax profit. If the ratio rose to 95 percent, the cost would be about $2.6 billion before tax and about $2.0 billion after a 21 percent tax assumption, which is 4.2 percent of twelve-month operating earnings (our arithmetic).[5][10] That is manageable alone. But Abel says industry prices are softening, Berkshire's primary insurers are shrinking property business, and the group profit of 2023 to 2025 was, in the words of the 10-K, 'exceptional compared to results over longer periods'.[16][5][10]

PacifiCorp's wildfire litigation adds a bill of unknown size. Juries have awarded about $1.25 billion in net damages to 201 plaintiffs, an average of about $6.2 million each, and 1,760 more class members filed mass complaints seeking much larger sums per person. If every one of them received the average to date the total would be about $10.9 billion, or 1.5 percent of equity (our arithmetic, an illustration and not a forecast).[6][2] Leadership is changing as well: a new CEO since January, a new GEICO chief since December 2025, a portfolio manager who left, and a chief financial officer who is retiring after 34 years, according to the annual meeting summary.[16][17][11]
