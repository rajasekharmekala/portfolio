Before you read

SGOV is the iShares 0-3 Month Treasury Bond ETF from BlackRock, a fund that holds Treasury bills and pays out their interest each month.[5][1] This report rates it REJECTED as a shortlist name, meaning it is not a holding meant to beat the market, and the fund itself is sound: it costs 0.09% a year and holds $116.78 billion.[5][2] Its price cannot rise enough to pay for risk (reward-to-risk is 0.21 to 1), the repository counts only broker cash as cash, and its 3.76% yield is close to the 3.6% Robinhood Gold may pay on idle cash.[1][24] The book does not hold SGOV, and its holding NBIL, once described as a Treasury ETF, is a 2x leveraged fund on Nebius.[22] The call changes if the cash rules count Treasury bill funds as cash or sweep rates fall well below the fund's yield, and the Fed meets on October 27-28, 2026[13].

1. What the company does

SGOV is an exchange-traded fund (ETF), a basket of securities that trades on a stock exchange all day like a share. It is issued by iShares, the fund brand of BlackRock, trades on the NYSE and began on May 26, 2020.[1] Its stated aim is to track the ICE 0-3 Month US Treasury Securities Index, which measures U.S. Treasury debt with three months or less to run, and its adviser is BlackRock Fund Advisors.[5] The fund must put at least 80% of its assets in index securities and at least 90% in U.S. Treasury securities, and the index held 39 issues, each with at least $1 billion outstanding, on February 28, 2026.[5] On October 23, 2026 it held net assets of $116,778,844,862 across 1,160,150,000 shares, so each share is worth about $100.66.[1]

The money is made in a simple way. A Treasury bill is a short-term loan to the U.S. government that is sold below face value and repaid at face value, and the IRS treats that gap as interest income.[26] SGOV buys many bills, with an average maturity of 0.12 years (about six weeks), and rolls the cash from each maturing bill into new ones, so its income follows short-term rates within weeks.[1] The Federal Reserve raised its target range to 3-3/4 to 4 percent on September 16, 2026, the 3-month bill yielded 4.22% on October 22 and the fund's 30-day SEC yield (its income per share after costs, annualized) was 3.76%.[12][9][1]

You are paid in two ways at once, and that explains the price chart. Interest builds up inside the fund every day, which lifts the share price a little each session, and once a month the fund pays that interest out as a distribution and the price drops by the same amount on the ex-dividend date, the first day the shares trade without the payout.[7] For example, the close was $100.68 on September 30 and $100.41 on October 1, the day a distribution of $0.300547 went ex (our calculation from daily closes).[15][1] The next scheduled ex-date is November 2, 2026, payable November 5, which is our reading of the BlackRock schedule because SGOV's past ex-dates match that row.[7]

The book does not hold SGOV. Neither account log shows a SGOV buy or sell, so there is no closed position to explain, and the options book has no SGOV contract. The watchlist note called the fee "0.07%", but the 2020 launch prospectus shows 0.07% was the net fee after a waiver that ran only to June 30, 2022, and today's stated fee is 0.09%.[6][5] The note's other claim, more than $25 billion in assets, holds: assets were $116.78 billion, about 4.7 times that.[2] The watchlist's earlier text for NBIL described it as a Treasury ETF, but NBIL is the GraniteShares 2x Long NBIS Daily ETF, which seeks twice the daily move of Nebius Group stock.[22]

The price chart is a sawtooth, not a trend

Over the 12 months to October 2026 the fund paid 12 distributions that add up to $3.666 a share, which is 3.64% of today's price and matches the 3.64% trailing 12-month yield iShares reports (our calculation from the Yahoo dividend record).[15][1] Each payout was followed by a one-day price drop of about 0.28 dollars on average, and then the price climbed again for the rest of the month. A chart of the price alone therefore shows a repeating ramp and drop with no upward trend, and every technical indicator in Chapter 4 reads that pattern as if it were a stock.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Net asset value (NAV) is the value of everything the fund owns, minus what it owes, divided by the number of shares; for SGOV it is almost the same as the market price.
  • Treasury bill is a U.S. government loan that lasts one year or less, sold below face value and repaid at face value.
  • 30-day SEC yield is a standard yield figure that shows the interest earned over the last 30 days after fund costs, stated as a yearly rate.
  • Distribution yield means the payouts of the last 12 months divided by the share price; for SGOV it is 3.64%.
  • Ex-dividend date is the first day a share trades without the next payout; the price drops by about the payout amount that day.
  • Effective duration is a measure of how much a bond fund's price moves when interest rates move; SGOV's 0.12 years means roughly a 0.12% price change for each one-point move in rates.
  • Expense ratio is the yearly fee taken out of fund assets, here 0.09%, or $9 for every $10,000 held.
  • Bid-ask spread is the gap between the highest price a buyer offers and the lowest price a seller accepts; SGOV's median is 0.01%, about one cent.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The fund's strengths are scale, cost and ease of trading, and each is measurable. At $116.78 billion it is the largest of the four bill funds we compared, ahead of BIL at $51.15 billion, SHV at $23.55 billion and TBIL at $7.60 billion.[2][19][20][21] Its 0.09% fee is the lowest of the four (BIL charges 0.1353% before waivers, and SHV and TBIL charge 0.15%).[5][19][20][21] The median spread is 0.01% and the 30-day average volume is 22,506,363 shares, about $2.27 billion traded a day at today's price (our calculation).[1] A $1,300 order is a rounding error in that market.

Capital safety has also been very good in practice. Over the 501 daily closes we downloaded, the worst one-day total return, counting distributions as reinvested, was -0.0100% and the deepest peak-to-trough loss was the same -0.0100% (our calculation).[15] The fund's own risk section is careful, though: it says you could lose all or part of your investment, that the market value of Treasury obligations is not guaranteed, and that shares can trade at a premium or discount to NAV.[5] Treat the history as a starting guide, not a promise.

The weaknesses are about purpose. A bill fund cannot grow in price, so it can never meet the asymmetry tests (the ratio of possible gain to possible loss) that the repository applies to names meant to beat the market. Its income also trails rates, because it holds bills bought weeks ago: the 3-month Treasury yield was 4.22% on October 22, while the fund's 30-day SEC yield was 3.76% and its average yield to maturity was 4.00%.[9][1] The gap is partly the 0.09% fee and partly timing, but our sources compute these yields on different bases and we cannot split the gap exactly. The Treasury's own par curve for October 23 showed 4.11% at one month and 4.22% at three months, so bills a little longer than the fund's six-week average were paying a bit more.[14]

How it compares with cash at the brokers

Robinhood pays a 3.6% annual percentage yield (APY) on eligible cash for Gold members, a $5 monthly subscription, as of September 17, 2026, and the Robinhood log shows monthly Gold fees.[24] A third-party tracker reported on October 1, 2026 that Schwab's standard bank sweep pays 0.01%, and the Schwab log agrees: bank interest of $0.02 on October 16 and $0.01 in August, on a balance of $164.69.[25] The comparison depends on one fact the records do not show: whether your Robinhood account is enrolled in Gold's cash program. All income figures in this report use one basis, the quoted annual rate times the balance for one year, with no compounding. If the account is enrolled, SGOV's 3.76% is 0.16 points above the 3.6% sweep, which is about $4.18 a year on the $2,615.51 held there. If it is not enrolled, the gap could be far larger, up to about $98.34 a year if that cash earns nothing. At Schwab the gap is large in percentage terms but small in dollars, about $6.18 a year on $164.69.

Closure and sponsor risk

BlackRock closes funds that fail to attract assets: its closures page says iShares "continually reviews its range of products," and lists recent closures such as the iBonds Oct 2026 Term TIPS ETF, which made a final distribution in October 2026.[8] When a fund closes, shareholders receive a cash distribution near the final date, which can force a taxable sale you did not choose.[8] SGOV is the opposite of a sub-scale fund: assets grew 57% from $74.32 billion on February 28, 2026 to $116.78 billion on October 23, 2026 (our calculation).[4][2] The prospectus names one structural weak point, Authorized Participant concentration: only a few firms may create and redeem shares, and if they step away, shares can trade at a discount and even face trading halts or delisting.[5]

Why that makes it a poor fit for an alpha slot

The CORE_ALPHA label on the watchlist expects a growing business with a return on capital above 12% and a reward-to-risk ratio of at least 2.0 (the repository's scoring file). A Treasury bill fund has no business, no growth and no return on capital, and its price reward over the stop distance is 0.21 to 1 at the current price. Cash is its own bucket in the scoring file, with a 5% to 10% range, and Chapter 7 shows why this report places SGOV there rather than in an alpha bucket.

Management here means BlackRock Fund Advisors, and its promises are the ones written in the fund documents. We checked four against what happened, and one could not be checked from what we read. 3. The numbers, in plain words

A company report starts with revenue, profit and cash. SGOV has none of those, because it is a fund that passes Treasury bill interest to its owners, so the numbers that matter are the fund's returns against its index, its cost, its size, its yield and how its price behaves around payouts. The tables below give those numbers for five calendar years where the sources allow it, and they say plainly where a standard company measure does not exist.

Net debt is debt minus cash, and it is one of four standard company measures that a fund cannot produce. The other three are return on invested capital (ROIC), free cash flow (FCF) and stock-based compensation (SBC), and the debt test is stated against EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization). None can be computed for a fund, and inventing them would be false precision. We mark them N/A with the reason and replace them with fund measures in the Layer 1 table.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one weak assumption, and the bear case is the strongest argument against our verdict that the fund is not worth shortlisting. The bear case says: SGOV is the cheapest, safest and most liquid way to hold cash, the Robinhood sweep rate of 3.6% is "subject to change" by its own terms and applies only to enrolled accounts, and Schwab pays 0.01%, so the rule that ignores T-bill funds is the real problem and rejecting the fund hides it.[24][25] We think the case is partly right, which is why the verdict is a classification choice and not a warning about the fund.

Fatal flaw 1: the Fed cuts faster than we assume

The weakest assumption behind the income estimate is that the yield stays near 3.76% or goes up. The Fed raised rates on September 16, 2026 to a 3-3/4 to 4 percent range, and the effective funds rate was 3.88% on October 22, but the Fed also cut 75 basis points in three cuts of 25 basis points in late 2025.[12][11][4] If cuts come, the fund's income falls within weeks because its bills mature in about six weeks, the prospectus names this Income Risk, and the sweep rate would likely fall as well.[5] Evidence that we are wrong would be a drop in the 3-month bill yield below 3.5%; the next FOMC meeting is October 27-28, 2026.[13]

Fatal flaw 2: a Treasury market or fund-structure shock

Our price estimate assumes the price tracks accrued interest. The prospectus warns that although Treasury obligations are backed by the full faith and credit of the United States, circumstances could arise that prevent timely payment, and that shares can trade at a premium or discount to NAV if Authorized Participants step back.[5] Our two years of daily closes show a worst one-day total return of -0.0100%, so this is a tail risk that the data cannot rule out.[15] A close below $100.37 on a day that is not an ex-date would be the first sign.

Fatal flaw 3: our reading of the cash rule is wrong, or the rule changes

We read the repository as counting only broker cash balances as cash, because the positions snapshot sums account cash and values every fund share, including bill funds, in equities. The scoring file sets a cash band of 5% to 10% without saying whether bill funds qualify. If the owner decides they qualify, a $1,338.24 purchase would no longer lower measured cash and the verdict would change from REJECTED to a cash-vehicle approval. We will know this when the owner states the rule, which is a decision, not an event with a date.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED. In this report that means SGOV is not added to the shortlist as a holding meant to beat the market. The repository's verdict list has no value for a cash substitute, so REJECTED is the nearest label, and the mismatch is deliberate. It is not a statement that the fund is a poor product. It is not an endorsement to buy it either. The rule that triggers it is the asymmetry floor. Layer 1 needs a ratio of possible gain to possible loss of at least 2.0 for the COMPOUNDER archetype and 2.5 for the other two. SGOV scores 0.21 at the current price, so the gate is a FAIL.

Several terms need a plain definition. Layer 1, Layer 2 and Layer 3 are the repository's three checks. Layer 1 tests the business and its direction. Layer 2 scores company-specific operating measures (Chapter 2B). Layer 3 sets the entry level, stop and size, and it is this chapter. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold. Structural invalidation means the stop sits just below the price level that, if broken, shows the setup has failed. CMP is the current market price, here $100.66. A basis point (bps) is one hundredth of a percent.

Tiers set position size. TIER_1 to TIER_4 are four conviction levels with caps of 10%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2%. TIER_0 means zero size, and it is the tier we assign. The yardstick tier for the risk budget is TIER_3 at 50 bps, which is $411.84 of net worth. Risk-unit sizing sets a position's weight so that hitting the stop loses no more than a fixed share of net worth. Portfolio heat is the sum of those possible losses across the book, capped at 12% of net worth. With TIER_0 the risk budget is zero, so the size is zero and the head shows 0.0%.

The let-winners-run rule says never to trim a rising winner only because RSI passes 70 or its weight crosses 15%. It does not apply here, because the fund is not a winner. The moving averages are simple averages of closes over 50 and 200 days. The 21-day exponential average (EMA) gives recent days more weight.

The stop uses the CORE_ALPHA rule in its Stage 2 form, which the table prints in its generic cell. Take the lower of the 20-day swing low ($100.40) and the 50-day average ($100.56). Subtract one ATR ($0.03). The stop is $100.37. The strategy bucket is CASH, the scoring file's 5% to 10% sleeve, and not the CORE_ALPHA label on the watchlist. The table prints CORE_ALPHA stop wording only because the scoring file has no stop wording for CASH. The support level for the ladder is the 50-day average at $100.56.
