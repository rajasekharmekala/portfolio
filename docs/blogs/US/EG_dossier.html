1. What the company does

Everest Group, Ltd. is a holding company based in Hamilton, Bermuda, and its shares trade on the New York Stock Exchange as EG.[2][9] It is a member of the S&P 500 index and describes a 50-year record as a reinsurer.[9] Reinsurance is insurance for insurance companies. A primary insurer pays Everest a premium, and Everest agrees to pay part of the insurer's biggest losses from storms, earthquakes, liability claims and specialty risks.[9] Since January 1, 2026 the company reports three segments. Reinsurance Treaty and Global Wholesale & Specialty are the two that sell new business, and together they are called the Core businesses. Legacy holds the commercial retail insurance business that is being sold or run off.[9]

An insurer collects premium first and pays claims later. The money it holds in between is called float, and the insurer invests it. Everest held $43,965 million of investments and cash at September 30, 2026, against $35,288 million of reserves for unpaid claims.[1] Net investment income set a record of $634 million in the third quarter, including $144 million from the private equity portfolio.[1][18] The yield on the bonds already owned is 4.6 percent, and new money is invested at about 5.1 percent, so investment income has room to rise if rates hold.[18] Cash from operations was $1,606 million in the first nine months of 2026 against $3,466 million a year earlier.[1] Most of that cash is premium waiting to be paid out, so this report does not treat free cash flow (cash left after running the business and buying assets) as owner cash for Everest.

The yardstick for underwriting is the combined ratio: claims plus expenses divided by premiums earned. Below 100 percent means an underwriting profit. The Group ratio was 107.1 percent in the third quarter, so claims and costs took 7.1 cents more than each premium dollar.[1] The ratio has four parts. Current-year losses were 58.0 points, the charge for older-year reserves was 8.5 points, catastrophe losses were 10.5 points, and commissions and other costs were 30.0 points (23.7 plus 6.3).[1] The parts add to 107.0, and the release shows 107.1 because of rounding. For the nine months the Group ratio was 96.3 percent, against 98.7 percent a year earlier.[1]

Customers are insurance companies. Reinsurance Treaty sells cover on whole portfolios, such as a share of an insurer's casualty policies (called pro rata) or protection against one large loss (called excess of loss).[1] In the third quarter its premium fell 24.8 percent in casualty excess of loss and 15.8 percent in casualty pro rata, while property premium fell 1.8 percent after adjusting for reinstatement premiums.[1] Global Wholesale & Specialty sells insurance through the wholesale and specialty markets. Its lines include financial lines, marine, aviation, political risk, surety and accident and health, plus a US excess and surplus lines business.[11][21] Its third-quarter premium grew 3.3 percent, led by financial lines up 27.5 percent and specialty up 21.5 percent.[1]

What the company does with its profit

Everest returns most of its profit through share repurchases. A buyback is when a company buys its own shares, so each remaining share owns a bigger slice of the business. In the third quarter Everest bought 1,268,158 shares for $474 million at an average of $374, and in nine months it bought 3,426,047 shares for $1.2 billion.[1][18] Shares outstanding fell to 37.4 million from 42.0 million a year earlier, a drop of 11.0 percent (our arithmetic).[1] The dividend is $2.00 a quarter, which cost $75 million in the third quarter.[1] Management calls $300 million a quarter its repurchase floor and says it will go higher when it can.[18] Dividends and buybacks together took about $1.43 billion in nine months, which is 88 percent of the $1.62 billion of operating income (our arithmetic).[1]

What changed in 2025 and 2026

Five things changed. First, Everest sold the renewal rights to its commercial retail insurance business in October 2025. The rights covered an estimated $2 billion of gross premium, and AIG paid $252 million for the US, UK and Asia Pacific parts and $49 million for the European Union part.[6][11] Canada is being sold to Wawanesa, with closing expected in the second half of 2026, and Colombia to AIG, with closing expected in early 2027.[12][9] Second, the company bought protection on old reserves: an adverse development cover with a $1.2 billion limit for accident years 2024 and earlier in the North American insurance business.[6] Third, it launched Annapurna Re, a casualty and specialty sidecar, with Stone Point in June 2026. A sidecar lets outside investors take part of the risk and profit, and it earns Everest fees.[9][19]

Fourth, management changed. Elias Habayeb is now chief financial officer, a new group chief actuary named Katy Bradica joined, and the former general counsel left under a $7.25 million separation agreement.[18][15] Jim Williamson has been chief executive since January 2025.[10] Fifth, the Bermuda Monetary Authority told Everest it will become its group supervisor, with a twelve-month transition that ends in January 2027.[9] A group supervisor can set group-level capital and reporting rules, so the cost and the capital effect are still open.[9]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio: claims plus expenses divided by premiums earned; below 100 percent is an underwriting profit.
  • Attritional combined ratio: the combined ratio without catastrophe losses, reinstatement premiums and changes to old reserves; it shows the everyday margin.
  • Prior-year development: changes to the estimate of old claims. Unfavorable means the earlier estimate was too low and the extra cost hits the current quarter.
  • Float: premiums collected now that will be paid out as claims later and invested in the meantime.
  • Book value per share: shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding; the main scoreboard for an insurer.
  • Operating return on equity: after-tax operating income divided by average equity; it leaves out investment price swings, currency swings and one-time deal costs.
  • Adverse development cover (ADC): reinsurance Everest bought to pay for old claims that cost more than expected, up to a limit.
  • Catastrophe loss: claims from one large event such as an earthquake or a storm.
  • Sidecar: a vehicle that lets outside investors share the risk and profit of part of a reinsurer's book.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is profit earned on each dollar invested in the business; for an insurer we use operating return on equity instead.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before financing and accounting charges; insurers do not publish it.
  • FCF is short for free cash flow, which is the cash left after running the business and buying assets; for an insurer it is mostly float, so we mark it N/A.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying employees in shares.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast the price has moved; above 70 is hot and below 30 is cold.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in dollars.
  • A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the potential gain divided by the potential loss: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Everest is a capable reinsurer with a reserve problem that it is now trying to close. The strength shows in the scoreboard. Book value per share without unrealized bond losses was $414.00 at September 30, 2026 against $368.29 a year earlier, up 12.4 percent (our arithmetic), and the company's annualized total shareholder return, which it defines as the growth in that book value plus dividends, was 14.2 percent for the first nine months.[1] Operating return on equity was 14.0 percent over nine months against 11.7 percent a year earlier.[1] The weakness shows in the third quarter. The Group posted a $223 million underwriting loss and a 107.1 percent combined ratio, and it was the fourth quarter in the past eight with net adverse prior-year reserve development above $100 million (the fourth quarter of 2024, the third and fourth quarters of 2025, and the third quarter of 2026).[1][5][24][23] We call such a quarter a reserve hit in this report.

Three facts frame the rest of this chapter. Premium is shrinking on purpose: Group premium earned fell 18.1 percent in the quarter and 12.6 percent over nine months.[1] Property prices are falling, with the Guy Carpenter global property catastrophe rate-on-line index down 16 percent so far in 2026, the largest annual fall since the late 1990s.[30] And all three rating agencies hold a negative outlook on the operating companies.[6][18] Each fact is explained below, with the evidence and the level that would change our view.

Where the competitive position is strong

Start with the lead-market franchise. Management says Everest is a top-tier lead market that high-quality insurers seek out.[18] That is the company's own claim, but the mid-year renewals support it. Property catastrophe pricing in the market fell 15 to 20 percent at June 1 and July 1, and Everest's own portfolio finished down about 10 percent.[19] The CEO says Everest writes the majority of its catastrophe excess-of-loss business on a non-concurrent basis and that this shields it from some of the competitive pressure.[18] Management also says attachment points, the loss level where cover starts to pay, remain relatively stable.[19]

Underlying margin is steady. The Core attritional combined ratio was 87.0 percent over nine months against 87.2 percent a year earlier, and it was 87.0 percent in the third quarter against 87.6 percent.[1] Reinsurance Treaty ran 84.9 percent in the quarter and Global Wholesale and Specialty 93.7 percent, against 93.4 percent a year earlier.[1] The reserve charge and the catastrophe losses sit on top of that underlying margin, not inside it.

The investment engine adds a third support. Net investment income was $1,725 million over nine months against $1,563 million, up 10.4 percent (our arithmetic), and the third quarter was a record at $634 million.[1] The bonds are rated double-A minus on average, according to the CFO.[18] A fourth strength is the balance sheet: equity of $14,252 million, total investments and cash of $43,965 million, and a reserve margin that management says is over $850 million above its central estimate.[1][18]

Where the competitive position is thin

Casualty is the weakest area. The CEO called his view of casualty reinsurance "fairly bearish" and said ceding commissions, the fee a reinsurer pays the insurer for the business, are "stubbornly high" and not justified by the results.[18] Everest cut written premium by 24.8 percent in casualty excess of loss and 15.8 percent in casualty pro rata in the third quarter.[1] He also said he will not regrow that book until conditions improve materially.[18] The business is being shrunk, which protects margin and also lowers future earnings.

Property pricing is also soft. The Guy Carpenter global property catastrophe index is 23 percent below its 2024 peak, and it is still about 32 percent above the 2017 soft-market low.[30] Moody's says prices fell more than 20 percent in the 18 months since 2024, and Global RE reports outlooks of a further 10 to 15 percent decline at January 1, 2027.[31][32] Everest itself expects another competitive renewal with some more rate decreases and says it will become more selective.[18]

Costs are creeping up. Core other underwriting expenses were 5.2 percent of premium earned in the quarter against 4.6 percent a year earlier, because smaller premium spreads fixed costs thinly.[1] The AIG transition services payment of about $30 million a quarter expired at the end of the third quarter, so the fourth quarter loses that credit.[18]

Reserves: how much can you trust the numbers?

Reserves are the money an insurer sets aside for claims it has not yet paid. If they are set too low, the shortfall hits a later quarter. Everest's record is mixed. In the fourth quarter of 2024 it added $1.7 billion pre-tax, mostly for US casualty insurance, and 2024 included $1.5 billion of unfavorable development on prior-year reserves.[7] In October 2025 it bought the adverse development cover.[6] On April 30, 2026 management said the overall reserve position was strong, especially in reinsurance, with no material movements in US casualty.[20] In the second quarter it added just under $200 million to North America casualty, and in the third quarter it added about $600 million gross, of which about two-thirds was North America casualty.[19][1][18] After offsets the net charge was $253 million in Reinsurance Treaty and $268 million for the Group, worth 8.5 points on the Group ratio.[1]

Management's defense is specific. It says booked reserves are now over $850 million above its actuarial central estimate, and that the margin rose about $300 million since June while the central estimate itself rose by a few hundred million.[18] On short-tail lines the CFO says about $500 million may have been released year to date, but that most of the favorable emergence in the third quarter was held back, and that 2026 loss picks needed no change.[18] The adverse development cover protects the North American insurance book, not the treaty book where this charge sat, and about $1 billion of net limits remain on it.[6][18] The CFO also said he is not sure net favorable releases will come in the near term.[18]

We read this two ways. A charge of this size, the fourth reserve hit in eight quarters, shows that earlier numbers were too low. But a charge taken on purpose, with a wider margin above the central estimate, is better than small charges every quarter. The test is the fourth quarter. Management said a few reserve studies remain, covering the international insurance business, cyber and smaller lines, and it said it would consider updating the year-end loss triangles that it first published on August 31, 2026.[18][14] Another reserve hit would end our patience. What this means for an owner: the book value of $381.04 now looks cleaner than it did in July, but it is not yet proven.

Catastrophe load, ratings and regulation

Catastrophe losses were $322 million pre-tax in the quarter, which is 10.5 points on the Group ratio, against $50 million and 1.3 points a year earlier. The main events were the Colombia earthquake, events in the Middle East and weather losses.[1][22] Over nine months catastrophe losses were 5.4 points, against 5.2 points a year earlier, so the quarter was a spike, not a new level.[1] Management's own model puts the largest one-in-100-year net loss from a single zone at about 8.1 percent of shareholders' equity at June 30, 2026, down from 11.0 percent at December 31, 2025, with Southeast US wind the largest zone at $1,249 million after reinsurance, taxes and reinstatement premiums.[9] Management expects that exposure to shrink slightly again in 2027.[18]

All three agencies rate the main operating companies A+ (A.M. Best and S&P) or A1 (Moody's), and all three hold a negative outlook, set between January 2025 and October 2025.[6][25] The CFO said on October 29, 2026 that the outlooks reflect past reserve volatility, not balance sheet strength, and that this quarter's actions should help.[18] An analyst also said that about $2.4 billion of Everest debt is about to lose its Bermuda and S&P capital treatment. The CFO answered that S&P's notice still left Everest above its threshold and that regulators are discussing options that mostly avoid new coupons.[18] We could not retrieve the S&P notice, so this is management's account. A downgrade of the operating companies would hurt a business whose customers rely on its ratings.[6]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter takes the weakest assumption behind each estimate, builds the strongest honest case against our conclusion, names the one claim the verdict depends on, and says when we will know.

The red-team case starts with quality of earnings. The 14.0 percent nine-month operating return benefited from favorable short-tail development: the CFO said about $500 million may have been released year to date, even though most of the third-quarter favorable emergence was held back, while the casualty charges ran through the same period.[1][18] Premium earned is down 12.6 percent, the Group lost $223 million on underwriting in the quarter, and the fourth reserve hit in eight quarters came after management had called the position strong twice in 2026.[1][20][21] A rating cut, a Bermuda group supervision cost or a fourth charge would each hit a stock priced at 1.05 times book. If earnings land at $45 and the market pays 0.95 times a book of $438, the stock is worth about $416. In the bear case it is worth $322.

Flaw 1: another casualty or reserve surprise

Our base case assumes that net development stops being adverse after the third-quarter action and that book value grows on buybacks and earnings. Management says the margin over its central estimate is over $850 million and that 2026 loss picks needed no change.[18] But a margin is a cushion, not a ceiling. A Munich Re board member, quoted by The Royal Gazette from an interview with The Insurer, said last month that the market should not be surprised if it keeps reporting reserve strengthening in the coming years, because US liability loss development for underwriting years 2014 to 2019 is still rising sharply.[22] Management also said it has reserve studies to finish in the fourth quarter for the international insurance business and cyber.[18] Evidence against us would be a net adverse quarter of more than $100 million, a new charge on 2023 to 2025 accident years or a decision to buy cover for the treaty casualty book. We will know when the fourth-quarter results arrive (date not confirmed; the fourth quarter of 2025 was reported on February 4, 2026) and when the 10-K follows.[5][18]

Flaw 2: prices fall faster and the earnings base shrinks

Our base earnings of $54.00 for 2027 assume property catastrophe rates fall 10 to 15 percent at January 1 and that terms hold.[32][18] The Guy Carpenter index is already down 16 percent in 2026, the biggest annual decline since the late 1990s, and Everest itself says it may become more selective and that its net probable maximum loss will shrink.[30][18] Casualty premium is being cut by double digits, and management expects to cede roughly $200 million of premium a quarter over the next three years to the Annapurna sidecar.[1][19] If rates fall more than 15 percent and the premium base keeps shrinking, 2027 EPS could land near $45 or lower. Evidence would be broker reports of declines above 15 percent in early January 2027, and a Core gross premium decline above 15 percent with an attritional ratio above 90 percent, which would turn the premium KPI to FAIL and reject the name.[30][32]

Flaw 3: ratings, regulation and the debt capital question

All three agencies hold negative outlooks on the operating companies, and the CFO says they relate to past reserve volatility.[6][18] A downgrade from A+ would hurt a franchise that depends on cedents trusting its ability to pay. The Bermuda Monetary Authority's group supervision begins its requirements after a transition that ends in January 2027, and it could change capital rules, the cost of compliance and the terms of the group's debt.[9] An analyst raised the question that about $2.4 billion of debt is about to lose capital treatment, and the CFO's reply was that options exist that mostly avoid new coupons.[18] The $1.0 billion of FHLB borrowings also begin to expire in 2026.[6] Evidence would be an agency downgrade, an announcement of new capital rules from the BMA, or a debt refinancing at higher cost. The BMA transition date and the agencies' year-end reviews are the next dated checks.[9]
