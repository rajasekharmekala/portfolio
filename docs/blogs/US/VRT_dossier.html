1. What the company does

Vertiv designs, builds, installs and services the equipment that supplies power to a data center and removes the heat it creates. The product list covers uninterruptible power supplies (batteries that carry a site through a grid failure), switchgear, busbar, rack power distribution, air-cooled and liquid-cooled thermal systems, modular and prefabricated buildings, and software to manage it all.[1] The company traces back to 1946, was part of Emerson Electric as Emerson Network Power, was spun off in 2016 and became a listed company on February 7, 2020 through a merger with a blank-check company.[1] Customers fall into three end markets: data centers (hyperscale cloud, colocation, neocloud and enterprise), communication networks, and commercial and industrial sites.[1] Data centers are the growth engine. The other two markets grow at the pace of telecom spending and the wider economy.[1]

Who pays, and how? Vertiv sells products and services. In 2025, product sales were $8,390.6 million and services and spares were $1,839.3 million out of $10,229.9 million of net sales.[1] By region, 62% of 2025 sales were booked in the Americas, 20% in Asia Pacific and 18% in Europe, Middle East & Africa.[1] Large customers pay in stages. Deferred revenue, which is cash received before the equipment is delivered, rose from $1,814.7 million at the end of 2025 to $3,633.7 million on June 30, 2026, and management says the build comes from advance payments at order placement plus milestone payments.[1][5][7] That means cash often arrives before revenue, which flatters free cash flow while orders are growing and would reverse if they stalled.

The AI angle is simple physics. Each new generation of chips draws more power and gives off more heat, so racks need higher-voltage power and liquid cooling. Management describes a complete chain from the grid connection to the chip: a powertrain (the electrical path) and a thermal chain (the cooling path).[7] Its roadmap includes 800-volt direct-current power, which is under customer validation in 2026 with deployment planned for 2027.[7] Services matter too. The company runs over 300 service centers and about 5,000 service engineers, and it keeps buying specialist firms: PurgeRite (fluid flushing for liquid-cooled sites, bought for $1,138.3 million net of cash, closed December 4, 2025), Strategic Thermal Labs (April 2026), ThermoKey (heat-rejection equipment, June 2026), King Environmental Services (agreed September 24, 2026) and Utility Innovation Group (microgrid power controls, announced September 2, 2026).[1][16][17][14][12]

Competition comes in two kinds. Niche players include Delta Electronics, Stulz, Johnson Controls and Socomec. The large global rivals are Schneider Electric, Eaton, Legrand and Huawei.[1] For an owner, the consequence is that Vertiv's earnings depend on how many data centers get built, how fast, and how much of each build Vertiv wins. It is a capital-goods business with a growth story attached, so its results follow its customers' spending plans closely.

How an order turns into cash

A customer places an order and usually pays a deposit. Vertiv builds the equipment in its plants, ships it in phases, and recognizes revenue as it delivers. Backlog is the value of signed orders not yet delivered. At the end of 2025 it was $15.0 billion, up from $7.2 billion a year earlier, and most of it was firm and expected to ship within 12 to 18 months.[1] Customers can cut or delay orders in some cases, usually with penalties, so the backlog is a strong signal and not a guarantee.[1] The most recent backlog figure Vertiv has published is the December 2025 one, so the number the market most wants to see next is the one in the October 28 report.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Backlog: signed orders not yet delivered. Vertiv reported $15.0 billion at the end of 2025.
  • Book-to-bill: new orders divided by sales in the same period. Above 1.0 means the order pile is growing.
  • Organic growth: sales growth after removing currency moves and the sales of companies bought in the last year.
  • Adjusted operating margin: operating profit, with items such as amortization of purchased intangibles added back, divided by sales.
  • Hyperscaler: a very large cloud provider such as Microsoft, Amazon or Google that builds data centers at massive scale.
  • Colocation: a data center where the owner rents space and power to other companies. Neocloud: a newer provider that rents AI computing capacity.
  • Deferred revenue: cash a customer has paid for equipment or work not yet delivered.
  • Powertrain and thermal chain: the electrical path and the cooling path from the grid or building down to the server chip.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position is strong but not a monopoly, whatever the portfolio label says. The annual report names Schneider Electric, Eaton, Legrand and Huawei as large rivals and does not publish market share.[1] The case for a moat rests on breadth and delivery. Vertiv says it has the most complete portfolio of critical digital infrastructure offerings, and large customers now value speed of deployment, so in our view factory-built modules and a field service network are hard to copy quickly.[1][4] The service network of over 300 centers and about 5,000 engineers is part of that, because a customer who runs a Vertiv cooling loop also needs someone to flush, test and maintain it.[1] The evidence for the claim is mixed. Vertiv grew 17.8% organically in the second quarter, ahead of Eaton at 14%, in line with Schneider Electric at 17% for its third quarter, and far behind nVent at 47%.[5][28][30][29] Vertiv is growing quickly, but it is not outgrowing every peer.

The economics have improved sharply. Gross margin, the share of sales left after the direct cost of making and installing the goods, rose from 28.4% in 2022 to 36.3% in 2025 and reached 37.7% in the second quarter of 2026 against 34.0% a year earlier.[2][1][4] Adjusted operating margin was 22.6%, up 410 basis points from a year earlier, and management credits operating productivity and favorable price-cost, which means selling prices have risen faster than input costs, even after tariffs.[5] Management also says pricing should keep beating inflation in 2026 and that it expects positive price-cost including the effect of tariffs.[6][7] A margin that expands while the company is also absorbing new plants and acquisitions is evidence of pricing power, at least for now.

The weaknesses are about execution, customer power and dependence on one theme. Large cloud, neocloud and colocation customers have the purchasing power to demand better terms, and the annual report says they make up a material part of the customer mix, without naming a percentage.[1] Projects are getting bigger and more complicated. In the second quarter management said revenue slipped by what it called minor timing shifts because of multi-phase projects and temporary supply chain congestion, and it said the delay should resolve in the second half of 2026.[5][7] Inventory rose from $1,456.5 million at the end of 2025 to $2,522.7 million on June 30, 2026, which supports the second-half ramp but ties up cash if shipments slip again.[1][5] Acquired companies added 5% to second-quarter sales growth, so part of the headline growth was bought and not built.[5]

Tariffs and component costs

Tariffs raised input costs, but pricing has so far covered them. Management says it expects positive price-cost in 2026 including the current effect of tariffs and its countermeasures.[7] There is also a possible gain. In February 2026 the Supreme Court invalidated tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, and Vertiv has started filing refund claims.[4] The amount, timing and likelihood of any refund are uncertain, and accounting rules allow a gain of this kind to be booked only once it is realized or realizable, so none is in the numbers or in our estimates.[4] A refund would be a bonus. The weak point is the reverse: if the pricing power fades, the margin gains of the last two years would be the first thing to go.

Liquid cooling and power: what the mix looks like

Investors want to know how much of Vertiv is liquid cooling and how much is power. The company does not publish that split, so the figure is N/A here and we do not estimate it.[1] What is published is the split between products and services. In the second quarter, product sales rose 23.0% (19.7% organic) and services and spares rose 28.6% (10.1% organic), with fluid-management acquisitions supplying most of the gap.[5] Management said new capacity is coming online in Johor, Malaysia, five large plant expansions in the Americas and more chiller capacity in Europe, Middle East & Africa.[7] For an owner, the missing split means that the exposure to liquid cooling cannot be checked against a number. The report treats Vertiv as a diversified data-center power and cooling supplier.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of filings tell a clear story. Net sales more than doubled, from $4,998.1 million in 2021 to $10,229.9 million in 2025. From 2022 that is a three-year compound growth rate of 21.6%. The twelve months to June 2026 reached $11,479.6 million.[2][1][5] Operating profit rose from $259.9 million to $1,829.7 million. Net income and earnings per share are noisier. Vertiv carried warrants, and their fair-value swings ran through profit. There was a $90.9 million gain in 2022, then charges of $157.9 million in 2023 and $449.2 million in 2024.[2][1] So operating profit is the cleaner guide to the business. The three-year profit growth rate we report, 159% a year, starts from a tiny 2022 figure of $76.6 million. Read it as a small-number effect and not as a run rate.

Cash is the second story. Free cash flow is cash from operations less capital spending and capitalized software. It was negative in 2022, at minus $263.8 million. It then climbed to $1,887.4 million in 2025, which is 18.4% of sales. Over the last twelve months it was $2,924.0 million, or 25.5% of sales.[2][1][5] Much of the recent jump is working capital. That is the cash tied up in inventory and customer bills, net of supplier credit. Working capital released $1,113.3 million over the last twelve months. The main cause is deferred revenue. The cash flow statement shows it adding $1,822.7 million in the first half of 2026 alone, while the balance sheet figure rose $1,819.0 million, from $1,814.7 million to $3,633.7 million.[1][5] Without that release, free cash flow would be $1,810.7 million, or 15.8% of sales. That still clears our 12% floor. We use this lower, normalized figure when we ask what the share price assumes in Chapter 5.

The balance sheet moved from a burden to a strength. Net debt, which is debt minus cash and investments, was $2,930.3 million in 2022, or 5.6 times EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization). It was $1,085.1 million in 2025, or 0.51 times. On June 30, 2026 Vertiv held $3,110.6 million of cash and investments against $2,939.8 million of debt. That is a net cash position.[3][1][5] Stock-based compensation, the cost of paying staff in shares, is tiny at 0.45% of 2025 sales. The diluted share count rose 1.1% in 2025.[1]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above rests on an assumption that can fail. For 2027 earnings, the weakest assumption is that sales grow about 30% to $18.27 billion while margins keep widening. The bear case, in one paragraph. Hyperscalers and neoclouds find financing harder. They defer phases of projects that sit in Vertiv's backlog, which customers can reduce or defer, usually with penalties. The second-half ramp slips again. The $2.5 billion of inventory sits on the balance sheet. The market pays 17 times a lower $7.60 of earnings, which is $129.20.[1][5] That is 33.5% below today's price and 26.3% below the stop. It is why the stop and the pre-report cap exist.

Fatal flaw 1: the customers cannot or will not pay for the build-out

The backlog is only as good as the customers' ability to finance it. Meta's negative free cash flow, the new non-voting share class, Nvidia's remark that capital is scarce and CoreWeave's 10% drop show that the funding chain is under strain.[23][22] Vertiv's annual report warns that customers can reduce, defer or cancel firm orders, usually with penalties, and that large buyers can demand better terms.[1] If deferrals start, revenue falls faster than costs and the margin gains reverse. We would see it in orders and backlog on October 28 if Vertiv reports them, and in each large customer's next capital spending plan after that.

Fatal flaw 2: execution cannot keep up with project size

The second quarter slipped on multi-phase projects and supply chain congestion, yet the company needs second-half sales of about $8,076 million against $5,924 million in the first half, a 36% step-up, to reach the $14,000 million midpoint.[5] The third-quarter guide of $3,650 million to $3,850 million is 11% to 18% above the second quarter's $3,274.3 million.[5] Inventory rose 73% in six months to $2,522.7 million.[1][5] If shipments slip again, cash is tied up, fixed-price project costs rise and the 2026 guidance has to be cut. At 21.1 times 2027 earnings the stock is not cheap if growth slows. A fall to 18 times $9.19 is $165.42, which is 14.87% below $194.32.
