1. What the company does

Vertiv sells the equipment that keeps a data center powered and cool, and then services it for years. On the power side that means uninterruptible power supplies (batteries that carry the load when the grid blinks), switchgear and busbar (the heavy electrical gear that routes power), and rack power distribution. On the thermal side it means chillers, cooling units and liquid cooling, which move heat away from computer chips. The company says it serves three end markets: data centers (hyperscale cloud, colocation, neocloud and enterprise), communication networks, and commercial and industrial sites.[1]

Who pays? The customers are the companies that build and run data centers. A cloud giant or an AI-focused landlord orders equipment for a new building, often years in advance, and pays in stages. Customer deposits are visible on the balance sheet as deferred revenue, which rose from $1,814.7 million at 2025-12-31 to $3,633.7 million at 2026-06-30.[1][4] Management has said that the increase reflects advance deposits on orders and milestone payments on large projects.[16] Orders can be reduced or deferred in some cases, usually with penalties or other termination consequences.[1]

How does it make money? In 2025 net sales were $10,229.9 million, of which products were $8,390.6 million and services and spares were $1,839.3 million.[1] Products earned a gross margin of about 35.1% and services about 42.0% (net sales less the cost of sales in each line, divided by net sales).[1] Services matter more than their share of sales suggests: the company runs more than 300 service centers and about 5,000 service engineers, which keeps equipment it sold running and brings in repeat work.[1] Customers pay for both the equipment and for the service contract that protects it. We also track free cash flow (FCF), which is the cash left after running the business and paying for equipment and software, because it shows whether profit turns into cash.

The company has also been buying capability. In the fourth quarter of 2025 it spent about $1 billion on acquisitions, and it bought a rack and cabinet maker for about $200 million.[7][1] On 2026-10-26 it closed King Environmental Services Ltd., a Europe-based provider of fluid management, commissioning and load-testing services; terms were not disclosed and are not expected to be material.[14] On 2026-10-27 it closed Utility Innovation Group (UIG), a maker of microgrid controls and on-site power systems, for about $1.45 billion in cash plus up to $1.15 billion more if profit targets are met.[12] Both deals fall inside our price date and matter for the balance sheet, which Chapter 3 recalculates.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The evidence for a strong business starts with demand and then moves to profit. Vertiv's order backlog grew from $7.2 billion at 2024-12-31 to $15.0 billion at 2025-12-31, equal to 1.47 times 2025 net sales (15.0 divided by 10.23), and the company reported a fourth-quarter book-to-bill ratio of about 2.9 times, meaning it booked $2.90 of new orders for every $1.00 it billed.[1][7] Gross margin rose from 28.4% in 2022 to 36.3% in 2025, and operating margin — the share of revenue left after operating costs — from 3.9% to 17.9%, which shows the company can pass costs through and spread fixed costs over more sales.[2][1]

Is it a moat? A moat is a lasting advantage that keeps rivals from taking customers. Vertiv names two kinds of competitor: niche players such as Delta Electronics, Stulz, Johnson Controls and Socomec, and large global groups such as Schneider Electric, Eaton, Legrand and Huawei.[1] So this is not a monopoly. The advantages it claims are the broadest portfolio, field service coverage and speed of delivery, and the best evidence for them is that gross margin has risen while sales have doubled.[1] The rivals are doing well too: Schneider reported triple-digit data center demand growth in the third quarter, Eaton's global electrical backlog was up 103% at 2026-06-30, and nVent's organic sales grew 47% in the second quarter.[24][21][22] That tells us the whole industry is being lifted, so Vertiv's growth proves a strong market more than it proves a share gain.

What the customers are spending matters as much as what Vertiv does. Meta guides 2026 capital expenditure (cash spent on long-term plant, equipment and data centers) of $135 billion to $145 billion, and Amazon said it expects about $220 billion this year.[25][26] These budgets are the demand source. They are also the risk, because the same customers fund them with cash flow and new financing, and a few buyers account for a large share of the market. Vertiv's 10-K warns that the loss of any one customer could have an outsized effect, and it does not publish what share of sales any customer represents, so we record customer concentration as not published.[1]

Evidence that the position is weaker than the headline numbers

Execution is the clearest weak spot. In the second quarter, sales missed analyst expectations by 3.22% and the shares fell 17.26% on 2026-07-29. Management blamed minor timing shifts from multi-phase projects and temporary supply chain congestion.[29][6] Post-cutoff (2026-10-28 call): the CFO said the delayed revenue was about $100 million to $150 million and came back in the third quarter. On the same call an analyst asked about talk of compressors burning out and power systems overheating, and the CEO said the company was optimistic about what its quality data showed.[19] No filing we read settles the question either way.

Inventory and receivables are growing faster than comfort allows. Inventories rose from $1,456.5 million at 2025-12-31 to $2,522.7 million at 2026-06-30, a 73% increase in six months, and receivables rose from $3,109.0 million to $3,750.3 million.[1][4] A factory building ahead of demand looks like this when demand is real and like this when it is not, which is why orders matter. Management has not published quarterly orders or backlog since the February 2026 report: the first-quarter and second-quarter 2026 releases carry no such figures.[5][6] Outside readers therefore lose their best early-warning signal. Post-cutoff (2026-10-28 call): management said it does not plan to resume.[19]

Cyclicality, tariffs and the balance sheet history

This is a capital-goods business that sells to a spending cycle. In 2022 its operating margin was 3.9%, interest cost nearly equalled operating profit (operating profit of $223.4 million against net interest of $147.3 million, a cover of 1.5 times) and net debt was 5.6 times EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating cash profit).[2] That is the downside the company has already lived through. Since then net debt has fallen from $2,930.3 million to a net cash position at 2026-06-30 of $170.8 million, calculated in Chapter 3, and in February 2026 the company received its first investment-grade ratings (Baa3 from Moody's and BBB- from S&P).[2][4][5]

Tariffs are a cost risk the 10-K discusses: the company says it has at times failed to anticipate tariffs and duties on materials and freight quickly enough to price them in.[1] In 2026 it says pricing has been favorable and it expects positive price-cost for the year, including tariffs and countermeasures.[6][16] The number to watch is the gross margin: a fall below the 36% to 38% range of the last 18 months would be the first sign that price no longer covers cost.[1][4]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of audited numbers show a company that doubled its sales and then learned to earn money on them. Net sales rose from $4,998.1 million in 2021 to $10,229.9 million in 2025, a 19.6% yearly rate, and the three-year rate from 2022 to 2025 was 21.6%.[3][1] Operating profit rose faster, from $223.4 million in 2022 to $1,829.7 million in 2025, a 101.6% yearly rate over three years, because the operating margin (operating profit divided by sales) moved from 3.9% to 17.9%.[2][1] That is the central fact of the business: growth plus a rising margin.

Net income needs care. GAAP net income rose from $76.6 million in 2022 to $1,332.8 million in 2025, a 159.1% yearly rate, but the series is distorted by the accounting for warrants (rights to buy shares) that were marked to market: a gain of $90.9 million in 2022, then charges of $157.9 million in 2023 and $449.2 million in 2024.[2][1] We therefore lean on operating profit and cash flow, which those entries do not touch, and show GAAP net income only for completeness.

Cash flow improved even more sharply. Adjusted free cash flow, which is cash from operations less capital spending and capitalized software, went from a loss of $263.8 million in 2022 to $1,887.4 million in 2025 and $2,924.0 million for the twelve months to 2026-06-30.[2][1][4] A large part of the recent figure is working capital, meaning cash tied up in inventory, bills customers owe and bills Vertiv owes: working capital released $1,113.3 million over the twelve months, mostly through customer deposits that sit in deferred revenue.[1][4] Deposits are real cash, but the company owes the product in return, so we also show a figure with that release removed.

The balance sheet changed from a burden to an asset. Net debt, which is debt minus cash and short-term investments, was $2,930.3 million at the end of 2022, 5.6 times EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), and operating profit covered net interest only 1.5 times.[2] At 2026-06-30 the company held $3,110.6 million of cash and short-term investments against $2,939.8 million of debt, a net cash position of $170.8 million.[4] Two events on 2026-10-26 and 2026-10-27 change that: the UIG purchase takes $1.45 billion of cash and carries up to $1.15 billion more, so the net debt after the deal is recalculated in the Layer 1 table below.[12]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions, and the honest question is which one is weakest. Here the answer is the same for almost all of them: the numbers describe a company whose orders and costs we can no longer see directly, so we lean on sales, deposits and margins that arrive late. Below are three ways the thesis could fail, a matrix of the weakest assumptions with the evidence and date that would settle each, and the single claim that everything hangs on.[5][6]

One date matters for all of them. Vertiv has not announced a date for its next earnings report after the 2026-10-28 results; the fourth-quarter 2025 report came out on 2026-02-11, so a February 2027 report is likely but not confirmed, and we record the date as not published.[7][17] The quarterly filing for the period ended 2026-09-30 was not yet in the filing index when we checked, and it will show backlog-related lines such as deferred revenue.[20]

Red-team case 1: orders turn before sales do

The bear story is simple. Customers fund data centers with cash flow and new financing, and in October the sector fell on inflation and financing fears.[29] If a few large customers delay projects, Vertiv would see it first in new orders, which it does not publish, and only later in sales. Deposits then stop growing and the working capital inflow reverses, so the $1,113.3 million that flattered trailing cash flow becomes a drag.[4] Orders can be reduced or deferred, usually with penalties, which softens but does not remove the risk.[1]

The evidence that would show this is wrong in our favor is deferred revenue holding above $3,633.7 million at the next filing and customers' capital spending guides staying where they are (Meta $135 billion to $145 billion, Amazon about $220 billion).[4][25][26] The evidence that would confirm the bear case is a cut in those budgets or a fall in deferred revenue. Post-cutoff, the company reported deferred revenue of $4,120.5 million at 2026-09-30, which points the right way.[18]

Red-team case 2: delivery and product quality

Vertiv is shipping more complex, factory-built systems at a record pace. In the second quarter that produced revenue timing shifts that management attributed to multi-phase projects and supply chain congestion.[6] Post-cutoff (2026-10-28 call): management put the delay at about $100 million to $150 million, an analyst asked about compressors and power systems failing in the field, and the CEO said the company was optimistic about what its quality data showed. No filing we read decides the question.[19] The company also named a new head of manufacturing on 2026-10-05, a change it did not link to margins.[29] If quality problems brought warranty costs or lost orders, gross margin would fall below the 36% to 38% range of recent periods and the adjusted operating margin would miss its 23.3% to 24.3% forecast range.[1][4][6]

What would show this wrong is another quarter of sales inside or above the company's own range, with stable margin. What would confirm it is a margin below forecast or a rise in warranty reserves in the next filing.

Red-team case 3: the multiple falls even if earnings are delivered

The 10-year Treasury yield was 5.00% on 2026-10-26, and the stock's multiple has already fallen from about 49 times to about 26 times next-twelve-month earnings.[30][29] If earnings of $9.19 arrive but the market pays 17 times, the price would be $156.23, 25% below the close. Acquisitions add a second strain: UIG cost about $1.45 billion, or about 13 times its expected 2027 EBITDA, with up to $1.15 billion more owed if it hits profit targets, and the company called the deal accretive within a year.[12][13] Integration problems would reduce the return on that cash.

What would show this wrong is a stable or rising multiple while rates stay high. The signal is the price itself, which is why the plan uses a stop and a limit price instead of a forecast.
