1. What the company does

Vertiv designs, builds, installs and services the equipment that supplies electricity to a data center and removes the heat the computers give off. Think of a data center as a very large, very hot computer room: the power side keeps the electricity clean and uninterrupted, and the thermal side moves heat out so the chips do not throw errors or burn. The company traces its roots to 1946, was spun out of Emerson in 2016, and has traded on the New York Stock Exchange since February 7, 2020.[1] In 2025 it booked net sales of $10,229.9 million, of which 62% came from the Americas, 20% from Asia Pacific and 18% from Europe, the Middle East and Africa (our shares of $6,386.3, $2,019.2 and $1,824.4 million).[1] Products were $8,207.0 million (80%) and services and spares $2,022.9 million (20%).[1]

Who pays is the part the filings only half answer. Vertiv sells to three end markets: data centers (hyperscale cloud, colocation, neocloud and enterprise), communication networks, and commercial and industrial sites.[1] The AI build-out sits mainly in the first group. The company does not publish what share of sales comes from hyperscalers, and it names no customer above a stated percentage of sales, so concentration cannot be measured from the filings. We record that as not published rather than guess.[1][15] What can be seen is where the growth landed: Americas sales rose from $4,500.6 million in 2024 to $6,386.3 million in 2025 (up 41.9%), and Americas organic growth, which strips out currency and acquisitions, was 21.1% in the second quarter of 2026 and 31.0% for the first half.[1][6]

Why liquid cooling matters is simple physics. Chips built for artificial intelligence draw far more power per rack than older servers and turn almost all of it into heat, so air alone stops being enough. Vertiv describes the response as hybrid air and liquid cooling across the whole thermal chain, from the chip to the point where heat leaves the building.[1][4] It bought PurgeRite, which flushes and filters the fluid in liquid-cooled systems, for about $1.0 billion in December 2025, and on September 24, 2026 it agreed to buy King Environmental Services, a European fluid and load-testing service business, with closing expected in the fourth quarter of 2026.[12][11] Both deals add field services around the equipment, which is a steadier, higher-margin revenue stream than one-off hardware sales.

How the money is made depends on project timing. Large projects are built over many months, so $2,188.1 million of 2025 revenue was recognised over time and $8,041.8 million at a point in time.[1] Customers pay deposits and milestone payments along the way: deferred revenue (cash received before the work is delivered) doubled from $1,814.7 million at December 31, 2025 to $3,633.7 million at June 30, 2026.[4] Competitors split into two groups: niche specialists such as Delta Electronics, Stulz and Socomec, and large global groups such as Schneider Electric, Eaton, Legrand and Huawei.[1] Vertiv spent $441.7 million on engineering and development in 2025, which is 4.3% of sales (our division of $441.7 by $10,229.9).[1]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The business case rests on three things that the filings show. The first is breadth: Vertiv sells both power and cooling, and says its edge is the most complete portfolio, a global service network of more than 300 service centers and about 5,000 service engineers, and early design work with chip makers.[1][8] The second is pricing and margin. Gross profit rose from 28.4% of sales in 2022 to 38.0% over the twelve months to June 30, 2026 (our calculation from the filed statements), and the adjusted operating margin was 22.6% in the second quarter, up 410 basis points from 18.5% a year earlier.[2][4][6] Management credits operating execution and favorable price-cost, which means selling prices rose faster than input and tariff costs, even after tariffs and the countermeasures it took.[6][15] The third is scale: net sales grew 27.7% in 2025 and 26.7% in the first half of 2026.[1][4]

Demand evidence is strong but has gone quiet. Backlog, the signed orders still to be delivered, reached $15.0 billion at December 31, 2025, up from $7.2 billion a year earlier, and the fourth-quarter orders were up about 252% with a book-to-bill ratio (orders divided by sales) of about 2.9 times.[1][8] That backlog equals 1.07 times the midpoint of 2026 sales guidance of $14.0 billion, and most of it is expected to ship within 12 to 18 months.[1][6] The difficulty is that the first and second quarter 2026 releases carry no orders or backlog figure at all (we searched both full texts), so the last measured reading dates from December 31, 2025.[6][7] Management says the pipeline is accelerating and gives visibility into 2027, but a claim is not a number, and a hardware company that stops publishing orders at the top of a cycle asks investors to trust guidance.[15][16]

The weak points are timing, the second-half ramp and the balance sheet. Second-quarter sales of $3,274.3 million landed inside the guided $3,250 to $3,450 million range but near its bottom and below the $3.38 billion analysts expected, and organic growth was 17.8% against a guided 20% to 24%.[4][7][21] Management blamed minor timing shifts, temporary supply chain congestion and multi-phase projects.[4][15] The full-year guide now needs second-half sales of about $8,076 million (14,000 less 5,923.8), which is 45.4% above the second half of 2025, with the third quarter guided to 40% growth and the fourth implied at about 50%.[4][6][25] Inventories rose 73% in six months to $2,522.7 million and receivables 21% to $3,750.3 million, while deferred revenue doubled, so customer deposits are financing the build.[4] If orders slow, that cash cycle runs in reverse.

Three further points temper the picture. Goodwill of $2,283.3 million and other intangibles of $1,800.8 million together equal 86% of equity of $4,757.6 million (our sum), so past acquisitions carry real weight, and H1 2026 included $62.0 million of contingent consideration charges.[4] Europe, the Middle East and Africa sales fell 8.4% in the first half and organic sales fell 14.8%.[6] Finally, the shares are volatile: the beta (a measure of how much the shares move against the market) is 2.05 on the vendor's five-year basis and 2.79 on the one-year basis in the holdings record, and the stock fell about 15% in the last week alone, with analysts citing yields, valuation and a Sell initiation at $188.[18][22][24][29][30] What this means for an owner: the company is strong, but proof of the second-half ramp now arrives with the October 28 results, not before.[23]

5. What could happen next

Chapter 5 asks what growth the current price already assumes, how analysts are moving, where we differ, and what a year could look like. The method for the first question is a reverse discounted cash flow: instead of estimating a fair price from growth, we solve for the growth that makes ten years of free cash flow, discounted at 10% and capped by an 18 times free cash flow multiple in year ten, worth today's equity value. The equity value is 384,988,173 shares times $206.42, or $79.47 billion.[4][29][31]

On reported twelve-month free cash flow of $2,929.8 million the answer is 9.58% a year. The arithmetic: free cash flow reaches $7,316 million in year ten at that growth rate. The ten yearly cash flows discounted at 10% add to $28,695 million. The year-ten terminal value of 18 times $7,316 million is $131,695 million, which discounted back ten years is $50,774 million. Together they equal $79,469 million. Without the working-capital inflow the base is $1,816.5 million and the implied growth is 15.88% a year. On the 2026 guided adjusted free cash flow of $2,500 million it is 11.66% a year.[1][4][6]

Two cautions apply. First, 64% of the value in the first case sits in the terminal multiple, so the answer is sensitive to the 18 times assumption. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter names the three ways the report could be badly wrong, the evidence that would show it, and the date we would know. The red-team view is that the quality of the business is real but the stock is a bet on a back-end-loaded year, a multiple that rates can crush, and a disclosure gap that hides the first sign of trouble.

Fatal flaw 1: the second-half ramp does not arrive

The 2026 guide of $13,800 to $14,200 million needs second-half sales of about $8,076 million, 45.4% above the second half of 2025, with the third quarter guided at $3,650 to $3,850 million (organic growth of 34% to 36%).[4][6] The first half grew 26.7% and the second quarter fell short of the guided organic range. If supply congestion, project timing or customer readiness delays shipments again, sales slip into 2027 and the 2026 EPS guide of $6.65 to $6.75 is at risk. The weakest assumption is that capacity added in Malaysia, five large Americas plants and European chillers is ready in time.[15] We would know on October 28, 2026, when the third quarter is reported.[23]

Fatal flaw 2: deposits reverse and spending pauses

Deferred revenue doubled to $3,633.7 million and inventories rose 73% to $2,522.7 million, which means customers have prefunded a build that Vertiv has partly paid for in advance.[4] If a hyperscale customer (the buyers of the largest data centers) delays a site, deposits stop arriving, deferred revenue unwinds and free cash flow can fall below the 12% margin floor (our bear case is 7.9%). We cannot size this risk because Vertiv publishes neither the share of sales from the largest customers nor orders and backlog after 2025.[1][7] The signal would be a fall in deferred revenue or a cut to the $2,400 to $2,600 million cash guide in the third-quarter 10-Q filed with the results.[6]

Fatal flaw 3: the multiple compresses faster than earnings grow

The base case needs a 32.39 times forward multiple on 2027 EPS, while the stock is at 22.5 times that EPS today after the 10-year Treasury yield jumped to 5.14% in late September and analysts cut targets.[19][24][22] Higher yields lower the present value of distant cash flows, and the reverse DCF shows 64% of today's value is terminal value. A target price needs earnings and a multiple, and only one of the two is in the company's control. The weakest assumption is that the 3-year median multiple (three year-end points, vendor data) is a fair anchor for a year of high rates.[19] We would know by watching the forward multiple on the weekly close. A reading under 22 times with unchanged EPS would say the market is repricing, not doubting.
