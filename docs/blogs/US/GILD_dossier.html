Chapter 1

Dr. Michael Riordan founded Gilead Sciences in June 1987 in Foster City, California [1]. At age 29, the doctor-turned-venture-investor licensed antisense chemistry to block viral RNA and DNA growth [1]. When Gilead went public in January 1992, it had no revenue and burned cash on nucleotide research. Its first real breakthrough came not from gene silencing, but from small-molecule antiviral drugs. These molecules mimic the building blocks of viral DNA and halt replication. That chemistry led to Vistide for CMV retinitis in 1996 and Tamiflu (with Hoffmann-La Roche) for flu in 1999. Most importantly, it produced Viread (TDF) in 2001. In 2003, Gilead bought Triangle Pharmaceuticals for $464 million to pair TDF with emtricitabine (FTC), launching Truvada in 2004 and Atripla in 2006 [1].

That single-tablet regimen (STR) combined three active HIV drugs into one daily pill. It turned HIV from a fatal illness requiring handfuls of toxic pills into a chronic disease. Patients took their medicine far more reliably when one pill replaced complex multi-drug schedules. It also built Gilead's core economic engine: high virology share and ~80%–86% product gross margins. Selling costs stayed low because HIV clinics and Ryan White / 340B buyers are concentrated [1]. Later, Gilead upgraded its core backbone from TDF to tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) and added bictegravir to launch Biktarvy in 2018. Over the past 15 years, Gilead's history has followed three capital cycles. Each cycle shows both strong virology science and uneven results in outside M&A.

Gilead's $11B Pharmasset bet remains one of biopharma's greatest deals, whereas its 2017–2020 oncology spree ($37.8B across Kite, Forty Seven, and Immunomedics) produced severe capital-efficiency drag; post-2024 bolt-ons (CymaBay, Arcellx) reflect tighter clinical derisking.

The first cycle was the Hepatitis C (HCV) boom from 2014 to 2016. In November 2011, Gilead paid $11.0 billion for Pharmasset, a deal many critics called reckless. That bet secured sofosbuvir, the core drug behind Sovaldi (2013), Harvoni (2014), and Epclusa (2016). Cure rates jumped above 95% in 8 to 12 weeks without harsh interferon shots. Revenue surged from $11.2 billion in 2013 to $32.6 billion in 2015, and operating cash flow topped $17 billion in 2016. Then the cure paradox hit. As Gilead cured millions of patients and AbbVie launched Mavyret, HCV sales fell fast. Total revenue dropped from $30.39 billion in 2016 to $22.13 billion in 2018 [1].

The second cycle was the 2017–2021 diversification push and COVID-19 windfall. Seeking a second growth leg beyond HIV and shrinking HCV, Gilead bought Kite Pharma for $11.9 billion in 2017 [13]. In March 2019, it hired Roche veteran Daniel O'Day as Chairman and CEO. O'Day set a bold goal to make cancer drugs one-third of Gilead's revenue by 2030. To speed up that shift, Gilead spent $25.9 billion in 2020 alone. It paid $4.9 billion for Forty Seven (magrolimab) and $21.0 billion for Immunomedics (Trodelvy) [12]. At the same time, its antiviral Veklury (remdesivir) became a standard hospital COVID-19 drug, hitting $5.6 billion in 2021 sales and masking weak core growth. Later, Veklury faded to $1.8 billion in 2024, $911 million in 2025 [3], and ~$300 million projected in 2026 [4]. That drop exposed Gilead's cancer M&A missteps. Magrolimab failed in MDS/AML trials and was dropped, while Trodelvy's lung and bladder setbacks forced a $2.4 billion write-down in 2024 [19].

The third cycle, from 2024 to 2026, marks a return to Gilead's core strength in long-acting virology, paired with sharper late-stage deals. In HIV, the capsid blocker lenacapavir achieved 100% efficacy in cisgender women (PURPOSE-1) [6]. It also showed ≥99.9% protection in men and gender-diverse groups (PURPOSE-2) [7]. The FDA approved it as twice-yearly PrEP (Yeztugo) on June 18, 2025 [5]. Meanwhile, Gilead settled patent suits to push U.S. Biktarvy generics from December 2033 to April 1, 2036 [14]. It will also absorb a 38% Medicare Maximum Fair Price cut under the IRA starting January 1, 2028 [15]. In liver and cancer care, the $4.3 billion CymaBay deal brought in Livdelzi [11]. Trodelvy won key first-line mTNBC approvals in June 2026 [8], and Gilead paid $7.8 billion in April 2026 for Arcellx to own anito-cel [9], [10].

Cycle Lesson: Virological Craftsmanship vs. M&A Hubris

Over nearly four decades, Gilead's internal chemistry—tenofovir, bictegravir, sofosbuvir, and lenacapavir—has built lasting market leaders. By contrast, buying early cancer assets at peak prices without Phase 3 survival proof (Forty Seven, Immunomedics) led to heavy GAAP write-downs. Separating the steady virology cash engine from the M&A record is the core task of this audit.

To ground these numbers in real care, each of the five segments follows one patient from clinic visit to manufacturing and payment.

1. HIV Treatment & Prevention (Biktarvy & Yeztugo): Picture a 34-year-old newly diagnosed HIV-1 patient in Atlanta visiting a 340B clinic. The doctor prescribes Biktarvy (bictegravir 50 mg / emtricitabine 200 mg / TAF 25 mg). It is a single small pill made at Gilead's chemical plants and shipped via wholesale and specialty pharmacies. Biktarvy pairs an unboosted integrase blocker with a high resistance barrier and a bone-safe TAF backbone. Patients need no genetic screen or viral-load wait and can start the same day. That ease of use helped Biktarvy reach $14.3 billion in FY2025 sales and $4.1 billion in Q3 2026 alone [3], [4]. Payment flows through commercial plans (with copay cards), Medicaid, Ryan White ADAP, or Medicare Part D. Gilead absorbs statutory rebates, 340B discounts, and Part D catastrophic costs [1]. For prevention, picture a 26-year-old at high HIV risk who struggles with daily pills or stigma. Under CDC guidelines [16], the clinic gives Yeztugo (lenacapavir). The patient takes two starter pills on Days 1–2 and gets two small abdominal shots in the clinic, then returns every 26 weeks [5]. Yeztugo hit $305 million in Q3 2026 sales, pushing Gilead's total PrEP book to a $4 billion annual pace [4]. Priced near $28,000 a year in the U.S., it is also licensed royalty-free to generic makers like Dr. Reddy's at ~$40/year in 120 lower-income countries [17]. Yeztugo removes 363 days of daily pills each year [6], [7].

2. Oncology — Antibody-Drug Conjugates (Trodelvy): Picture a 52-year-old woman in Chicago with newly metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (mTNBC). This fast-growing tumor lacks estrogen, progesterone, and HER2 receptors. Before June 2026, her doctor had to start with generic chemo and wait for progression before using Trodelvy. After the June 24, 2026 FDA approvals from ASCENT-03 and ASCENT-04 [8], the doctor checks PD-L1 status first. If CPS ≥ 10, she gets Trodelvy + pembrolizumab (Keytruda); if PD-L1-negative, she gets Trodelvy alone. Trodelvy links a Trop-2 antibody to SN-38, an active chemo payload, delivering it directly to tumor cells and nearby tissue. It ships as a vial and is infused by IV on Days 1 and 8 of a 21-day cycle in an outpatient clinic. Clinics bill it under the medical benefit via Medicare Part B or commercial plans while managing side effects like low white blood cells and diarrhea. First-line use lifted Trodelvy sales 42% YoY to $506 million in Q3 2026 [4].

3. Oncology — Autologous Cell Therapy (Kite: Yescarta, Tecartus & Anito-cel): Unlike pills or off-the-shelf vials, Kite's CAR-T drugs are custom living therapies made for one patient at a time. Picture a 61-year-old patient with relapsed large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL) at MD Anderson Cancer Center. First, the patient undergoes leukapheresis, a blood draw that collects T-cells. Couriers ship those frozen cells under strict tracking to a Kite plant in El Segundo, Frederick, or Hoofddorp. Over 14 to 17 days, Kite uses a viral vector to add a CD19 receptor, grows millions of cells, runs safety tests, and ships the bag back. After mild chemo, the patient receives one Yescarta infusion and is watched for cytokine release syndrome (CRS). Hospitals bill via inpatient DRG payments, Medicare add-on payments, or commercial case rates above $400,000 per dose. Through the April 2026 Arcellx deal [9], Kite is applying this same plant network to anito-cel for multiple myeloma ahead of a December 23, 2026 FDA PDUFA date [10].

4. Liver Disease (Livdelzi, Epclusa, Vemlidy, Hepcludex): Picture a 55-year-old woman in Boston with Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC), an autoimmune liver disease. Generic UDCA has failed to control her liver enzymes or her severe itching. Her specialist prescribes Livdelzi (seladelpar), a once-daily oral pill gained in the $4.3 billion CymaBay deal [11]. Unlike older second-line drugs that often worsened itching, Livdelzi lowers alkaline phosphatase (ALP) while easing itch. That edge drove Q3 2026 sales up 93% YoY to $203 million [4]. Specialty pharmacies ship Livdelzi under Medicare Part D and commercial plans, alongside Gilead's mature HCV cures (Epclusa/Harvoni), chronic HBV drug Vemlidy, and HDV drug Hepcludex.

5. Veklury (Remdesivir — Acute Antiviral): Finally, picture a 76-year-old immune-weakened patient admitted to a local hospital with COVID-19 pneumonia and low oxygen. The hospital pharmacy stocks Veklury as an IV drug given over 3 to 5 days under bundled inpatient DRG pay. As hospital COVID-19 admissions fell, Veklury shrank from a $5.6 billion peak to $911 million in FY2025 [3] and ~$300 million guided for FY2026 [4]. Today it acts as a small seasonal buffer rather than a core growth driver.

Geographic Mix, Trade Tariffs & FX Exposure: Gilead's revenue base remains heavily U.S.-centric. In Q3 2026, U.S. product sales totaled $6,361 million (76.7% of $8,289 million total product sales), while Europe contributed $1,058 million (12.8%) and Rest of World contributed $869 million (10.5%) [4]. Because Gilead manufactures key active ingredients and biologics across U.S. and European (Ireland/Netherlands) sites, its Q3 2026 Form 10-Q explicitly flags changes in U.S. trade policies, including tariffs, and new U.S. tax legislation alongside foreign-currency translation swings as material operational risks. Meanwhile, Q3 2026 also brought FDA approval of Bixlenvo (bictegravir 75 mg / lenacapavir 50 mg) for virologically suppressed HIV patients and a definitive agreement to acquire PepStar Therapeutics (oral IL-23R inhibitor HCXT-2001) in inflammation [4].

Chapter 3

Gilead's distribution and gross-to-net (GTN) model splits into two distinct systems. One is high-volume pill and biologic shipping (HIV, Liver, Trodelvy, Veklury). The other is custom cell-therapy logistics (Kite). In the U.S., Gilead ships most small-molecule and biologic drugs through three big wholesalers (McKesson, Cencora, and Cardinal Health) and specialty pharmacies [1]. List prices for Biktarvy (~$4,200/month WAC) and Yeztugo (~$28,000/year WAC) face steep discounts before cash reaches Foster City. As a result, unit profits depend on Gilead's gross-to-net bridge. That bridge includes commercial PBM rebates, Medicaid rebates, 340B discounts, and the IRA Medicare Part D redesign [1].

Two channel forces shape Gilead's HIV profits from 2025 to 2028. First is 340B and government mix. U.S. HIV care relies heavily on Ryan White clinics and 340B hospitals, which receive statutory discounts of at least 23.1% plus inflation rebates. Second is the Medicare Part D redesign that began in January 2025. That law shifted catastrophic-phase costs onto drugmakers, requiring a 20% discount in that tier. Gilead reported a $1.1 billion hit to FY2025 HIV sales from Part D redesign. Even so, strong prescription volume lifted total HIV sales 6% to $20.8 billion [3], [4]. Next, CMS picked Biktarvy in January 2026 for IPAY 2028 Medicare price talks. The agency set a 38% Maximum Fair Price (MFP) cut to $2,600 per 30-day supply starting January 1, 2028. That cut creates an estimated $1.5 billion annual sales headwind that Yeztugo and cancer drugs must offset [15].

By contrast, Kite's cell-therapy supply chain is an engineering-heavy, patient-by-patient network. Kite runs GMP plants in El Segundo (California), Frederick (Maryland), and Hoofddorp (Netherlands), plus a viral-vector site in Oceanside. It keeps >96% batch success rates and 14–17 day vein-to-vein turnaround across hundreds of treatment centers. That reliable turnaround helps hospitals avoid long waitlists and failed patient batches. Small-molecule pills earn >88% gross margins. Custom CAR-T therapies earn lower margins (~65%–70% before amortization) due to single-batch labor, viral vectors, release testing, and cold-chain couriers. Across the whole company, small-molecule virology still drives the mix. That mix produced a 78.8% GAAP gross margin in FY2025 ($23,209 million gross profit on $29,443 million revenue) and over 86% non-GAAP product gross margin [1], [3].
