Before you read

The Allstate Corporation (NYSE: ALL) sells car, home and other personal insurance in the United States, and its shares closed at $250.20 on October 28, 2026.[34] Over twelve months it earned an adjusted 44.2 percent on shareholders' money, far above our 12 percent floor, and it reports results on November 4.[6][5] We do not recommend buying at $250.20: our $281 central target pays only 0.85 dollars for each dollar at risk to a $214.03 exit price.[36] We would wait for $228 to $234, where the pay-off is 2.35 to 1, and only after the November 4 results confirm the target. The idea is wrong if the underlying combined ratio runs above about 85 for several quarters, or if heavier storms and fewer reserve releases arrive together[6][2].

1. What the company does

An insurer sells a promise. Customers pay a premium up front, and the company pays claims later if something goes wrong. Allstate does this for roughly 38.9 million Property-Liability policies, mostly cars and homes, and the total policy count across all its businesses is 215.9 million, which includes protection plans sold through retailers.[6] Think of it as three engines running at once. The first is underwriting: premiums collected minus claims and costs. The second is investing: Allstate holds the premiums until claims are paid, and that pool of money, called the float, was an $87.8 billion portfolio at June 30, 2026 that earned $1.0 billion in the quarter.[6] The third is a set of fee-and-warranty businesses called Protection Services, which earned $53 million in the quarter on $935 million of revenue.[6]

The measure that tells you whether underwriting is making money is the combined ratio. It adds claims and all running costs and divides by premiums earned. Below 100 percent the insurer makes an underwriting profit; above 100 it loses money before investment income. Allstate's Property-Liability combined ratio was 86.6 percent in the second quarter of 2026 and 84.3 percent for the half-year, which means about 15.7 cents of every premium dollar was left over before tax and investment income.[6] Two adjustments make the number easier to read. The underlying combined ratio strips out catastrophe losses (storms, hail, wildfire, hurricanes) and changes in reserves for old claims. It was 79.4 percent in the quarter. The gap between the two, about seven points, is what weather and reserve releases did that quarter.[6]

Car insurance is the biggest line. In the second quarter Allstate wrote $9.57 billion of auto premiums, $4.75 billion of homeowners premiums, $886 million of specialty lines such as motorcycles and boats, and small amounts of commercial and brokered business, for $15.43 billion in all.[7] The Property-Liability businesses (called Allstate Protection in the filings) are 93.9 percent of consolidated insurance premiums.[12] They are sold through three doors: more than 27,400 exclusive Allstate agents, about 58,700 independent-agent locations that sell under the National General and Allstate brands, and a direct channel of about 2,200 sales staff plus online sales.[12] Customers can buy the same product three ways, which is why Allstate describes its strategy as Transformative Growth: lower prices, simpler products and more places to buy.[12]

Allstate also runs a capital machine on top of the insurance. Equity was $33.7 billion at June 30, 2026, against debt of $7.5 billion, so debt was 18.2 percent of total capital, down from 26.8 percent fifteen months earlier.[6][7] In the second quarter it returned $1.3 billion to shareholders, $1.0 billion of it through share repurchases, and over the last year it returned $3.5 billion, which it states was 6.7 percent of its market value.[6] That cash return, and not fast-growing premium volume, is what most of the shareholder case rests on.

Why the profit line swings so much

Insurance profit is lumpy because claims are not smooth. A hailstorm in Texas or a wildfire in California can cost hundreds of millions in a week. Allstate defines a catastrophe as an event that causes more than $1 million of losses and affects several policyholders, and it reported $4.96 billion of catastrophe losses in 2025, $4.96 billion in 2024 and $5.64 billion in 2023.[12] Over the last ten years the average catastrophe cost has been 8.8 points of the loss ratio, with a range of 7.1 to 11.6 points.[12] Third-quarter 2026 losses of $1.65 billion are about 11 points of the quarter's premiums, above that average.[2][6]

Reserves add a second source of swing. Allstate sets aside money for claims it expects to pay on policies already written. When the estimates prove too high, it releases the extra into profit; when too low, it takes a charge. In 2025 releases were $1.81 billion, which was 14.1 percent of that year's net income after tax. In the first half of 2026 they were $1.65 billion, about 23 percent of first-half net income after tax on the same basis (our calculation at the 21 percent rate Allstate uses for such items).[12][8] The 10-Q puts the half-year release at 5.6 points of Allstate Protection premiums, and the earnings release at 5.8 points of Property-Liability premiums. These are the same release on two premium bases.[8][6] In 2023 the movement went the other way, an adverse $550 million.[12] A buyer has to ask how much of today's profit is repeatable and how much is a one-time release.

Finally, accounting adds noise. Net income includes gains and losses on investments, such as $1.1 billion of net investment gains in the second quarter, mostly from stock valuations, against a $144 million loss a year earlier.[6] Allstate therefore also reports adjusted net income, which excludes those gains, pension remeasurement and the amortization of purchased intangibles. Adjusted net income was $2.33 billion, or $8.99 per diluted share, in the second quarter.[6] We use the adjusted figure for earnings power and the book value per share for the capital foundation, and we say which is which.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio: claims plus all costs divided by premiums earned. 100 percent is break-even before investment income.
  • Underlying combined ratio: the combined ratio without catastrophes and old-claim reserve changes.
  • Policies in force: the count of active insurance items, so a family with two cars counts two.
  • Float: premiums held until claims are paid, invested in the meantime.
  • Book value per share: shareholders' equity divided by shares; for an insurer it is the closest figure to what the business owns.
  • Adjusted net income return on equity: twelve-month adjusted net income divided by average equity before unrealized gains and losses on bonds.
  • Earnings per share (EPS): profit divided by the number of shares; Allstate reports it on a diluted basis, counting stock awards.
  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a profit measure that ignores financing and accounting charges. It suits industrial companies, not insurers.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment. It is not a clean measure for an insurer, because premiums arrive before claims are paid.
  • net debt is debt minus cash. price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share.
  • dilution is the rise in share count when new shares are issued. A buyback is the reverse: the company buys its own shares and the count shrinks.
  • The RSI, or relative strength index, is a 0-to-100 meter of how fast a price has moved. Above 70 is called overbought and below 30 oversold. The ATR, or average true range, is the typical daily price swing in dollars.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • A base is a sideways stretch in which a stock builds a floor. The pivot is the top of that base. A breakout is a move above the pivot on strong volume.
  • The entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy, using a limit order, which fills only at the stated price or better.
  • Reverse DCF: a valuation run backwards. Instead of forecasting profit to find a fair price, it starts from today's price and solves for the yearly profit growth, over ten years, that the price needs. A negative answer means the price already assumes profit will shrink.
  • Discount rate: the yearly return a buyer demands, which is why money received later is worth less today; we use 10 percent. Exit (terminal) multiple: the price-to-earnings ratio assumed for the business at the end of year ten; we use 18 times, and "12x exit" in a table cell means the same calculation with 12 times.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Allstate's competitive position rests on three things we can check in the filings: scale in distribution, pricing skill, and a balance sheet that rating agencies still trust. On distribution, the company reaches customers through exclusive agents, independent agents and direct sales, with one national product set sold in all three.[12] In the second quarter new auto applications were 2.35 million, up 8.8 percent from 2.16 million a year earlier, and the supplement shows all three channels (exclusive agency, independent agency and direct) grew.[7][6] That growth is evidence that the wider set of doors works. It is also a sign that Allstate is paying for volume: advertising expense was $524 million in the second quarter against $442 million a year earlier, up 19 percent, and the expense ratio rose 1.0 point.[8]

On pricing skill, the best evidence is the margin. The Property-Liability underlying combined ratio fell from 91.2 percent in 2023 to 84.6 in 2024 and 79.4 in 2025, and was 79.8 in the first half of 2026.[17][6] Much of that came from price increases earned through the book, so it shows pricing worked after the 2022-2023 losses, when the combined ratio was 106.6 and 104.5 percent.[15] A sharper test is what happens when prices stop rising. Allstate's average earned auto premium was $1,486 in the second quarter against $1,510 a year earlier, about 1.6 percent lower, while the average underlying loss per policy fell 3.5 percent.[7] Auto margins are still widening because costs are falling faster than prices, but the gap is narrowing. Implemented auto rate changes were effectively zero (shown as a dash) in both the first and second quarters of 2026.[7] The company-wide underlying ratio is flat: 79.4 percent in the second quarter against 79.5 a year earlier.[6]

On balance-sheet strength, the Allstate Insurance Company holds financial-strength ratings of Aa3 from Moody's and A+ from both S&P and A.M. Best, and the 10-Q states that none changed in the first half of 2026.[12][8] The holding company had $9.45 billion of deployable assets at June 30, and the insurance subsidiaries could pay up to $7.98 billion of ordinary dividends through February 2027, of which $4.00 billion was already paid in the first six months.[8] Estimated statutory surplus at year-end 2025 was $23.0 billion.[10] Moat language needs care. A moat in insurance is not a patent; it is cost and data. Allstate's cost position shows in a 21.5 percent half-year expense ratio, but the industry is still competing on price, so we call the moat narrow and cycle-dependent.[8]

The weak side is just as clear. The industry has moved from a hard market, where prices rose fast, to a softer one. Boston Consulting Group says premium rates are softening, particularly in personal auto, and that personal auto combined ratios improved to 92 percent in 2025 from 112 percent in 2022.[30] A summary of S&P Global data puts the industry combined ratio for the first quarter of 2026 at 91.9, the best first quarter since 2006, and says S&P projects industry combined ratios will deteriorate past break-even by 2028.[33] Argus noted in August that Allstate premium growth had slowed to low single digits, that rivals were cutting prices, and that some Allstate policy prices were lower in the second quarter.[31] Hartford's personal auto earned premium fell 8 percent in the third quarter, which it attributes to continued pressure on new business in a competitive market.[23] These are the conditions in which top-of-cycle margins fade.

Is Allstate gaining or losing ground against Progressive?

Progressive is the fast-growing rival. Its policies in force were 40.49 million at September 30, 2026, up 6 percent, its quarterly net premiums written were up 4 percent, and its third-quarter combined ratio was 88.7 percent.[20] Allstate's Property-Liability policies were up 2.6 percent and written premiums up 2.6 percent in the second quarter, with auto written premiums up only 0.4 percent.[6] Total policies and total premiums therefore grew at the same pace. In auto the two diverge: auto policies were up 2.8 percent while auto written premiums were up only 0.4 percent, which means Allstate is cutting the average auto price to win customers, while Progressive is adding policies faster. Allstate's reported combined ratio was 86.6 percent in the second quarter, close to Progressive's 88.7 percent for the third quarter; the quarters differ in weather, so we treat the two as similar rather than ranked.

What this means for an owner: the business is sound and highly profitable today, but it is not growing into a bigger company at the pace of its best rival. The return you earn from here comes from buybacks, book value growth and whether margins hold, not from rapid premium expansion. That is why Chapter 3 asks what return on equity the market is already assuming, and Chapter 5 tests what happens if margins slip.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A company's record is easier to judge than its promises. We read what management said in each earnings release from February 2024 to February 2026 and checked what happened. The pattern is of a team that has delivered on profit, on capital return and on its stated rollouts, while its newest growth promise is only partly delivered. The profit improvement plan announced in February 2024 is the best example: management said it was confident of attractive shareholder returns, and the Property-Liability combined ratio went from 104.5 percent in 2023 to 94.3 in 2024 and 85.2 in 2025.[15][14][10]

Two items are worth separating. The first is accuracy of estimates: in February 2025 the company said the January California wildfire losses would be about $1.1 billion before tax, net of reinsurance, and the 2025 annual report shows wildfire losses of $1.05 billion, so the estimate held.[14][12] The second is growth: in November 2025 management said new products, more advertising, broad distribution and retention programs were expected to drive continued growth. Six months later, total Property-Liability policies and written premiums were each up 2.6 percent in the quarter. In auto the two diverged: policies rose 2.8 percent but written premiums only 0.4 percent, because the company cut average prices.[11][6][9] That is delivery of volume in auto, not of premium growth, and we mark it partial.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter reads Allstate's last five years in plain figures, then sets the static levels against the direction of travel (Layer 1). For an insurer, two standard screens do not apply as written. Free cash flow margin is not a clean measure, because an insurer's cash from operations includes premiums received before claims are paid, and the net-debt-to-EBITDA test makes little sense for a company whose debt is a small slice of its capital. We therefore use the financials equivalents named in the scoring file: return on equity at or above 12 percent in place of return on invested capital, debt as a share of capital and interest coverage in place of net debt to EBITDA, and we show cash from operations only for reference.[36][12]

The five-year table that follows is the most important single picture. For a buyer, that is useful: doing well then needs only a smaller fall than the market expects.[36]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A report that only argues for an idea is not research. This chapter takes the strongest honest case against our conclusion and asks, for each of three weak spots, what we are assuming, what evidence would show us wrong, and when we will know. The date that matters most is November 4: Allstate reports third-quarter results after 4:15 p.m. Eastern, and the call is at 9 a.m. on November 5, so the first price reaction comes on the opening on November 5, which is a gap risk for any order resting in our entry zone.[5] The average daily swing is $5.48, or 2.2 percent, but earnings gaps are larger than that.[34]

Fatal flaw 1: underwriting margins are at a cyclical peak and will mean-revert

The weakest assumption in our base case is that the underlying combined ratio only drifts from 79.8 to about 83.5 in 2027. The evidence for a faster fade is already visible. Allstate's average earned auto premium fell 1.6 percent year on year, its Allstate-brand average gross premium fell 3.6 percent, implemented auto rates were effectively zero in the first half, and the average underlying loss per policy fell 3.5 percent, a gain that cannot repeat once repairs and injury costs stop falling.[7] Rivals are cutting prices; Argus says some Allstate prices were lower in the second quarter; Hartford reports a competitive market in personal auto; and S&P Global is said to project industry combined ratios past break-even by 2028.[31][23][33]

The levels we use are these. A third- or fourth-quarter 2026 underlying ratio above 83 is ahead of our path (about 80 to 81 in the second half of 2026, rising to an average of 83.5 in 2027) and downgrades the idea. An underlying ratio above about 85 for two quarters in a row in 2027 means the idea is wrong. Auto written premiums turning negative, or loss cost per policy growing again, would be early warnings. We will see the first evidence on November 4 and the next on the fourth-quarter release in early February 2027. Each point of combined ratio is about $2.00 of annual earnings per share, so a 4-point slip takes about $8 off earnings and, at 10 times, about $80 off the price.

Fatal flaw 2: storms and reserve releases revert, and the earnings base is smaller than it looks

The second assumption is that catastrophe losses average about 8.8 points and that reserve releases of 0.6 points a year are enough in 2027. Recent history is the other way. Third-quarter catastrophe losses of $1.65 billion are about 11 points, which means storms at that level are already happening. Nine months are about 10.3 points, and 2023 was 11.6.[2][6][12] At the same time, reserve releases were $1.81 billion in 2025, 14.1 percent of that year's net income after tax, and $1.65 billion in the first half of 2026, about 23 percent of first-half net income after tax on the same basis (about 25 percent of adjusted net income), by our calculation.[12][8] If storms stay near 10.5 points instead of 8.8 and the small release in our base case stops, earnings per share would be about $4.60 lower, which is $3.40 from storms and $1.20 from the lost release, or about $46 of price at 10 times.

What would show us wrong is a third-quarter report with no reserve release and a heavier catastrophe load than the $1.65 billion already disclosed, or a 2026 hurricane in the fourth quarter. The monthly catastrophe releases for October and November will give early readings; they have come out in mid-month so far (August 20, September 17 and October 15).[4][3][2] The data gaps matter here: we did not find a fetched source for Allstate's reinsurance program terms, so we cannot confirm how much of a large event is retained, and we use the $3.1 billion modeled 1-in-100 net loss only for the three named perils.[12]

Fatal flaw 3: the stock is priced for a 16 percent return on equity, and a lower one costs more than the table suggests

The third assumption is about the multiple. At 2.03 times book the market already implies a long-run return of about 16 percent. If the true figure is 12 percent, the justified price to book is 1.3 times, or about $200 on book value of $150, which is the region of our bear case.[6] A return above 40 percent is new for this company: no year in the five-year table before the latest twelve months was above 38.3 percent, the average is 16.5 percent, and in 2022 and 2023 it earned about nothing.[16][15][14][10] A stock at five times earnings has been a trap before, when the earnings were at a peak.

What would show us wrong is not the next quarter but the pattern over several: adjusted return on equity falling below 30 percent by the end of 2026 and below 25 percent by mid-2027, while book value per share growth slows to single digits. We will see it on each quarterly release, with the strongest test the full-year 2026 report in February 2027. A larger share buyback at a lower price would partly offset the multiple compression for patient holders.

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule behind it is the asymmetry floor in the scoring file. A Core Alpha candidate must offer at least 2.0 dollars of base-case gain for each dollar at risk to the stop. At $250.20 our $281 target gives 0.85, which fails. At $234 it gives 2.35, which passes.[36][34] The business passes every financial gate and scores 3.5 of 5.0 on the company tests. The chart passes the recovery test at today's price. So the stock is not rejected. It is a quality business at a price that does not yet pay us for the cycle risk in Chapter 6. This verdict is also not an order. No buy order is live until the entry check below has been run on the filed third-quarter numbers.

We reclassify the idea from TACTICAL_MOMENTUM, the bucket on the watchlist row, to CORE_ALPHA. There are three reasons. First, the stock fails the momentum tests that a tactical trade needs: the 50-day average is falling and 63-day relative strength is negative. Second, the thesis rests on capital return and book value compounding over three or more years, not on a three-to-six-month price move. Third, the Core stop is the structural level that matters here. It is the lower of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average, less one average true range, which is $214.03.[34][36] The archetype is COMPOUNDER on static quality: adjusted return on equity of 44.2 percent against a 12 percent floor, and debt at 18.2 percent of capital. The honest qualification is that the earnings stream is cyclical. The stock does not fit TURNAROUND_VALUE_INFLECTION, because profits are at a high and not at a trough. It does not fit TACTICAL_BREAKOUT, because it is not a Stage 2 leader.

The provisional entry zone is $228 to $234, for a limit order only. The 2.0 to 1 floor is met at any price up to about $236, because the break-even is $236.35. We set the top at $234 because it leaves a margin (2.35 to 1) and sits near the level the stock reached on September 22.[32][34] The price of $234 is 2.96 average true ranges below the close, and $228 is 4.05 below. The bottom of the zone sits just above the 200-day average of $227.72. That choice is deliberate. A close under the 200-day average is the rejection level in Chapter 4, so no part of the zone is allowed to sit below it. The zone moves up as the 200-day average rises, which it has done by about 22 cents a day over the last 63 sessions. Sizing follows the risk-unit rule. A Tier 3 position risks 50 basis points of the portfolio. With a stop 8.5 percent below the $234 entry, that formula gives 5.9 percent. So the tier cap of 3.5 percent applies.

The entry check has four parts, and all four must hold before any order is placed. First, the filed third-quarter numbers must show an underlying Property-Liability combined ratio of 83 or lower, no adverse reserve development and auto written premiums that are not falling. Second, we recompute the base target as revised FY2027 consensus earnings times 10.0, and the order price may not be higher than (target plus 428.06) divided by 3, which is the price that still leaves 2.0 to 1 against the $214.03 stop. With the $281 target that price is $236.35. With the $241.16 stress target from Chapter 6 it is $223.07, below the 200-day average, so we would not buy at all. Third, we rerun the stage and momentum gate on the session before the order. The stock must not be in Stage 4 and must close at or above the 200-day average. The floor, support-distance and selling-pressure conditions of the recovery test (C1, C3 and C4) must pass. Fourth, no downgrade trigger below may be active.

We state a limit of this check openly. The short-term condition of the recovery test (C2) needs the price at or above the 20-day average, now $239.59, and a price of $234 is below it. So a fill in the zone would not pass C2 as the gate is written. We treat C2 as informational for a pullback entry, because the scoring rule for a Stage 3 pullback above a rising 200-day average is to wait for support. That is a judgment, not a gate result, and it is why the size is capped at half until the results are digested.[34][36]

Two rules decide what happens around the earnings date and after a fill. First, rejection and downgrade rules beat price. If a downgrade trigger is active, or the stock closes below the 200-day average before a fill, every resting order is cancelled and the idea moves to QUALIFIED_DEFERRED, even if the price is inside the zone. Second, no limit order is allowed to rest through the third-quarter release. Allstate reports after 4:15 p.m. Eastern on November 4, and the call is at 9 a.m. on November 5.[5] A gap at the open could fill an order at a poor moment, at the same time as the news that triggers a downgrade. So we cancel any order before the close on November 4, and the first order we would place is after the call on November 5. The stop is a planned exit, not a guaranteed loss cap. A gap can fill below it. As an exposure illustration, a 3.5 percent position bought at $234 and sold at our $171 bear value would lose 26.9 percent of the position, or 0.94 percent of the portfolio. That is more than the 0.50 percent risk budget.

Once filled, the let-winners-run rule applies. We would not trim only because the relative strength index passes 70 or the weight passes 15 percent. We would raise the stop to the rising 50-day average as the trend improves. Targets are $281 base, $334 bull and $171 bear. No position is held today. If one were held, we would keep it above $214.03 and judge it against the bull target and revisions, not the base target.[36]
