1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Stock Cycles

Core Investor Question: How did a 1911 truck axle machine shop transform into a $136B global power management powerhouse, and what drove its historical boom-bust cycles?

Eaton Corporation plc (NYSE: ETN) has undergone a major corporate transformation. The business was founded in Bloomfield, New Jersey in 1911 by Joseph Oriel Eaton, Viggo Torbensen, and Henning Taube. It began as Torbensen Gear and Axle Company, pioneering internal-gear truck axles. Over the ensuing century, Eaton executed disciplined acquisitions and strategic divestitures. It also completed a corporate re-domiciliation to Ireland. Today, Eaton has evolved from an automotive supplier into a $136 billion intelligent power management titan.

The Strategic Architecture of Transformation (1911–2026)

Eaton's corporate evolution rests on five key strategic transactions. First, in 1978, Eaton acquired Cutler-Hammer for $378 million. This established entry into electrical controls and circuit protection. Second, in 1994, Eaton bought Westinghouse Electric's Distribution and Control Business Unit for $1.1 billion. This vaulted Eaton into top-tier North American low- and medium-voltage distribution. Third, in 2004, Eaton bought Powerware for $560 million, securing leadership in datacenter uninterruptible power systems. Fourth, in 2012, Eaton executed a $13 billion merger with Cooper Industries plc. This added Crouse-Hinds and Bussmann fuses, while re-incorporating Eaton in Dublin, Ireland. Fifth, between 2020 and 2025, CEO Craig Arnold pruned Lighting ($1.4B) and Hydraulics ($3.3B). He redeployed capital into Cobham Mission Systems ($2.83B), Tripp Lite ($1.65B), and Boyd Thermal.

Transformer Supply Chain Bottleneck Forensics: Grain-Oriented Electrical Steel & Skilled Labor

The defining supply chain dynamic of the current electrification supercycle is the severe physical bottleneck in pad-mounted and substation transformers. Large power transformers require high-permeability grain-oriented electrical steel (GOES), a highly specialized crystalline alloy produced by only a handful of global steel mills (such as Cleveland-Cliffs in the US, Nippon Steel, and JFE). Because GOES manufacturing requires extreme chemical precision and specialized annealing furnaces, global steel supply cannot expand rapidly.

Concurrently, precision transformer assembly requires master core-winding artisans who manually layer miles of copper and insulated steel windings to millimeter tolerances. The multi-decade stagnation of the North American manufacturing workforce created a critical deficit of certified winders. In response, Eaton invested over $1.5 billion across facilities in Nacogdoches, Texas, and Queretaro, Mexico, deploying automated robotic winding technology and establishing proprietary technical apprentice academies to de-bottleneck factory throughput.
