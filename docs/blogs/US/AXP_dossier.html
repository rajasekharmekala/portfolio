Before you read

American Express is a payments company that issues premium cards, signs up the shops that take them, and runs the network between the two. We judge the business strong and the shares fairly priced for good news, so our verdict for a new buyer is to wait for a lower price. At the $317.94 close on 28 October 2026 the gain to our $361.65 target is only 1.62 times the loss at our $290.88 stop, under our 2.0 minimum. We would buy only after a confirmed turn, at a price between $301.00 and $305.00, where that ratio is 4.0 and still about 2.0 on a harsher valuation test, at no more than 3.5% of the portfolio. We would be wrong if the October consent orders grow into limits on the business, if a federal 10% interest-rate cap becomes law, or if the shares close below $290.88.

1. What the company does

American Express sells payment cards to people and businesses, and it also runs the network that carries each payment from the shop to the bank. Most card companies do only one of those jobs. Visa and Mastercard run networks but do not lend to cardholders. Many card-issuing banks lend to cardholders but rent a network from one of them. American Express issues the card, signs up the merchants who accept it, and runs the network in between. People call this a closed-loop network. In 2025 the company earned $72.2 billion in revenue after interest costs, and by the third quarter of 2026 there were 155.4 million American Express cards in force, of which 87.5 million were issued by the company itself.[3][1]

Revenue comes from four lines. The largest is discount revenue, which is the fee a merchant pays each time a customer uses the card. It was $37.4 billion in 2025 and equals about 2.24% of every dollar spent on the cards.[3][1] The second is net interest income, the interest cardholders pay on balances they carry, less the interest the company pays on deposits and bonds. It was $17.4 billion in 2025.[3] The third is net card fees, the annual fees that premium cardholders pay. It was $10.0 billion in 2025 and is the fastest-growing line, up 16% in the third quarter of 2026.[3][1] The fourth is service fees and other revenue, such as network partner payments and foreign-exchange income, at $7.5 billion.[3]

Who pays, and why? Cardholders pay annual fees because the cards come with rewards points, airport lounges, travel credits and concierge help. The average fee per card was $136 a year in the third quarter, up 14% from $119.[1] Merchants pay the discount fee to reach cardholders who spend freely. Average spending per proprietary basic card was $6,735 in the quarter, up 5%.[1] Airlines, hotels and retailers pay to share those customers. Delta cobrand cards alone were about 13% of worldwide billed business and about 21% of worldwide loans at the end of 2025.[3] Goods and services made up 71% of the $454.0 billion billed in the third quarter, and travel and entertainment the other 29%.[1]

The company reports four segments, shown in the table below. The consumer segment issues cards to people in the United States. The commercial segment serves small businesses and corporations. The international segment issues cards outside the United States. The merchant and network segment runs the network, signs up merchants and licenses the brand to other card issuers.[1]

How the company turns revenue into profit

Four costs stand between revenue and profit. The first is rewards, benefits and partner payments, which the company calls variable customer engagement expense. It was 44.3% of revenue in the third quarter, up from 41.8% a year earlier.[1][16] The second is marketing, $1.77 billion in the quarter.[1] The third is salaries and other running costs. The fourth is credit losses, charged as provisions of $985 million in the quarter.[1] What is left was $4.5 billion of pretax income, including a one-time gain on the sale of a travel-company stake.[1]

The company funds its loans mostly with customer deposits. Deposits were $155.0 billion of the $215.0 billion total of deposits and debt, or 72%, with long-term bonds making up most of the rest.[1] Because American Express is a bank holding company, the Federal Reserve supervises it and sets minimum capital levels.[1] Its return on equity (ROE) is profit divided by the money shareholders have in the business. It was 33.9% in 2025, which is high because the company holds little capital for each dollar of revenue.[3]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares, so it shows profit for one share.
  • CET1 (common equity tier 1) is the highest-quality capital a bank holds, shown as a share of its risk-weighted assets. Regulators set a minimum, and a higher ratio means a bigger cushion.
  • net interest margin (NIM) is the interest a lender earns on its loans minus the interest it pays for funding, divided by its loans. American Express calls its version the net interest yield.
  • return on invested capital (ROIC) is profit earned on each dollar invested in the business. For a lender it is hard to measure, so we lean on ROE.
  • stock-based compensation (SBC) is the cost of paying employees in shares, which dilutes owners.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operations minus spending on buildings and equipment. For a lender it is distorted by loan growth, so the scoring rules treat it as not applicable.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit before financing costs and accounting charges. It is not meaningful for a deposit-funded lender.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share. A P/E of 18 means the buyer pays $18 for each $1 of yearly profit.
  • buyback is the company buying its own shares, which shrinks the share count.
  • dilution is the rise in share count from issuing new shares. A buyback does the opposite.
  • guidance is the company's own published forecast for the coming year.
  • consensus estimate is the average of Wall Street analysts' forecasts for a number.
  • relative strength index (RSI) is a 0-to-100 meter of how fast the price has been rising or falling. Above 70 is stretched and below 30 is washed out.
  • average true range (ATR) is the average daily price swing over 14 days, in dollars. We measure distances from support in ATRs.
  • 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days. The 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the same idea over 50 and 200 days; they act as slow-moving price floors and ceilings.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend in the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. Stage 1 is a basing phase between a decline and a new advance.
  • base is a flat resting phase the price builds after a decline, the platform a new advance starts from. A pivot is the top of that base, the price a rally must clear. A breakout is the move above the top of a base on rising volume, and a retest is the pullback that tests the breakout level from above.
  • stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold.
  • limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, so it never chases a rising price. The entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). Our core floor is 2.0.
  • net debt is borrowing minus cash. cash flow from operations is the cash the business itself generates, and capital expenditure is cash spent on buildings, equipment and software.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The first strength is the closed loop. Because American Express sees the cardholder, the merchant and the transaction, it can, in our reading, fund richer rewards from the merchant fee and still price its credit risk with more information than a lender that sees only the loan. Its customers are loyal: one year after the U.S. Platinum card refresh, spending retention on the U.S. consumer Platinum portfolio stood at 99%.[15] It ranked first in the J.D. Power 2026 U.S. credit card satisfaction study for the seventh year in a row, and merchant coverage reached more than 190 million locations, up from 160 million a year earlier.[2][15] A merchant that accepts the card more widely makes the card more useful, which is the network effect that protects the model.

The second strength is a customer base that pays fees and rarely defaults. The company's 30-day past-due rate on consumer and small business cards was 1.2% in the third quarter, and it has stayed between 1.2% and 1.3% for 15 straight quarters.[1][15] The net write-off rate, which counts the share of balances given up as lost, was 1.8% on principal.[1] Synchrony, another large card lender, reported a 4.79% net charge-off rate in the same quarter. Its book is store-card lending with a weaker average borrower, so part of the gap is business mix.[31] Fee income is a cushion: net card fees of $2.97 billion in the quarter do not depend on borrowers repaying.[1] In the Q3 call, management said the fee line grew 17% on a currency-adjusted basis, up from 15% in the second quarter.[16]

The third strength is capital generation. Return on equity has stayed between 31.5% and 34.6% every year since 2021, and the company returned $7.6 billion to shareholders in 2025 and $7.4 billion in the first nine months of 2026, about 77% of net income to common holders.[4][3][1] The share count has fallen from 790 million diluted shares in 2021 to 696 million in 2025.[4][3] Capital is tight but adequate: the CET1 ratio was 10.9% against a 7.0% effective minimum, and inside the company's own 10% to 11% target range. The Federal Reserve has held its stress capital buffer at the 2.5% minimum.[1][14] The weak side of that tightness is that there is little spare capital, so a surprise loss comes straight out of buybacks.

The weaknesses are real and some are new. Spending on customers is rising faster than revenue: card member services expense jumped 36% in the third quarter because of the Platinum refresh, and the variable customer engagement ratio reached 44.3% of revenue from 41.8%.[1] Commercial billed business grew only 3%, partly because the company exited two small business cobrand portfolios, and 3.1 million new cards were acquired against 3.2 million a year ago.[1][15] Discount revenue as a share of billed business slipped to 2.23% for nine months from 2.25%, which tells us merchants are paying a little less per dollar, a trend that regulators and retailers are pushing.[1] The October 8 consent orders add a governance weakness, covered next.

The October 2026 consent orders: what regulators found

On 8 October 2026 the Federal Reserve and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency announced enforcement actions about the company's financial crimes compliance. The comptroller's office issued a cease-and-desist order and a $350 million civil money penalty against American Express National Bank for deficiencies in its anti-money-laundering program.[12][13] The agency said the bank had focused on its narrow deposit products and not enough on its dominant credit and charge card products, and that its staff, internal controls and testing were inadequate.[12] The company said the orders do not impose an asset cap, that part of the penalty was already reserved, and that remediation costs are not expected to affect its 2027 guidance.[9][1]

Why this matters for an owner: an asset cap would have limited growth, and the orders do not impose one. But the findings say the control environment lagged the business for years, and the 10-Q warns that failure to meet the orders could lead to further settlements, fines or restrictions.[1] The CEO told analysts the deficiencies cited in the order should not have occurred.[15] We treat the orders as a governance flag, not a hard red line, because there is no accounting fraud, no solvency threat and no growth restriction. We carry it as a cost of capital in the valuation instead: the target multiple is held at today's level and not raised toward its three-year median.

Rules that could change the economics

Three policy risks sit outside the company's control. First, a 10% cap on credit card interest rates was called for by the President in January 2026, but it is still not law. The proposal did not say how it would be enforced, and experts say a cap would need Congress.[23][24][25][26] Net interest income was $14.3 billion in nine months, about 24% of revenue, so a cap that applied to its balances would matter. One financial planner quoted by CBS expects higher-income customers to be little affected.[1][25] Second, the Credit Card Competition Act was reintroduced in January 2026 and would force large issuers to offer merchants a choice of networks, which would pressure fees on card networks.[27] Third, merchant antitrust suits (Pizza Hazel and 5-Star General Store) challenge the rules that stop merchants steering customers away from its cards, and the Kabbage estate is suing to recover up to about $746 million.[1] The company puts its reasonably possible additional legal loss at zero to $150 million, which is small against $35.8 billion of equity.[1]

3. The numbers, in plain words

The five-year record shows a business that grew. Revenue rose from $42.4 billion in 2021 to $72.2 billion in 2025. The compound growth rate from 2022 to 2025 was 10.97% a year for revenue and 12.97% for net income.[4][3] Earnings per share grew faster, at 16.0% a year, because the diluted share count fell from 790 million to 696 million.[4][3] The year 2021 is not a clean starting point. The company released credit reserves that year, so the provision line was negative $1.4 billion. That flatters 2021 profit.[4]

Credit is the line that moves most. The net write-off rate on principal rose from 0.7% in 2021 to 2.0% in 2024 and 2025. It eased to 1.9% for the first nine months of 2026.[4][3][1] Net interest income more than doubled over the period, from $7.75 billion in 2021 to $17.4 billion in 2025, as balances grew and rates rose.[4][3] Interest cover is not a useful test for a deposit-funded lender. For completeness, pretax income plus interest expense was 2.68 times interest expense in 2025.[3]

The latest nine months show the trend continuing, with some distortion. Revenue rose 10% and reported pretax income rose 16%. Take out the gain on the travel-stake sale and the related restructuring charge, and pretax income was 11,734 million. The underlying pretax margin was 20.0% against 20.1% a year earlier.[1][2] The 10-Q books the gain in other expenses, not in revenue, so the revenue figure needs no adjustment.[1] The prior-year quarter did include a gain on a travel-company equity transaction in revenue, and its size is not published, so underlying revenue growth is if anything a little above 10%.[1] Growth is real. Profit margin has been flat, not rising.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 asks three questions. Does the company pass the hard red lines? Does it meet the quality tests for its type? And what do the trends say, not just the levels? Here the Stage 4 test also matters. A Stage 4 breakdown means the price is below a falling 50-day average that is itself below a falling 200-day average, and the recovery test also fails. It is the only chart pattern that disqualifies a name outright. If the recovery test passes, a Stage 4 structure is a warning and not a disqualifier. That was the case on 23 October: the structure was Stage 4 and the recovery test passed 4 of 4, so the name was passed conditionally. Every estimate has a weakest link. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each one, states the evidence that would show it wrong, and says when that evidence arrives. It also answers the strongest bear arguments we could build against the central target, with arithmetic.

Estimate 1: 2027 earnings per share of $20.17

The weakest assumption is that credit costs stay as low as they were in 2026. The third-quarter card reserve release of $235 million, including a $211 million model update, lowered provisions, and the reserve is now 2.5% of balances against 2.9% a year ago.[1][15] If the company rebuilds reserves as balances grow 7% to 8% a year, provisions rise even with flat write-offs, and earnings can fall before the published write-off rate moves.
