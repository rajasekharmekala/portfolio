1. What the company does

American Express does three jobs at once. It issues cards to individuals and businesses, it lends money on those cards, and it runs the network that carries each payment from the shopper to the store. Visa and Mastercard run networks but leave the lending to banks. American Express does both, which is called a closed loop: it sees the cardholder and the merchant in the same transaction. In 2025 its customers spent $1,670 billion on its cards. It had 86.6 million of its own cards in force worldwide, plus 66.2 million cards issued by other banks on its network[5].

The company is paid in four ways, and the mix matters more than the total. Total revenue net of interest expense was $72,229 million in 2025[2]. Discount revenue was $37,401 million, or 51.8% of the total. This is the fee a merchant pays each time a card is used, a percentage of the purchase. Net card fees were $9,993 million, or 13.8%: the annual fee a cardholder pays, booked evenly over the twelve months of membership, so it behaves like a subscription. Net interest income was $17,364 million, or 24.0%: interest earned on balances that cardholders carry, minus the interest the company pays on its own deposits and debt. Service fees and other revenue made up the last $7,471 million, or 10.3%[2].

The business is run in four segments, and one of them earns far more per dollar of revenue than the rest. US Consumer Services brings in $34,814 million of revenue (48.2% of the total) and $6,810 million of pretax income. Commercial Services brings in $16,926 million and $3,668 million. International Card Services brings in $13,000 million and $1,603 million. Global Merchant and Network Services brings in only $7,759 million of revenue but $3,968 million of pretax income, a 51.1% margin, because it earns fees from merchants and partners without lending money[6]. By region, the United States produced $56,015 million, or 77.6% of revenue, so the company is mainly a bet on the American consumer and American businesses[6].

The costs show the strategy. Expenses were $53,178 million in 2025, 73.6% of revenue, and the largest items were Card Member rewards ($18,409 million), marketing ($6,252 million) and the cost of lounges, credits and other benefits ($6,057 million)[2]. Provisions for credit losses, the money set aside for loans that may not be repaid, were $5,256 million, or 7.3% of revenue[2]. The company deliberately spends to attract affluent customers with generous benefits and a high annual fee, because such customers spend more and fall behind on payments less often. Management said that 75% of new accounts in the second quarter of 2026 were on fee-paying products and that 65% of new consumer accounts this year came from Millennials and Gen Z[31]. The funding side is a bank's: customer deposits of $152,488 million (50.8% of total assets of $300,052 million) and long-term debt of $56,387 million at the end of 2025[3]. One partner stands out: the Delta cobrand portfolio was about 13 percent of worldwide billed business and about 21 percent of worldwide Card Member loans[5].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Card Member — a person or business that holds an American Express card; the company's name for its customer.
  • Billed business — the total dollars spent on American Express cards, which is the raw volume that drives merchant fees.
  • Discount revenue — the fee merchants pay for accepting the card, a share of each purchase.
  • Net card fees — annual card fees paid by Card Members, spread over twelve months.
  • Net interest income — interest earned on card balances and loans, minus the interest the company pays on deposits and debt.
  • Net write-off rate — the share of card balances the company gives up collecting in a year, after recoveries; it is the main measure of loan losses.
  • Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) ratio — the main bank safety buffer: the highest-quality capital as a percentage of risk-weighted assets.
  • Return on average equity (ROE) — profit divided by the average money shareholders have invested in the business.
  • Variable customer engagement (VCE) costs — rewards, partner payments and benefits that rise with how much customers spend.
  • Closed loop — a payment system where one company both issues the card and runs the network, so it sees both sides of the transaction.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of American Express is that its customers choose to pay for the card. An annual card fee is easy to avoid by cancelling the card, so the fee only survives if the benefits are worth more to the customer. The evidence is that they do. Net card fees rose 15.4% in the second quarter of 2026 and have grown at a double-digit rate for 32 consecutive quarters[31]. After the company raised the US Platinum annual fee by $200 and repriced about a quarter of that portfolio by the end of the first quarter, management said retention rates were flat year over year and that it was not seeing anything on attrition[31]. Spending is also accelerating, not fading: billed business grew 9.4% after adjusting for currency, and US consumer spending grew 11%, the fastest since the first quarter of 2018 excluding the pandemic[31]. A closed loop with loyal, high-spending customers is hard to copy, and these figures are the proof rather than the claim.

The second strength is credit quality. The net write-off rate on principal for consumer and small-business cards was 2.0% in the second quarter, and cards 30 days or more past due were 1.2% of balances, against 1.3% a year earlier[13]. Management said both measures sit below 2019 levels and that the Federal Reserve's stress test gave American Express the lowest projected credit-card loss rate of any bank tested[31]. The monthly report for September 2026 shows US consumer balances of $114.3 billion with 1.2% past due and a 1.5% write-off rate for the month, and small-business balances of $46.6 billion with 1.4% past due[19]. The peers lose much more on their cards: Capital One's net charge-off rate was 3.23%, JPMorgan's card services rate was 2.91%, and Synchrony's was 5.43%[38][40][39]. Lower losses are the reason the company can pay for generous benefits and still earn a 36.4% return on equity[13].

The weaknesses are specific. The first is compliance. On 8 October 2026 the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency fined American Express National Bank $350 million and ordered it to fix its anti-money-laundering program, finding that the bank failed to report about $13 billion of suspected trade-based money laundering over the past decade[22]. The Federal Reserve issued a parallel order covering board oversight, transaction monitoring, third-party information and sanctions compliance[23]. The company said part of the penalty was already reserved, that the orders do not impose an asset cap, and that neither the 2026 nor the 2027 guidance, the company's own published forecast, changes[21]. It had warned in its second-quarter 10-Q, filed on 24 July 2026, that it expected enforcement action[14]. The orders are cheap in dollars and expensive in reputation: a decade of missed reports is a control failure, and remediation will consume management time.

The second weakness is policy. In January 2026 the President proposed a one-year 10% cap on credit card interest rates, and the stock fell 4.3% in one session[33][43]. CFRA Research estimated that American Express could see an earnings decline of more than 50% if such a cap applied, because the company earns 24.0% of revenue from net interest income[32][2]. The bipartisan bills to impose the cap were filed in 2025 and have not advanced[34]. A separate bill, the Credit Card Competition Act of 2026, would force large issuers to offer a choice of networks, but it exempts three-party systems such as American Express[35]. Policy risk has not become law, which is why it is a risk and not a reason to sell, but it caps how high a multiple the stock can hold.

Where the moat is thinner than it looks

Three facts argue for caution. First, concentration: one partner, Delta, accounts for about 13 percent of billed business and about 21 percent of card loans[5]. Second, leverage: total assets of $308,203 million sit on equity of $34,280 million, so each dollar of shareholder money supports about $9.0 of assets[12]. The 36.4% return on equity is partly the product of that leverage: a 1% loss on assets is $3,082 million, which is 9.0% of equity. Third, new competition at the edges: in middle-market corporate cards management named Ramp and Brex as competitors and piloted its own expense-management platform to answer them[31].

None of these is a reason to doubt the business. They are the reasons a regulated lender with a 36% return on equity still trades at 17.8 times this year's earnings and not at the 24 times it reached in December 2025[41]. The market pays a premium for the card fees and for the closed loop, and a discount for the leverage and the compliance record.

The company promised a 17% dividend increase for 2025 and a 16% increase for 2026, and the quarterly dividend moved from $0.82 to $0.95, up 16%[17][16][13]. Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 tests the financial floors, the hard red lines and the chart. The framework's standard floors were written for industrial companies. One is a floor on ROIC, return on invested capital, the profit earned on each dollar invested in the business. Another is a floor on the free cash flow (FCF) margin, where free cash flow is the cash left after running the business and buying assets. A third is a ceiling on net debt to EBITDA, where net debt is borrowings minus cash and EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. A lender does not publish ROIC, free cash flow or EBITDA, and the company says nothing about them[44]. Debt is the raw material of a bank, not a risk to be minimised, so the table uses return on equity, capital ratios and interest cover as the lender's equivalents and states each substitution. The hard red lines still apply in full. Two more tests need a word. The asymmetry test is the reward-to-risk ratio: the gain if the target is hit divided by the loss if the stop is hit. The technical floor asks for a breakout, a move above a flat trading range on strong volume, from Stage 1, or for Stage 2, the confirmed-uptrend phase.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could fail. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong and says when that evidence arrives. It then builds the strongest honest case against owning the stock, describes three scenarios that would end the thesis, and names the single claim on which everything hinges.

Weakest assumption behind the $371.60 base target

The target assumes that FY2027 EPS reaches $20.13, which is 14.1% above FY2026, and that the market pays 18.46 times it. Both can fail. The EPS figure is a consensus of 25 analysts and depends on the company's reinvestment turning into revenue, on credit staying near 2.0% and on the tax rate staying near 23.6%[1][15]. The multiple depends on interest rates and policy: the stock paid 24.05 times earnings in December 2025 when yields were lower and paid 17.15 times in March 2026 after the rate-cap scare[41][1]. The evidence that would show the assumption wrong is a cut in FY2027 consensus of more than 5%, to below $19.12, or a Q3 net write-off rate above 2.5%. The first evidence arrives on 23 October 2026 and the next, the FY2026 results with 2027 guidance, is expected in late January 2027 (date not yet announced)[27].

Weakest assumption behind FY2026 EPS of $17.64

The number assumes second-half EPS of $8.83, up 15.1% from last year's second half, while marketing grows about 10% and the variable customer engagement ratio sits at 44% to 45%[31][43]. The hidden assumption is that credit remains benign: the second quarter included a $191 million reserve release, and the prior year had a reserve build[31]. If the reserve release does not repeat, provisions rise even with flat write-offs. The evidence that would show it wrong is a third-quarter provision above the $1.1 billion of the second quarter, or a September-to-December rise in 30-day delinquency above 1.5%. The September monthly data already show consumer delinquency rising from 1.1% in August to 1.2%, a small step that is worth watching[19][20].

Weakest assumption behind the 4.6% implied-growth reading

The reading treats trailing net income of $11.446 billion as the cash the business generates for owners. That is generous for a lender. A lender that grows loans must hold capital against them, and the company keeps CET1 at 10% to 11% of risk-weighted assets[13]. If growth consumes more capital than the company expects, distributable cash is less than net income and the implied growth is higher, so the stock is less cheap than the reading suggests. The evidence would be a CET1 ratio drifting below 10% or a cut in the pace of buybacks, which is visible in the quarterly filing[13].

Weakest assumption behind the $350.13 probability-weighted value

The probabilities are judgement, and the weakest one is the bear weight of 35%. If the credit cycle turns, the bear case is the likely case and the weight should be well above one-half. The evidence is the sequence of monthly write-off and delinquency reports that the company files each month: the next one is expected in mid-November 2026 (date not announced) and covers October[19][20]. Three consecutive monthly rises in the consumer write-off rate from the 1.5% of September would be our signal to raise the bear weight.

The red-team bear case

The strongest honest argument against owning AXP runs as follows. The company earns a 36% return on equity with 9.0 times asset leverage, on a customer base that has not seen a real recession since it was repriced for $200 higher fees[12][31]. Credit at 2.0% is near its best, and the second quarter's profit growth leaned on a reserve release, not on operating leverage: expenses grew 12% against revenue growth of 10%[15][31]. The company is under two consent orders for a ten-year failure in controls that touched about $13 billion of suspected money laundering, and the orders constrain how fast it can expand[22][23]. Policy is a live risk: a 10% rate cap would hit earnings by more than half on CFRA's estimate, and the political support has not gone away even though the bills have stalled[32][34]. At 17.8 times earnings the stock is not cheap against its own three-year history, and it sits in a downtrend in a market correction with a 5.10% ten-year yield[41][1]. A stock like that can fall further before it recovers.

The reply is that none of these facts has yet shown up in the numbers. Credit improved in the quarter, spending accelerated, card fees are growing at 15.4% and the company raised its revenue guide[15][31]. The bear case describes risks that are priced into a stock that is already 19.07% below its high[1]. We do not adopt the bear case as the base case, but we weight it at 35% and we hold a stop at $291.29 for exactly this reason.

Three fatal-flaw scenarios

Scenario one is a credit turn. If the net write-off rate rose 1.5 percentage points, from 2.0% to 3.5%, on card balances of $212.2 billion, pretax income would fall by about $3.2 billion, or about $3.58 per share after a 23.6% tax rate, roughly 20% of FY2026 EPS[12][43]. That is our own illustration and ignores the extra reserve build that would accompany a turn. It would put the stock into our bear case or below it and would make the Layer 2 credit score a FAIL.

Scenario two is a regulatory escalation. The October consent orders do not impose an asset cap and the company says they do not change 2026 or 2027 guidance[21]. If remediation were judged inadequate, regulators could add growth restrictions or further penalties, and the Federal Reserve's order covers the holding company and not just the bank[23]. Separately, a 10% interest-rate cap that became law would cut earnings by more than half on CFRA's estimate before any management response[32].

Scenario three is a premium-customer fatigue. The company has raised the Platinum fee by $200 and is spending more on marketing and benefits, with more than 70% of new accounts on fee-paying products[31]. If the economy weakens, affluent customers can cancel annual-fee cards, fee growth could fall from 15.4% toward single digits and the reinvestment would no longer pay back.
