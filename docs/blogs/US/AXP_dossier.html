1. What the company does

American Express is a global payments and premium lifestyle brand that was founded in 1850 and is headquartered in New York. [1] It is also a bank holding company, which means the Federal Reserve is its main US regulator and it must hold bank-style capital. [1] The company does three jobs at once: it issues cards to consumers, small businesses and large companies, it signs up the merchants that accept them, and it runs the network that moves the payment. [3] Because it sits at both ends of every purchase, it sees who is spending and where. Management calls this an integrated payments platform and says it sets the company apart from the bankcard networks, which depend on other banks to issue their cards. [3]

Its money comes from four sources. In the third quarter of 2026 (the quarter ended 30 September), revenue net of interest expense was $20,062 million. [1] The biggest line, discount revenue, was $10,169 million, or about 51 percent of the total; this is the fee a merchant pays on each card purchase. [1] Net card fees, the annual fees that card holders pay for premium cards, were $2,969 million. Net interest income, which is interest earned on card balances minus the company's own funding cost, was $4,940 million. Service fees and other revenue, such as travel commissions and foreign-exchange income, added $1,984 million. [1] Card spending drives the first line, product design drives the second, and borrowing drives the third, so a change in spending, in annual fees or in lending rules moves the profit in different ways.

The customers are consumers, small businesses and corporations. The company counts 49.7 million proprietary cards in force in its US consumer segment and 24.1 million outside the US, and in the third quarter its US consumer segment billed $194.2 billion. [1] Billed business, which is the total amount customers charge to the cards, was $454.0 billion across the company in the quarter, up from $421.0 billion a year earlier. [1] Management says Millennial and Gen-Z card holders are its largest and fastest-growing cohort, and card acceptance reached more than 190 million merchant locations, up from 160 million a year earlier. [1] [13] The company employed about 76,800 people at the end of 2025. [3]

The company is not a pure lender, and that helps explain its return on equity of 33.9 percent in 2025, against 21.0 percent for Synchrony, a pure card lender, in its latest quarter. Fees made up $54,865 million of 2025 revenue, about three-quarters of the total, and fee income does not depend on borrowers repaying; only the interest line does. In 2025, discount revenue was $37,401 million, net card fees were $9,993 million and net interest income was $17,364 million out of $72,229 million of revenue. How a card purchase pays the company

Take a $100 restaurant bill paid with a card. The merchant pays the company a discount fee, which becomes discount revenue. If the card holder pays the balance in full, the company earns no interest; if the holder carries a balance, the company earns interest and bears the risk that some balances are never repaid. [1] The company spends part of what it earns on reward points for card holders and on payments to business partners, which appear as expenses of $5,090 million for card member rewards and $1,795 million for business development in the quarter. [1] This arrangement explains why growth in spending lifts both revenue and costs, and why the question that matters is how much profit is left after the rewards are paid.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Billed business: the total amount customers charge to American Express cards in a period.
  • Net card fees: the annual fees that card holders pay for premium cards.
  • Net write-off rate: the share of card balances the company gives up collecting in a year; a lower rate means better credit quality.
  • CET1 ratio: Common Equity Tier 1 capital divided by risk-weighted assets; the main measure of a bank's loss-absorbing capital.
  • ROE: return on equity, annual profit divided by average shareholders' equity.
  • Reserve build or release: money added to, or taken back from, the pot set aside for expected card losses.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence for the business is credit quality, because a card lender that attracts affluent customers loses less money when the economy weakens. In the third quarter the net write-off rate on consumer and small-business balances, measured on principal only, was 1.8 percent, down from 1.9 percent a year earlier. [1] Balances 30 or more days past due were 1.2 percent of the total, down from 1.3 percent, and the company's September statistics show 1.2 percent for US consumer and 1.4 percent for US small-business balances. [1] [10] A summary of the earnings call says the delinquency rate has stayed between 1.2 and 1.3 percent for 15 straight quarters. [13] Synchrony, a pure card lender, reported net charge-offs of 4.79 percent of average loans in the same quarter on its own definition, so the gap in direction is clear even though the two measures are not identical. [27] Consumer health looks sound in these figures: US consumer billed business grew 9.4 percent in the quarter according to the call summary, and Synchrony says its customers' spending rose across credit cohorts and generations. [13] [27]

The second strength is the pricing power of the annual-fee model. Net card fees grew 16 percent in the third quarter to $2,969 million, and they grew 18 percent over 2025 to $9,993 million. [1] [3] The company ties the growth to its cycle of product refreshes, including the U.S. Platinum refresh that launched at the end of the third quarter of 2025, and says that spend retention on the U.S. consumer Platinum portfolio was 99 percent one year later. [3] [13] New card acquisition supports the same story: 3.1 million new cards in the quarter, 76 percent of them on fee-paying products. [13] The cost of that strategy is visible too. Card Member services expense, which the company links to the new U.S. Platinum benefits and higher use of card benefits, rose 36 percent to $2,006 million, so in our reading part of the extra fee income is paid back in benefits. [1]

The third strength is reach. Management says its cards are now accepted at more than 190 million merchant locations, up from 160 million a year earlier, with acceptance outside the US more than doubling over four years. [2] [13] Still, the network is exposed to rules the company does not control. In the nine months to September, average merchant discount rates were lower because of shifts in the mix of spending, and the filing lists surcharging (merchants adding a fee for card use), steering and regulated fee caps as continuing risks. [1] The company has already left the network business in the European Union and Australia because of regulation. [1] Visa and Mastercard each reported net revenue growth of 14 percent in their latest quarters, against 9 percent for American Express. The three are not identical businesses, so we use the comparison for growth only. [24] [25] [1]

The weaknesses are concentration, cost and regulatory standing. The Delta co-brand agreement runs through the end of 2029, and the filing says the company expects to keep making significant investments in it. [3] Commercial Services shows the cost of portfolio exits: its cards in force fell to 13.7 million from 15.4 million, and billed business grew only 3 percent. [1] On 8 October 2026 the company and its card bank consented to Federal Reserve and Office of the Comptroller of the Currency orders over parts of its financial-crimes compliance program, with a $350 million civil money penalty. [9] Management says the orders impose no asset cap and that the cost of fixing them should not affect its 2027 outlook, but also that the problems cited should not have occurred. [9] [13] [14] In the second quarter of 2026 the company also became a Category II firm under US bank rules, because its cross-jurisdictional activity passed $75 billion, and that brings heightened capital, liquidity and reporting requirements. [1]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 shows five years of results and then the Layer 1 test. Two of the standard tests do not apply to a card lender. Free cash flow (FCF), the cash left after running the business and buying equipment, is not meaningful because the company's loans are its inventory: cash from operations swings with card-balance timing, and the growth in card loans sits in investing cash flow instead. [3] Net debt, which is borrowings minus cash, divided by earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is not meaningful either, because deposits and borrowings are the raw material of lending. The scoring rules allow this: for banks and card lenders, Layer 1 uses return on equity (ROE) in place of return on invested capital (ROIC) and marks the other two tests N/A.

A few chart words are used from here on. A base is a flat stretch of trading before a move. A pivot is the top of that base, the price a stock must clear. A breakout is a move above the pivot on rising volume. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend phase of a four-stage price cycle, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend phase. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing, and we use it as a ruler for distances on the chart.

The five-year record shows a company that has grown revenue every year and earned a steady return. Revenue net of interest expense rose from $42,380 million in 2021 to $72,229 million in 2025, a three-year growth rate of 11.0 percent a year from 2022 (our calculation). [4] [3] Net income grew 13.0 percent a year over the same three years and EPS, the profit per share, grew 16.0 percent a year because the share count fell. [4] [3] Two years need care. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Fatal flaw 1: credit losses rise while profit leans on reserve releases

The estimates assume credit stays near today's level. Net write-offs on card balances were $1,130 million in the third quarter, up 2 percent, while provisions fell 23 percent to $985 million because the company released $235 million of reserves on card balances instead of building them. [1] [13] If the company had to rebuild the reserve to its year-end ratio, it would need about $0.66 billion before tax, or about $0.77 a share at a 21 percent tax rate (our calculation). That is 3.8 percent of the $20.16 consensus for 2027 and would not on its own break the thesis, but it shows how much of the earnings beat depends on one accounting estimate.
