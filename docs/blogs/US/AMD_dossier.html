1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Business Cycles

Core Investor Question: How did Advanced Micro Devices evolve from a 1969 Silicon Valley second-source chipmaker into a fabless data center compute powerhouse, and how did its revenue and margins behave across past semiconductor boom-bust cycles?

1.1 Founding Roots (1969–2009): The Second-Source Survivor Goes Fabless

Advanced Micro Devices, Inc. (AMD) started on May 1, 1969, in Sunnyvale, California [1]. Jerry Sanders and seven former Fairchild Semiconductor engineers founded the firm [1]. For three decades, AMD lived in Intel's shadow as a second-source x86 chip supplier. In 1982, IBM forced Intel to sign a cross-license with AMD to secure a backup PC vendor. That legal pact gave AMD its lasting right to design x86 chips. Yet owning chip factories nearly broke the firm.

In 2006, AMD paid $5.4 billion for graphics maker ATI Technologies. That deal added Radeon GPUs and seeded AMD's future in accelerated compute. Soon after, ATI's debt hit the 2008 financial crisis and rising factory costs. AMD could not match Intel's fab spending. In March 2009, AMD spun off its fabs into GlobalFoundries and turned fabless [1]. The move avoided bankruptcy, but rigid Wafer Supply Agreement minimums tied AMD to older nodes for years.

1.2 The Near-Death Trough & Lisa Su's Chiplet Revolution (2014–2021)

By October 2014, when Dr. Lisa Su became President and Chief Executive Officer, AMD stood on the brink of distress. Its Bulldozer CPU architecture trailed Intel on performance and power efficiency, server CPU share had collapsed below 1%, and annual revenue fell to $4,319 million in FY2016 with a GAAP operating loss of $373 million and a net loss of $498 million [5]. Stockholders' equity stood at just $477 million against $1,435 million in long-term debt [5].

Su made two defining strategic bets that reshaped the semiconductor industry. First, she concentrated AMD's scarce R&D budget on a clean-sheet CPU core called Zen, led by chief architect Jim Keller and later Mike Clark. Second, rather than building giant monolithic silicon dies that suffered poor manufacturing yields, AMD pioneered a modular chiplet architecture stitched together by high-speed Infinity Fabric interconnects. By pairing small compute chiplets built on Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company's (TSMC) leading-edge nodes with mature I/O dies, AMD slashed manufacturing costs per core and scaled core counts faster than Intel.

The payoff was dramatic. Zen debuted in 2017 across Ryzen PC processors and EPYC server chips, returning AMD to GAAP operating profitability ($127 million on $5,253 million revenue) [5]. As Intel stumbled on its 10-nanometer manufacturing node between 2018 and 2021, AMD captured wave after wave of cloud server and desktop share. Revenue surged from $6,731 million in FY2019 to $16,434 million in FY2021, while GAAP operating income climbed nearly sixfold from $631 million to $3,648 million [5]. Free cash flow jumped from $276 million in FY2019 to $3,220 million in FY2021, allowing AMD to eliminate virtually all long-term debt [5].

1.3 The Xilinx Diversification & The 2022–2023 Semiconductor Digestion Cycle

In February 2022, AMD closed its transformative $49 billion all-stock acquisition of Xilinx, the global leader in Field Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) and adaptive System-on-Chip (SoC) silicon [1]. The deal added high-margin embedded revenues across aerospace, defense, industrial, automotive, and communications, while also bringing $24.1 billion in acquisition-related intangibles and nearly $24 billion in goodwill onto AMD's balance sheet [5]. Later in 2022, AMD also acquired Pensando Systems for $1.9 billion to add programmable Data Processing Units (DPUs) for cloud networking.

During the 2023 slump, AMD kept R&D high and pushed hard into AI chips. Its Instinct MI300X GPU scaled past $5 billion in FY2024 sales. Total FY2024 revenue rose 13.7% to $25,785 million, while GAAP EBIT recovered to $1,900 million [1][5]. Next, AMD bought key pieces to close the systems gap with NVIDIA. It bought European AI lab Silo AI for $665 million cash in August 2024 [13]. In March 2025, it bought hyperscale rack designer ZT Systems for $4,409 million [2]. Seven months later, AMD sold ZT's low-margin factories to Sanmina for $2.4 billion headline cash plus stock and an earn-out [2][8].

1.4 The AI Pivot, Rack-Scale Engineering & Mega-Deals (2024–2026)

Rather than cutting R&D during the 2023 trough, AMD accelerated its pivot into generative AI accelerators. The Instinct MI300X GPU ramped from zero to over $5 billion in FY2024 data center AI sales, lifting consolidated revenue 13.7% to $25,785 million and restoring GAAP operating income to $1,900 million [1][5]. To close the software and systems gap against NVIDIA, AMD executed an aggressive sequence of capability acquisitions: European AI lab Silo AI ($665 million cash, August 2024) for enterprise model optimization [13]; hyperscale server designer ZT Systems ($4,409 million total consideration, March 2025) to build full rack-scale AI systems, followed by the October 2025 divestiture of ZT's low-margin manufacturing arm to Sanmina ($2.4 billion headline cash plus stock and earn-out) to preserve AMD's capital-light model [2][8].

In FY2025, AMD hit $34,639 million in revenue (+34.3% YoY) despite absorbing an ~$800 million Q2 2025 inventory and reserve charge after U.S. export controls restricted MI308 shipments to China [1][9]. Then, between October 2025 and November 2026, AMD fundamentally altered its scale and risk profile. It signed twin 6-gigawatt Instinct MI450 GPU partnerships with OpenAI (October 2025) and Meta Platforms (February 2026), issuing each partner a performance-based warrant for up to 160 million shares at $0.01 per share [6][7]. In Q3 2026, initial Helios rack-scale and MI450 shipments drove Data Center revenue up 97% YoY to $8,546 million and total revenue to a record $13,377 million (+45% YoY), with Q4 2026 revenue guided to roughly $17.0 billion [2][3]. Finally, in November 2026, AMD closed two frontier AI acquisitions totaling ~$11.1 billion: Fei-Fei Li's spatial-intelligence lab World Labs (~$8.2 billion all-stock) and specialized inference-chip startup Taalas (~$2.9 billion cash and stock) [2][10][11][12].

2.2 Segment 1: Data Center (59.8% of 9M 2026 Revenue; $6,584M Operating Income)

Data Center sells EPYC server CPUs, Instinct AI GPUs, Pensando DPUs and AI NICs, data-center FPGAs, and Helios rack-scale systems [1][2][14]. EPYC CPUs run cloud servers, enterprise workloads, and AI host nodes. By Q1 2026, EPYC hit a record 46.2% share of x86 server CPU revenue per Mercury Research, up from 41.3% in Q4 2025 [17][21]. On the GPU side, AMD moved to a yearly release pace: MI300X in 2024, 3nm MI350/MI355X in June 2025, and HBM4-based MI450 in H2 2026 [3][14][20].

Concrete Real-World Example (Oracle Cloud Infrastructure & OpenAI Helios): Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI) builds giant AI clusters for model training and inference [19]. OCI faces two major pain points: steep single-vendor GPU prices and intense rack power limits. To solve both, OCI ordered an initial 50,000-GPU supercluster built on AMD Helios racks starting in Q3 2026 [3][19]. Each liquid-cooled Helios rack packs 72 Instinct MI455X GPUs, 31 TB of HBM4 memory, 18 6th Gen EPYC 'Venice' CPUs, and Pensando 'Vulcano' AI NICs [3][14]. The rack links chips at 260 TB/s and runs open ROCm 10 software [3][14]. AMD earns revenue by selling these silicon modules, NICs, and rack designs to cloud buyers and partners like HPE, Lenovo, and Sanmina [2][3][8]. OpenAI and Meta also signed binding 1-gigawatt initial MI450 deals within 6-gigawatt frameworks, backed by equity warrants [6][7].

2.3 Segment 2: Client and Gaming (31.8% of 9M 2026 Revenue; $1,730M Operating Income)

Client and Gaming pairs two very different units [1]. Client ($10,640 million in FY2025; $8,896 million in 9M 2026, +17.9% YoY) sells Ryzen PC chips, AI PC APUs, and Threadripper workstations [1][2][3]. Gaming ($3,910 million in FY2025; $2,272 million in 9M 2026, -25.9% YoY) makes semi-custom chips for PlayStation and Xbox plus Radeon graphics cards [1][2]. As current consoles age, Gaming sales dropped 40% YoY to $773 million in Q3 2026 [2][3]. That decline pulled segment operating margin down from 19.6% in FY2025 to 15.5% in 9M 2026 [1][2].

Concrete Real-World Example (Commercial AI Laptops & Consoles): A large software firm refreshing 10,000 developer laptops wants staff to run local AI coding models offline without draining batteries. PC makers like HP and Lenovo buy AMD's Ryzen AI Max PRO 400 Series chips for these mobile workstations and Halo desktops [3]. Each chip pairs Zen CPU cores, Radeon graphics, and an XDNA NPU on unified memory. AMD books revenue per chip shipped to OEMs on forecast purchase orders, earning stronger margins on commercial PRO lines [1][3]. In Gaming, Sony co-funds semi-custom chip design and buys millions of lower-margin console APUs over a seven-year cycle [1][2].

Built on the 2022 Xilinx deal, Embedded sells Versal adaptive SoCs, FPGAs, System-on-Modules, and embedded CPUs [1][2]. 3. Distribution, Supply Chain & Unit Economics

Core Investor Question: How does AMD manufacture and distribute leading-edge AI silicon without owning wafer fabs, what bottlenecks and off-balance-sheet obligations emerged in 2026, and how does $100 of FY2025 revenue convert into free cash flow?

3.1 Three-Tier Go-to-Market: Hyperscaler Co-Design, Tier-1 OEMs & Global Distribution

AMD's ~31,000 staff sell products through three main channels [1]. First, dedicated engineering teams sell directly to cloud giants (Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, Google, AWS) and AI labs (OpenAI, Anthropic) [1][3][6][7]. These deals involve 12-to-24-month co-design of Helios racks, liquid cooling, custom MI450 variants, and ROCm software [3][7][14]. Second, AMD sells through Tier-1 OEMs and ODMs like HPE, Dell, Lenovo, Supermicro, and Sanmina [1][3][8]. Third, global distributors like Avnet and Arrow reach thousands of industrial, embedded, and retail buyers [1].

In Item 1 of its FY2025 Form 10-K, AMD notes that normal commercial orders run on customer forecasts without binding minimums [1]. To lock in multi-gigawatt AI demand, AMD changed tactics in late 2025 and 2026. It secured binding initial 1-gigawatt commitments from OpenAI and Meta, backed by penny warrants for 160 million shares each (320 million total) [6][7]. Meanwhile, MG&A expenses were $4,144 million (12.0% of sales) in FY2025 and $3,952 million (11.2%) in 9M 2026 [1][2].

3.2 Fabless Supply Chain: TSMC Concentration, CoWoS/HBM Bottlenecks & ATMP Partners

AMD owns zero wafer factories [1]. Instead, it relies on TSMC as its sole foundry for nearly all leading-edge CPUs, AI GPUs, and FPGAs (4nm, 3nm, and 2nm nodes) [1][14][20]. GlobalFoundries supplies older 12nm and 14nm I/O dies, while UMC and Samsung make legacy logic chips [1]. AI GPU output also faces two tight physical choke points: TSMC CoWoS packaging and HBM3E/HBM4 memory from SK Hynix, Micron, and Samsung. Each Helios rack uses 31 TB of HBM4 across 72 MI455X GPUs, so memory supply directly caps quarterly sales [14].

For final chip assembly and test (ATMP), AMD uses Asia-Pacific partners [1]. Key partners include its two Tongfu Microelectronics joint ventures (in China and Malaysia), plus SPIL and KYEC in Taiwan [1]. For full Helios racks, selling ZT Systems' factories in October 2025 made Sanmina a key capital-light rack builder [2][8]. AMD kept ZT's rack-design engineers while avoiding factory overhead [2][8]. This asset-light setup kept FY2025 continuing-ops CapEx at $974 million (2.8% of sales), though 9M 2026 CapEx rose to $1,845 million (5.2%) for internal testbeds [1][2].

3.3 Working Capital Strain & The $81.8 Billion Off-Balance-Sheet Commitment Surge

Owning no fabs saves factory capex, yet locking in wafers and HBM4 requires heavy working capital. From year-end FY2025 to September 26, 2026, AMD's inventory jumped 44.7% from $7,920 million to $11,463 million [1][2].
