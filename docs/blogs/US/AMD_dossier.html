1. What the company does

AMD is a chip designer. It does not own factories, and it relies on outside manufacturers to build what it designs.[3] It reports three segments. Data Center sells EPYC server processors, Instinct accelerators (the graphics-style chips that train and run AI models), networking chips and programmable chips. In the second quarter of 2026 it earned $6,718 million, which is 58% of the company's $11,536 million of revenue and more than double the $3,240 million of a year earlier.[3] Client and Gaming sells Ryzen processors for laptops and desktops, Radeon graphics cards and custom chips for game consoles, and it earned $3,841 million. Embedded sells chips for networking gear, factories, aircraft and test equipment, and it earned $977 million.[3]

Customers pay AMD in two ways. Most revenue comes from selling chips, either to cloud providers such as Amazon, Microsoft and Google or to server makers such as HPE, Dell, Lenovo and Supermicro.[8] A newer stream is rack-scale systems. Helios packages EPYC processors, Instinct accelerators and networking into one rack. AMD develops and licenses it for customers and their manufacturing partners to build, and the company says it does not manufacture or sell the finished racks.[2] AMD also owned the manufacturing arm of ZT Systems and sold it in 2025, which brought in $1,356 million of cash.[1] The structure matters because AMD keeps the design and the chip profit while others carry the assembly work.

The AI business rests on a few very large customers, measured in gigawatts. One gigawatt is one billion watts of power capacity. OpenAI agreed in October 2025 to deploy up to six gigawatts of AMD GPUs, with the first gigawatt of MI450 chips set to begin in the second half of 2026.[11] Meta signed a six-gigawatt agreement in February 2026.[12] Anthropic will deploy up to two gigawatts of MI450 chips in Helios racks, with the first gigawatt starting in the first half of 2027.[15] On October 27, 2026 AMD said Helios racks are running at Meta, where initial systems are installed and being tested before a broader deployment.[16] These are announced deployments, not reported sales, and that difference matters throughout this report.

Since July AMD has also made four moves that use cash or shares. It committed in July to invest up to $5 billion in Anthropic, one of its customers, with payments expected through fiscal 2028.[15][2] It sold $4.75 billion of senior notes on August 17, which more than doubles its borrowings.[13] On September 28 it agreed to buy World Labs, an AI research lab led by Fei-Fei Li, in an all-stock deal valued at about $8.2 billion that should close by the end of 2026.[14][40] On October 27 it said it will invest $1 billion in Meta's equity offering.[16][17] Each move supports the AI story, and each is a claim on cash or on the future share count.

What a customer warrant is, and why it matters to an owner

A warrant is a right to buy shares at a fixed price. AMD gave OpenAI in October 2025 and Meta in February 2026 a warrant each for up to 160 million shares at an exercise price of $0.01, which is almost free.[11][12] The shares vest, meaning the right becomes usable, in steps as the customer buys more Instinct GPUs and as AMD's stock price reaches set levels, rising to $600 per share for the final step.[11][12] As of June 27, 2026 no warrant shares had vested, so the warrants had no effect on the financial statements yet.[2] Together the two warrants cover 320 million shares, which is 19.6% of the 1,632 million shares outstanding.[2]

This is the price AMD pays for committed demand. If every step were reached, each owner's slice of the company would shrink by about 16%. Full vesting needs twelve gigawatts of purchases, so it is a multi-year outcome. The first step for each customer comes after the first gigawatt is delivered or shipped, and the filings we read do not give the size of each step.[11][12] The sources we read also do not say whether analysts' 2027 forecasts already count these shares, so we treat the dilution as a risk to those forecasts (Chapter 5 measures it).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

AMD's clearest proof of competitive strength is share in server processors, the market it has attacked for a decade. Mercury Research reports that AMD held 34.5% of server processor units in the second quarter of 2026, up from 27.3% a year earlier. If only Intel Xeon and AMD EPYC chips were counted, AMD would hold 46.4%.[21] Management says server revenue set a record for the fifth quarter in a row, that cloud and enterprise sales each grew more than 70%, and that demand for the next server chip, Venice, is stronger than for any earlier EPYC generation.[8] A server stays in use for years, so each win lasts.

The financial signature of that strength is gross margin, the share of revenue left after the direct cost of making the chips. GAAP gross margin was 49.4% in fiscal 2024 and 49.5% in fiscal 2025, and it was 53.2% over the twelve months to June 27, 2026.[1][3] On the non-GAAP basis it was 57% in the fourth quarter of 2025 and 56% in the second quarter of 2026, and the third-quarter outlook is about 56%.[5][3] Management says server prices and unit sales both rose, and that AMD has been adding supply.[8] The chief financial officer also said that data center AI margins are currently "slightly below company average."[8]

The weaker side of the position is AI accelerators. Nvidia's latest quarter shows the gap: revenue of $96.2 billion, up 106%, including $89.0 billion from data centers, against $6.7 billion for AMD's whole Data Center segment.[32][3] Nvidia earns a 74.7% gross margin and a 65.2% operating margin over twelve months. AMD earns 55.7% and 15.8% on the same data vendor's basis.[34][25] That gap is the market's verdict on pricing power today. AMD does not report Instinct revenue separately and discloses only that Instinct sales more than doubled.[8] The multi-gigawatt agreements are future installations, and the proof arrives in the next two or three quarters.

AMD competes on four fronts at once. Intel is the largest x86 rival and reports on October 29.[35] Arm-based server chips reached a record 13.6% of server units in the second quarter, by Mercury's estimate.[21] AMD depends on outside manufacturers and on supplies of memory, substrates and manufacturing capacity, which its own risk list names.[3] Export rules can also cost real money. An $800 million inventory charge tied to a United States export control on the MI308 chip hit the second quarter of 2025, and China including Hong Kong was $7,751 million, or 22%, of fiscal 2025 revenue.[3][1]

What this means for an owner

The server business is a proven franchise that earns well today. The AI accelerator business is a large and credible promise whose proof arrives with the first Helios shipments, due in the fourth quarter, and with the 2027 ramp.[8] That split is why the stock can fall hard on a good report: investors already pay for the promise. The next chapter turns the promise into five measurable tests and then checks whether management has delivered on past statements.

The five-year record shows a business that was reshaped by acquisitions and then grew quickly. Revenue rose from $16,434 million in fiscal 2021 to $34,639 million in fiscal 2025 and to $41,305 million over the twelve months to June 27, 2026.[19][1][3] AMD bought Xilinx and Pensando in 2022, which lifted goodwill from $289 million to $24,177 million and added large amortization charges that depress GAAP profit.[20] Amortization of acquired intangibles was $2,254 million in fiscal 2025 and $2,214 million over the last twelve months, so GAAP operating margin of 15.7% understates the cash earning power.[1][3]

The planned investments of $5 billion and $1 billion will then use cash and reduce net cash.[13][2][16] Cash flow has also not kept pace with revenue lately: analysts expect fiscal 2026 free cash flow of $7.6 billion, about the same as the last twelve months, because capital spending is set to more than double to $2.2 billion.[26]

Weakest assumption 3: customer commitments convert into shipments without new strings
