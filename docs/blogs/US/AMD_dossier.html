1. What the company does

AMD designs chips and sells them to companies that build computers. It owns no factories for its main products: wafers for its server, PC and adaptive chips are made by Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC), and a second contract maker, GLOBALFOUNDRIES, supplies some older nodes.[2] The company had about 31,000 employees at the end of 2025.[2] A chip designer of this kind earns money from the quality of its designs and from the price its customers pay per chip, so the report keeps asking one question: how many chips will customers buy, and at what price?

Fiscal 2025 revenue was $34,639 million, split across three reporting segments.[4][3] Data Center, which sells EPYC server processors, Instinct AI accelerators, networking cards and programmable chips to cloud and AI companies, brought in $16,635 million, or 48.0%.[3] Client and Gaming, which sells Ryzen PC processors, Radeon graphics cards and chips for game consoles, brought in $14,550 million, or 42.0%.[3] Embedded, which sells programmable and embedded processors to industrial, communications and aerospace customers, brought in $3,454 million, or 10.0%.[3] By the June 2026 quarter the mix had moved sharply: Data Center was $6,718 million of $11,536 million, or 58.2%.[15]

Who pays? Cloud providers such as Amazon Web Services, Microsoft, Google and Oracle buy EPYC processors for their data centers, and AI laboratories such as OpenAI, Meta and Anthropic buy Instinct accelerators to train and run AI models.[32] PC makers such as Dell, HP and Lenovo buy Ryzen chips, and console makers buy custom chips. No single customer reached 10% of revenue in fiscal 2025 or 2024, although one Client and Gaming customer was 18% of revenue in 2023.[6] By billing location, $11,363 million came from the United States, $7,751 million from China including Hong Kong (22.4% of revenue), $5,186 million from Taiwan and $4,284 million from Singapore.[6]

The product that matters most for the next two years is a full AI rack called Helios. It combines 72 Instinct GPUs (graphics processing units, chips that do the heavy AI arithmetic), EPYC processors, Pensando networking and ROCm software, the free programming tools that let developers run AI models on AMD chips.[40][32] Selling a whole rack means AMD sells more content per customer and competes directly with Nvidia's rack systems. To avoid running a factory, AMD sold the manufacturing arm of ZT Systems, a rack builder it had bought, to Sanmina for $2.4 billion in cash and shares and kept the design team.[7]

Three large AI customers have signed multi-year commitments. OpenAI agreed in October 2025 to deploy 6 gigawatts of AMD GPUs, with the first gigawatt starting in the second half of 2026.[26] Meta agreed in February 2026 to deploy up to 6 gigawatts, with the first shipments in the second half of 2026.[28] Anthropic announced in 2026 up to 2 gigawatts, starting in the first half of 2027.[32] A gigawatt is a unit of electrical power, used here as a measure of data-center size. The chief financial officer put the revenue at double-digit billions of dollars per gigawatt.[32] OpenAI and Meta each also received a warrant, a right to buy up to 160 million AMD shares for $0.01 each, which vests only as purchases and stock-price targets are met.[17]

Management sizes the prize in its own numbers. It expects the market for data-center AI accelerators to grow more than 45% a year to about $1.4 trillion by 2030, and the server processor market to grow more than 50% a year to about $220 billion by 2030.[32] These are company estimates, not audited facts, and the whole investment case depends on how much of that market AMD wins and at what profit.

  • Hyperscaler: one of the very largest cloud companies, such as Microsoft or Amazon.
  • HBM (high-bandwidth memory): the fast stacked memory that sits next to an AI chip; its supply and price affect AI-chip cost.
  • ASIC: a custom chip built for one customer's workload, an alternative to a general-purpose GPU.
  • Relative strength index (RSI) is a 0-to-100 meter of how fast a price has moved; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • Average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing in dollars; a stop placed closer than one ATR sits inside normal daily noise.
  • Earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit divided by the number of shares.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and paying for equipment.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is the profit a business earns on each dollar invested in it.
  • Gross margin is the share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of making the product; operating margin is the share left after all running costs; net margin is the share left after every cost.
  • Net debt is borrowings minus cash and short-term investments; a negative figure means net cash.
  • Price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by earnings per share; a forward P/E uses expected earnings.
  • Guidance is a company's own forecast for the coming period.
  • Dilution is the rise in share count that shrinks each existing share's slice of the company.
  • A base is a flat stretch of trading that comes before a move, and its pivot is the top of that stretch; a breakout is a move above the pivot on heavy volume.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend; Stage 3 is a topping or pullback phase.

  • Entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the gain to a target divided by the loss to the stop: (target - price) / (price - stop).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A strong position is not the same as a safe one. AMD's strength is breadth: it sells server CPUs, data-center GPUs, PC processors and programmable chips under one roof. The 10-K names Intel and Nvidia as its main rivals in the data center, and notes that some customers build their own chips.[1] AMD says it gained x86 server revenue share in the June 2026 quarter, that it has had five consecutive record server CPU quarters and that its fifth-generation EPYC chips power nearly one-third of more than 1,600 public cloud instance types.[32] These are company statements, so we treat them as evidence of momentum, not as audited share data.

In AI accelerators, AMD is the challenger. Nvidia reported revenue of $96,221 million for its July 2026 quarter, which is more than eight times AMD's $11,536 million for the whole company, and Nvidia's GAAP operating margin was 66.2% against AMD's 17.3%.[43][13] AMD's argument is cost and openness. It says Helios delivers up to 15% more throughput at the same rack power and up to 30% more tokens per dollar than the competition on a range of inference workloads, a claim made by the company and not yet checked by us.[32] Meta's chief executive described the 6-gigawatt deal as a way to diversify its compute, which is the real demand signal: large buyers want a second source.[28]

The competitive edge also depends on technology cadence. AMD plans a new rack-scale platform every year: Helios with MI450-series GPUs now, then a platform with MI500 GPUs and the Verano CPU in 2027.[32] It showed a 192-core Verano chip at the Open Compute Project summit on October 12.[40] A yearly cadence helps if every launch lands on time, and each late launch hands a quarter to Nvidia.

Where the business is weak

First, profit per sales dollar is far lower than the leaders'. AMD's GAAP gross margin, the share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of making the product, was 53.2% over the twelve months to June 2026, against 75.0% for Nvidia and 69.1% for Broadcom in their latest quarters.[13][43][44] Management says Helios gross margin will be slightly below the company average, so the AI ramp may dilute the margin before it lifts profit.[32]

Second, AMD relies on one foundry. The 10-K says AMD relies on TSMC for all wafers at 7 nanometers or smaller, which covers its newest chips.[2] Helios also uses about 50% more high-bandwidth memory than a rival design, according to an analyst on the August call, and AMD said it has good visibility into memory supply for 2027 while customers watch memory cost.[32] A memory shortage would hit AMD's costs and its customers' budgets at once.

Third, the biggest customers are paid in shares. The OpenAI and Meta warrants cover up to 320 million shares, which is 19.6% of the 1,632.475 million shares outstanding, and the vesting schedule and stock-price targets are redacted in the exhibit filed with the SEC.[17][27] At today's price of $528.75, one 160-million-share warrant would be worth about $84.6 billion if it vested and was exercised in full, which is large next to the 'tens of billions of dollars' of revenue AMD said in October 2025 it expects from the OpenAI deal.[26] We do not know how much will vest, so this is a size check, not a forecast.

Fourth, AMD is putting its own balance sheet behind the AI buildout. Unconditional purchase commitments rose from $12,166 million at December 27, 2025 to $30,276 million at June 27, 2026.[8][16] AMD also guarantees up to $4.1 billion of data-center lease payments, has leases not yet started worth $4.5 billion, signed another $9.5 billion of data-center leases after quarter-end, and committed up to $5.0 billion of investments.[16] On October 13, a data-center developer, Fermi America, announced a credit support agreement with AMD for the 222-megawatt first phase of its Texas project.[39] Each of these commitments makes sense if customers buy the racks, and each becomes a cost if they do not.

What this means for an owner: the business is strong on product breadth, server-CPU momentum and customer wins, and weaker on margin, single-foundry reliance, share-based customer incentives and a fast-growing pile of fixed commitments. Chapter 2B scores these points one at a time.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has a clear pattern of guiding low and delivering higher. In each of the last six quarters, revenue came in above the midpoint of the range given three months earlier, by 3.0% to 7.0%.[24][23][22][21][20][19][18] Guidance is management's own forecast, so repeated beats mean the forecast is cautious. The company also kept its gross margin guidance in five of six quarters and beat it in the sixth.[21][20]

Two details temper the record. The December 2025 beat of $670 million over the $9.6 billion guide included about $390 million of MI308 chip sales to China that the guide excluded, and the 57% non-GAAP gross margin included about $360 million of reserve release.[21][20] Capital allocation is the weaker half: AMD repurchased $1,316 million of shares in 2025 but only $221 million in the first half of 2026, while it issued two large warrants, agreed an $8.2 billion all-stock acquisition and raised $4.75 billion of debt.[5][14][30][29]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue grew from $16,434 million in fiscal 2021 to $34,639 million in fiscal 2025, a compound rate of 20.5% a year, and the twelve months to June 27, 2026 reached $41,305 million.[10][4][13] The path was uneven: revenue fell 3.9% in 2023, and operating income, the profit left after all running costs, was only $401 million that year.[4] The 2022 purchase of Xilinx, paid for in stock, explains much of this. The balance sheet now carries $25,470 million of goodwill and $15,635 million of acquired intangibles, mostly from that deal, and the amortization of those intangibles ($2,254 million in 2025) lowers GAAP profit every year.[11][12][13][5]

Two things have changed since 2024. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one weak assumption. We name it, say what evidence would prove it wrong, and say when that evidence arrives. The next dated events are Meta's results on October 21, Microsoft's on October 28 and AMD's own on November 3, 2026.[41][42][31] Meta and Microsoft are both AMD customers, so their capital spending plans arrive before AMD's own numbers.[28][32]

The reverse DCF assumes cash flow can grow twelve-fold

The weakest assumption behind 28.4% is that free cash flow can rise from $7.736 billion to about $94.2 billion in year ten. The cost of getting there is already visible: unconditional commitments rose from $12.166 billion to $30.276 billion in six months, and $17.386 billion of that falls due in the second half of 2026.[8][16] In the June quarter AMD generated $1.6 billion of free cash flow, and inventory rose to $8.468 billion.[32][13]

The evidence that would show this wrong is two quarters in which cash from operations falls below net income, or inventory grows faster than revenue. The first test is the September quarter report on November 3 and the 10-Q that follows.[31] If either shows up, the growth needed from the base rises and the case weakens at once.

The earnings estimate assumes the AI ramp starts on time and customers keep paying

The $15.76 figure for 2027 assumes the fourth-quarter Helios ramp happens and that Data Center revenue more than doubles in 2027, as management says.[32] Consensus fourth-quarter revenue is $16.11 billion, which is 24% above the September guide of $13 billion.[34][18] The risks named on the August call and at the October keynote are supply and complexity: Helios is a 72-GPU rack, yields are expected to improve over several quarters, and memory is a cost and supply constraint.[32][40]

A cut to 2027 spending plans by Meta or Microsoft would hit the low case of $9.60. They report on October 21 and October 28.[41][42] AMD also signed a credit support agreement for a data-center developer's lease on October 13, which links AMD's results to the financing of its customers' buildouts.[39][16]

The price target assumes the multiple stays near its median

Our central price multiplies earnings by 38.76, the median of three year-end readings of 44.35, 27.74 and 38.76.[36] Three data points are thin. The 10-year Treasury yield was 5.10% on October 16, and the volatility index jumped 21% that day.[34]

The stress that matters most is the multiple. Hold earnings at $15.76 and drop the multiple to the three-year low of 27.74: the price is $437.18, which is 17.3% below today's close, so the central case then offers a loss. Hold the multiple at 38.76 and cut earnings to the Street low of $9.60: the price is $372.10, 29.6% below the close. The first stress leaves the stock 3.1% above the $424.03 base low, and the second takes it through that low. The first stress also changes the probability-weighted result. If only the central branch is de-rated to 27.74, the weighted value becomes 0.25 x $898.09 + 0.45 x $437.18 + 0.30 x $266.30 = $501.14, which is 5.22% below the close instead of 9.6% above it. A positive scenario-weighted gain is therefore fragile and is not an edge.

The share count may grow faster than the estimate assumes

We assumed about 1.66 billion shares, the company's own count for the September quarter.[32] The OpenAI and Meta warrants could add up to 320 million shares, and the World Labs deal adds about 15.5 million at today's price.[17][30] We do not know whether the $15.76 consensus includes the warrants. If all 320 million shares vested and were exercised, the count would be 1.98 billion and 2027 earnings would be $13.21 a share, which gives $512.13 at the median multiple, 3.1% below the close. Vesting needs GPU purchases and stock-price targets, so full dilution is unlikely, but the schedule is redacted in the filed exhibit and cannot be checked.[27] The practical consequence is that the earnings estimate carries a dilution risk that grows with success.
