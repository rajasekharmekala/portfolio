1. What the company does

AMD is a chip designer. It does not own factories; it relies on outside manufacturers to make its products[3]. It reports three segments. The Data Center segment sells EPYC processors for servers, Instinct accelerators (the graphics-style chips that train and run AI models), networking chips and programmable chips. In the second quarter of 2026 it earned $6,718 million, which is 58% of the company's $11,536 million of revenue and double the $3,240 million of a year earlier[4]. The Client and Gaming segment sells Ryzen processors for laptops and desktops, Radeon graphics cards and custom chips for game consoles, and it earned $3,841 million. The Embedded segment sells chips for networking gear, factories, aircraft and test equipment, and it earned $977 million[4].

Customers pay AMD in two ways. Most revenue comes from selling chips, either to cloud providers such as Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Oracle or to server and PC makers such as Dell, HP and Lenovo[17]. A newer stream is rack-scale systems. AMD's Helios product bundles EPYC processors, Instinct accelerators and networking into one rack, and AMD says it develops and licenses these platforms for customers and their manufacturing partners to build[3]. AMD previously owned the manufacturing arm of ZT Systems, which built such racks, and sold it; the divestiture brought in $1,356 million in 2025[1]. The point of the structure is that AMD sells the design and the chips, while others carry the assembly work and its thin margins.

The AI business rests on a handful of very large customers, measured in gigawatts of computing power (one gigawatt is the output of a large power plant, and AMD says each gigawatt is worth "double-digit billions" of revenue to it[17]). OpenAI agreed in October 2025 to deploy 6 gigawatts of AMD GPUs, starting with 1 gigawatt of MI450 chips in the second half of 2026[11]. Meta agreed in February 2026 to deploy up to 6 gigawatts, with first shipments also scheduled for the second half of 2026[10]. Anthropic will deploy up to 2 gigawatts, with the first gigawatt beginning in the first half of 2027, and Oracle plans a 50,000-GPU cluster starting in calendar Q3 2026[17][12]. These are announced commitments, not reported sales, and the difference matters throughout this report.

In the last two months AMD also agreed to buy World Labs, an artificial-intelligence research lab led by Fei-Fei Li, in an all-stock deal worth about $8.2 billion that is expected to close by the end of 2026[14]. It sold $4.75 billion of new senior notes on August 17, 2026, which more than doubles its borrowings[15]. Both moves show a company spending heavily to secure its position in AI, which is good for the long-term story and a cost to today's owners.

What a customer warrant is, and why it matters to an owner

A warrant is a right to buy shares at a fixed price. AMD gave OpenAI and Meta a warrant each for up to 160 million shares at an exercise price of $0.01, which is almost free[9][3]. The shares vest, meaning the right becomes usable, in steps as the customer buys more AMD GPUs and as AMD's stock price reaches set levels, and for Meta the last step requires a price of $600 per share. As of June 27, 2026 no warrant shares had vested, so the warrants had no effect on the financial statements yet[3]. Together the two warrants cover 320 million shares, equal to 19.6% of the 1,632 million shares now outstanding. That is the price AMD pays for committed demand. If every step is reached, dilution would be about a fifth, which means each owner's slice of the company shrinks by that much. It would happen only after AMD has sold roughly twelve gigawatts of product at the stock prices the warrants require.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

AMD's strongest evidence of competitive power is share gained in server processors, the market it attacked for a decade. Mercury Research reports that AMD held 34.5% of server processor units in the second quarter of 2026, up 7.3 percentage points from a year earlier and 1.3 points from the previous quarter[22]. Mercury also estimates that, if AMD and Intel counted data center chips the same way, AMD would hold 46.4%[22]. Management says it gained revenue share again, that cloud and enterprise server sales each grew more than 70%, and that demand for its next server chip, Venice, is stronger than for any earlier EPYC generation[17]. This is a real moat in the practical sense: cloud buyers move to AMD because each server does more work per watt and per dollar, and a server stays in use for five years or more, so each win lasts.

The financial signature of that strength is the gross margin, which is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of making the chips. Gross margin was 44.9% in 2022 and 49.5% in 2025, and it was 53.2% over the last twelve months[2][1][4]. AMD guides to about 56% on a non-GAAP basis for the third quarter, meaning before stock pay and acquisition amortization[4]. Management says that second-quarter server sales rose on both more units and higher prices per chip, and that it has been adding wafer, back-end and substrate capacity all year to meet server demand[17].

The weaker side of the position is AI accelerators. Nvidia still dominates that market, and the gigawatt agreements are future installations that have not yet become booked revenue[28]. AMD does not report Instinct revenue separately, so the size of its AI business today cannot be checked; the only disclosure is that Instinct sales "more than doubled" year over year[17]. Management also concedes that data center AI margins are currently slightly below the company average[17]. A second weakness is the number of customers. A handful of AI labs will account for most of the new demand, and each of them is also building its own chips or buying from Nvidia.

Where AMD is exposed

AMD has to compete on four fronts at once. Intel is growing again, with second-quarter revenue up 25% to $16.1 billion and its Data Center and AI division up 59% to $6.3 billion[22]. Arm-based server chips from Amazon, Google, Microsoft and Nvidia could take some of the growth that x86 chips once captured[22]. AMD also depends on third parties to make its chips, and its own risk list names the supply of memory, substrates and manufacturing capacity[3][4]. Finally, export rules can cost real money: the April 2025 China licensing rule caused an $800 million inventory charge in the second quarter of 2025, partly offset later, for a net charge of about $440 million for the year[1][3]. The company has since won licenses to ship some MI308 and MI325 chips to China, but any MI325 shipments carry a 25% tariff for the required inspection in the United States.

What this means for an owner: the server business is a proven franchise that earns well today, while the AI business is a large, credible promise whose proof arrives in the next two quarters. In price terms, the setup we want is a pullback that holds support before a breakout, which is a move above a level where the stock has stalled before. A pivot is the exact price level that triggers such a breakout. The next chapter turns the promise into measurable tests.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above has a weakest link. For the reverse DCF it is the margin: the price needs free cash flow to rise to about 25% of revenue, and the trailing figure of 18.7% is helped by $2,274 million of extra supplier credit in the second quarter[4]. For the central price it is the multiple: 38.7 times is above the 27.7 times of the end of 2024 and well above the 17 to 19 times paid for Nvidia and Broadcom[23][25][26]. For the bull case it is timing, because every gigawatt shipped in 2027 depends on racks that have not been delivered yet[17]. For the bear case it is that customers keep paying: the bear numbers assume delay, not cancellation.

The strongest case against the thesis is short. AMD is priced as the second source to Nvidia in the largest build-out in the history of computing, yet the funding for that build-out comes from a small group of buyers whose own cash flow is turning negative, and AMD has paid for part of the demand with warrants, purchase commitments of $30.3 billion and an all-stock acquisition. If the buyers slow down, AMD is left with inventory of $8.5 billion, fixed commitments and a multiple that falls faster than earnings.

Fatal flaw 1: the customers cannot fund the build-out

Meta's third-quarter report showed negative free cash flow for the first time since 2012, and Nvidia's chief executive said that newer AI cloud companies have strong demand but lack the working capital to pay for it[18][19]. AMD's biggest announced customers are OpenAI, Meta and Anthropic; Meta has just reported negative free cash flow, and no source we fetched covers how the two AI labs fund their orders. If financing tightens, orders slip even if the product is excellent. The signal is a lower fourth-quarter revenue guide or a gigawatt start date that moves into 2027.

Fatal flaw 2: rack-scale execution and supply

Helios is the first rack-scale product AMD has shipped. Management expects yields to improve over the first few quarters and says data center AI margins are slightly below the company average[17]. The 10-Q lists memory supply and third-party manufacturing among the risks, and the Lam Research warning that moved the market on October 22 pointed to supply limits that run into 2027[3][19]. If a rack is late, revenue slips and the $17.4 billion of commitments due in 2026 become stock on the shelf instead of sales.

Fatal flaw 3: dilution and the multiple

The two warrants cover 320 million shares, the World Labs deal will add shares worth $8.2 billion at the price on the closing date, and diluted shares already rose 1.8% in a year[3][13][4]. At today's price the World Labs shares would number about 15.9 million, about 1% of the total, and if the price falls, more shares are issued. A stock priced at 33 times next year's earnings with growing share count has little room for a bad quarter.
