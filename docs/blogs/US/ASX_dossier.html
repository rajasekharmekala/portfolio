1. What the company does

ASE Technology Holding Co., Ltd. was formed on 30 April 2018 when Advanced Semiconductor Engineering and Siliconware Precision Industries swapped their shares into one holding company[2]. A computer chip starts as a pattern etched on a silicon wafer, made by a foundry such as TSMC. The wafer must then be cut up, wired to a package that can be soldered onto a circuit board, and tested. ASE does that back-end work for companies that design chips and for companies that make their own. It had 114,179 employees on 30 June 2026[5]. Its customers pay by the unit of work, and the biggest end markets in the second quarter of 2026 were communications chips at 41% of packaging-and-testing revenue and computing chips at 30%[6]. The five largest customers made up about 44% of that revenue, and one customer was above 10%[5].

The company reports two businesses. Assembly, testing and materials (ATM, the chip work) brought in NT$125.1 billion, or 65.5% of second-quarter sales; electronic manufacturing services (EMS, building circuit-board modules through the Universal Scientific Industrial subsidiary) brought in NT$65.4 billion[6]. The first is far better. The gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of the work, divided by revenue, and the operating margin is the same after all running costs. ATM earned a 27.3% gross margin and a 15.7% operating margin in the quarter, against 8.9% and 2.4% for EMS[5]. ATM was 66% of group revenue but 94% of operating profit[7], so the investment case is an ATM case, and EMS is mostly a low-margin passenger. In this report NT$ means New Taiwan dollars, ASE's reporting currency; at 31.94 NT$ per US dollar, NT$1 billion is about $31 million[27].

The fast-growing part is what ASE calls leading-edge advanced packaging and test, or LEAP. These are the packages that join an AI processor and its memory chips on one carrier board, such as chip-on-wafer-on-substrate (CoWoS), plus the extra testing those chips need. LEAP revenue was US$0.6 billion in 2024 and US$1.6 billion in 2025, 13% of ATM revenue[11]. For 2026 management first guided US$3.2 billion[11], then said it was tracking above US$3.5 billion[6], and it aims to double LEAP again in 2027[7]. ASE also says blended factory use ran at 80-85% in the quarter and that near-term growth is limited by how fast it can install machines and build factory space, not by orders[7].

That growth is costly. Capital spending, meaning cash spent on machines and buildings, was about US$5.5 billion in 2025 (US$3.4 billion machinery and US$2.1 billion buildings)[11]. The 2026 plan is about US$10.5 billion, of which US$4 billion is buildings and US$6.5 billion equipment[7], raised from US$8.5 billion[21]. Cash from operations was NT$168.8 billion over the twelve months to June 2026, while net payments for plant and equipment were NT$206.6 billion[2][5]. Chapter 3 shows what that gap does to free cash flow, and Chapters 5 to 7 show how it drives the verdict.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

ASE's first strength is that it does only packaging and testing. Its chief operating officer, Tien Wu, told analysts that as a pure-play OSAT ASE has no conflict with the foundry, with the substrate provider or with anybody, which he called a possible competitive advantage[7]. The second is reach: ASE says its global presence spans Taiwan, China, South Korea, Japan, Singapore, Malaysia, the Philippines, Vietnam, Mexico, Tunisia, the United States and Europe[5]. The third is timing: capacity is full. Management said wire-bond and traditional advanced packaging capacity was tight, wafer sort and final test were running near full, and blended use was 80-85%[7]. When a buyer cannot find another supplier with free machines, the supplier can pass on cost increases, and management said it is doing so in what it called a friendly pricing environment[7].

The numbers match the claim. ATM gross margin rose from 21.9% in the second quarter of 2025 to 27.3% in the second quarter of 2026[6], and management guided 28-29% for the third quarter and said the fourth quarter would probably pass its 30% ceiling[6][7]. At group level the gross margin rose from 17.0% to 21.0% and the operating margin from 6.8% to 11.1% in the same twelve months[6]. The reason is fixed cost. A packaging line costs the same to run whether it is half or fully loaded, so each extra order adds revenue with little extra cost; this is called operating leverage. That also works in reverse, which is why the next paragraph matters.

The weakness is that the same machines drain cash. In the second quarter ASE spent US$1,695 million on equipment against EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating cash profit) of US$1,450 million[6], and another US$658 million on buildings[7]. The chief financial officer, Joseph Tung, told analysts that the negative cash flow situation will remain for some time[7]. Funding comes from borrowing: interest-bearing debt rose from NT$272.9 billion at the end of 2025 to NT$306.2 billion at 30 June 2026[2][6], and in August ASE priced a US$1 billion zero-coupon convertible bond due 2031 to repay bank loans[18]. The bond converts at NT$927.2 a share, a 52% premium to the NT$610 close of 3 August[19]; at 31.94 NT$ per dollar that would add about 34 million shares, 0.8% of the total (our calculation).

The business is also cyclical. In 2023 revenue fell 13.3% to NT$581.9 billion and operating profit fell 48.7% to NT$41.6 billion, because fixed costs did not shrink with demand[2][3]. Competitors are real: the 20-F warns that foundries have expanded their own advanced packaging and names TSMC's integrated fan-out technology as a competing offering[2], Amkor reported second-quarter sales up 26% and guided $2.5-3.0 billion of 2026 capital spending[23], and China's JCET announced a RMB 7.8 billion advanced packaging plant[25]. Each of those adds capacity that could press prices if AI demand slows.

ASE sells to customers rather than to US consumers, so the effect arrives through customers' orders and pricing, and the sources fetched do not size it, so it is N/A here. Taiwan. ASE reports in NT$ and states its forecasts on an exchange-rate assumption, so a move in the NT$ changes its reported margins. The size of the effect per 1% move is not published, so it is N/A here.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on a weakest assumption. This chapter names each one, the evidence that would show it wrong, and when that evidence arrives. The red-team bear case comes first, because the verdict sits closest to it.

Red-team bear case: the capital cycle turns before the profit arrives

The strongest argument against ASE is that it is spending ahead of orders it cannot yet see. Spending is about US$10.5 billion this year and rising next year[7], funded by debt that grew NT$33 billion in six months[6][2]. Depreciation and amortization are already NT$73.5 billion over twelve months and rises with every machine installed[2][5]. If AI chip demand pauses, or if foundries and rivals take work in-house, utilization would drop from 80-85% and the high fixed costs would show. The 2023 precedent is plain: revenue fell 13.3% and operating profit fell 48.7%[2][3]. If 2027 EPS came in 21% below consensus and the multiple fell to its lower quartile, the stock would be worth $29.81, which is 24.6% below the close. The 15 and 16 October selloff shows how quickly the market can cut AI-linked stocks even after good news[32], and the position carries a one-year beta of 2.48 in the ledger, so a 10% fall in the S&P 500 could mean a 25% move in ASX[1].

Weakest assumption behind the target: that 2027 profit arrives on schedule

The base target multiplies 2027 EPS of $2.15, a 74% rise on 2026, by a median multiple. If the 2027 estimate is cut, both parts of the arithmetic move against us. Evidence that this assumption is wrong would be third-quarter EPS well below the $0.34 consensus[33], a cut of more than 5% to FY2 EPS, or ATM gross margin below the 28-29% guide. The first hard evidence comes with the third-quarter results on 29 October 2026[20]. Management said it would give LEAP detail for 2027 in about two quarters[7], so the decisive evidence is the fourth-quarter report in early 2027, whose date is N/A today.

Weakest assumption behind the cash-flow view: that spending peaks in 2026

The reverse DCF assumes cash flow can grow from a base that machine spending is currently eating. The chief financial officer said 2027 spending will be big and that it would take another quarter or so to say how big[7].
