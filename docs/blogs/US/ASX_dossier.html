1. What the company does

ASE Technology Holding is a Taiwan company. It files an annual report on Form 20-F with the US Securities and Exchange Commission and reports in New Taiwan dollars (NT$).[1] Its ordinary shares trade in Taipei under the code 3711. Each American depositary share (ADS) in New York, which is the ticker ASX, equals two ordinary shares.[4] This report quotes NT$ for company results and US dollars for the ADS price, and it uses a rate of NT$32.54 per US dollar, the latest Federal Reserve weekly figure.[39]

Customers are chip designers and chipmakers. They send finished silicon wafers to ASE. ASE cuts them, mounts them in protective packages, wires them to the outside world and tests them. The industry calls this work OSAT, short for outsourced semiconductor assembly and test, a finishing factory for chips that other firms designed and made.[1] The group has two arms. The first is assembly, test and materials (ATM), which is the chip business. The second is electronic manufacturing services (EMS), run through a subsidiary called USI, which builds finished circuit boards and modules for electronics brands.[4]

The two arms earn very different profits. In the second quarter of 2026, packaging was 52 percent of revenue, testing 13 percent and EMS 34 percent.[4] ATM made 66 percent of group revenue but 94 percent of operating profit.[16] The operating margin is profit after running costs as a share of revenue. ATM kept 15.7 cents of each revenue dollar on that basis, while EMS kept 2.4 cents.[4] EMS passes most component costs through to its customers, so its sales can swing with memory prices without the profit moving much. Management said memory price rises were behind an expected 40 percent third-quarter jump in EMS revenue.[16][5]

The part of ATM that matters most to the share price is LEAP, short for leading-edge advanced packaging and testing. These are the complex packages that join several chips side by side or stacked, such as the CoWoS method (chip-on-wafer-on-substrate) that the foundry TSMC developed for AI processors.[1][17] LEAP revenue was US$1.6 billion in 2025, or 13 percent of ATM revenue, up from US$0.6 billion in 2024.[12] LEAP guidance, which is the company's own published forecast, for 2026 started at US$3.2 billion in February, about 75 percent packaging and 25 percent testing.[12] By July the prior guidance was US$3.5 billion and LEAP revenue was tracking ahead of it, and management aims to double LEAP revenue again in 2027.[5][16]

Customers are concentrated, and most are American. The five largest customers made up 46.5 percent of 2025 revenue, and one of them, counting companies it buys for, was more than 10 percent.[1] On the ATM side in the second quarter, the top five were 44 percent of revenue and the top ten were 60 percent. In EMS the top five were 58 percent.[4] By the country where customers are headquartered, the United States was 56.7 percent of 2025 revenue and Taiwan was 18.0 percent.[1] The filings do not name the customers, so this report does not either.

The company is building as fast as it can. It had 114,179 employees on June 30, 2026, up from 105,947 at the end of 2025.[4][1] Its tester count rose to 8,348 from 6,797 a year earlier, and blended factory use ran at 80 to 85 percent, with growth held back by how fast it can install tools and finish buildings.[4][16] At the July call it had 13 new factory projects on empty land and eight purchases of existing buildings under way.[16][18]

How ASE gets paid, and what it costs to run

ASE charges a fee for each step it performs on a customer's chips, and the customer supplies the wafers. That keeps the business out of chip design and chip inventory risk. It also means that factory use matters a great deal. The 20-F says testing and leading-edge packaging carry relatively high fixed costs, so profit rises sharply when the lines are full and falls just as sharply when they are not.[1] In 2025, raw materials were 48.4 percent of revenue and labor 11.3 percent.[1]

That arithmetic explains the margin story of the last year. ATM gross margin moved from 21.9 percent in the second quarter of 2025 to 27.3 percent in the second quarter of 2026 as lines filled and the mix shifted to LEAP.[5] Management expects the fourth quarter to pass what it calls its structural ceiling of 30 percent.[16]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

ASE is strong for three reasons that the filings and the July call support. First, it is a pure-play packager: it does not make wafers or substrates, so it has no conflict with the foundries and chip designers it serves. Management calls this a competitive advantage and ties it to a first-mover edge in technology, speed, capacity and trust.[16] Second, it has held a strategic alliance with TSMC since 1997, and TSMC names it a non-exclusive preferred provider of packaging and testing for chips TSMC makes.[1] Third, its Taiwan cluster of suppliers, customers and engineers makes quick ramps possible, and management cites cluster efficiency as an advantage.[16]

Scarcity is the strongest evidence of pricing power. Management said that near-term growth is gated by installing equipment and finishing buildings, not by orders, and that even older wire-bond and test lines were close to full.[16] It also said the pricing environment is friendly and that it has been able to pass on material and component cost increases.[16] Packaging revenue rose 17.8 percent in 2025 and testing revenue 31.8 percent, and testing carries a 31.0 percent gross margin.[1] ATM revenue rose 36.3 percent year over year in the second quarter and, on unaudited figures, 51.0 percent in the third.[4][6]

Industry evidence says the demand is real beyond ASE. Amkor reported record computing revenue in the third quarter, with about a dozen customers paying in advance to secure capacity.[30][31] JCET in China reported computing revenue up 40.4 percent in the first half.[37] Powertech in Taiwan reported utilization of 85 to 90 percent in packaging.[36] TSMC's third-quarter revenue of US$46.72 billion beat its own range.[34] A press report says TSMC may raise CoWoS prices by up to 20 percent, which would point to tight supply, though it is only a report.[30]

The same facts show where the strength could crack. Fixed costs cut both ways, as the 20-F warns.[1] Customers are concentrated: one customer is more than 10 percent of revenue.[1] TSMC is both a partner and a possible competitor, because the foundry does some packaging itself. At the July call an analyst said the foundry partner was moving toward 3D and panel-level methods, and management answered that it will support whatever the customers choose, including Intel's EMIB bridge method.[16] A pure-play packager has no way to hold customers if a rival method wins, and that is the main long-term business risk.

Weak spots that do not show up in margins

The EMS arm is a low-margin business whose operating margin was 2.4 percent in the second quarter, below the 3.0 percent of the first quarter, because of product mix and higher component prices.[4] It makes up 34 percent of revenue, so it dilutes group margins and adds volatility to the sales line without adding much profit. It is not what the share price is paying for.

Cash is the other weak spot. Operating cash flow covered 59 percent of net payments for plant and equipment in the second quarter.[4] The CFO told analysts the negative cash flow will remain for some time because heavy spending runs through next year.[16] Chapter 3 shows the numbers and Chapter 5 shows what the price assumes about them.

Legal, governance and regulatory flags

The 20-F discloses a criminal case against Dr. Tien Wu, a director and the chief operating officer. It arose from a 2017 indictment that alleged insider trading in shares of SPIL during the tender offers and talks that led to ASE's takeover of SPIL. A High Court judgment of December 31, 2024 found him guilty on some charges with a 22-month sentence, the Supreme Court sent that part back in July 2025, and on February 11, 2026 the High Court found him not guilty. Prosecutors appealed that ruling on March 2, 2026. The outcome is pending and uncertain.[1] A related civil case seeks his removal from the board. None of this is a finding of company fraud, and Deloitte gave an unqualified audit opinion on the 2025 statements, but a conviction would hurt the company's reputation and management continuity.[1]

Other flags are smaller. A Kaohsiung wastewater case was settled in 2024 for an NT$0.3 million fine and an NT$50.0 million fund contribution.[1] The 20-F names semiconductor-specific US tariffs, which the US government intends to increase, as a risk that could raise costs ASE may not be able to pass on.[1] Most of the workforce is in Taiwan, with 67,870 of 105,947 employees there at the end of 2025.[1]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 separates static quality from direction of travel. A static test asks whether a number is above a fixed floor today. A trend test asks whether it is moving the right way. The scoring file defines three ways to qualify: a compounder passes the static floors, a turnaround misses a floor but is improving and cheap, and a tactical breakout is the scoring file's label for a leader in an uptrend, or a stock breaking out of a base, which is a sideways stretch where the price rests before a move, with accelerating earnings or revenue. In this report the label is met the first way: ASE is a leader in an uptrend. It does not mean the price is breaking out of a base today, and Chapter 4 shows that it is not. Only a Stage 4 breakdown, meaning a confirmed downtrend with the price below a falling 50-day average that is below a falling 200-day average, is a technical disqualifier.[43] ASE is in a Stage 2 uptrend, which is a confirmed rising trend with the price above rising long averages. The asymmetry, also called the reward-to-risk ratio, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, so a ratio of 2.5 to 1 is $2.50 of possible gain for each $1 of risk.

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on future cash. A reverse DCF works backwards: it takes today's market value, a discount rate and an exit multiple, and solves for the growth in cash flow that the price needs for the next ten years. It is whether the growth the price needs looks easy or hard compared with what the company can do.
