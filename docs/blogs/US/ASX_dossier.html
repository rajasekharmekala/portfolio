1. What the company does

ASE Technology Holding is a Taiwan company. It files an annual report on Form 20-F with the US Securities and Exchange Commission and reports in New Taiwan dollars (NT$).[1][12] Its ordinary shares trade in Taipei under 3711.[12] Each American depositary share (ADS) in New York equals two ordinary shares.[1] Customers are chip designers and chipmakers. They send finished silicon wafers to ASE, which cuts them, mounts them in protective packages, wires them to the outside world and tests them. ASE is the finishing factory that turns raw chips into parts a manufacturer can use. The industry calls this work outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT).

The group earns money in two ways. The first is assembly, test and materials (ATM), which sells packaging and testing to chip customers. The second is electronic manufacturing services (EMS), which builds finished circuit boards and modules for electronics brands through its USI group.[1][3] In 2025 packaging brought in NT$308.3 billion of revenue, testing NT$71.9 billion and EMS NT$257.2 billion, out of NT$645.4 billion.[1] The two arms earn very different profits. ATM posted a 15.7 percent operating margin in the second quarter of 2026. EMS posted 2.4 percent.[3]

The part of ATM that matters most to the share price is LEAP, short for leading-edge advanced packaging. ASE defines these packages as ones that use redistribution layer processes. Its list includes fan-out chip-on-substrate (FOCoS), 2.5D and 3D integration and co-packaged optics.[1] LEAP revenue was US$1.6 billion in 2025, or 13 percent of ATM revenue. That was up from US$0.6 billion in 2024.[8] In February 2026 management guided LEAP to double to US$3.2 billion. In July it said LEAP was tracking ahead of a US$3.5 billion target. It also aims to double LEAP revenue again in 2027.[8][4][13]

Customers are concentrated, and the business is tied to Taiwan. The five largest customers made up 46.5 percent of 2025 operating revenue. One customer, counting those it buys for, was more than 10 percent.[1] On the ATM side in the second quarter of 2026, the top five were 44 percent of revenue and the top ten were 60 percent. In EMS the top five were 58 percent.[3] Operations in Taiwan produced 58.9 percent of 2025 operating revenue.[1] The filings we read do not name the customers, so this report does not either.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business starts with scarcity. Management says growth is limited by how fast it can install machines and finish buildings. The chief operating officer said business demand is not a concern. Blended capacity use was between 80 and 85 percent in the second quarter.[13][14] A second signal is price. ATM gross margin was 27.3 percent in the second quarter. Management guided 28 to 29 percent for the third quarter. It says the fourth quarter is likely to pass what it calls its structural ceiling of 30 percent.[3][4][13] When a service is short of supply, the seller keeps more of each sale. The margins fit that story.

Scale and neutrality are the other pillars. ASE calls itself a pure-play packager with no conflict with foundries or substrate makers. Any chip designer can use it without feeding a rival.[13] It focuses on supporting the chip-on-wafer-on-substrate (CoWoS) packaging that the foundry TSMC developed. It is also working on a panel-based version that management says enters volume production in the first quarter of 2027, and on co-packaged optics.[14][1] The company expects about US$300 million of revenue this year from full-process work, where it handles more of the steps itself.[13] The best evidence of a lead is the ramp itself. Management expects ATM revenue to rise 35 percent in 2026.[4]

The weak points deserve equal space. EMS earns little. Its operating margin was 2.4 percent in the second quarter. Management guided EMS revenue to jump by about 40 percent in the third quarter, which pulls the group margin toward EMS even as ATM improves.[3][4] Value capture also sits elsewhere in the chain. TSMC reported a 66.4 percent gross margin in its third quarter. Amkor, a US-based packaging peer, reported 16.8 percent for its second quarter. Packaging earns far less per dollar than making the chip does.[28][27] Asked about Intel's embedded multi-die interconnect bridge (EMIB) packaging, the chief operating officer said ASE would welcome any technology that offers the same yield, given severe capacity constraints.[14]

Two more risks come from where and in what currency ASE works. Taiwan produced 58.9 percent of 2025 operating revenue. The annual report lists strained relations between the Republic of China and the People's Republic of China as the first risk under its Taiwan heading.[1] It also warns that US tariffs, including semiconductor-specific tariffs, could raise costs that ASE may not be able to pass on. Export-licensing rules on advanced computing items add more uncertainty.[1] On currency, management said its 2025 second and third quarter margins were heavily affected by NT dollar strengthening.[13] The NT dollar has since weakened to NT$32.40 per US dollar, which works in the other direction.[20]

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

This report judges every name in three layers, and this chapter covers the second. Layer 1 is a set of static quality levels and the direction each is moving. The levels are return on capital, cash generation, debt and share-based pay. Layer 1 also checks the chart stage and a reward-to-risk floor. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the possible gain divided by the possible loss. Layer 2 is a short scorecard of three to five measures that run this particular business. Layer 3 turns the chart's distance from support into an entry posture and a position size. It knows setups such as a breakout. A base is a flat stretch of trading before a move up. The pivot is the top of that base. A breakout is a move of the price above the pivot.

Each Layer 2 measure is scored PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0) on unrounded values. The layer qualifies when the weighted score is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 with no more than one FAIL. We picked five measures for a packaging and testing company. ATM gross margin shows whether scarce capacity earns a premium. LEAP revenue against guidance shows whether the AI-driven ramp is real. Capacity use shows whether the machines are full. Customer concentration shows how exposed revenue is to a few buyers. Operating cash flow against capital spending shows whether growth pays for itself.

Book-to-bill, the ratio of new orders to revenue, is a common measure in this field. ASE does not publish it, so we do not score it.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is weaker than the rest, and each has a dated test. The earnings estimate rests on a 13 percent operating margin on NT$1,050 billion of revenue. It is wrong if the third quarter, reported on 2026-10-29, shows an operating margin below 11.5 percent or an ATM gross margin below 28 percent. It is also wrong if the fourth-quarter ATM gross margin does not pass the 30 percent management said to expect.[4][13][18] The 24.7 times multiple rests on the idea that the stock gives back only part of its premium to the median. The price itself tests it after each report. The probability weights rest on a bull chance of 30 percent. Management's habit of beating guidance argues that this is too low. The 2027 LEAP guidance in early 2027 will show it.

The reverse DCF rests on a base cash flow of 75 percent of operating cash flow, which ignores spending far above depreciation. It is wrong if free cash flow stays negative into 2027. The cash cross-check rests on the US$10.5 billion plan being spent as announced. Management has already raised it once this year.[14] The bear case, put as strongly as the evidence allows, is this. The stock rose 142 percent from $15.98 at the end of 2025 to $38.69. It trades at 47.5 times trailing earnings against a three-year median of 21.64 times. It burns cash and is adding debt and a convertible bond. It depends on Taiwan for 58.9 percent of revenue and on a few customers for almost half. Its chart has already lost 19.28 percent from the high.[24][1][26]

Fatal flaw 1: the capacity is built and the demand pauses

ASE is adding capacity at full speed, with 13 greenfield and eight brownfield projects under way. Management itself says execution carries uncertainty.[14] Suppose AI customers digest what they have bought. Or suppose a large customer moves volume to another packager or to its own technology, such as Intel's bridge packaging. The new plants would then run below the 80 to 85 percent utilization seen now. Depreciation would climb, and the margin gains would reverse.[13][14] The warning sign would be ATM gross margin below the 28 to 29 percent guide on 2026-10-29, or softer comments on utilization.[4] Five customers made up 46.5 percent of 2025 revenue. One customer's decision can matter a great deal.[1]

Fatal flaw 2: Taiwan, tariffs and export controls

More than half of operating revenue, 58.9 percent in 2025, comes from operations in Taiwan.[1] The annual report lists strained relations between the Republic of China and the People's Republic of China as a risk, along with typhoons and earthquakes. It says US tariffs, including semiconductor-specific ones, may raise costs that ASE cannot always pass on. It also notes US licensing rules on exporting advanced computing items.[1] A new tariff, a licensing change or a shock in Taiwan would hit all customers at once. This risk cannot be diversified inside the business. ASE says it studies plants in Southeast Asia, Northeast Asia, Europe and the Americas. It gives no assurance that these would be as effective as expected.[1]

Fatal flaw 3: the returns on the new capital never arrive

Return on invested capital was 8.3 percent in 2025 and about 9.9 percent over the last 12 months. Both are below the 12 percent floor. Invested capital grew from NT$531 billion at the end of 2025 to NT$634 billion at June 2026.[1][3][4] If the new capacity earns a return near the current one, free cash flow stays negative for years. Debt rises, and the equity is valued on earnings that must keep growing. A stronger NT dollar, as in 2025, would make it worse. So would a faster shift of revenue to the low-margin EMS arm. The NT dollar moved from a second-quarter average of 31.59 to 32.40 per US dollar by mid-October, which shows how fast the rate can change.[3][20]

The verdict is TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING. It is the nearest label in the taxonomy for a holding, and we disclose that the fit is loose and is not an endorsement of the stock. Several terms need plain definitions, and we give them first so the numbers that follow make sense. CMP is the current market price, here the $38.69 close of 2026-10-23. A simple moving average (SMA) is the plain average of closing prices over a set number of days. An exponential moving average (EMA) is the same idea with more weight on recent days. The 20-day SMA is $43.94 and the 50-day SMA is $41.04. Structural invalidation means the price level where the reason for owning the stock has stopped holding. We place it below the 20-day swing low. A basis point (bps) is one hundredth of one percent. Portfolio heat is the share of the portfolio you would lose if every stop were hit. Risk-unit sizing sets each position so that hitting its stop costs a fixed number of basis points of the portfolio. A wide stop therefore gets a smaller position.

Tiers are a ranking of how sure we are, and each rank has a size limit. TIER_1 allows 7 to 10 percent of the portfolio, TIER_2 allows 4 to 6.5, TIER_3 allows 2 to 3.5 and TIER_4 allows 1 to 2. TIER_0 means zero size. The yardstick for ASX is TIER_4, with a 35 bps risk budget and a 2.0 percent ceiling. We chose it because the name fails two static quality levels and fails the reward-to-risk floor. The let-winners-run rule lets a Stage 2 winner with rising estimates grow to 22 percent of the portfolio while the stop trails the rising 21-day EMA or 50-day SMA. It does not apply to ASX today.
