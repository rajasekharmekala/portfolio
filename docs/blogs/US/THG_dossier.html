1. What the company does

The Hanover Insurance Group is a holding company based in Worcester, Massachusetts. Its roots go back to 1852, when The Hanover Fire Insurance Company was founded, and today its insurance companies sell cover to families and to small and mid-sized businesses across the United States.[9] It had about 4,900 employees at the end of 2025.[9] Customers do not buy from it directly. Policies are placed by independent agents and brokers, who are free to offer a customer the products of several insurers.[9] Hanover appoints the agencies it trusts and pays them commissions and bonuses tied to premium written, growth and profit.[9]

The business has three parts. Personal Lines sells car and home policies and was 41% of premium in 2025. Core Commercial sells business insurance to small and mid-sized firms and was 36%. Specialty sells niche products such as management liability, marine cover and surety bonds, and was 23%.[9] In 2025 the company wrote $6.3 billion of net premium, which is the premium it keeps after paying reinsurers to take part of the risk, and that was 3.9% more than in 2024.[8] In the first nine months of 2026 net premium was $5,009.0 million, up 3.6%.[1]

Hanover earns money in two main ways. The first is underwriting profit, which is premium collected minus the claims and costs paid. The yardstick is the combined ratio: claims plus costs divided by premium, so anything below 100% is a profit. In the first nine months of 2026 the ratio was 91.8%, which means the company kept 8.2 cents of each premium dollar before investment income.[1] The second source is investment income. Premium arrives before claims are paid, so the company invests the cash in between. Net investment income was $378.4 million in the nine months, up from $328.6 million a year earlier, a rise of 15.2%.[2] Fees and other income are small, at $18.6 million.[1]

The business is concentrated in two states. Michigan was 18.7% of net premium in 2025 and Massachusetts 8.0%, and about half of Personal Lines premium comes from those two states.[9] In Michigan the company writes about 6% of the state's personal market, and Michigan car insurance alone was $615.5 million, or 9.7% of all net premium.[9] Hanover says it is spreading out. It markets business cover in 44 states and car and home cover in 19 states, and it runs 33 local offices in 23 states.[9] Concentration matters because one bad storm season or one change in a state's rules can move the whole company's profit. Three businesses, three different risks

Personal Lines is built around accounts, meaning a customer who buys both car and home cover. About 89% of policies in force belong to account customers, and that tends to keep customers longer.[9] The risk is weather. Home claims from storms, hail and wind are the swing factor, and in 2023 homeowners cover ran a loss ratio of 106.1 with 46.1 points of catastrophe loss.[9] Car cover is a different risk: repair costs and injury claims drive it, and Michigan has had its own rules for injury cover since a major reform in 2019.[9]

Core Commercial is aimed at small accounts. Small commercial means annual premium up to $50,000 per policy, and middle market means $50,000 to $500,000.[9] The product mix is mostly package cover, business cars and workers' compensation, which are long-tail lines, meaning claims can take years to settle and costs can drift. Specialty has nine dedicated businesses and 18 distinct product areas, sold mostly through the same retail agents plus a few specialist channels.[9] Because the accounts are small, no single risk is large, and that is a stated part of the strategy.

The company also manages the balance sheet. It buys catastrophe reinsurance that, from July 1, 2026, covers losses up to $2.05 billion for a single event after a $200 million retention, which is the first slice the company pays itself.[3] It added a new $150 million catastrophe bond that month.[3] It holds $11.2 billion of cash and invested assets, about 93% in bonds and cash, with 95% of bonds rated investment grade.[1] Chapter 2 asks how much of this is a lasting edge and how much is a good run.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio is claims plus expenses divided by premium. Below 100% is an underwriting profit, and each point below 100 is one cent of profit per premium dollar.
  • Net premiums written is premium after paying reinsurers. Net premiums earned is the part of that premium that has been on risk during the period, and it is what the income statement counts.
  • Catastrophe losses are claims from large single events such as storms, hail, floods and wildfires. They are reported separately because they swing so much from year to year.
  • Reserves are money set aside for claims that have happened but are not yet paid. Prior-year development is the change in that estimate. Favorable means the old claims cost less than reserved, and unfavorable means more.
  • Reinsurance is insurance that an insurer buys for itself. It caps the loss from a very large claim or a storm and so protects the company's capital.
  • Operating income is profit before investment gains and losses and one-off items. Operating return on equity is a year of operating income divided by shareholders' equity, adjusted by the company to leave out unrealized bond losses.
  • Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by the number of shares. For an insurer it is the usual yardstick for price. Unrealized losses are paper losses on bonds that fall in value when interest rates rise, and they lower book value without changing claims or premium.
  • Policies in force are the count of live policies. Renewal price change is the average premium change on policies that renew, including changes in coverage, and rate change leaves coverage changes out.
  • earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. Operating EPS leaves out swings in investment values, so it shows the insurance business more cleanly.
  • stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given in shares or options. It is a real cost to owners because it creates new shares.
  • earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit before borrowing costs, taxes and non-cash charges. We use it only to test borrowing, as the scoring rules require.
  • return on invested capital (ROIC) is profit divided by the money invested in a business. For an insurer we use return on equity instead, because the equity is the capital.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after running the business and buying equipment. For an insurer it is mostly premium waiting to be paid out as claims, so it says little and we mark it not applicable.
  • net debt is borrowing minus cash. It matters when debt is large, so we also measure debt against total capital.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash that comes in from running the business. For an insurer it rises with premium and falls as claims are paid.
  • A buyback means the company buys its own shares in the open market. That shrinks the share count and raises each remaining owner's slice. dilution is the opposite: new shares are issued, for example to pay staff, and each existing share is worth a smaller slice.
  • guidance is a company's own forecast or target. We check later whether the company met it. A consensus estimate is the average forecast from the analysts who follow a stock.
  • price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share. It tells you how many years of current profit you pay for.
  • gross margin, operating margin and net margin are profit measures as a share of revenue. They matter less for an insurer, where the combined ratio does the same job. debt-to-equity is borrowing divided by shareholders' equity, and for insurers we use debt as a share of total capital instead.
  • Stage 2 is the rising part of a stock's life cycle, when the price is above a rising 50-day average and a rising 200-day average. Stage 4 is the falling part, when the price is below both and both are falling. A stock between the two is either topping or basing.
  • A 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 sessions. The 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the same measure over 50 and 200 sessions, and in an uptrend they act as moving floors.
  • A base is a stretch of weeks or months when a stock moves sideways after a decline. A pivot is the price at the top of that sideways range, a breakout is a rise through it that signals a new advance, and a retest is a return to a level the price recently bounced from, to see whether it holds.
  • relative strength index (RSI) is a 0 to 100 reading of how fast a price has moved. Under 30 is called oversold and over 70 is called overbought. average true range (ATR) is the typical size of one day's price swing over 14 days. It is the ruler we use to measure distances on a chart.
  • A stop loss is a price at which you sell to limit a loss. A limit order is an order to buy at a chosen price or lower, so you never pay more than you decided. The entry zone is the band of prices at which we would start buying.
  • asymmetry — upside reward relative to downside risk — and reward-to-risk mean the same thing here: the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop. The rules ask for at least 2 to 1 before we buy a new position.
  • Core Alpha is our bucket for steady businesses we would hold for years, and a new Core Alpha position must offer at least 2 to 1 reward-to-risk. A COMPOUNDER is a business that grows its value steadily because it earns high returns on the money it keeps. TIER_3 is a smaller position size class, capped at 3.5% of net worth. The risk budget is the most of net worth we accept losing if the stop is hit, which is 0.50% for TIER_3, and the position weight is that budget divided by the distance to the stop, up to the cap.
  • The support ladder is the rule that sorts a price by how many ATR it sits above support. Up to 0.6 ATR is full size, up to 1.2 is standard size, and up to 1.6 is the starter-only band, where only a small first purchase on a limit order is allowed. Beyond 1.6 we do not buy at the market. Chapter 2 now asks whether the business is strong enough to be worth that wait.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Hanover's first strength is pricing. In the third quarter of 2026 renewal prices rose 8.7% in Personal Lines, 7.4% in Core Commercial and 4.3% in Specialty, which is price change on renewing policies including changes in coverage.[1] Rate alone, which leaves coverage changes out, rose 4.9%, 6.1% and 1.9%.[1] Retention is steady: 86.3% of Core Commercial premium renewed in the quarter against 84.4% a year earlier, and 84.2% of Personal Lines policies renewed against 84.0%.[2] On the call an analyst noted that Hanover's pricing is holding up better than peers. Management put that down to its focus on accounts, to smaller account sizes and to being a market that the best agents want to keep.[16]

The second strength is the improvement in underlying results. The current accident year combined ratio excluding catastrophes, which measures this year's business before storms and before changes to old reserves, was 89.5% in 2024, 88.2% in 2025 and 86.9% in the first nine months of 2026.[8][1] Personal Lines drove most of it: 81.9% in the third quarter against 85.8% a year earlier, helped by earned price outrunning claims costs and by fewer small claims after deductible changes.[1][16] Specialty ran at 84.6% and Core Commercial at 93.2%.[1] The company's own measure of results excluding catastrophes was 85.5% in the quarter against 88.1% a year earlier.[1]

Third, Specialty gives the company a margin that does not depend on weather. It had a combined ratio of 85.7% in 2025 and 84.0% in the third quarter, with favorable reserve development of $14.8 million, or 4.0 points, in that quarter.[9][1] Management says that, excluding two lines it is deliberately holding back, Specialty grew 6.7% in the quarter, with surety up 8.8% and E&S growth speeding up to 11.7%.[16] The segment earned $296.1 million of operating income before interest and tax in 2025 on 22.8% of the premium.[9]

Fourth, the balance sheet is cautious. Management says reserves are set well above the actuarial central estimate and that it keeps adding to the incurred-but-not-reported reserve, which is the allowance for claims that have not yet been reported.[16] Claims for prior years have come in below reserve every year recently: favorable development was $64.8 million on non-catastrophe claims and $73.7 million on catastrophe claims in the first nine months of 2026, against $68.3 million in total a year earlier.[3] Asbestos and environmental reserves, the usual source of old-claim surprises, are small at $10.1 million net.[3] The company also completed a transfer of an old reinsurance pool, ECRA, to a third-party insurer in 2025 which released it from those liabilities.[9] Statutory capital and surplus in the insurance companies rose from $3,337.9 million at the end of 2025 to $3,540.9 million at June 30 and $3,700.8 million at September 30, after $180.0 million of dividends to the parent.[3][6]

The weaknesses are set out in the next four subsections: a record that is good only recently; weather and two states; slow growth in a softening market; and costs above plan together with a change of chief executive.

What is weakening: the good record is recent

The company's results have not always looked like this. The combined ratio was 97.0% in 2021, 99.8% in 2022 and 103.5% in 2023, then 94.8% in 2024 and 91.6% in 2025.[10][9] Net income fell from $422.8 million in 2021 to $116.0 million in 2022 and $35.3 million in 2023, and it was $662.5 million in 2025.[10][9] The 2023 loss came from catastrophes: 12.2 points of catastrophe loss in total and 46.1 points on homeowners cover.[9] The recovery is real, because the company raised prices, changed deductibles and policy terms, and tightened property underwriting.[19][7] But two good years are a short record against three straight years, 2021 to 2023, at or above 97%.

The compensation filing shows the same thing in a different way. For the 2023 to 2025 performance period the company's three-year average adjusted operating return on equity was 15.4%, and its three-year total shareholder return of 42.57% ranked at the 50th percentile of a peer set, so the market has not yet treated Hanover as a leader.[12] That is a fair reading: the earnings step-up arrived in 2024 and 2025.

What is weakening: weather and two states

Catastrophe losses were $300.4 million in the first nine months of 2026 against $249.3 million a year earlier, and that was with $73.7 million of favorable development on old storms.[3] The catastrophe ratio was 6.3% for the nine months, 6.8% in the third quarter and 4.5% for 2025, against 12.2% in 2023 and an average of 7.8% over the five years 2021 to 2025.[1][8][10][9] Management's yearly load, the amount of catastrophe loss it plans for, is 6.5%.[19] If the five-year average returned, 1.3 points of extra loss on about $6.4 billion of earned premium would cost roughly $83 million before tax, or about $1.83 a share after tax on 35.5 million diluted shares.

Concentration raises the stakes. Michigan and Massachusetts supply about half of Personal Lines premium.[9] Hanover buys catastrophe cover up to $2.05 billion, but its own retention is $200 million per event.[3] Even before the July 2026 renewal the 2025 program carried cover to $2.05 billion for Northeast named storms, which suggests where the company sees its tail risk.[9] A hurricane that hits the Northeast is the event where the reinsurance cover, the catastrophe bond and the company's capital would all be tested together.

What is weakening: growth is slow and the market is softening

Net premium grew 3.1% in the third quarter and 3.6% in the nine months, below the mid-single-digit growth management expected for 2026.[1][19] Policies in force in Personal Lines fell 1.2% from a year earlier, though that is better than the 2.9% fall in the third quarter of 2025.[2] Management says it is choosing to shift toward higher-value accounts and to hold back where competition is lower-priced.[16] The competition is plain in the price figures: Specialty renewal price change fell from 8.3% to 4.3% over a year, Core Commercial's from 9.9% to 7.4%, and Personal Lines' from 10.5% to 8.7%.[2] Prices are still above claims-cost growth, but the gap is narrowing, and that narrowing is the main way earnings could slip in 2027.

Core Commercial is the weak spot. Its combined ratio was 100.5% in the third quarter and 97.7% for the nine months.[1] Management blames catastrophes in a small book and says the loss ratio was unchanged at 60.5%, and it sees room to bring the nine-month ratio below 97% over time.[16] We take that at face value, but the segment has a 97.4% ratio for 2025 as well, so it has not yet shown the margin Specialty and Personal Lines show.[9]

What is weakening: costs above plan, and a change of chief executive

The expense ratio was 30.9% for the nine months, against the 30.3% the company guided for the year.[1][19] The CFO says it will miss that figure because better results raise agent profit-sharing and employee bonuses.[16] That is a fair trade, but it means a share of every improvement in the loss ratio is paid away.

John Roche, the chief executive, retires on December 31, 2026. Richard Lavey, 59, who has been chief operating officer since March 2025 and joined Hanover in 2004, takes over, and Jeffrey Farber, the chief financial officer, spoke on the third-quarter call with no change announced.[13][16] The handover is planned and the successor has run the agency business, so we count it as low risk. Even so, a new leader may reset targets, and the company held an investor day on September 17 to set out a five-year plan, whose targets we could not retrieve.[17][16]

What this means for an owner

Hanover is a good business with a recent record. Its pricing, its small-account focus and its Specialty margin are real strengths, and its reserves look cautious. What it has not shown is a full cycle at today's returns. Weather and a softening market are the two things that would pull a 19% return on equity back toward the mid-teens, and both are outside the company's control. The company can choose its prices, its deductibles and where it writes policies, but it cannot choose the storms. For an owner that means the price needs a cushion, because a stock bought at a full price has no room for a bad year. Our variant perception

The market is saying that Hanover's earnings have peaked: analysts expect operating EPS of $20.60 in 2026 and $19.64 in 2027, a fall of 4.65%.[23] Our view differs in size, not direction. How it was calculated

We solve for the growth rate g that makes ten years of operating income, each discounted at 10%, plus 18 times the tenth year's income, also discounted, equal to today's equity value. Value = sum of E0 x (1+g)^t / 1.10^t for t = 1 to 10, plus 18 x E0 x (1+g)^10 / 1.10^10. The answer is found by trial: at g = -2.9% the sum is $8,112 million.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report has a weakest assumption. This chapter states the strongest honest argument against buying, three ways the case could fail outright, and the single claim everything depends on. It also says when we will know. Chapter 7 then turns the view into a plan.

The red-team bear case: what the strongest honest argument against buying says

The bear says the earnings are at a peak that will not last. Hanover's combined ratio was 97.0% in 2021, 99.8% in 2022 and 103.5% in 2023, and only one full year, 2025, has been below 92%.[10][9] The 91.8% for the first nine months of 2026 includes $138.5 million of favorable reserve development, which is 2.9% of earned premium. Without it the ratio would be about 94.7%.[3][1] Catastrophe losses were 6.3% against a five-year average of 7.8%. The 19.6% operating return on equity leaves out $502.9 million of unrealized bond losses from equity; GAAP book value per share fell 5.4% in one quarter.[1]

The bear adds that the stock has already re-rated. Book value is 2.36 times against 1.82 at the end of 2025, and the stock is up 39% in a year.[25][24] If the combined ratio returned to 97%, EPS would be about $15, and at 10.5 times that is $157.

Our reply is that the bear has a fair point on duration and not on price. At 11.4 times earnings, a company with a 19% return does not need the 2026 margin to last forever. It needs it to hold through 2027. That is why we give the bear case 25% and refuse to buy at the market.

Fatal flaw 1: weather returns in a bad year

Hanover is a large writer of home and business property cover in states with frequent storms. In 2023 current-year catastrophe losses were $690.1 million and the catastrophe ratio was 12.2 points, with 46.1 points on homeowners cover.[9] If 2027 repeated 12.2 points on about $6.7 billion of earned premium, catastrophe losses would be about $812 million against our planned $433 million, and the extra $379 million before tax would cost about $8.63 per share after tax.

The company has made changes: higher deductibles, tighter property terms and a larger reinsurance program, with cover to $2.05 billion above a $200 million retention and a new $150 million catastrophe bond.[3][19] Those limit a single event. They do not limit the number of medium storms, which are the main source of the losses. The first sign would be a catastrophe ratio above 8% in two quarters running, and the first test is the second and third quarters of 2027, when storm losses concentrate.

Fatal flaw 2: old claims turn out to cost more than reserved

Loss reserves were $8,219.9 million at September 30, 2026, which is 2.4 times equity.[1] Most of the book is business cars, workers' compensation and liability, where claims settle slowly and legal costs and jury awards have been rising.[9] A 1% adverse change in reserves would be about $82 million before tax, or $1.87 per share; a 3% change would be about $5.60. Management says reserves are set above the actuarial central estimate and that the company keeps adding to the incurred-but-not-reported reserve.[16]

The record supports the claim: favorable development in every recent period, including all three segments in the third quarter.[1][3] But we note that the company strengthened car bodily injury reserves a little in the fourth quarter of 2025, that workers' compensation frequency has been rising for a year and that the CFO says liability picks have been left unchanged this year.[19][16] The first sign would be unfavorable development in Core Commercial, which we will see in the fourth-quarter report and the annual report in February 2027.

Fatal flaw 3: price falls behind claims costs

The margin gain of 2025 and 2026 came from price rising faster than claims costs. Renewal price is now 8.7% in Personal Lines, 7.4% in Core Commercial and 4.3% in Specialty, and each is lower than a year ago.[2] If claims costs keep rising at a similar pace, the gap closes and the current accident year combined ratio excluding catastrophes drifts back from 86.9% toward the 89.5% of 2024. That is 2.6 points, or about $3.90 per share.[8][1] Competition is the other face of the same risk: the company is already holding back two Specialty lines, one of them because of competition in larger property, and net premium growth of 3.1% is below plan.[16]

The first sign would be Personal Lines renewal price below 6% or Core Commercial price below 5% while the claims ratio rises. Selective, the closest agency-channel rival, already shows premium down 7% as it pushes for margin, which suggests rivals face the same market.[28]

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The business passes Layer 1 and Layer 2, so it stays on the list.
