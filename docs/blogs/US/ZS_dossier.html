Before you read

Zscaler sells cloud-delivered security that sits between employees, devices and applications so that no connection is trusted by default, an approach called zero trust. The business is real and growing: fiscal 2026 revenue was $3,353 million, up 25%, and free cash flow was $779.1 million, or 23% of revenue.[1] This report concludes that the stock does not qualify for our book today. The reason is not the product but the price of getting it: stock-based compensation was 24.5% of revenue against a ceiling of 5%, GAAP operating profit is negative, and the shares sit 4.87 ATR above their 50-day average after a 43% one-month run. The conclusion would be wrong if share-based pay fell sharply relative to sales, or if growth re-accelerated well above the 17% the company guided, because either would change the value math in chapter 5.

What the company does

Zscaler runs a global network of data centres that inspects traffic between a customer's users, offices, cloud workloads and now AI agents and the applications they use. A customer pays an annual subscription, so the revenue repeats. The company tracks this as annual recurring revenue (ARR), which reached $3,771 million at 31 July 2026, of which $141 million came from the Red Canary acquisition; excluding it, ARR grew 20%.[1] Customers are enterprises: 785 pay more than $1 million a year and 4,182 pay more than $100,000.[3] About 30% of new and upsell contract value now comes from products not priced per seat, such as security for AI and data protection, which matters because seat counts in a world of AI agents may not grow with headcount.[3]

What this means for an owner: the revenue is high quality and sticky, so the question for the rest of the book is not "is this a good business" but "is it a good price for a good business that pays its people largely in stock." The next chapter scores that.

Glossary of terms used in this report

Each term is explained here once and used the same way afterwards.

  • ARR (annual recurring revenue): the yearly value of subscriptions in force at a date, like the rent roll of a landlord.
  • Free cash flow (FCF): cash from operations minus spending on equipment and capitalised software; the cash the owners could in principle take out.
  • Stock-based compensation (SBC): pay delivered as shares. It costs no cash today but creates new shares, so existing owners hold a smaller slice.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital): operating profit after tax divided by the money tied up in the business.
  • ATR (average true range): the typical daily price swing, here $9.84; we measure distances in ATRs so that calm and wild stocks can be compared.
  • Stage 2: the Weinstein phase of a sustained uptrend, price above rising long-term averages.
  • DCF (discounted cash flow): the value today of future cash, with distant cash counted for less.
  • Asymmetry: potential gain to target divided by potential loss to stop. Our floor for tactical names is 3.5 to 1.

Why the business is strong or weak

Strength: the company combines scale, a platform that sells more modules to existing customers, and rising large-deal activity. Net new ARR grew 24% in the fourth quarter, remaining performance obligations (contracted revenue not yet recognised) were about $7.4 billion, up about 27%, and sales productivity reached its highest quarterly level.[1][3] Weakness: organic ARR growth is 20% and net new ARR excluding Red Canary grew only 17%; fiscal 2027 guidance is for revenue growth of 16.6% to 17.5% and ARR growth of 16.6% to 17.4%.[1] The chief revenue officer also changed on 1 October 2026, which adds execution risk to a year in which growth must hold.[5] In the same week the company reaffirmed guidance at its 6 October investor presentation.[4]

What could happen next

The method is a discounted-cash-flow model, and the target is anchored on it because the "forward EPS times three-year median P/E" route is N/A: GAAP earnings per share were negative in FY2025 and FY2026, so no usable multiple history exists, and non-GAAP P/E history was not fetched. The market currently pays about 9.3 times fiscal 2027 guided revenue (enterprise value about $36.3 billion, from roughly 163 million shares at $233.73, plus $1.7 billion of convertible debt, minus $3.5 billion of cash) and about 49 times trailing free cash flow.

The number

Base-case value per share of about $133.

How it was calculated

Free cash flow starts at 23.5% of the $3.923 billion fiscal 2027 revenue midpoint, rises to 25%, revenue growth fades from 15% to 10% over four years, discounted at 10% with 4% terminal growth, then net cash of $1.778 billion is added and the total is divided by 175 million diluted shares.

The inputs

FY27 revenue guidance of $3.908 to $3.938 billion and FCF margin of 23.0 to 23.5%;[1] net cash from the balance sheet;[1] about 170 to 173 million diluted shares guided;[1] discount rate and fade path are our assumptions, not sourced.

The range

Bull $240: free cash flow margin climbing to 29%, an 8% discount rate and 4% terminal growth. Bear $51: free cash flow after share-based pay near zero rising only to 12%, 10% discount rate, 3% terminal growth, 185 million shares.

If the number is off

The result is very sensitive to the discount rate: moving from 10% to 8% takes the base case up by more than 70%. If you believe in a lower rate and higher margins, the stock is fairly priced, not cheap; it is never an asymmetric bargain at $233.73.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate has one weak assumption, and each has a date when evidence arrives.

  • Discount rate (10% base). At 8% and a 29% margin the same model gives about $240. If rates or the equity risk premium fall, the base case moves a long way. Evidence: the ten-year Treasury yield and sector multiples through the next quarter.
  • SBC treated as a real cost in the bear and as shares in the base. The base case ignores future dilution beyond 175 million shares. If SBC falls toward 15% of revenue, the base case rises. Evidence: fiscal Q1 2027 SBC, expected with results around late November 2026 (date not confirmed by the company).
  • Growth fade. Guidance may prove conservative; the company reaffirmed it on 6 October.[4] A beat and a raise at the next report would move probabilities toward the bull case.
  • Data limits. Finviz and Zscaler's own investor site returned HTTP 403 and could not be fetched; TradingView was not fetched. Share count is derived from the par-value line and is approximate. NRR and CAC payback are not published. Three-year median P/E is N/A.
  • Source depth. The citation floor of 25 distinct sources for a full dossier was not reached; about ten sources were consulted. This is disclosed as a gap, not hidden.

Fatal flaws and red team

The red-team pass builds the strongest case against buying and names the single claim the case hinges on. Three flaws are fatal on their own.

  1. Profit belongs to employees. SBC is 24.5% of revenue and GAAP operating income is negative. Free cash flow after SBC is -1.3% of revenue. This fails two Layer 1 gates. The hinge claim of the whole bull case is that SBC is not a real cost; if it is, the business earns nothing for owners yet.
  2. Price already assumes the good outcome. At 9.3 times forward revenue and 49 times trailing free cash flow after a 43% monthly rally, the stock needs a low discount rate and rising margins together; even the bull case barely reaches the current price.
  3. Growth is slowing while capital spending rises. Organic ARR growth is 20%, guided to about 17%; fourth-quarter capital spending of $218.5 million cut free cash flow to 7%; a new chief revenue officer starts in the quarter that must prove the guide; a 3% headcount reduction with $30 to $33 million of charges signals reallocation, not new growth.[3][5]

The strongest argument against our rejection: the platform is expanding into AI security, large deals are at a record, and 20% organic growth with 23% free cash flow margin is a Rule of 40 score of about 43 to 49, which is rare. We weigh the SBC and price evidence higher because they are measured numbers, while the AI upside is a forecast.
