1. What the company does

BlackRock manages investments for other people and charges a fee for doing it. At September 30, 2026 it managed $15,488,263 million of assets, or $15.5 trillion, 15% more than a year earlier.[1] A fair comparison is a property manager that owns none of the buildings but collects a small percentage of the rent on every one: the fee is the revenue, so revenue rises when the assets under management (AUM) rise, whether clients add money or markets lift prices.

The clients are pension plans, insurers, governments, wealth managers and individual savers. The biggest product family is iShares, BlackRock's exchange-traded funds (ETFs), which are baskets of securities that trade on an exchange like a single share. ETFs held 41% of AUM and produced 46% of base fees, which are the percentage fees charged on AUM, in the third quarter of 2026. Active strategies, where managers choose the securities, held 24% of AUM and produced 42% of base fees. Index funds outside ETFs, mostly institutional, held 28% of AUM but only 6% of base fees because their fee rate is very low.[1] That gap between share of assets and share of fees is the key to reading the company: a dollar of active or private assets earns many times the fee of a dollar of institutional index assets.

Revenue in the third quarter was $7,752 million, up 19% from $6,509 million a year earlier. Base fees and securities lending were 78% of it, and performance fees, technology services and distribution fees made up most of the rest.[1] Performance fees matter because they swing: they were $630 million in the quarter, and $409 million of that came from liquid alternatives, which are hedge funds and funds of hedge funds. The company links most of the year-on-year rise in performance fees to a single hedge fund whose annual measurement period ends in the third quarter.[1] The five revenue lines in the first table add exactly to the total.

Since late 2024 BlackRock has bought three businesses to build a private markets and data arm. Global Infrastructure Management (GIP), an infrastructure investor, added $70 billion of AUM in October 2024. HPS, a private credit manager, closed on July 1, 2025 and added $118 billion of fee-paying AUM. Preqin, a private markets data company, closed on March 3, 2025 for about $3.2 billion in cash.[4] Private markets AUM stood at $335,994 million at September 30, 2026, which is 2% of total AUM but 11% of base fees.[1] The business therefore has two layers: a very large, low-fee public markets engine that produces most of the profit today, and a smaller, higher-fee private markets and software layer that management wants to grow to 30% or more of revenue by 2030.[13]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • AUM means assets under management, the market value of client money BlackRock manages. Revenue follows it.
  • Base fees are the percentage fees charged on AUM, including advisory and administration fees and securities lending revenue. Organic base fee growth is the new base fee earned from net client inflows, as a share of the starting fee run-rate; markets rising or falling do not count.[2]
  • Net flows are new client money in, minus money withdrawn. Positive flows are called inflows.
  • Aladdin is BlackRock's portfolio and risk software, sold to other investors. BlackRock reports its recurring contract value as annual contract value (ACV) and does not publish annual recurring revenue (ARR) as a separate figure.[1]
  • Private markets are investments not traded on public exchanges, such as infrastructure projects and loans made directly to companies. Private credit is the loan part of that.
  • As adjusted results leave out items management treats as non-recurring or non-cash, mainly acquisition costs. We show both the GAAP (official accounting) figure and the as adjusted figure wherever they differ.[1]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

BlackRock's competitive position rests on scale that rivals cannot copy quickly. It manages $15.5 trillion, its ETFs hold $6.36 trillion, and management says over 90% of the top 100 asset owners work with it.[1][13] Scale lowers cost per dollar managed, which lets the company keep a high margin while charging low fees on index products. The company reports an as adjusted operating margin of 47.8% in the third quarter of 2026, against 44.6% a year earlier, and 46.2% for the first nine months.[1] Management states it ranks top five in private credit and separately managed accounts and top three in infrastructure, cash management and outsourcing; these are the company's own claims and we did not find an independent ranking to check them.[13]

The second source of strength is that the pieces feed each other. The same relationships that place an index fund also lead to an insurance mandate, a pension outsourcing contract or a data-centre financing. The Chief Executive described the third quarter as winning mandates from hyperscaler financings to pension outsourcings and Aladdin and Preqin partnerships.[1] The Chief Financial Officer said private markets net inflows came mostly from deployment into infrastructure and private credit, and that the AI Infrastructure Partnership made its first deployment in the quarter.[11][1]

Technology and data as a second revenue stream

Technology services and subscription revenue was $588 million in the third quarter, up 14% from $515 million, and annual contract value, the yearly value of signed recurring contracts, rose 12%.[1] For the full year 2025 this revenue was about $2.0 billion, up 24%, and contract value rose 31% including Preqin and 16% without it.[4] Because contracts are multi-year, this line is more stable than performance fees, but at 8% of revenue it is too small to offset a stock market fall on its own. It also matters for the long-run story: if Aladdin becomes the standard system for private markets data and risk, it ties clients to BlackRock in ways a fund fee does not.

Investment performance, the licence to keep clients

Asset managers keep money when their results hold up. On the company's September 30, 2026 data, 71% of taxable fixed income assets beat their benchmark or peer median over one year, 85% over three years and 84% over five years. Systematic equity was at 92%, 93% and 95%.[1] The weak spot is fundamental equity, where the share beating benchmark is 73% over one year but 65% over both three and five years, and tax-exempt bonds, at 56%, 58% and 47%.[1] The consequence is that BlackRock's active growth is coming from fixed income and systematic strategies, not from traditional stock picking, which fits the net flow data in chapter 3.

Where the business is weak

The first weakness is market sensitivity. Equity assets produced 51% of third quarter base fees, so a fall in stock prices cuts revenue quickly. In 2022 AUM fell from $10.0 trillion to $8.6 trillion, revenue fell 7.7% to $17,873 million, and as adjusted earnings per share (EPS) fell from $40.51 to $35.36.[4] The second is fee rate pressure inside the index business. A commentator quoted on a financial news page claimed that flows into the flagship S&P 500 ETF, IVV, are far below their usual pace and that a fee cut could follow; we could not verify the flow figures against BlackRock's own data, so we treat this as a risk to watch and not as a fact.[26] The third is legal: thirteen state attorneys general are suing BlackRock, State Street and Vanguard in federal court in the Eastern District of Texas on an antitrust theory about coal supply, and the court largely denied a motion to dismiss in 2025. Management does not expect a material effect but warns there is no assurance.[7]

The fourth is concentration of authority. Laurence D. Fink is both Chairman and Chief Executive, balanced by a Lead Independent Director, and 16 of 19 director nominees are independent.[22][1] Shareholders gave the 2025 pay plan only 67% support, which the compensation committee called disappointing.[22] None of these is a solvency or accounting red line, but each lowers how much of a price premium we are willing to pay.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk means checking what management said it would do one or two years ago against what it did. BlackRock has made specific, checkable promises on buybacks, costs, growth and acquisitions, and it has mostly kept them. The strongest record is on capital return and growth: it repurchased $450 million in each of the first two quarters of 2026, raised the plan to $550 million a quarter in July, and bought $550 million in the third quarter.[12][8][11] It also reported nine consecutive quarters of organic base fee growth at or above its 5% target.[11]

The record is not spotless. The as adjusted operating margin was 44.1% for 2025, under the 45% through-the-cycle target, and only moved above it in 2026 with help from rising markets.[4][1] The private markets fundraising goal is at an early stage: $68 billion in the last twelve months is below an even pace of $80 billion a year, though the company has not said the target will be reached evenly.[1][13]

Two features of how BlackRock pays and rewards its people also bear on the investment case. In January 2026 the compensation committee adopted an Executive Carry Program, under which senior executives other than the Chief Executive can receive a share of carry, the profit split that private funds pay out when they beat their return targets, from a pool of flagship private markets funds.[29] That ties executive pay to private markets results, which is the part of the plan that is still unproven. Stock-based compensation (SBC), the cost of paying staff in shares, was $1,307 million in 2025, or 5.4% of revenue, up from $753 million in 2024, partly from retention awards tied to the HPS deal.[4] Chapter 3 tests that against our 8% limit.

The acquisitions are the second thing to track. Risk-unit sizing, also called portfolio heat, sets the size of a position by how much of the whole portfolio we would lose if the stop is hit, and not by how much we like the stock. The formula is the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. A basis point (bp) is one hundredth of a percent, so a 75 bp budget means losing 0.75% of net worth if the stop is hit. Tiers rank conviction: TIER_1 allows up to 10% of net worth with a 100 bp budget, TIER_2 up to 6.5% with 75 bp, TIER_3 up to 3.5% with 50 bp, TIER_4 up to 2% with 35 bp, and TIER_0 means zero size. We use TIER_2 as the yardstick because the business passes both layers but the price margin is thin: 75 bp divided by 7.41% is 10.1%, so the 6.5% ceiling binds. At the net worth of $82,368.77 that is $5,353.97, or 4.89 shares at $1,095. A stop hit at that size would cost 6.5% x 7.41%, about 48 bp, inside the budget.
