1. What the company does

Expand Energy Corporation, based in Spring, Texas, is the largest independent natural gas producer in the US by daily volume.[1][13] It drills in three areas: the Haynesville and Bossier shales in Louisiana and East Texas, the Marcellus shale in northeast Pennsylvania, and the Marcellus and Utica shales in Ohio and West Virginia. It holds working interests in about 6,600 gross wells and operates about 99% of its own output.[13] Gas is measured in thousand cubic feet (Mcf). Because the company also sells small amounts of oil and natural gas liquids (NGLs, such as propane and ethane), it reports everything in Mcfe, which converts liquids into a gas equivalent, and in Bcfe, which is a billion cubic feet equivalent. In the third quarter of 2026 it produced 7.54 Bcfe a day, 92% of it natural gas.[1] Haynesville supplied 3,178 million cubic feet a day, northeast Appalachia 2,593 and southwest Appalachia 1,767 million cubic feet equivalent a day.[3]

Who pays Expand? Buyers of gas, including utilities, power generators, industrial users and liquefied natural gas (LNG) exporters, which is our description because the 10-K does not list customers by type. Gas is sold at index prices or at the daily spot price where it is delivered, so the company is a price taker.[13] One buyer accounted for 11% of 2025 revenue before hedging, so customer concentration is modest.[13] The benchmark is Henry Hub, the Louisiana pipeline hub that sets the US gas price. In the third quarter the company's gas sold for $2.44 per Mcf before hedges against a benchmark average of $2.95, a gap of $0.51; by area, Haynesville gas sold for $2.68, northeast Appalachia $2.15 and southwest Appalachia $2.44, all below the benchmark because each region pays to move gas to the hub. Including hedges, the whole production stream earned $3.17 per Mcfe, against $2.71 before hedges.[3] The $0.46 difference came from hedging, which is where the earnings cushion in this report comes from.

Revenue has three parts. For the nine months to September 30, 2026, sales of gas, oil and NGLs were $7,026 million, marketing sales of other people's and the company's gas were $2,820 million, and gains on derivatives (price-protection contracts) were $784 million.[2] Marketing sales earn a thin margin, since the company pays out nearly as much as it takes in ($2,658 million of marketing cost against $2,820 million of sales), so the first line decides most of the result. The derivative line mixes cash settlements with paper gains and losses on contracts not yet settled, which is why we read cash flow, adjusted figures and reported figures side by side and say which one each number is.[2] In the third quarter cash receipts on settled derivatives were $297 million, against free cash flow, which means cash from operations minus capital spending, of $339 million.[2]

The cost side explains most of the story. We divide the third-quarter bill by 693.5 Bcfe of sales (7.538 Bcfe a day over 92 days). Well operating cost was $0.24 per Mcfe, gathering, processing and transportation $0.98, production taxes $0.09 and general and administrative cost $0.07, a total of $1.38. Interest was $0.06, and cash capital spending was $1.21.[2][3] Transportation is the large item because gas must be carried by pipe from the wells to buyers, and the company has about $8.9 billion of gross, undiscounted commitments for gathering, processing, transportation and storage that it owes whether or not prices are high.[9] Chapter 3 turns these figures into a break-even gas price.

The company already ran a marketing arm that sells its own gas and other producers' gas, and it earned $150 million of marketing adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) in the nine months before the deal.[1] On September 16, 2026 it bought Twin Eagle, a gas marketer that serves US and Canadian buyers, for about $1.4 billion, paid from cash and its credit line.[6] It booked $349 million of goodwill (the premium paid over the fair value of identifiable assets) and $1,089 million of intangible assets such as customer relationships and transport contracts.[6] Management said before the deal that Twin Eagle should add more than $200 million of annual EBITDA and $150 million of yearly savings by the end of 2028; for the fourth quarter it expects $80 million of marketing EBITDA, which includes Twin Eagle.[22][1] The company now reports two segments. In the third quarter, exploration and production earned $546 million of operating income and marketing $94 million.[12] The $94 million is GAAP operating income and includes $52 million of fees that the marketing segment charges the producing segment and that cancel in consolidation; the segment's adjusted EBITDA for the quarter was $35 million, so the $80 million guide for the fourth quarter is about 2.3 times that, which fits Twin Eagle's expected $200 million a year (about $50 million a quarter) added to the existing business. That reconciliation is our arithmetic.[4][22][1] That is a broader kind of earnings, steadier than a gas well but dependent on the quality of the trading book, and it arrives with a new chief executive, Daniel Brown, who started on October 26, 2026 after the previous chief executive left in February.[10][23][24]

The gas price cycle in plain terms

Natural gas is a cyclical business, which means the price swings widely and earnings follow it. The US Energy Information Administration (EIA) says Henry Hub averaged $7.72 per million Btu in January 2026 after Winter Storm Fern, then fell to a forecast low of $2.60 in April and May 2027, and it expects $3.48 for 2026 and $3.16 for 2027, against $3.53 in 2025 and $2.19 in 2024.[32] The reason for the forecast decline is supply: the EIA projects US dry gas output of 112.20 billion cubic feet a day in 2026 and 116.13 in 2027, against 107.64 in 2025, and says inventories above the five-year average limit price gains even though LNG exports are expected to rise from 15.1 to 17.6 and then 18.6 billion cubic feet a day.[32] For an owner this means the first quarter of 2026 was a windfall, not a run rate, and any twelve-month figure that includes it overstates a normal year. Management itself says mild weather and strong production weighed on prices in the first nine months and that demand from LNG, industry and data centers should tighten the market over the longer term, which is a hope and not yet data.[10]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Mcf, Mcfe, Bcfe: thousand cubic feet of gas; the same unit with oil and liquids converted to gas equivalent; a billion cubic feet equivalent.
  • Henry Hub: the Louisiana pipeline hub whose price is the US benchmark for natural gas. NYMEX is the exchange where its futures trade.
  • Hedge: a contract, such as a swap, collar or three-way collar, that fixes or brackets a future price. A collar sets a floor and a ceiling.
  • EBITDAX: profit before interest, tax, depreciation, depletion, amortization and exploration cost, adjusted by the company for unrealized derivative gains and a few one-off items.
  • Net debt: borrowings minus cash. Leverage is net debt divided by a year of adjusted EBITDAX.
  • Free cash flow: cash from operations minus cash capital spending, as the company defines it.
  • Return on invested capital: after-tax operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business (equity plus debt minus cash).
  • Proved reserves: gas the company can recover with reasonable certainty under current prices and costs; proved undeveloped reserves still need wells.
  • Stage 4: the Weinstein label for a stock whose price sits below a falling 50-day and a falling 200-day average. Test C is the recovery test described in Chapter 4.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Expand Energy's strength is scale and position, not pricing power. It produced 2,622 Bcfe in 2025 against 1,375 in 2024, because the Southwestern merger closed on October 1, 2024, and in the third quarter of 2026 it still produced 7.54 Bcfe a day.[13][1] Its proved reserves were 25,880 Bcfe at the end of 2025, of which 18,576 were already producing or behind pipe and 7,304 were undeveloped, which is about 9.9 years of 2025 output. The 10-K values those reserves at $19,374 million before tax at an average gas price of $3.39 per Mcf, against an enterprise value (market value plus net debt) of about $25.8 billion today, which is 1.3 times the reserve report's figure.[13][2][4]

Reserve life matters because a gas company must keep drilling to stand still. The 10-K estimates $4.2 billion of spending to turn the undeveloped reserves into producing ones, or about $0.58 per Mcfe, with $2,044 million due in 2026 and $1,379 million in 2027, and the company spent $658 million in 2025 to convert 1,585 Bcfe.[13] It holds 3,541 thousand net acres, of which 1,957 thousand are undeveloped, and says it expects no material lease expirations within three years.[13] That is the foundation of the investment case: a long, low-decline runway of drilling locations that a competitor would need years and billions to copy.

The weakness is that none of this sets the price. Revenue falls when gas falls, and the company cannot raise prices to cover costs. In 2024, when Henry Hub averaged $2.19 and merger costs weighed on results, the company lost $714 million and earned $8 million of free cash flow, and in 2025, with gas at $3.53, it earned $1,819 million and $1,839 million of free cash flow.[14][32] The same assets produced the two outcomes. Cost position and balance-sheet strength decide how deep the bad years go, which is why Chapter 2B scores them.

Cost position is average, not best. Our calculation of the third-quarter cash cost (well operating, gathering and transportation, taxes and administration) is $1.38 per Mcfe.[2][3] EQT reports operating costs of $1.09, but its definition includes its own pipelines, and Range reports $1.91 of total cash unit costs including $0.06 of interest, so definitions differ and the comparison is a guide, not a ranking.[25][29] Expand's gathering and transport bill of $0.98 per Mcfe is the lever: the 10-K shows $0.73 in Haynesville, $0.87 in northeast Appalachia and $1.30 in southwest Appalachia in 2025.[13]

Where the position is strong

Two things stand out. First, size and access: the company sells in the Gulf Coast, the Northeast and, after Twin Eagle, across the US and Canada, and it signed a 1.15 million tonnes a year LNG purchase agreement with Delfin, with a target start in 2031 and a Henry Hub price formula, which links part of its gas to world prices.[10][6] Second, the balance sheet: it holds investment grade ratings from all three agencies (BBB- from S&P, Baa3 from Moody's and BBB from Fitch, which upgraded it on May 6, 2026), with $3.9 billion of liquidity at September 30.[9] That rating is what lets a gas company buy back stock in a weak market, which it did to the tune of $909 million in the first nine months.[7]

Where the position is weak

The first weakness is that the lead in size is about to shrink. EQT has agreed to buy Range Resources and describes the result as America's largest and only vertically integrated natural gas producer, and EQT's third-quarter volume of 583 Bcfe (6.34 Bcfe a day) plus Range's 2.50 Bcfe a day would exceed Expand's 7.54 once the deal closes.[25][29] Closing is subject to approvals we have not verified, and a simple sum ignores overlap, so we treat it as a risk to the largest-producer label, not a finished fact. The second weakness is leadership change: the chief executive who led the company through its merger years left in February, an interim chief served for about eight months, a new chief financial officer joined in April, and Daniel Brown, who ran oil producer Chord Energy, started on October 26.[10][23][24] The third is the shift from a producer with a small marketing arm to an integrated marketer, which adds a business whose profits depend on trading skill, not on rock quality.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management promised specific things in 2025 and early 2026, so we compare each promise with the filings. On operations the record is good. The company cut its 2025 capital guidance (its own published forecast) by $75 million to $2.85 billion in October 2025, raised production guidance to 7.15 Bcfe a day, and then spent $2.85 billion and produced 7.18 Bcfe a day.[21][15][18]

On capital returns the record is mixed. The 2025 policy was a $2.30 regular dividend, $1.0 billion of net debt reduction and 75% of remaining free cash flow returned.[10] Net debt fell about $970 million by our calculation from the balance sheets, close to the target, and the company returned $865 million, more than the $629 million that 75% of the remaining free cash flow implies.[14][18] In 2026 the promise was at least $1.0 billion of debt reduction and a balance sheet built for cycle lows. The company redeemed about $1.3 billion of notes in April and bought back about $900 million of stock, then spent $1.4 billion on Twin Eagle, so net debt rose to $4,656 million from $4,409 million at year-end.[18][19][20][4]

Neither the acquisition nor the buyback (the company buying its own shares) is a bad use of cash, but a reader who relied on the February message would not have expected the balance sheet to be used for an acquisition within five months. Capital spending guidance has also moved from about $2.85 billion to about $3 billion, and the LNG story has been rewritten: the earlier Delfin and Gunvor agreements were terminated and replaced by one smaller Delfin agreement that starts in 2031.[18][1][10]

Fatal flaw 1: the 2027 earnings estimate is too high

The weakest assumption is that fiscal 2027 earnings stay at $8.84 a share while the EIA forecasts a 9% lower Henry Hub price. The vendor page counts two analysts for the 2026 estimate and gives no count for 2027, the estimate may include hedge gains (the 10-Q describes protection only through 2027) and profit from Twin Eagle, which has been owned for two weeks, and consensus targets have drifted down 4.5% in three months.[33][35][32][22] Each 10% change in price moves the company's gas revenue by about $613 million over nine months.[11]

Fatal flaw 2: free cash flow is thinner than it looks

The weakest assumption is that trailing free cash flow of $2.6 billion means the dividend and buybacks are safe. $1.7 billion arrived in the first quarter, hedges supplied $297 million of the $339 million in the third quarter, and capital guidance has risen to about $3 billion.[19][2][1] Unhedged, the third quarter covered about a third of the $132 million dividend, and the break-even gas price is about $3.08, above the 2027 April–May forecast of $2.60.[2][32]

An entry plan is a rule written before the trade: the price to buy at, the price at which we are wrong, and how much to risk. Ours is a limit order, which fills only at your price or better, with an entry zone, the band of prices where buying is planned, of $85.00 to $87.30. The top is solved from the $99 target and the stop. The bottom is $85.00. It sits between the $83.90 swing low and the 20-day average of $90.63.[38][37] The stop is $82.68. That is the $83.90 swing low less half an ATR, or $1.23, our rule for a tactical position. The central target is $99, the bull target is $120 and the bear value is $68.[38][37]

Test C is judged on the frozen closing snapshot, and the October 28 snapshot passes it, so the stock carries no disqualifier today. We apply the Stage 4 rule exactly as written to that snapshot. The snapshot is re-run at each later close. A price inside our zone is below the 20-day average of $90.63, so condition C2 (price at or above that average) would fail at any fill price in the zone, and the rule as written would then flag the Stage 4 disqualifier. The buy plan is therefore a stated exception to the rule, and it is our judgment. We make it because the idea is a pullback buy above a base floor that has held, and because waiting for the price to recover above $90.63 would mean buying at a reward-to-risk ratio below 1 to 1. The reader, or the orchestrator, should accept or reject the exception. If it is rejected, the verdict is WAIT until the price recovers above the 20-day average, now $90.63, and the lower zone is dropped.[37][38] Under the exception, an order placed while the snapshot passes is not cancelled merely because C2 fails on the way down. We watch the floor condition instead, which is condition C1: the price must stay more than 1% above the 63-day low of $83.25, which is $84.08.[37][38] That one level is our invalidation for new money. A close below $84.08 before a fill cancels the order and turns the verdict to REJECTED. After a fill, the stop of $82.68 governs the shares we hold, because our rules do not exit a holding mechanically while it trades above its stop. A close below $84.08 after a fill means we add nothing, and the verdict is REJECTED for new capital.[38] If no order was placed while the snapshot passed, and a fresh reading shows Stage 4 with a failing Test C at the time an order would be placed, the idea is REJECTED for new money until the price recovers above $90.63.

The size is the smallest tier, and an example shows why. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent. The risk budget for the smallest tier (TIER_4) is 35 basis points, meaning we accept losing 0.35% of the portfolio if the stop is hit. The stop distance is the gap between the entry and the stop, here $87.30 less $82.68, or 5.29%.[38] On a $100,000 portfolio, a position that loses $350 at that stop distance would be $350 divided by 5.29%, or $6,616, which is 6.6% of the portfolio. The tier also has a cap of 2.0%, or $2,000, which is the lower number and therefore the size we use. That is about 23 shares at $87.30, and a stop-out would lose about $106, or 0.11% of the portfolio. We chose the smallest tier for three reasons: the stock is in Stage 4, the return gate is passed by 0.1 point, and the 2026 estimate rests on two analysts with no count found for 2027. We keep the TACTICAL_MOMENTUM bucket from the watchlist, because a commodity price taker with a Stage 4 chart does not fit a multi-year compounder and the tactical stop rule is tighter.

Once the stock is held, the let-winners-run rule applies: we do not trim only because the RSI passes 70, the weight may grow to 22% as the stock appreciates, and the stop trails the rising 21-day or 50-day average.[38] For a gas producer we would still reduce if the thesis breaks, which means a close below the trailing stop or a 2027 estimate below $8.00. We recommend QUALIFIED_DEFERRED, not ADD_TO_SHORTLIST, because the shortlist already holds three gas producers (EQT, AR and GPOR) that move on the same Henry Hub driver, so Expand adds the same risk with a weaker chart and clears the floor only at a price the market has not offered.[39] If the orchestrator has a free tactical slot and wants a lower-multiple gas name, this dossier supports adding it on the conditions above.
