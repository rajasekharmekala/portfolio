1. What the company does

McKesson Corporation buys drugs and medical supplies from manufacturers and sells them to pharmacies, hospitals, doctors' offices and cancer clinics. Its fiscal year ends on March 31, so fiscal 2026 means the twelve months to March 31, 2026. In that year revenue was $403.4 billion, gross profit was $14.55 billion and operating income was $6.21 billion.[1] That is 3.6 cents of gross profit and 1.54 cents of operating income per sales dollar (our calculation). Think of a toll road: each trip pays a tiny fee, so profit depends on volume and on keeping costs flat as volume grows. All profit figures here are GAAP, short for generally accepted accounting principles, unless we say adjusted.

Since the second quarter of fiscal 2026 the company reports four segments. North American Pharmaceutical moves branded, generic, specialty and biosimilar drugs through a network of 27 distribution centers in the US.[1] Oncology & Multispecialty supports community doctors who treat cancer and other complex diseases; it includes the US Oncology Network, and management said on the August call that it serves more than 14,000 providers across community-based specialties.[13] In June 2025 McKesson paid $2.5 billion in cash for a 70 percent controlling interest in Core Ventures, the business arm of Florida Cancer Specialists.[1] Prescription Technology Solutions sells software and services that connect patients, pharmacies, insurers and drug makers. Medical-Surgical Solutions sells supplies to physician offices, surgery centers and nursing homes, and McKesson plans to separate it into a new company named Wellverse, which is expected to start operating under that name in January 2027.[13]

Who pays depends on the product. Pharmacies and providers pay McKesson for the drugs they receive. Manufacturers are customers too, for distribution and for programs that help patients get therapy. For branded drugs the contract pays a fee for service: management said more than 95 percent of the branded business works that way.[14] A list-price cut therefore lowers reported revenue far more than it lowers profit. The mix still matters. First-quarter fiscal 2027 revenue from GLP-1 medications, a drug class that management reports on its own line, was $15 billion, up 24 percent.[13] Customer concentration is high. CVS Health was about 24 percent of fiscal 2026 revenue, and the next two customers were 11 percent and 10 percent.[1]

Profit is spread differently from revenue. North American Pharmaceutical is 83 percent of sales but 48 percent of adjusted segment profit, because its margin is about 1 cent per dollar. Oncology & Multispecialty is 12 percent of sales and 20 percent of segment profit. Prescription Technology Solutions is under 1.5 percent of sales and 16 percent of profit, at a 19 percent margin.[5] So McKesson is a low-margin pipe plus two higher-margin service businesses. Any forecast must also allow for the Medical-Surgical separation, which removes 14.5 percent of segment profit. On June 1, 2026 an Apollo fund bought about 13 percent of that business for $1.25 billion, and on June 9 McKesson put in place a $2.25 billion Term Loan B to support the separation.[6] A trade-press report of the 2025 investor day puts completion in the second half of calendar 2027.[23]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Adjusted earnings per share is McKesson's own profit-per-share measure. It removes items the company calls one-time, such as deal gains, restructuring and litigation charges, so it can run above or below the audited GAAP figure.
  • Wholesale acquisition cost (WAC) is the list price a manufacturer sets for a drug. McKesson's revenue follows it, but its fee does not.
  • Noncontrolling interest is the slice of a subsidiary's profit that belongs to outside owners, such as Apollo's stake in Medical-Surgical Solutions.
  • Group purchasing organization is a buying club that pools many practices' orders to win lower drug prices.
  • Accelerated share repurchase is a deal in which a bank delivers shares to the company up front and the company pays for them over time.
  • Equity method is an accounting treatment in which a minority investment shows up as a share of the investee's profit rather than as consolidated sales.
  • Reverse DCF (reverse discounted cash flow) works backward from today's market value to the growth rate in cash flow that the price needs.
  • Pivot is the price level, usually the top of a resting range, that a stock must clear to confirm a breakout.
  • Asymmetry is the ratio of the gain if the idea works to the loss if it fails, also called reward-to-risk.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest part of the case is scale and position. The 10-K, the annual report filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, names Cencora and Cardinal Health as McKesson's primary competitors in distribution.[1] A health-system research group puts the three at about 96 percent of US pharmaceutical distribution.[35] That is a short field of scaled national rivals, so entry by a new competitor is unlikely. We have no cited share figure for McKesson alone, so the moat claim rests on renewals and margins, not on a share number. The renewal evidence is good. On October 1, 2026 McKesson signed an agreement in principle to extend its largest customer, CVS Health, through June 2032, five years past the old June 2027 expiry. The company reaffirmed its fiscal 2027 guidance in the same release.[15] Cardinal Health, which also supplies CVS, announced a binding letter of intent to extend through June 30, 2032 the same day.[32] The definitive McKesson contract is still unsigned and its terms are not public. The EDGAR filing list shows no 8-K since October 6, so nothing new has been filed.[24]

The second strength is a specialty and oncology platform that earns more per dollar than distribution. Oncology & Multispecialty grew adjusted segment profit 53 percent in fiscal 2026, to $1,435 million, and its adjusted margin rose from 2.54 to 2.96 percent.[5] In the first quarter of fiscal 2027 its profit was up 41 percent, and management said that excluding Core Ventures revenue grew about 24 percent and profit about 15 percent, in line with the segment's 13 to 16 percent long-term target.[13][12] Industry analysts say the three wholesalers have spent more than $20 billion buying stakes in physician management companies, which lets them earn a wholesale margin and a share of practice revenue on the same drug.[36] That strategy is why McKesson is paying $2.25 billion for a research services firm and $1.4 billion for an infusion company: it is buying more of the care chain. The returns on those deals are not yet visible in any filing.

The weaknesses are thin margins, concentration, policy exposure and an old legal bill. The consolidated adjusted operating margin was 1.56 percent in fiscal 2025 and 1.60 percent in fiscal 2026 (our calculation from the segment schedules).[5][1] At that margin a change of 0.1 point moves operating profit by about $400 million on $403 billion of revenue (our calculation). The top three customers were 45 percent of fiscal 2026 revenue, and CVS alone was 21 percent of trade receivables.[1] The 10-K names the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 as a drug-price risk, and management called the 340B drug-discount program proposal preliminary because it is still in a comment period.[1][13] Industry data show distribution revenue growth slowing from double digits to mid-single digits in 2026, because of list-price cuts, slower GLP-1 growth and more biosimilars.[36] The opioid settlement liability was $5,692 million at March 31, 2026, with $601 million due within twelve months.[1]

The last point is the balance sheet. McKesson has a total deficit of $1,777 million at March 31, 2026, and by June 30 it was $3,864 million.[1][6] It has bought back so many shares that equity is negative, and it funds inventory with $59,973 million of payables to suppliers.[1] That float is a real advantage while sales grow. It also means working capital can swing operating cash flow by billions of dollars in one quarter. The first quarter of fiscal 2027 showed it: free cash flow was an outflow of $372 million, against an outflow of $1,107 million a year earlier.[6] The cybersecurity incident adds a new risk to watch, since the company said investigation continues and has not called it material.[19][20]

Customer concentration, in numbers

CVS was about 28 percent of McKesson revenue in fiscal 2024 and about 24 percent in each of fiscal 2025 and fiscal 2026.[3][2][1] The ten largest customers were about 43 percent of trade receivables at March 31, 2026.[1] Cardinal Health depends on the same buyer even more: CVS was 28 percent of its fiscal 2026 revenue.[33] Industry data put five buyers, CVS, Cigna, Walgreens, Walmart and UnitedHealth, at more than 40 percent of all US drug distribution revenue in 2026.[36] A loss of CVS volume would show up first as lower revenue. Fixed distribution costs would stay, so operating profit would fall too. That is why the extension to June 2032 matters more than any quarterly number, and why the unsigned definitive contract is the first item in the risk chapter.
