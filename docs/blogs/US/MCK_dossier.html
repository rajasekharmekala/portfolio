1. Origin, Corporate Evolution & Past Stock Cycles

Core Investor Question: How did a 19th-century New York drug importer survive two historic accounting scandals, hostile takeovers, and an $8 billion opioid reckoning to become the largest pharmaceutical logistics engine in North America?

1833–1938: The Manhattan Importer, National Roll-Up, and the Coster Scandal

McKesson Corporation's roots stretch back nearly two centuries to 1833. John McKesson and Charles Olcott opened Olcott & McKesson on Maiden Lane in New York City [16], [17]. Their model was simple: import botanical drugs, therapeutic chemicals, and apothecary goods from Europe, then sell them to local druggists across the United States [16], [17]. After Olcott died in 1853, longtime employee Daniel Robbins became a partner, creating McKesson & Robbins [16], [17]. Two years later, in 1855, the firm entered drug manufacturing. It made fluid extracts, tinctures, pills, and tablets that won global acclaim [16], [17].

By the early 1900s, U.S. drug distribution remained split across hundreds of local jobbers. In the 1920s, McKesson & Robbins launched a sweeping roll-up, persuading regional wholesalers to join as subsidiaries [16], [17]. From 1928 to 1937, the firm bought wholesale drug houses in 47 U.S. and Canadian cities [17]. Sales hit $140 million by 1929, creating the nation's first coast-to-coast drug network [17].

That rapid growth set the stage for McKesson's first crisis in 1938. CEO F. Donald Coster was later unmasked as Philip Musica, a twice-convicted fraudster using an alias [16], [17]. A skeptical treasurer spotted cash-flow gaps in the crude-drug unit [16], [17]. Probes showed that roughly $19 million—one-quarter of the $86.6 million in reported assets—was fake inventory and phantom Canadian warehouses [16], [17]. The scandal reshaped U.S. corporate rules. It pushed the SEC and accounting bodies to require physical inventory checks and direct receivables confirmation [16]. Even so, McKesson's wholesale routes were vital to U.S. pharmacies. The firm survived receivership in the early 1940s with its core distribution network intact [16], [17].

1967–2017: Conglomerate Detours, the HBOC Collapse, and Specialty Seeds

In 1967, San Francisco-based Foremost Dairies took over McKesson & Robbins in a hostile bid. That formed Foremost-McKesson Inc., an unfocused group spanning dairy, liquor, chemicals, real estate, and drug wholesaling [16], [17]. After fending off a 1976 raid by Victor Posner, management spent years selling side businesses [17]. In 1982–1983, the firm sold its Foremost dairy unit and adopted the name McKesson Corporation [16], [17]. It then committed to pure-play healthcare distribution [16], [17].

In the early 1990s, McKesson built PCS Health Systems, an early pharmacy benefit manager (PBM). After Merck bought Medco for $6.6 billion in 1993, McKesson sold PCS to Eli Lilly for $4 billion in cash in 1994 [17]. It paid $76 per share to owners and invested $600 million in warehouse automation [17]. Five years later, management hit its second major accounting crisis. In 1999, McKesson bought healthcare IT firm HBO & Company (HBOC) to form McKessonHBOC [16]. Within months, auditors found widespread premature revenue-recognition fraud at HBOC, leading to prosecutions and a governance overhaul [16].

Over the next fifteen years, McKesson rebuilt trust by focusing on operations and adding high-margin adjacencies. It bought US Oncology in 2010 to anchor community cancer care and grew in Canada and Europe [16]. In 2017, it bought CoverMyMeds, a key software deal that later anchored Prescription Technology Solutions (RxTS) [16].

2018–2026: Opioid Reckoning, European Exit, and the Specialty Pivot

From 2016 to 2022, McKesson and its Big Three peers faced their worst legal threat: thousands of opioid lawsuits. McKesson had paid $13.25 million in 2008 and $300 million in 2017 for Controlled Substances Act reporting failures [16], [21]. Industry-wide trial exposure was estimated up to $95 billion [16], [21]. In FY2021, McKesson booked huge litigation charges that drove a $5.04 billion GAAP operating loss [15]. In 2022, McKesson, Cencora, and Cardinal Health reached a $21 billion national settlement over 18 years [16], [21]. McKesson's share was capped at $7.9 billion, turning an open-ended threat into a predictable annual cash payment [16], [21].

At the same time, McKesson moved its headquarters from San Francisco to Irving, Texas in April 2019. That move coincided with Brian Tyler becoming CEO [16]. Tyler also became Board Chair on May 1, 2026, and Kenny Cheung became CFO on May 29, 2026 [1], [5]. Under their lead, McKesson executed a three-part shift. First, it exited lower-margin European retail and distribution, closing the final Norway sale on January 30, 2026 [11]. Second, it exited Change Healthcare in 2020 and invested in oncology and specialty MSOs [5], [13]. It bought controlling stakes in Florida Cancer Specialists' Core Ventures, PRISM Vision Holdings, and Precision Medicine Group ($2.25 billion, closed November 2, 2026) [5], [8], [13], [14]. It also signed a $1.4 billion deal for a 49% stake in Option Care Health [5], [12]. Third, in FY2026–FY2027 it moved to spin off Medical-Surgical Solutions as Wellverse, selling a 13% stake to Apollo Funds for $1.25 billion in June 2026 [9].

Past Business Cycles: Why Wholesale Volume Endures While Margins Breathe

Unlike cyclical industrials, McKesson's prescription volumes grew through recessions, pandemics, and rate shocks because chronic drugs are non-discretionary [18].

Instead, McKesson's past earnings cycles stemmed from three internal and policy forces. First, generic drug price deflation hit from 2016 to 2018 [19]. Second, major contract shifts moved volume, such as winning UnitedHealth's $35B+ OptumRx contract from Cardinal Health in mid-2025 while absorbing Rite Aid's bankruptcy [9], [19]. Third, one-time legal charges hit GAAP results, led by the FY2021 opioid accrual [15].

The pattern across two centuries is clear. Accounting scandals and conglomerate detours hurt shareholders temporarily, yet they never broke the core delivery routes. Whenever management returned to disciplined capital allocation, cash flow recovered quickly. Today's North American focus leaves McKesson leaner and far more profitable per dollar of invested capital [2], [11].

Branded drugmakers sometimes raise or cut Wholesale Acquisition Cost (WAC). In January 2026, branded WAC cuts shaved ~4% off North American Pharma's Q2 FY2027 revenue growth, yet operating profit barely budged [8].

Likewise, $16 billion per quarter in GLP-1 pass-through volume boosts revenue faster than profit because branded fees are mostly fixed per unit [8], [19]. Segment operating profit dollars—not gross revenue—are the true yardstick of value [8], [19].

3. Distribution, Supply Chain & Unit Economics

Core Investor Question: How does a distributor earning a 1.54% GAAP operating margin generate $5.41 billion in annual Free Cash Flow and an extraordinary return on invested capital?

Physical Cold-Chain Infrastructure & DSCSA Serialization

McKesson runs a coast-to-coast network of automated distribution centers, ultra-cold specialty hubs, and forward depots. It delivers next-morning to tens of thousands of pharmacies, hospitals, and clinics [1], [18]. Under the federal Drug Supply Chain Security Act (DSCSA), every package needs unit-level electronic track-and-trace data from maker to dispenser [1]. Staying compliant takes ongoing spending on barcode robotics, warehouse software, and cold-chain gear. Total capex was $745 million in FY2026 ($436 million PP&E and $309 million software), or just 0.18% of sales [2]. Over 60% of that capex funds supply-chain upgrades, automated picking, and ultra-cold storage for cell therapies and GLP-1s [8], [19].

On procurement, McKesson pools generic buying power through ClarusONE, its joint venture with Walmart. That mirrors Cardinal Health's Red Oak Sourcing venture with CVS Health [22]. This scale wins deep generic discounts while passing through branded prices under fee-for-service deals [18], [22]. Independent and chain pharmacies rely on daily auto-replenishment to avoid tying up cash in slow stock. McKesson's hubs thus act as shared regional vaults for the U.S. pharmacy system [18].

The Negative Working Capital Flywheel & Credit Risk Management

The secret to McKesson's cash machine sits on its balance sheet. On March 31, 2026, McKesson held $27,985 million in net receivables (~25 days sales) and $24,207 million in inventories (~23 days on hand) [2]. Those $52,192 million in assets were funded by $59,973 million in drafts and accounts payable (~56 days payable) [2]. McKesson collects from pharmacies and hospitals faster than it pays drugmakers. Net trade working capital is therefore negative by $7,781 million [2]. As volume grows, payables rise faster than receivables and stock, turning growth into a cash source rather than a drain.

That working-capital engine carries credit risk when retail pharmacy chains stumble. During Rite Aid's bankruptcy, McKesson booked a $189 million pre-tax bad-debt charge in Q1 FY2026 before recognizing a $206 million pre-tax credit upon resolution [2], [9], [11]. Importantly, McKesson's negative working capital is genuine supplier float—not off-balance-sheet accounts-receivable factoring—with zero factoring facilities disclosed in its FY2026 Form 10-K or Q2 FY2027 Form 10-Q [1], [2]. On a 90-day year-end basis in the FY2026 10-K, customer receivables stood at 22 days, inventories at 24 days, and drafts and accounts payable at 59 days; by September 30, 2026, drafts and accounts payable reached $67,761 million against $30,806 million of receivables and $28,217 million of inventories [1], [15]. Meanwhile, Medical-Surgical Solutions (Wellverse) showed unit-economics friction in Q2 FY2027, with adjusted operating profit falling 10% YoY to $225 million on ambulatory product-mix headwinds, even as consolidated 6M FY2027 Free Cash Flow reached $2.6 billion ($2.9 billion in Q2 alone) [5], [8].

In Q2 FY2027, GLP-1 revenue jumped 26% YoY to $16 billion in one quarter (~$64B annualized) [8]. Because GLP-1s have high prices, cold-chain needs, and capped fees, they lower McKesson's gross margin percentage [8], [19].

As CFO Kenny Cheung noted on the Q2 FY2027 call, gross profit dollars and operating leverage matter most. Consolidated operating expenses as a share of gross profit fell ~141 bps YoY in Q2 FY2027, proving scale absorbs GLP-1 volume profitably [8].

In sum, negative working capital and low capex turn a 1.54% GAAP operating margin into 106% free cash flow conversion [2]. Each new dollar of specialty or GLP-1 volume rides on existing automated rails, lifting cash generation without heavy new capital.

4. Industry Structure, Competitive Moat & External Learning Hub

Core Investor Question: Why have Amazon, Walmart, and integrated PBMs failed to disintermediate the Big Three drug wholesalers—and where are the real cracks in McKesson's moat?

The Big Three Oligopoly (>90% U.S. Market Share)

U.S. pharmaceutical distribution is a textbook three-firm oligopoly. Together, McKesson ($403.4B FY26 revenue), Cencora ($321.3B FY25 / ~$337.4B FY26 unaudited), and Cardinal Health ($254.2B FY26 revenue) control more than 90% of U.S. prescription drug distribution [18], [20]. Each giant anchors a distinct set of mega-accounts: McKesson distributes for CVS Health (~24% of MCK revenue, extended through June 2032) and OptumRx (won from Cardinal Health in mid-2025, >$35B annual volume); Cencora anchors Walgreens Boots Alliance (supply agreement through 2029); and Cardinal Health partners with CVS on generic sourcing via Red Oak Sourcing [5], [18], [19], [22].

Why has no tech giant or new entrant cracked this oligopoly? First, minimum efficient scale requires dozens of FDA- and DEA-licensed, DSCSA-serialized, cold-chain warehouses capable of 99.9% fill rates within 12 hours [1], [18]. Second, the sub-2% wholesale operating margin leaves virtually zero profit pool for a challenger to undercut [18]. Third, switching distributors is operationally perilous for a hospital or national pharmacy chain: it requires re-integrating EDI ordering, DEA controlled-substance compliance, 340B split-billing software, and automated pharmacy replenishment [18], [19]. Even Walmart, which could easily run its own warehouses, partners with McKesson in ClarusONE rather than replicating national pharma logistics [22].
