1. What the company does

MercadoLibre is two businesses that share users. The first is a marketplace where people and merchants buy and sell, much like Amazon and eBay combined for Latin America. The second is Mercado Pago, which began as the payment button on that marketplace. It now offers digital wallets, card machines for shops, savings accounts, insurance, credit cards and loans. In the second quarter of 2026 the company reported net revenues and financial income of $10,169 million, up 50% in dollars and 43% once currency moves are removed[1]. Gross merchandise value (GMV), the total dollar value of goods sold through the marketplace, was $21.9 billion, up 44% in dollars and 36% without currency effects[1]. Total payment volume (TPV), the dollar value of everything paid through Mercado Pago, was $101 billion, up 56%[1]. The phrase FX-neutral means last year's sales restated at this year's exchange rates, which strips out the effect of a rising or falling currency.

Money comes in through two revenue groups. Commerce revenue comes from marketplace fees, shipping fees, sales of goods the company buys and resells itself (called first-party or 1P sales), advertising, classified listings and memberships. Fintech revenue comes from fees on payments, financing fees, interest on consumer and merchant loans, and interest on cash balances, net of the interest paid to users who keep money in the account[1]. In the second quarter Commerce revenue grew 40% and Fintech revenue grew 47% without currency effects[1]. Advertising is the highest-margin Commerce line. It grew 62% without currency effects, and the company says it passed a 10% share of the Latin American digital advertising market for the first time[1].

Credit is the second engine. The loan book passed $16 billion, up 75% in a year, and the company sets aside money for loans that will go unpaid. Those provisions were $1,276 million in the quarter against $690 million a year earlier[1]. Two ratios show how lending is going. The 15 to 90 day NPL ratio, the share of loans overdue by 15 to 90 days, was 7.0%. NIMAL, net interest margin after losses, was 20.7%, down from 23.0% a year ago[1]. Logistics is the third engine. MercadoLibre ships most items through its own network, and in Brazil it cut the order value above which shipping is free. That wins buyers but costs money on each parcel[1].

Who pays depends on the product. Buyers pay for goods, shipping and credit. Sellers pay fees, shipping and advertising, and many borrow from the company. Shops pay for card machines and payment processing. Users also leave money in Mercado Pago accounts, $16.0 billion at 30 June 2026, and the company pays them a yield to keep it there[1]. Where the money comes from matters because Brazil produced $5,530 million of second-quarter revenue, Mexico $2,337 million, Argentina $1,839 million and other countries $463 million[1]. Brazil alone is 54% of the total. That is why a Brazilian election moved this stock by 7.8% in one day[28].

How the pieces fit together

The company's argument is that shopping and money reinforce each other. Users who use both sides, which the company calls ecosystemic users, grew 37% in the second quarter and generated 70% more GMV and 55% more items per user than marketplace-only users[1]. People who hold the Mercado Pago credit card are two to three times more likely to remain in that group[1]. The card book alone grew 104% in a year to $6.6 billion by the first quarter[3]. The cost is that a new card holder starts out unprofitable: card NIMAL was -2.5% in the second quarter, and the company issued 2.6 million new cards against 1.6 million a year earlier[1]. The logic is the same as free shipping. Spend first, earn it back as users mature. Chapter 2B tests whether that has happened.

Who runs it and how they are paid

Ariel Szarfsztejn became chief executive on 1 January 2026 in a transition the company had planned, and founder Marcos Galperin is executive chairman[4]. The Galperin family trust holds 7.00% of the shares and Baillie Gifford holds 7.46%[11]. 2. Why the business is strong or weak

MercadoLibre's strength is that one app handles shopping, delivery and money, and each part makes the others cheaper to run. The company says users who use both the marketplace and Mercado Pago grew 37% in the second quarter and generate 70% more merchandise volume than marketplace-only users[1]. That is a company claim, not an audited figure. The volumes behind it are in the filed results, though. GMV grew 36% without currency effects in the second quarter, and the rate has stayed between 35% and 37% in each of the last five quarters[1]. Management also says its market shares are reaching new highs[3], which we treat as a claim we cannot verify from filings.

Three assets are hard to copy. The first is a delivery network. The company says free or slow shipments now earn a positive contribution after variable costs across half of the price ranges between R$19 and R$79[1]. The second is credit data. Lending to people who already shop and get paid through the platform lets the company see their behaviour before it lends, and loans overdue by 15 to 90 days were 7.0% of the book[1]. The third is advertising: sellers pay to be seen by buyers who are already in the app, and advertising revenue grew 62% without currency effects[1]. A rival would have to build all three at once, in several countries, to match it.

The moat is under pressure from three directions. Competition is the first. Shopee, owned by Sea Limited, says it raised its Brazilian order-handling capacity by 60% in a year[31] and moved from 16 to 26 distribution centres[32]. The company's own annual report says the online commerce market is highly competitive and that rivalry has intensified as international players expand, mainly in Brazil[5]. The second pressure is price. To win share the company lowered its free-shipping threshold, gave PIX discounts to buyers (PIX is Brazil's instant payment system) and cut seller fees. Gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct costs of selling and delivering. It fell to 40.9% in the second quarter from 45.6% a year earlier (our calculation from gross profit of $4,159 million and $3,094 million)[1]. The third pressure is the cost of devices: the chief financial officer said higher chip prices raised card-machine costs and that this is "there to stay"[23].

Concentration and credit cycles are the weak points. Brazil is 54% of revenue and Argentina 18%[1]. Argentina grew 48% in local terms but only 20% in dollars, which shows how much a falling currency can take back[1]. Lending adds a cycle that a pure marketplace does not have. Provisions for bad loans were 6.2% of revenue in 2021, 10.2% in 2022, 8.9% in 2024, 10.7% in 2025 and 12.5% in the second quarter of 2026 (our calculation from [6][7][5][1]). A further risk is tax: the company discloses legal actions with a reasonably possible loss of up to $622 million, none of it accrued[2].

What this means for an owner: demand for the product is strong and well spread across customers, but the business has not yet shown that this growth turns into profit at the old rate. Chapter 2B scores the five numbers that decide that question, and Chapter 3 shows five years of results.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of results tell a story of very fast growth and uneven profit. Revenue grew from $7,069 million in 2021 to $28,893 million in 2025, which is 4.1 times as much[6][5]. Net income grew from $83 million to $1,997 million, and diluted profit per share from $1.67 to $39.40[6][5]. The last year looks different. In 2025 revenue grew 39% but net income grew only about 4.5%. For the twelve months to June 2026 net income was $1,863 million, below the 2025 figure, and second-quarter profit per share of $9.19 was 11% lower than the $10.31 of a year earlier[5][1].

The reason is margin. Operating margin peaked at 14.6% in 2023 on the company's recast figures, was 11.1% in 2025 and fell to 6.7% in the second quarter of 2026[5][1]. Management attributes this to deliberate spending on shipping, lower seller fees, cross-border trade, first-party sales and credit cards[4][1]. It estimated the combined drag at 5 to 6 points of margin in the fourth quarter of 2025[4]. Provisions for bad loans rose from 10.2% to 12.5% of revenue over the past year as the loan book grew faster than revenue[1].

Cash flow needs care. Cash from operations was $12,116 million in 2025, but $5,341 million of that was growth in funds payable to customers, which is deposits and not profit[5]. The company lends much of that cash: loans receivable rose by $6,659 million in 2025 and by $4,069 million in the first half of 2026[5][1]. Its own measure, adjusted free cash flow, deducts that lending and was $1,481 million in 2025 and only $158 million in the first half of 2026, against $512 million a year earlier[5][1]. That is the number we use.

The balance sheet is funded by debt and deposits against a small amount of equity. Total debt was $13,176 million at June 2026 against equity of $7,834 million, and net debt (debt minus cash and liquid investments) was $6,425 million[1]. That is 1.65 times EBITDA and interest is covered 13.7 times by operating income. Both are comfortable, and S&P rates the company BBB- with a stable outlook[33]. The stock trades at 45.5 times trailing earnings and 2.4 times sales, with a market value of $84.82 billion[18].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report rests on one assumption that could be wrong. This chapter names each one, says what evidence would show it wrong and says when we would know. The weakest assumption in each estimate

For return on invested capital, the weakest assumption is that equity plus net debt is the right measure of capital. A large part of the balance sheet is customer money ($16.0 billion of funds payable to customers) and loans that fund the credit book[1]. If that mix changes, the return changes. A rise in invested capital faster than operating income in the third report would show the 16.2% is falling toward the 12% floor.

For adjusted free cash flow, the weakest assumption is that new lending is a choice that earns its keep. If the loans do not perform, the cash is gone. We would see it first in the 15 to 90 day NPL ratio rising above 8% (it was 7.0% in Q2 and 8.0% in Q1) or NIMAL falling below 18% (17.8% in Q1)[1][3]. For net debt to EBITDA, the assumption is that EBITDA holds. A real that is 8% weaker cuts the dollar value of Brazilian profit, and a higher funding cost cuts it again.

For the reverse DCF, the weakest assumption is that trailing net income is a fair starting point. If margins recover, the starting point is low and the implied growth is too high. If bad loans rise, the starting point is flattering. For our 2027 profit per share of $50.0 the weakest input is the net margin of 4.9%, which is above the 4.6% of the first half of 2026. A third-quarter net margin below 4.5% would tell us it is too high.

For the $2,000 value the weakest input is the multiple of 40 times earnings. It is a judgement between today's 45.5 times and Sea Limited's forward 17.7 times[18][34]. For the $1,873 weighted value it is the probabilities. We set them from the evidence in Chapter 5, but a change of ten points moves the value by about $140.

Beyond 1.6 ATR is the NO_BUY band, where we do not chase the price. The top of the zone is the edge of the STANDARD band, and today's price sits at the edge of the LIMIT_ORDER_REDUCE band. A stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong and we sell. For a Stage 1 basing stock the rule sets it at the base low minus 1.0 ATR, which is $1,506.72. For TIER_3, the tier we use for a Core Alpha idea with one failing KPI and a live event risk, the risk budget is 50 basis points and the position range is 2.0% to 3.5% of net worth. The formula takes the smaller of 3.5% and 50 basis points divided by the 8.55% stop distance, which is 5.85%, so the 3.5% cap binds: $3,088 of net worth of $88,229.73[41].
