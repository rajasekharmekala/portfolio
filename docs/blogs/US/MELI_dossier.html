1. What the company does

MercadoLibre is two businesses that feed each other. The marketplace lets people and companies buy and sell goods online in 18 countries, and Mercado Pago, its payments and banking arm, operates in 8.[1] Outside sellers account for most sales, and the company sells from its own stock in selected categories, which is under 10% of gross merchandise value (GMV), the dollar value of all goods sold on the marketplace.[1] In the second quarter of 2026, GMV was $21.9 billion, up 44% in dollars and 36% after removing currency swings, and 89 million unique buyers shopped in the quarter, up 26%.[7][5]

The company reports two revenue lines. Commerce earns marketplace fees, shipping fees, advertising, subscriptions and sales from its own stock. Fintech earns fees on payments made away from the marketplace, interest on loans and credit cards, and income on customer cash and investments.[1] In 2025, revenue was $28.9 billion, and Fintech brought in $12.6 billion of it, up 46.2%, so Fintech is now about 44% of revenue.[1] Credit is the fastest part: the loan book passed $16 billion in the second quarter of 2026, up 75% in a year.[7]

Mercado Pago does more than move money. It pays interest on balances, issues credit cards, makes consumer and merchant loans, sells insurance, and processes card payments in stores and online. Total payment volume (TPV) is the dollar value of the payments it handled, and it reached $101 billion in the second quarter of 2026, up 56% after removing currency effects.[7] Its monthly active users numbered 88 million, up from 68 million a year earlier.[5] Assets under management, meaning customer money held in its yielding accounts and funds, reached $23 billion, up 68%.[7]

Where the money comes from by country matters. In the second quarter of 2026, Brazil produced 54% of revenue, Mexico 23% and Argentina 18%.[5] Argentina is small by sales but produced 40% of the profit measure the company calls direct contribution, which is segment revenue less the costs a country team controls.[5][32] Results are reported in US dollars while sales are made in reais and pesos, so currency moves matter: revenue grew 50% in dollars and 43% after removing currency effects.[7]

How the ecosystem works

Users who engage with both the marketplace and Mercado Pago are called ecosystemic users. They grew 37% in the second quarter of 2026, and they generated 70% more GMV than users who only shop.[7] Holders of the Mercado Pago credit card are 2 to 3 times more likely to stay ecosystemic.[7] The company's loyalty program, MELI+, grew its subscribers 72% in the quarter.[7] This is why management says it will accept lower profit today to bring more users into both halves: the economics improve for each user who uses both.[7] Chapter 2 tests whether that claim is a competitive advantage or a spending habit.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is the after-tax operating profit earned on each dollar of capital put into the business.
  • free cash flow (FCF) is cash left after running the business and buying equipment.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before financing and accounting charges.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is net profit divided by the number of shares.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is pay in shares; MercadoLibre pays its long-term bonus in cash instead.
  • dilution is the fall in each owner's share of the company when new shares are issued.
  • capital expenditure is money spent on equipment, buildings and technology that last more than a year.
  • gross margin is revenue less the direct cost of sales, as a share of revenue.
  • operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue.
  • net margin is net income as a share of revenue.
  • price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share, and it tells you how many dollars investors pay for one dollar of yearly profit.
  • net debt is borrowing less cash and liquid investments.
  • guidance is what management says it expects for future results.
  • consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who follow the stock.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been rising; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in dollars.
  • 50-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 50 sessions.
  • 200-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 200 sessions, a gauge of the long-term trend.
  • Stage 2 is the rising phase of a stock's cycle, with the price above rising 50-day and 200-day averages, and Stage 4 is the falling phase below falling averages.
  • base is a flat resting zone where a stock trades sideways before a new move.
  • pivot is the price above which a base is broken to the upside.
  • breakout is a move above the pivot on heavy volume.
  • stop loss is a preset price at which we sell to cap the loss.
  • limit order is an order that fills only at the stated price or better.
  • entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy.
  • asymmetry is the gain to our target divided by the loss to our stop; we need at least 3.0 for a Core Alpha name.
  • reward-to-risk is another name for the same ratio.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The first source of strength is a delivery network that is hard to copy. In 2025 the network handled 41% more volume, close to 500 million extra shipments, without a drop in service levels.[9] More than half of shipments move through the company's own fulfillment centers, where sellers store goods so they ship fast.[1] Cost per shipment is falling: in Brazil, the local-currency cost fell 11% in the fourth quarter of 2025 and 17% in the first quarter of 2026.[8] That matters because the company cut the free-shipping threshold in Brazil from R$79 to R$19 in mid-2025, and free shipping on small orders only pays if each parcel gets cheaper.[10][7]

The second source is the link between shopping and money. Ecosystemic users spend more and stay longer, and the credit card is the tool that pulls shoppers into the ecosystem.[7] Advertising shows the same pull: ad revenue grew 62% after removing currency effects, and the company says it passed a 10% share of Latin American digital advertising.[7] Advertising is one of the highest-margin revenue lines, because an extra ad costs little to deliver.[7] In the second quarter, product development costs fell to 7.2% of revenue from 8.4%, which management links to AI coding tools, even though AI spending rose about $80 million.[7]

The moat is real but it is not free. The annual report says competition has intensified, mainly in Brazil and Mexico, as international players expand and local fintech firms grow.[1] Sea Limited's Shopee grew its GMV 28.4% in the second quarter and its Monee lending arm grew loans 62.5%.[27] Amazon's international segment grew sales 15% but earned only a 4.1% operating margin, which shows how thin returns are for a well-funded rival abroad.[24][32] MercadoLibre is paying to defend its position: in April it added discounts for buyers who pay by PIX, Brazil's instant-payment system, and cut commission rates for some sellers, and gross margin fell 2.8 percentage points from the first quarter.[7] The company calls its commission a take rate, the share of each sale it keeps as a fee, so a lower take rate on some sales cuts revenue directly.

Four weaknesses deserve plain words. First, the loan book carries real credit risk, covered in Chapter 3. Second, 40% of direct contribution comes from Argentina, where average inflation in 2025 was 44.5%.[1][5][32] Third, Mexico's tax reform pushed small sellers to raise prices; Mexican GMV growth slowed to 26% after removing currency effects, and Mexican direct contribution margin fell about 4 points from the previous quarter.[7][8] Fourth, leadership changed on January 1, 2026, when Ariel Szarfsztejn became chief executive and founder Marcos Galperin became executive chairman.[9] At the June 2026 meeting 34.4 million shares voted for executive pay and 4.4 million against, and director Stelleo Passos Tolda received 13.4 million votes for and 25.7 million withheld.[17]

3. The numbers, in plain words

MercadoLibre is a hybrid: it looks like a retailer on one side and a lender on the other. That breaks the usual tests, because normal companies borrow to buy buildings and trucks, while most of this company's new borrowing funds loans to customers, and the interest on those loans is counted as revenue. We therefore show two views of each test and say which one we trust. Every number in this chapter comes from the company's filings or from our arithmetic on them.[1][5]

Over five years revenue grew from $7.1 billion to $28.9 billion and net income from $83 million to $2.0 billion.[4][1] Operating margin rose from 6.2% in 2021 to 14.6% in 2023, slipped to 11.1% in 2025 and was 6.7% in the latest quarter.[1][7][32] Profit did not keep pace with sales for three reasons: more spending on cheap shipping and discounts, higher credit-loss provisions, and higher device and technology costs.[7]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter attacks our own conclusion. A verdict that sells a fast-growing company deserves hard questions, and the first one is whether our rules are tilted. We list the weakest assumptions, give the strongest bear case against our verdict and against the stock, and name the single claim that decides the outcome.

Weakest assumption 1: the 15% haircut to 2027 EPS

Our base EPS of $45.10 depends on cutting the Street's FY2027 figure of $55.70 by 15%. We chose 15% from four observable pressures, not from a model: provisions, Brazil discounts, device costs and falling estimates.[28][7] If the cut were only 4.6%, the base target would be $2,501 and the ratio would clear 3.0.[32] A reader who thinks margin recovery is already under way should use that figure. We would know within one quarter: the November report shows operating margin and gross margin, and a margin above 8% would point to the Street's path.[18]

Weakest assumption 2: late loans will not turn into large losses

The loan book is $16.4 billion gross, 2.1 times equity, and $3.1 billion of it is 90 or more days late.[5][7][32] The allowance covers those 1.43 times, which looks adequate, but we cannot see recoveries on written-off loans and the book is mostly unsecured consumer and card debt. If 90-plus loans keep rising by 2 points of the book every six months, the allowance charge would rise again and operating margin would fall again. Evidence that we are wrong in the good direction would be a falling 90-plus share in the third-quarter 10-Q, which appears with the November results.

Weakest assumption 3: customer money is not ours

Our free cash flow verdict depends on removing $5.0 billion of 2025 customer-fund reserves from cash flow. The company set that amount aside out of a $5.3 billion rise in funds owed to customers.[9][1] If an investor reasonably treats those balances as a permanent, free funding source, which they partly are because customers keep leaving money in accounts, the margin passes at 12.9% to 14.2%. We disclose both and call the gate FAIL because the company's own adjusted figure also excludes the reserves.[7][32]
