1. What the company does

MercadoLibre is two businesses that share users. The first is a marketplace where millions of people and merchants buy and sell, similar to a mix of Amazon and eBay for Latin America. The second is Mercado Pago, which started as the payment button on that marketplace and now offers digital wallets, card terminals for shops, savings accounts, insurance, credit cards and loans. In the second quarter of 2026 the company reported net revenues of $10,169 million, up 50% in dollars and 43% once currency moves are removed[1]. Gross merchandise value (GMV), the total dollar value of goods sold through the marketplace, was $21.9 billion, up 44% in dollars and 36% without currency effects[1]. Total payment volume (TPV), the dollar value of everything paid through Mercado Pago, was about $101 billion, up 56%[1].

The company counts its revenue in two groups. Commerce revenue comes from marketplace fees, shipping fees, first-party sales (goods MercadoLibre buys and resells itself), advertising, classified listings and memberships[1]. Fintech revenue comes from fees on payments made away from the marketplace, financing fees, interest earned on consumer and merchant loans, and interest on cash and investments, net of the interest passed on to users[1]. In the second quarter Commerce grew 40% and Fintech grew 47% without currency effects[1]. Users who use both sides, which the company calls ecosystemic users, grew 37% and generated 70% more GMV than marketplace-only users[1]. That overlap is the heart of the story: a shopper who also holds the Mercado Pago credit card tends to buy more, and a merchant who sells on the marketplace tends to borrow and take payments through the same company.

The money flows through three engines. Advertising is the highest-margin line, and the company says it passed 10% of the Latin American digital advertising market with ad revenue up 62% without currency effects[1]. Credit is the second engine: the credit portfolio passed $16 billion, up 75% from a year earlier[1], and it earns interest but also forces the company to set aside money for loans that go bad, called provisions. Logistics is the third: MercadoLibre ships almost every item it sells through its own network, and it has lowered the order value above which shipping is free in Brazil, which wins buyers but costs money per parcel[1][3]. Customer money held in Mercado Pago wallets, $16.0 billion at June 30, 2026, funds part of this, and the company pays users a yield on it to keep them[1][4].

Where the money comes from matters because reported revenue is in dollars while sales happen in reais, pesos and Mexican pesos. Brazil produced $5,530 million of second-quarter revenue, Mexico $2,337 million, Argentina $1,839 million and other countries $463 million[1]. Currency moves added to growth: Brazil grew 59% in dollars but 42% without currency effects, while Argentina grew 20% in dollars and 48% without them[1]. Argentina is only 18% of revenue (our calculation from the segment table) but produced 40% of the direct contribution, the profit of each country before shared corporate costs. That makes Argentine inflation and exchange rates a bigger swing factor for profit than the revenue share suggests.

What this means for an owner

An owner is buying a company that is deliberately giving up near-term profit to win habits. Management says its investments in free shipping, first-party selling, cross-border trade from China and the credit card cost about 5 to 6 points of operating margin in the fourth quarter of 2025[4]. The payoff, if it comes, is a larger and stickier customer pool. The risk is that rivals and a loan cycle make the payoff smaller than the cost. Plain-English glossary for this report

  • GMV (gross merchandise value): total dollar value of goods sold on the marketplace.
  • TPV (total payment volume): dollar value of payments processed by Mercado Pago.
  • NIMAL: net interest margin after losses, the spread between credit revenue and the cost of funding plus provisions, shown as a percentage of the average loan book[1].

  • Direct contribution: segment revenue minus the costs the segment controls, before shared corporate costs[1].

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position is strongest where physical reach matters. MercadoLibre leased about 3.4 million square meters of logistics space in Brazil at the end of 2025, against 1.2 million for Shopee and 709,000 for Amazon, according to a real-estate consultancy cited by a Brazilian business newspaper[22]. The same article puts MercadoLibre at about 40% of Brazilian e-commerce sales and notes that the company plans to invest R$57 billion in Brazil in 2026[22]. Warehouses, trucks, a driver network and years of delivery data are slow and expensive to copy, which is why a rival can spend heavily and still not match two-day delivery everywhere.

The company's own risk disclosure is plain about the threat. It says competition has intensified as local players grow and as international players expand, mainly in Brazil and Mexico[5]. The newspaper article lists the foreign rivals in Brazil as Amazon, Alibaba and AliExpress, Shein, Temu and Shopee[22]. MercadoLibre's response has been to cut prices to buyers and fees to sellers: since April 2026 it offers PIX (Brazil's instant bank transfer) discounts for buyers and lower fees for sellers whose listings are priced competitively, and it said the result was a sharp rise in active sellers, up 29%, but also a hit to revenue[1]. That is a sign of a leader defending its lead at its own cost.

Engagement data show the defense working so far. Unique buyers grew 26% and fintech monthly active users grew 30% in the second quarter[1]. Conversion in Brazil rose 1.1 percentage points a year after the free shipping threshold was lowered, and the share of users buying three or more product categories rose almost 6 points to an all-time high[1]. Brazil's gross merchandise value grew 39% without currency effects[1]. Mexico grew 26% on the same basis, held back by a tax reform the company flagged earlier, and Argentina grew 38% in a weak consumer climate[1].

The weaknesses are on the profit side. Second-quarter income from operations fell 17% to $683 million even as revenue rose 50%[1]. Three things squeezed gross margin by 2.8 points from the first quarter: discounts to Brazilian buyers and sellers, shipping costs that higher revenue did not fully cover, and the cost of card terminals and new-merchant acquisition, mainly in Mexico[1]. The company also operates in places with regulatory and tax friction, including a Brazilian tax dispute for which it holds a $632 million provision, offset by $593 million of court deposits[2]. A strong moat that costs this much to keep up earns lower returns than a moat that sells itself.

Competition from Amazon, Shopee and Temu

Amazon restructured its Brazilian management in 2026 to speed decisions and says its goal is to lead e-commerce there[22]. Surveys the article cites ranked Amazon third in traffic, behind MercadoLibre and Shopee, which it describes as locked in intense competition[22]. A peer comparison in chapter 3 shows that Sea Limited earns a similar 8% operating margin on trailing figures while Coupang lost money[18][21]. That tells you the price of winning logistics scale is high, and that MercadoLibre's 8.3% trailing margin (our calculation) is not unusually low for a company still building. It also tells you the margin could stay low if all four fight for the same buyers.

Argentina, currency and inflation

Argentina's inflation was 31.5% in 2025, 117.8% in 2024 and 211.4% in 2023, and its official exchange rate weakened 41.0% in 2025[5]. Inflation in the first half of 2026 was 16.8%, and the average year-over-year rate for the half was 32.9%[2]. MercadoLibre reports in dollars and sets the dollar as the working currency of its Argentine units, so a peso that falls faster than prices rise cuts reported revenue, while a peso that falls slower raises it[5]. Because Argentina earns 40% of direct contribution, a bad quarter there would be felt more than its 18% revenue share suggests.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has been consistent in telling investors that it will give up margin for growth, and the results match what it said. In the letter for the fourth quarter of 2024 it reported a 13.5% operating margin and said it was optimistic about 2025[7]. In May 2025 it said it would continue to reinvest in the business[8]. By 2025 the full-year operating margin was 11.1% (our calculation) and by the second quarter of 2026 it was 6.7%[5][2]. The promise to reinvest was kept.

A softer promise has not yet been met. In October 2025 the company said that as the Brazilian credit card matured, its margin pressure would ease[9]. Two quarters of margin since then (6.9% and 6.7%) show no easing[3][1], and card NIMAL fell from breakeven a year ago to -2.5% as new cohorts dilute the book[1]. Management points to maturing older cohorts, and says about 75% of the Brazilian card portfolio had reached its first breakeven month by the end of 2025[4]. That is plausible and not yet visible in the total.

On capital allocation the company issued $1,000 million of 5.850% notes due 2036 in September 2026 for general corporate purposes[10][11][12]. Consolidated debt including that issue would have been $11,626 million against $10,626 million at June 30[12]. The company repurchased $356 million of its own shares in 2023 and just $1 million in 2025, and its board suspended the dividend in 2018[5][6]. Growth is being funded with borrowing and customer deposits rather than with returns to owners.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Over five years MercadoLibre grew net revenues from $7,069 million in 2021 to $28,893 million in 2025 and net income from $83 million to $1,997 million[6][5]. Our calculation gives a three-year revenue growth rate of 40.0% a year and a three-year net income growth rate of 60.6% a year, measured from 2022 to 2025. Years 2021 and 2022 are shown as first reported, and 2023 to 2025 on the current presentation, which moves some financial income into revenue (2023 revenue is $15,107 million recast against $14,473 million as first reported)[5][6]. Operating margin rose to 14.6% in 2023, slid to 12.7% in 2024 and 11.1% in 2025, and is 8.3% over the twelve months to June 2026 (our calculation).

Reported cash flow from operations cannot be used as free cash for this company. In 2025 it was $12,116 million against net income of $1,997 million, but $5,341 million of that came from customer balances held in Mercado Pago rising, and provisions for bad loans of $3,091 million were added back as a non-cash cost[5]. The loans themselves, $6,659 million of net new lending, sit in investing activities and so never reduce the operating figure[5]. Subtracting capital expenditure alone would give a free cash flow of 37% of revenue, which is an artifact of the accounting and not money an owner can take out. We use the company's adjusted free cash flow instead, which deducts the growth in customer-fund holdings, capital spending and net lending and adds back related funding: $1,481 million in 2025, or 5.1% of revenue[4].

Debt rose with the loan book. Loans payable and other financial liabilities were $10,626 million at June 30, 2026, up from $9,193 million at the end of 2025 and $4,758 million at the end of 2022[1][5][6]. On the company's own definition, net debt — total debt minus cash and liquid securities — (debt and leases less available cash and investments) was $6,425 million, or 1.65 times trailing EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) (our calculation: operating income of $2,907 million plus depreciation of $985 million)[1][5]. A further $1,000 million of 5.850% notes due 2036 closed on September 14, 2026[11], so debt has risen since the last balance sheet. Operating income covers interest expense about 13.7 times (our calculation).

The price already reflects a premium. At $1,810.15 the stock trades at 49.2 times trailing earnings per share (EPS) of $36.76 (our calculation from the filings) and 32.2 times the consensus estimate for 2027 of $56.24[15]. Equity is $7,834 million, so the market value of $91.77 billion is 11.7 times book value (our calculation)[1][14]. A company earning 25.6% on average equity over the last twelve months (our calculation) can justify some of that, but the multiple depends on profit growth that the last two quarters did not show.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on one weakest assumption. For the reverse DCF it is that net income is a fair measure of owner cash, when the company must retain capital to fund loans. The bear case, the strongest honest argument against our own conclusions, is set out below as three ways the thesis could fail completely.

Bear case 1: the Brazil price war never pays back

In this case Amazon, Shopee and Temu keep subsidizing prices and delivery, and MercadoLibre must keep cutting its fees and shipping prices to hold its roughly 40% share[22][5]. Brazil's direct contribution margin has already fallen from 15.6% to 9.9% in a year[1]. The loan book grew 75% in a year to above $16 billion, so a large part of it has never been through a downturn[1]. Provisions already take 12.5% of revenue, and each extra point of revenue lost to provisions removes about $352 million from yearly operating income (our calculation on $35.18 billion of revenue), which is 12% of the trailing $2,907 million. Evidence against us is the late-payment rate staying near 7.0% as the new cards age; the company's own cohort data say a card reaches breakeven in 12 to 18 months[4].
