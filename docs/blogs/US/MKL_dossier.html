1. What the company does

Markel Group is a holding company based in Virginia with specialty insurance at its core.[1] Its insurance units cover risks that standard insurers avoid or price badly, such as marine war cover, hard-to-place liability, and workers' compensation for small firms. The company sells about 100 different products around the world, and the international arm operates in 15 countries.[12][5] Customers pay a premium up front. Claims arrive later, sometimes years later.

That gap is the heart of the model. Markel collects premiums and invests the money until claims are paid. The pool of money it holds in the meantime is called float, and it stood at $18.8 billion at the end of 2025.[5] An insurer makes money in two ways: an underwriting profit when premiums exceed claims and costs, and the investment income earned on the float. The yardstick for the first is the combined ratio, which is claims and expenses divided by premiums earned. A ratio below 100 means an underwriting profit. Markel's combined ratio was 95 percent in 2025, the eighteenth year in the last 20 with an underwriting profit.[5]

The investing side is unusual for an insurer. At September 30, 2026 Markel held $13.1 billion of listed stocks that cost $4.1 billion, which is an unrealized gain of about $9.0 billion before tax.[2] Those stocks equal 71 percent of shareholders' equity of $18.5 billion.[2] The investment strategy is led by the chief executive, Tom Gayner, and it aims for long-term returns above bonds.[5] The five-year annual return on the stock portfolio was 11.9 percent at the end of 2025, and the ten-year return was 13.5 percent.[5] The cost is volatility: stock prices move book value every quarter.

Alongside insurance sits Markel Ventures, which reports in three segments. Industrial owns makers and builders such as bakery equipment, precast concrete, fire and life safety, and equipment leasing.[12] Financial holds State National, which provides fronting (it lends its insurance licenses to other risk takers) and lender-placed cover, and Nephila, which manages insurance-linked securities.[2] Consumer and Other includes ornamental plants, whose profit peaks in the second quarter because spring is the selling season.[12] About 22,900 people work across the group.[5]

Management judges itself on adjusted operating income for the short run. This is operating income without stock-market gains and losses and without the amortization of acquired intangibles, and it was $2.27 billion over the twelve months to September 2026.[1] For the long run it uses five-year average operating income per share, which has grown 16 percent a year, while average equity grew 8 percent a year.[1] Executive pay is tied to the same two ideas: five-year average operating income and the five-year growth in the share price.[10]

Two events shape the picture today. On September 8, 2026 Steven Markel agreed to retire as Chairman, Mr. Gayner took the Chairman role as well, and Simon Wilson and Andrew Crowley became Co-Presidents.[9] On October 27, 2026 the insurance units signed an adverse development cover with Enstar for $4.8 billion of reserves in the run-off Global Reinsurance business and other exited lines, a deal we explain in Chapter 2B.[8] Both were announced around the third-quarter results, which came out after the close on October 28.[1][8]

How the money flows between the parts

The parts are designed to feed each other. Insurance produces float and profit. The investing arm puts the capital that supports insurance into listed stocks. The Ventures businesses throw off cash that can be moved to the best use, whether that is a new acquisition, more stock, or Markel's own shares.[5] Management calls this its 360-degree view of capital, and on the October 29 call Mr. Gayner said Markel's own stock "seems to be at the top of the list" when he weighs those choices.[12]

Buying back shares is the visible result. Markel repurchased $685 million of stock in the first nine months of 2026, against $344 million in the same period of 2025, and it does not pay a common dividend.[2][16] The share count has fallen about 10 percent over five years, from 13.685 million to 12.250 million.[1] What this means for an owner: per-share value can grow faster than total value, but only if the repurchase price is below what the business is worth. Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPS stands for earnings per share: the company's profit divided across all shares.
  • EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization: profit before accounting and financing adjustments. It is a poor yardstick for an insurer, so we use debt to capital instead.
  • ROIC stands for return on invested capital: the profit earned on each dollar put into the business. For an insurer we use return on equity and return on tangible common equity, which count only the shareholders' money.
  • FCF stands for free cash flow, which is the cash left after running the business and buying assets. It is not meaningful for an insurer, because premiums arrive before claims are paid.
  • SBC stands for stock-based compensation: the cost of paying employees in shares.
  • net debt is interest-bearing debt minus the cash held.
  • A buyback is the company buying its own shares back, which shrinks the share count.
  • Dilution is the rise in share count when new shares are issued.
  • A consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for a number.
  • RSI stands for relative strength index: a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has been moving. Below 30 is oversold and above 70 is overbought.
  • ATR stands for average true range: the average daily price swing, in dollars. We use it to measure distance from support.
  • The 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days, and the 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average work the same way over 50 and 200 days. Price tends to bounce off these slow lines.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein stage scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. Stage 1 is a basing floor and Stage 3 is a pullback or top.
  • A base is a sideways stretch the price builds before a move, the platform the next advance starts from. The pivot is the top of a base, and a breakout is a move above it on volume. A retest is the pullback that tests that level from above.
  • A stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong and the position is cut. A limit order is an order that only fills at your price or better, so it does not chase. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry is the ratio of potential gain to potential loss from the entry, and it is the same as reward-to-risk: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The competitive position rests on three things: underwriting that earns a profit in most years, a capital pool that compounds in listed stocks, and operating businesses that run on their own. The test of each is the record. Markel Insurance earned an underwriting profit in 18 of the last 20 years, with a combined ratio of 90 percent in 2021, 92 in 2022, 99 in 2023, and 95 in both 2024 and 2025.[5] The nine months of 2026 came in at 92.[1] That range is not the best in the industry, but it is steady for a book that is mostly specialty and liability.

Return on equity for Markel Insurance averaged 13 percent a year over five years, with a loss year in 2022 (minus 3 percent) and a high of 20 percent in 2021.[5] For the whole company, our own arithmetic on the filings gives a return on common equity of 18.1, minus 1.9, 14.5, 17.7 and 11.9 percent for 2021 to 2025, an average of 12.1 percent, and 9.8 percent for the twelve months to September 2026.[7][5][2] The swings come from the stock portfolio, not from the underwriting.

Where the business is strong

The first strength is permanence of capital. Markel pays no common dividend, carries low debt, and has an investing arm that can hold stocks for decades.[16][2] Debt is 19 percent of total capital, and interest cost was covered 14.3 times by pre-tax profit before interest in 2025.[2][5] That means pressure from outside creditors is small, so management can sit through a bad stock market without being forced to sell.

The second strength is the shape of the book. Markel writes small and mid-sized specialty risks, where it can charge for expertise, and it has pulled back where pricing does not match claims trends. In U.S. Wholesale and Specialty, premiums fell 3 percent in the third quarter because the company chose to cut construction-related liability, and that division still ran an 89.0 percent combined ratio.[12] Programs and Solutions improved to 87.0 percent from 94.2 percent a year earlier, and International ran at 89.9 percent with premiums up 45 percent.[12]

The third strength is reserve philosophy. Markel states that it sets reserves so they are more likely to prove redundant than deficient.[5] Evidence supports the claim in the ongoing divisions: they released $445 million of reserves in the first nine months of 2026, against $397 million a year earlier.[12] Releases like these lift profit in the period they are booked, which is useful to know when you read a good combined ratio.

Where the business is weaker

The first weakness is legacy. The Global Reinsurance division stopped writing in August 2025 when Markel sold its renewal rights, yet it still produced an underwriting loss of $54.3 million in the third quarter of 2026 and $106.5 million in nine months, which cost three points on the Markel Insurance combined ratio in the quarter.[5][2] The loss came from adverse development on professional liability lines.[2] The October 27 reinsurance cover with Enstar exists because management wants to limit this risk.[8]

The second weakness is counterparty risk. In the second quarter of 2026 the Financial segment booked a $205.3 million provision for expected credit losses tied to one capacity provider in bankruptcy, because reserve work on the programs it supported showed higher loss trends and the collateral did not cover the increase.[2] The provision came from one counterparty, but the risk it reveals applies to any fronting arrangement: the insurer is on the hook if the risk taker cannot pay.

The third weakness is the cycle. Management says property prices in its book are down 25 to 30 percent this year, and that construction liability claims are trending higher at double-digit rates.[12] In the aggregate, prices across the portfolio were relatively flat in the first nine months, with increases in U.S. casualty and management liability and decreases in U.S. property, international cyber, professional liability and energy.[2] A 92 percent combined ratio is harder to hold as the market softens.

The fourth weakness is reach. Return on equity for the whole company is 9.8 percent over the last twelve months, well below the 19 to 28 percent that W. R. Berkley, RLI, Kinsale and Arch report (see the peer matrix in Chapter 3).[22][23][24][25] The price-to-book multiple of 1.22 reflects that gap, and it is why the stock is not the obvious bargain it appears to be next to those names.

What the five-year record says about compounding

Book value per common share, our arithmetic on the filings, went from $1,034.92 at the end of 2021 to $935.65 in 2022, $1,095.95 in 2023, $1,276.31 in 2024 and $1,477.18 in 2025, then $1,513.35 at September 30, 2026.[5][7][2] That is a compound rate of 8.3 percent a year over 4.75 years, with one down year. Management's own measure, five-year average operating income per share, grew 16 percent a year.[1] The two numbers answer different questions. Book value is what the company owns. Average operating income is what it earns before stock-market swings.

What this means for an owner: the business has compounded, but not at the 15 to 20 percent pace that the five-year intrinsic value figure in the 10-K suggests (15 percent a year).[5] We found no single dollar figure for intrinsic value per share in the company's filings or calls, so we cannot check that claim line by line. We therefore anchor our target on book value, which is published, and bring the question of earnings power into Chapter 5.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter states the strongest case against our view, names the one claim the verdict hinges on, and says when we will know if we are wrong.

The red-team bear case

The strongest honest case against buying MKL starts with earnings quality. Nine-month adjusted operating income is down 2 percent, and the third-quarter gain of 14 percent leaned on items that may not repeat: $164.1 million of additional gross premiums from breach premiums on marine war cover tied to the Middle East and Russia-Ukraine conflicts, and $387.1 million of favorable reserve development in nine months.[1][2] A combined ratio of 92 held up by reserve releases and war premiums is not the same as one earned from pricing power, and management says property prices in its book are down 25 to 30 percent.[12]

The second part of the bear case is that management paid for protection. The Enstar cover costs a $120 million risk premium and moves $2.4 billion of assets, with $500 million handed over outright and the rest held in trust, on $4.8 billion of reserves in what Markel calls run-off and exited lines.[2][8] Management says the deal reduces the prudence it needs in its best estimate of losses.[2] A bear reads that as admission that the legacy book may need more reserve than it holds. The same CFO said Global Reinsurance's adverse trend came from claims received in the last three to six months, which means the loss pattern has not yet settled.[12]

Third, the price is not cheap on returns. When a company earns a return on equity equal to its required return, fair price-to-book is 1.0. Markel earns 9.8 percent against a 10 percent required return, and the stock trades at 1.22. The cheapness case rests on the belief that return on equity will recover toward 12 to 14 percent, and the proof of that is not yet in the numbers.[16][31] Fourth, stocks equal 71 percent of equity, so a 20 percent market fall cuts about $2.6 billion before tax, 14 percent of equity.[2] Fifth, consensus cut 2026 earnings 9.8 percent in 90 days, and the leadership change adds execution risk during a softening market.[13][9]

The bear value in Chapter 5 changes two inputs at once. Changing one at a time shows how much each matters. Keep base growth of 7 percent and replace the 1.27 multiple with 1.11, the year-end 2021 reading, and the value is $1,828.07, which is 0.90 percent below the close. Keep the 1.27 multiple and replace growth with minus 10 percent a year, and the value is $1,684.79, which is 8.67 percent below the close.

The first result is the sharper lesson. A return to the lowest year-end multiple erases the whole upside even if book value grows as planned, so part of the 13.4 percent gap to our base value rests on the multiple and not only on compounding. That is a reason to want the price lower before buying, and to size the position at 3.5 percent at most.[17]

The hinge claim

The single claim the verdict depends on is this: Markel Insurance can hold a combined ratio in the low 90s through the softening of property and casualty prices while book value per share compounds near 7 percent a year. If it holds, the base value of $2,092 and the 2.10 to 1 reward-for-risk at $1,810 are sound. The line we use everywhere in this report is a combined ratio above 95 for two quarters in a row. That breaks the claim, and book value growth then falls toward the bear case with the multiple. A ratio of 93 or lower confirms it. A ratio of 94 to 95 is a grey zone: the claim is neither confirmed nor broken, and we keep waiting.[1][12] The table that follows lists each weak assumption, the evidence that would break it, and when we will know.
