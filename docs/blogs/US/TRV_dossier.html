Before you read

The Travelers Companies (TRV) is one of the largest US property and casualty insurers. It sells business, bond and personal insurance, mostly through independent agents, and it earned $8.3 billion in the last twelve months[1]. We rate the business high quality and the stock WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: at the $397.03 close our base value is $389, and a limit zone of $352 to $360 pays about 2.1 for each 1 of risk only if our earnings estimate beats the Street[19][15]. The idea fails if prices fall behind claim costs, if a year of heavy storms or reserve top-ups arrives, or if investors stop paying 2.8 times book value for peak-level returns[16][3].

1. What the company does

Travelers sells insurance. A customer pays a premium, and the company promises to pay for covered losses: a fire at a factory, a crash involving a delivery truck, a hailstorm on a roof, or a contractor that fails to finish a bridge. In 2025 the company wrote $44.4 billion of net written premiums, which is the premium it keeps after paying reinsurers to take part of the risk[6]. Total revenue was $48.8 billion, and the company employed about 34,000 people, roughly 90 percent of them in the United States[4].

The business has three segments. Business Insurance covers companies of every size and is half of the premium. Bond & Specialty Insurance sells surety bonds, which guarantee that a contractor will finish a job, and liability cover for company directors and professionals. Personal Insurance sells car and home policies to families. The table below shows how big each segment is, how fast it is growing and how profitable it is.

How an insurer makes money

An insurer has two ways to earn a profit, and the second is easy to forget. The first is underwriting: collect more in premiums than is paid out in claims and costs. The measure is the combined ratio, which is claims plus operating costs divided by earned premium. Below 100 percent is a profit. In the third quarter of 2026 the loss ratio was 57.9 percent and the expense ratio was 28.9 percent, so the combined ratio was 86.8 percent: for each $100 of premium earned, $13.20 was left over before investment income[1].

The second source is investing. Premiums arrive first and claims are paid months or years later, so Travelers holds claim reserves of $68.1 billion that it invests in the meantime[3]. That pool is called float. Net investment income was $1.098 billion before tax in the quarter, a record, and $908 million after tax[1]. Put the two together and you can see where profit came from: after-tax underwriting income was $1,147 million, investment income was $908 million, and interest and other costs took $108 million, which leaves core income of $1,947 million[1].

Core income is the company's measure of profit before gains and losses from selling investments. We use it together with reported net income because the sale gains and losses are random and tell you little about the business.

Who pays Travelers, and how it reaches them

Most customers never deal with Travelers directly. The majority of US products are sold through independent agents and brokers, who shop several insurers for the customer and are paid a commission[4]. That makes the agents a second customer. If Travelers prices too high, or is slow to quote, the agent sends the business elsewhere. It also explains why management stresses retention, the share of expiring policies that renew. Business Insurance retention was 86 percent in the quarter, and management calls it the best signal of whether the market is stable[10].

On the cost side the main payment is claims. About 12,300 of the company's employees are claim professionals, so claims handling is a large fixed investment, not an outsourced service[4]. Management says that more than half of claims are now eligible for straight-through processing, which means no adjuster touches them, and it has cut call-centre staffing by 30 percent[11].

What the company does with its profit

Travelers earns more than it can use inside the business, so it returns the excess. Since the start of 2025 it has returned $8 billion through share repurchases and retired 12 percent of its shares[10]. In the third quarter alone it bought 4.0 million shares for $1.508 billion at an average price of $376.00, and it still has $2.415 billion of repurchase capacity[1]. Because the share count falls, earnings per share grow faster than profit. Core income per diluted share rose 13 percent in the quarter ($9.18 against $8.14) while core income itself rose only 4 percent[1].

For an owner, the point is that buybacks add to per-share results only if the price paid is sensible. At $376 the company paid about 2.7 times its reported book value, which is a lot more than the 1.3 to 2.0 times the stock fetched at the end of each year from 2021 to 2025[1][16]. We come back to that in Chapters 3 and 5.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio: claims plus operating costs divided by earned premium; below 100 percent means an underwriting profit.
  • Underlying combined ratio: the combined ratio with storm losses and changes to old reserves removed, so it shows how this year's business performs in a normal year.
  • Net written premiums: premiums written in a period after paying reinsurers; a measure of sales volume.
  • Catastrophe losses: losses from named events such as tornadoes, hail, hurricanes and wildfires, reported net of reinsurance.
  • Prior-year reserve development: the change in the estimated cost of claims from earlier years. Favorable means earlier estimates were too high.
  • Float: premiums held and invested between collection and claim payment.
  • Renewal premium change: how much the average price changes when a policy renews, counting both rate and the amount of risk insured.
  • Retention: the share of expiring policies that renew.
  • Core return on equity: annualized core income divided by average shareholders' equity excluding unrealized investment gains and losses.
  • Book value per share: shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding; adjusted book value excludes unrealized investment gains and losses.
  • Asbestos reserves: money set aside for old liability policies that cover injuries from asbestos exposure, which still produce claims decades later.
  • Reinsurance: insurance that insurers buy to pass part of their risk to other companies.
  • Social inflation: rising claim costs from more lawsuits, larger jury awards and higher attorney involvement.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A good insurer is one that can write business at a profit through a full cycle, not only in good years. The test has three parts: whether it prices risk better than rivals, whether its reserves are honest, and whether it can survive a bad year without damaging its capital. Travelers passes the first and third clearly. The second is mostly good but carries one recurring charge, asbestos, that we treat as a watch item.

Scale, data and the agent network are the moat

The moat is a mix of size and information. Travelers writes $44.4 billion of premium across many lines and states, which gives it more loss data than most competitors and lets it price small differences in risk[6]. Management says it was early to see the rise in litigation costs, at the end of 2018, and that this gave it a head start on tools to settle claims faster, including a digital tool that more than 80 percent of the unrepresented claimants who were offered it chose to use[10]. Management also says scale will matter more, including the ability to invest at the level it does[10].

The evidence that this matters is in the numbers. Underlying underwriting income after tax was $5,497 million in 2025 against $2,073 million in 2022, and the underlying combined ratio fell from 86.2 percent in 2024 to 83.9 percent in 2025[1][6]. It was 84.5 percent for the first nine months of 2026, and the third quarter was the ninth in a row below 86 percent[1][10]. The peer table in Chapter 3 sets these figures against rivals.

The weak point is the price cycle

Insurance prices move in cycles. When prices are high, insurers earn a lot and money flows in, which pushes prices back down. Travelers is clearly in the part of the cycle where prices still rise, but more slowly. Business Insurance renewal premium change, the average price change on renewing policies, was 7.1 percent in the third quarter of 2025, 6.1 percent in the fourth quarter and 4.1 percent in the third quarter of 2026[12][11][1]. Excluding property it was still 7.0 percent, so the slowdown is concentrated in large property accounts, where Travelers is deliberately writing less[1].

Management calls the market 'stable' and says it is 'not prepared to call the bottom'[10]. Personal Insurance is further along: automobile renewal premium change was 'slightly negative', and automobile premiums fell 8 percent in the quarter[10][1]. Total net written premiums were $11.48 billion, unchanged from a year earlier, and up only 2 percent after removing the Canadian business that was sold[1]. The business is strong on profit and weak on growth. That is the main thing to weigh against the high profit level.

Reserves: honest, with one recurring charge

Reserves are the insurer's estimate of what it still owes on claims it has already incurred. If they are too low, profits are overstated today and corrected later. Travelers reports net favorable development of $1.225 billion before tax for the first nine months of 2026, with favorable results in all three segments[1]. The finance chief says that the company does not call its picks conservative but is 'leaving a provision' for uncertainty in long-tail liability lines, and that it has 'not run to the other side of the boat'[10].

The recurring charge is asbestos. The annual review in the third quarter added $295 million to net asbestos reserves in 2026 after $277 million in 2025, mostly because payments for settlement and defense cost more than expected[3]. Net asbestos reserves stood at $1.44 billion at September 30, 2026, against $1.45 billion a year earlier, and $215 million was paid in nine months[3]. In plain terms, the company adds roughly a fifth of the reserve each year and pays out about as much. Excluding asbestos, Business Insurance had $336 million of favorable development in the quarter[10]. We read this as a cost of doing business that other lines have so far covered, but it is a reason to give reserves a WATCH mark.

Catastrophes and Personal Insurance

Storm losses are the largest swing factor. After tax they were $2,915 million in 2025, $2,632 million in 2024 and $1,459 million in 2021[1]. In the first nine months of 2026 they were $1,817 million before tax, which is 5.6 points of combined ratio, against $3,595 million and 11.0 points a year earlier[1]. That lighter year is a large part of why 2026 profit is so high. Management said in January that its 2026 catastrophe plan was higher than the five- and ten-year averages, and in October that Personal Insurance catastrophe losses ran favorable to plan[11].

Travelers has also reduced the swing. It cut property exposure in high-catastrophe areas by 10 percent, and it lowered the point at which its catastrophe reinsurance starts paying from $4 billion to $3 billion for 2026[11]. Homeowners underlying combined ratio was 68 percent in the third quarter, and the car business was 87.3 percent, helped by about 1.5 points from re-estimating earlier quarters[10].

Balance sheet and capital

The balance sheet is a strength with one blemish. Debt was $9.813 billion, which is 25.3 percent of capital, or 21.7 percent after removing unrealized bond losses. The second figure is inside the company's target range of 15 to 25 percent[1]. The blemish is the $6.352 billion after-tax unrealized loss on bonds, which came from higher interest rates and, with buybacks and dividends, cut reported equity by 12 percent since year-end 2025[1]. Management says the loss comes from rates, not credit quality, and that the bonds are generally held to maturity[10]. The bond book has an average credit grade of Aa3, only 1.2 percent is below investment grade, and the weighted duration is 5.3 years[3]. A bond with a long duration loses more value when rates rise, so the loss is real on paper but not a cash loss unless the bonds are sold.

What this means for an owner: the franchise is hard to copy, and the profit level is the highest in the company's recent history. The risks are that prices are cooling, that heavy storms and asbestos charges can return, and that the stock already pays for the good news. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above rests on an assumption that could be wrong. We name the weakest one for each and then the strongest case against our own conclusion. The 2027 EPS estimate rests on underlying underwriting income of $5.30 billion, which is flat with 2025. It is weakest because prices are cooling and the cost of claims is not. The Reverse DCF rests on a 10 percent discount rate and an 18-times terminal multiple; these are fixed repository settings and not market quotes, and a 15-times multiple moves the result from -3.9 percent to -2.8 percent. The base value rests on an 11.37 times forward multiple that comes from an aggregator, not a filing. If it is 10.84 times the base is $371. The probabilities rest on judgment, and Chapter 5 shows what happens if they move.

Now the case against us, as a bear would make it. Our base EPS is above the consensus and the stock is still priced above our base value. The 29 analysts who cover it have an average target of $376.54, which is below the price, and only six rate it a buy while six rate it a sell or strong sell[15]. The stock had risen 45.75 percent in the year to October 28[14]. Earnings are at a high, helped by $1.2 billion of reserve releases and light storms. The trailing multiple of 10.4 looks low only because earnings are high. At the 2023 year-end, when earnings were still depressed, the trailing multiple was 14.65 times, against the 11.37 forward multiple that analysts' rebound estimates implied[16][15]. A bear would add that the company is using its capital to buy back shares at 2.7 times book, which lowers book value per share even as earnings per share rise. We do not dismiss this. It is the reason we call the stock good but not cheap.

Failure 1: prices fall behind claim costs

Insurers earn what they do today because prices have risen faster than claim costs since 2023, which is what the fall in the combined ratio from 97.0 percent in 2023 to 89.9 percent in 2025 implies[5][4]. Business Insurance renewal price change has slowed from 7.1 percent to 4.1 percent in a year, and Personal Insurance automobile renewal price change is slightly negative[12][1][10]. If liability claim costs keep rising by 6 to 8 percent from social inflation while prices rise 2 to 4 percent, the underlying combined ratio drifts up. Management says the loss environment is 'as we have expected', but it also says the drivers remain active[10].

The size of the risk is clear from the numbers. Each point of underlying combined ratio is about $350 million after tax, or $1.75 a share. A 3-point deterioration over two years takes about $5 a share off earnings, and a market that is paying 11 times takes about $58 off the price (our arithmetic). Put as a full case: if the underlying ratio is two points worse than our base case, underlying income is about $4.60 billion, core income about $6.11 billion, EPS about $30.70 and the base value about $349, which is -1.6 percent from the $360 zone top once the $5.00 dividend is added (our arithmetic). The reserve side is the second half of this risk. Management keeps a provision for uncertainty in long-tail lines. It says some of that provision would prove unneeded only if its claim initiatives bend the loss curve, and it is not projecting that[10]. The signal is the underlying combined ratio and Business Insurance renewal price change in each quarterly report. The first test is the fourth quarter report in late January 2027, which will also show January 1 renewals.

Failure 2: a heavy storm year and another reserve charge

Storm losses have no trend we can count on. They cost $2,915 million after tax in 2025 and only $1,435 million in the first nine months of 2026[1]. Personal Insurance is where they land: storm losses were 9.2 points of the segment's combined ratio in the third quarter and 8.4 points for nine months[1]. If 2027 storms match 2025's $2.9 billion rather than our $2.5 billion, EPS is about $2.00 lower, and with the multiple unchanged the value is about $23 lower.

Asbestos is the other recurring item: $295 million in the third quarter of 2026 and $277 million in 2025, against net asbestos reserves of $1.44 billion[3]. Our base case assumes net favorable development of $0.5 billion after tax, which would still be net of an asbestos charge of about this size. A larger charge, for example from a court ruling that widens liability, would hit the base. The 10-K warns that exposure to asbestos and mass tort claims remains uncertain[4]. We would see it first in the annual review in the third quarter of 2027, though a ruling could come at any time.

Failure 3: investors stop paying 2.8 times book for peak returns

The stock trades at 2.81 times reported book value and 2.30 times adjusted book value of $172.47 (our arithmetic at the ledger close)[1][19]. At the last five year-ends the multiple was 1.33, 2.04, 1.75, 1.96 and 1.97[16]. Return on equity has risen from 11.5 percent to 24.1 percent, so the higher multiple is partly earned. But if investors judge that the return is at its peak, they pay for the future return, not the current one. At 2.0 times adjusted book value of $172.47 the stock would be $345, close to our stop (our arithmetic).

The second part of this risk is the bond book. The 10-year Treasury yield was 4.99 percent on October 27, down from 5.12 percent a week earlier[19]. A renewed rise would add to the $8.049 billion of unrealized loss and could push reported book value per share below $141.48, which is already flat on a year ago[1]. Management says the rise in yields is a tailwind to later investment income, and the numbers agree: new money is invested 120 basis points above the portfolio yield[10]. The harm is to the multiple that investors pay for reported book value, not to cash flow. We would see it in the quarterly book value per share and in the price itself.

7. What we would do, and when

Our decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. Do not buy Travelers at the $397.03 close. The rule is the support ladder in our scoring file: the close is 5.7 ATR above the verified support of $353.79, and the ladder allows a purchase only within 1.6 ATR[19][20]. Reward-to-risk at the close is -0.16 to 1, against a floor of 2.0 to 1 for a core compounder, because our $389 base target is below the price. The business passes Layer 1 and scores 4.0 of 5.0 in Layer 2, so we do not reject it. No part of the zone is purchase-authorized today. It is a watch level. The decision would change only if the price falls into the zone and every check in Trigger 1 passes, including the 10 percent return test, or if the facts move as listed in Triggers 2 and 4. Trigger 3 describes the only route to a purchase above the zone, and it needs a fresh underwriting run.

The zone is built from three levels. Its top, $360.00, is the highest price at which the $389 base target pays 2.0 to 1 against the stop; the working is ($389 - $360.00) / ($360.00 - $346.20) = 2.10. That top is 0.82 ATR above the swing low of $353.79, inside the 1.2 ATR standard band. Its bottom, $352.00, is 0.24 ATR below the swing low, where the payoff is (389 - 352) / (352 - 346.20) = 6.38 to 1. The stop of $346.20 is the 20-day swing low of $353.79 minus one ATR of $7.59. For a core holding in a Stage 2 advance, our rule is the lower of the swing low and the 50-day average ($372.70), less one ATR[19][20]. The stop is 3.83 percent below the zone top.

The size follows the risk-unit rule. TIER_4 is our smallest position size class, with a risk budget of 35 basis points of net worth, where one basis point is one hundredth of a percent. The most we accept to lose if the stop is hit is 0.35 percent of net worth. At the zone top the stop is 3.83 percent away, so the formula allows 9.14 percent of net worth, and the tier ceiling of 2.0 percent binds[20]. Net worth is $85,454.62 and cash is $2,780.20, so 2.0 percent is $1,709.09, which buys 4 shares at $360 ($1,440, or 1.69 percent of net worth). If every Trigger 1 check passes, we would buy 2 shares at $360 and 2 at $352. If the stop is hit after a full fill at $360, the loss is about $55, or 6.5 basis points of net worth[21]. We chose the smallest tier because Layer 2 has two WATCH marks, the reward-to-risk rests on an EPS above consensus, and the expected return from the zone top is about 4.7 percent against our 10 percent hurdle.

Two stress tests show how fragile the 2.10 is. If the multiple is 10.84 instead of 11.37, the base target is $371 (34.22 x 10.84 = $370.94), the reward-to-risk at $360 is 0.79 to 1 and the expected total return at $360 is about 4.4 percent on the base target alone (not a re-weighted expectation). If 2027 EPS is the $31.11 consensus, the base target is $354 and the ratio at $360 is negative. So the zone is a watch level, not a standing order. We would place an order only after the fourth quarter report confirms our earnings assumption. Travelers is not held in either account, so there is no position to audit. If we do buy and it works, the let-winners-run rule applies: do not sell merely because RSI passes 70 or the weight passes 15 percent, trail the stop under the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average, and let the weight grow to the 22 percent ceiling[20].
