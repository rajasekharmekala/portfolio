Before you read

Adobe makes the software most professionals use to create images, video and documents, and it sells marketing software to large companies. We rate it a high-quality compounder that is not yet a buy, because at the $248.36 close the reward-to-risk is 1.66 to 1 against our floor of 2.0 to 1[30]. The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, with a limit order, which fills only at your price or better, in the zone between $232.00 and $244.50. What decides the case is whether net new ARR (annual recurring revenue), the subscription value added each quarter, stops shrinking, and the next evidence is fourth-quarter results, estimated for December 9, 2026 and not yet confirmed by the company[5]. The case is wrong if the stock closes below $215.40 or if fourth-quarter net new ARR is below about $0.5 billion.

1. What the company does

Adobe sells software subscriptions. People and companies pay a monthly or yearly fee to use tools for creating images, video and design (Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere, Lightroom and the Firefly generative AI app), for working with documents (Acrobat, the PDF format and Express), and for running marketing and customer-data programmes (Experience Manager, Experience Platform and GenStudio). Subscriptions were $22,904 million of fiscal 2025 revenue of $23,769 million, about 96 percent of the total[1]. Since the first quarter of fiscal 2026 Adobe reports one operating segment and two customer groups, Business Professionals & Consumers and Creative & Marketing Professionals[4]. The old Digital Media and Digital Experience labels remain only as supplemental disclosure[9].

Customers pay in advance and Adobe recognises the revenue over the contract term, so the balance sheet carries a large pool of deferred revenue: $7,094 million of the current kind at August 28, 2026[5]. The headline operating metric is ARR, the yearly value of subscription contracts in the two customer groups. Adobe holds exchange rates fixed during the year and re-values the closing balance each year, which lifted fiscal 2025's closing $25.20 billion to $25.66 billion at the start of fiscal 2026[9]. A second measure, remaining performance obligations, stood at $22.16 billion, 67 percent of it due within twelve months[5].

The business is global and cash generative. In fiscal 2025 the Americas supplied $14,120 million of revenue (59 percent), Europe, the Middle East and Africa $6,289 million (27 percent) and Asia-Pacific $3,360 million (14 percent), with 31,360 employees[1]. In the first nine months of fiscal 2026 revenue rose to $19,776 million from $17,575 million a year earlier[5], and trailing twelve-month free cash flow, the cash left after running the business and buying equipment, was $10.59 billion[22].

The strategy has changed in the last year. Management now routes new users into free tiers of Acrobat, Express and Firefly first and asks them to pay later; creative free-tier monthly users passed 100 million, up more than 70 percent[17]. Management told investors in June that this shift 'will come at the cost of short-term ARR'[20]. Adobe also bought Semrush, a brand-visibility and search-marketing platform, for $1.87 billion on April 28, 2026[4][21], agreed to buy Topaz Labs for about $340 million[4], and will change chief executive on December 1, 2026[11].

How a subscription becomes revenue and ARR

A new subscriber adds to ARR on the day the contract starts. The money shows up as revenue over the following months, so ARR leads revenue by two to four quarters. That lead is why investors watch net new ARR, the change in ARR from one quarter to the next, more closely than revenue. A slowdown in net new ARR today is a slowdown in revenue growth later, and the reverse is also true.

Adobe's pay plan follows the same logic: from fiscal 2026 the executives' performance shares are earned on growth in Total Adobe ending ARR[15]. That gives management a direct reason to convert free users into paying ones, and a reason to care about how ARR is counted.

Who the customers are

Three groups pay Adobe. Individuals and small businesses buy through adobe.com and resellers. Creative professionals and teams buy Creative Cloud seats. Enterprises, more than 20,000 of them according to management, buy the full stack in multi-year deals[17]. A keyword search of the fiscal 2025 annual report found no customer-concentration disclosure; the dependency it does describe is on distributors, resellers and app stores[1].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ARR
    Chart • Finviz (annual recurring revenue): the yearly value of subscriptions that renew.
  • Net new ARR: ARR at the end of a period minus ARR at the start.
  • RPO (remaining performance obligations): signed contracts not yet recognised as revenue; cRPO is the part due within twelve months.
  • MAU: monthly active users, including free users.
  • Freemium: a free tier used to attract users who may pay later.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation): the cost of paying staff in shares.
  • FCF (free cash flow): operating cash flow minus capital spending.
  • Reverse DCF: the growth rate the current share price implies, solved backwards from today's price.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Adobe's competitive strength rests on formats, workflow and distribution. People open more than 400 billion PDFs a year in Acrobat, and Adobe's products reach more than one billion monthly active users across all businesses, including free users, according to management[17]. Designers, video editors and marketing teams build their working habits, templates and asset libraries inside Adobe's tools, and enterprises wire the marketing stack into their own data systems. Leaving costs time and retraining, which helps explain the 89.25 percent trailing gross margin and the 40.79 percent free cash flow margin (on filing-based free cash flow)[22]. Return on invested capital, our own calculation from filings, is 40.1 percent (Chapter 3).

The weakness is a change in who competes. The fiscal 2025 annual report now lists 'AI-first creative tools' and AI companies among its competitors, in addition to established software firms[1]. Two datapoints show the pressure. First, the stock-imagery business, about $450 million a year, was, in the first-quarter call summary, a 'steeper decline than we expected' that was shifting 'more quickly than we had planned for'[19]. Second, management deferred planned second-half Creative Cloud pricing changes in June, and said roughly half of the second-half ARR impact came from that deferral and half from steering traffic into free tiers[20].

What protects the business

Three things protect cash flows for the next several years. The first is the group of existing paying subscribers: ending ARR is $27.50 billion, up 11.2 percent from $24.74 billion a year earlier on the same exchange rates[4]. The second is the multi-year enterprise book: remaining performance obligations of $22.16 billion, up 8 percent[4]. The third is balance-sheet freedom. Net debt is $0.72 billion against $10.04 billion of trailing EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) (Chapter 3), so Adobe is not forced to sell anything to fund its buyback.

Adobe is also putting AI into the same workflows rather than selling it separately. AI-first ARR, which counts products built around AI such as Firefly, exceeded $650 million and grew more than 150 percent from a year earlier; Firefly ending ARR across the app and credit packs grew 40 percent from the previous quarter[17]. That is encouraging growth, but $650 million is only 2.4 percent of the $27.50 billion ARR base, so it cannot yet replace the slowdown elsewhere.

What is eroding

The erosion is in new business, not the base. Net new ARR was down 36 to 37 percent from a year earlier in the third quarter, a figure put to management on the call that they did not dispute[17]. Management links it to a deliberate choice: more free users now, more paying users later, and Creative Cloud price increases on hold[17][20]. The risk is that free users stay free, or that AI-native rivals pull creators away before conversion happens.

Remaining performance obligations grew 8 percent, the first single-digit growth since early fiscal 2023, according to an analyst's remark on the call. Management replied that the shape of the year is normal: both measures step up in the fourth quarter and stay flat for three quarters[17]. We note the disagreement and weight the reported 8 percent because it comes from the filing[4].

Management and governance

Leadership is changing quickly. Chief executive Shantanu Narayen announced in March that he would step down once a successor was named[8]. The chief financial officer, Dan Durn, left on June 15, with Steve Day as interim[6]. David Wadhwani, president of the creative and productivity business, stepped down on September 27[11]. On September 2 the board named Anil Chakravarthy, who has led the enterprise marketing business and field operations and was previously chief executive of Informatica, to run the company from December 1, with Narayen as executive chair[11][12]. In July the compensation committee approved a retention letter for the chief legal officer that runs until twelve months after the new chief executive starts, a sign the board is trying to hold the team together[13].

Governance quality otherwise looks sound. The company's insider-trading policy bars pledging and hedging of shares[15]. Form 4 filings dated October 19, 2026 appear on the EDGAR index[16]; the four we opened show only share-vesting and tax-withholding codes, not open-market trades, but we did not review all seven.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 asks whether the operating numbers that drive this particular business are healthy. For a subscription software company the useful ones are how fast recurring revenue grows, how much new recurring revenue is added each quarter, whether the AI products earn money, whether the operating margin holds, and whether buybacks are paid for by cash flow. We also set what management guided, meaning its own published forecast for a period, against what it delivered. We score each as PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). The rule is a weighted score of at least 3.0 out of 5.0 with no more than one FAIL.

Has net new ARR stabilised? Not yet on a year-over-year basis, but the sequence is flattening. Net new ARR was $0.40 billion in the first quarter, about $0.56 billion in the second after removing the $0.48 billion Semrush added at purchase, and $0.40 billion in the third[7][6][5]. The third quarter was 36 to 37 percent below the prior year according to the analyst question on the call[17]. Management guides the full-year ARR growth rate to 10.2 percent, which implies $0.78 billion in the fourth quarter, against $0.92 billion a year earlier (our arithmetic, Chapter 3). That would be a 15 percent decline, much narrower than 36 percent. A narrowing decline is what stabilisation looks like in the numbers, and the fourth-quarter print is the test.

What do the CEO change and Semrush do to organic ARR growth? Semrush added about $480 million to ARR at the end of the second quarter[6]. Guidance in March excluded it and still said 10.2 percent growth[7]; guidance in June included it and said 10.2 percent again[6]. Stripping Semrush out, the implied organic growth target fell from 10.2 to about 8.3 percent (our arithmetic: ($28.28B target less $0.48B less $25.66B) divided by $25.66B). The new chief executive starts on December 1, eight days before the estimated results date, so the first outlook for fiscal 2027 is likely to carry his judgment on how much freemium to run and when to resume price moves. Narayen said the board wanted the successor in place to shape fiscal 2027 planning[20]. After the Semrush anniversary on April 28, 2027, reported ARR growth will lose its acquired boost, so reported and organic growth converge from the second half of fiscal 2027.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Did management do what it said it would? On the targets it controls directly (revenue, earnings per share (EPS), margins and buybacks) the record for the last two years is strong. Fiscal 2025 revenue of $23.77 billion beat the $23.30 to $23.55 billion range set in December 2024, Digital Media ARR growth of 11.5 percent beat the 11.0 percent target, and non-GAAP earnings per share of $20.94 beat the $20.20 to $20.50 range[10][9].

The record is weaker on the metric that matters most to the stock. The December 2025 guide for 10.2 percent ARR growth was described as about $2.6 billion of net new ARR, the highest opening guide the company had given[18]. Ten months later the same percentage still stands, but it now includes $0.48 billion of acquired ARR, and the planned second-half Creative Cloud pricing changes were deferred[6][20]. Leadership continuity was also not kept, with a CFO exit, a CEO transition and a divisional president leaving since March[6][11].

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of filings show a business that grew revenue at a steady 10 to 12 percent a year, from $15.8 billion in fiscal 2021 to $23.8 billion in fiscal 2025, and then at about 12.5 percent in the first nine months of fiscal 2026[2][1][5]. Gross margin sits near 89 percent. Operating margin dipped to 31.3 percent in fiscal 2024 because of a $1,000 million payment to end the Figma deal, and returned to 36.6 percent in fiscal 2025[1].

Cash flow is the strongest part. Free cash flow is cash flow from operations, the cash the business itself collects, less capital expenditure, the spending on equipment and property. It rose from $6.9 billion to $9.9 billion over the five years, and the trailing figure is $10.6 billion, a 40.8 percent margin. Our own subtraction of capital spending from operating cash flow gives that figure; the market-data ledger used for the Reverse DCF in Chapter 5 shows a lower vendor figure of $9.345 billion that does not reconcile to the filings, a difference we disclose rather than resolve[1][4][30].

Two numbers are less comfortable. Stock-based compensation was above 8 percent of revenue in each of fiscal 2022 to fiscal 2025 and is only now just under it, because revenue growth sped up. And the company has moved from net cash of $4.2 billion in fiscal 2023 to net debt of $0.72 billion, because buybacks exceeded free cash flow in fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2025[2][1][4]. Neither is a threat at this scale, since the net debt is 0.07 times EBITDA, but both show the direction of travel.

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on future cash flow. The Reverse DCF turns the bet around: given today's price and trailing free cash flow, what growth rate for ten years, with a 10 percent discount rate and an 18-times free-cash-flow multiple at the end, would justify the price? For Adobe the ledger says the market is pricing in a 2.99 percent annual decline in free cash flow for a decade[30]. That is a low bar for a business whose cash flow has grown every year except one in the last five.

Analysts are not cutting numbers. Consensus earnings for fiscal 2026 are $24.48, up 0.63 percent over 90 days, and for fiscal 2027 $27.51, up 0.58 percent, which the ledger classes as stable[30]. Stable estimates and a falling share price mean the multiple is doing the falling: the forward price-to-earnings ratio has moved from 35.27 at the end of fiscal 2023 to 25.95 in fiscal 2024, 14.03 in fiscal 2025 and about 9.0 now[24].

Our variant perception

Where do we differ from the market? The market sees a one-way story: AI-built tools shrink the creative software market, so growth fades. We see a split. The part of the book that is contracted (the $22.16 billion of RPO and the enterprise marketing products whose ARR management says is growing more than 20 percent) is not shrinking[4][17]. The part that is under pressure is the flow of new subscribers, and management chose to depress it through the second half of fiscal 2026, with more of the effect expected in 2027, in exchange for more users. At a forward earnings multiple near 9, the market is paying nothing for the chance that conversion works.

We also think the December 9 event is a lower-information event than it looks. The new chief executive starts eight days earlier, and a new chief executive often sets a cautious first-year outlook. Our base case therefore assumes the first fiscal 2027 outlook disappoints on ARR growth and is still met with an 11-times multiple, rather than a bounce to the 14-times level of a year ago. That is why the base target is $303 and not higher.

Our caution is on the entry price, not on quality. At $248.36 the reward-to-risk is 1.66 to 1, below the 2.0 floor, and the price has fallen 3.97 percent in a day without news that we could find in company filings, so it has not yet found the bottom of its range. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate has a weakest assumption. For net new ARR the weakest is that Semrush ARR stayed at $0.48 billion after the purchase; Adobe stopped separating it, so organic net new ARR may be lower than we show. For the return-on-capital figure the weakest is the definition of capital: any reasonable choice passes. For the fiscal 2027 EPS the weakest is that the new chief executive keeps the 45 percent margin target while spending to grow users.

For the price target the weakest assumption is the 11.0 times multiple: it assumes investors will pay more for earnings before ARR growth is proven. For the Reverse DCF the weakest is the base cash flow, because the ledger's $9.345 billion and the filings' $10.592 billion disagree. For the expected value, the weakest is the 30 percent bear probability, which is judgment.

Fatal flaw 1: AI substitution makes ARR growth permanently lower

In this scenario AI-native tools and general-purpose AI models do part of what Photoshop, Premiere and Acrobat do, so fewer people pay and the free tiers become the product. Adobe's own annual report lists 'AI-first creative tools' among its competitors[1], and the first-quarter call summary describes the roughly $450 million stock-imagery business as declining more than management expected[19]. If net new ARR stays at $0.4 billion a quarter, total ARR growth drifts to about 6 percent, earnings growth depends on buybacks and margins, and the multiple falls to the bear 7.65 times.

Evidence that would show it: Q4 net new ARR below $0.50 billion, RPO growth below 5 percent, or a second quarter of falling AI-first ARR growth. It would be known on December 9 (estimated) and in each quarter of fiscal 2027.

Fatal flaw 2: freemium does not convert and pricing power is gone

Management paused Creative Cloud price moves and said the freemium strategy plays out more fully in 2027[20]. A new chief executive could extend that, or restart price increases into a market that now has free alternatives. If conversion is slow, ARR growth in fiscal 2027 could be guided below 8 percent, which would fail our KPI floor and make Layer 2 a FAIL.

Evidence that would show it: creative free-tier users above 100 million but Firefly ARR growth slowing from the 40 percent quarterly rate[17]; or a fiscal 2027 outlook for ARR growth below 8 percent. The first read is December 9 (estimated, unconfirmed) and the second is at the first fiscal 2027 quarter in March 2027.

Fatal flaw 3: capital allocation and execution erode the base

Adobe paid about $366 a share for fiscal 2025 buybacks (our arithmetic: $11,281 million over 30.8 million shares)[1][9] and is now buying at about $261 for the nine months[4], against a share price of $248.36. Buybacks beyond free cash flow, a CFO and CEO change, two acquisitions (Semrush, with $1,251 million of goodwill, and Topaz at about $340 million) and an earlier $70 million goodwill write-down for the Publishing & Advertising unit raise the odds of a stumble[4][6]. SBC could cross 8.3 percent on the company's own measure[5].

Evidence that would show it: net debt above 0.5 times EBITDA, goodwill write-downs larger than the $70 million taken, or SBC above 9 percent of revenue. These would show in the next quarterly report, due around March 2027.
