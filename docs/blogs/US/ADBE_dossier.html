Before you read

Adobe sells creative and marketing software to about one billion people each month, mostly on subscription. In the last twelve months it turned $25.97 billion of revenue into $10.59 billion of free cash flow (FCF) — cash left after all business spending — and kept the difference. The stock is not in a momentum setup: it trades about 34 percent below its 52-week high and below both major moving averages, so the system waits. This report covers the business, the numbers, the price, and the exact conditions that would change the decision.

What the company does

Adobe makes software for people who create or publish visual content. Its best-known tools are Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere Pro, Acrobat, and Lightroom. In fiscal 2025, the year ended November 28, 2025, the company earned $23.77 billion. Of that, $22.90 billion came from subscription fees, 96 percent of the total.

About one billion people used Adobe products in an average month last year. The company sells to two customer groups: business professionals, such as designers, marketers, and editors, and creative professionals, such as photographers and film makers. In the September 2026 quarter, the business-facing group (Business Publishing and Communication) brought in $1.91 billion, up 16 percent a year ago. The creative-facing group (Creative and Marketing Pro) brought in $4.65 billion, up 13 percent.

In January 2026 Adobe reorganized into three reporting units. Digital Media, the creative tools, sold $17.39 billion in fiscal 2025. Digital Experience, the marketing platforms, sold $5.41 billion. Publishing and Advertising, the print-era unit, sold $106 million.

Most customers pay a subscription, usually per seat, billed monthly or annually. The tools sit inside team workflows, so switching away is slow and costly. That lock-in is the heart of the business.

Words used in this report: segment — a piece of the business reported separately; seat — one user license; subscription — a recurring payment for the right to use software; run-rate — a quarterly number multiplied to show a full year; fiscal year — Adobe's reporting year, which ends in late November.

Why the business is strong or weak

Adobe's strength starts with pricing power. The gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of the product; at about 89 percent it is among the highest in software. Customers pay full price year after year, and the company keeps adding features, including AI ones, without losing them.

Scale is the second strength. annual recurring revenue (ARR) is the annualized value of all active subscriptions. It reached $27.50 billion in the September 2026 quarter, up 10.2 percent a year ago. ARR for products where AI is the main selling point grew 150 percent in the same year. That is small in absolute dollars today, but it is the growth engine the bull case leans on.

The third strength is a backlog. remaining performance obligations (RPO) is the value of contracts already paid for but not yet delivered. It stood at $22.16 billion at the end of the September 2026 quarter, up 13 percent a year ago. 67 percent of it (current RPO) turns into revenue within twelve months. In plain words: a large share of next year's revenue is already in the bank.

Cash is the fourth. Adobe collected $7.65 billion of operating cash in the first nine months of fiscal 2026. It holds about $5.6 billion more in cash and short-term investments than it owes on its notes, a net-cash balance sheet (worked out in chapter 3).

The weaknesses are real, and the filings say so plainly. First, competition in creative tools. Adobe paid $1 billion to walk away from its plan to buy Figma, because the deal hit regulatory walls. Figma is now a public company in its own right. Court records show a securities class action accusing Adobe of downplaying the Figma threat; the court let that case go, but the underlying point stands: creative software is no longer Adobe's alone.

Second, the cost of equity. Adobe pays its employees partly in its own stock. In fiscal 2025 that stock-based compensation totaled $1.83 billion, about 7.7 percent of revenue. That dilutes owners over time (chapter 6) and is the reason Adobe fails one of the four sub-tests in the core screen.

Third, execution risk. The company closed a $1.87 billion deal for the marketing company Semrush on April 28, 2026, and signed a roughly $340 million deal for the AI company Topaz Labs in June 2026, which is still pending. It also wrote down $70 million on its Publishing and Advertising unit, a sign that the old print business is fading. Finally, leadership is in transition: Shantanu Narayen is stepping down as CEO, with Anil named as the successor in the September 2026 release, and the CFO role is filled on an interim basis by Steve Day.

Two things in that table deserve attention. First, goodwill — the premium paid over value for acquisitions — reached $14.0 billion, nearly half the balance sheet, so a heavy buyer's mistakes show up on the books. Second, the debt and net-cash rows use the broad measure that stockanalysis.com shows, which includes lease obligations. The September 2026 quarter (first quarter of fiscal 2026) earned $6.76 billion of revenue, up 13 percent a year ago; the first nine months earned $19.78 billion. Net profit was $1.83 billion in the quarter and $5.43 billion in the nine months. Diluted earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares; it was $13.47 for the nine months. The company's guidance is the outlook it publishes for the period ahead: revenue of $6.80 to $6.85 billion in the fourth quarter, and $26.576 to $26.626 billion for fiscal 2026, with GAAP EPS of $18.12 to $18.17.

Margins tell the same story. The gross margin for the trailing twelve months is 89.25 percent. The operating margin is profit after the costs of running the business, divided by revenue; it is 36.32 percent.

Trailing free cash flow (FCF) is $10.59 billion, a 40.79 percent margin. The capital expenditure is spending on long-lived assets; it was only $179 million in fiscal 2025, less than 1 percent of revenue. A buyback is when the company buys its own shares off the market, shrinking the share count. Adobe repurchased 9.5 million shares in the September quarter, and 30.8 million shares for $11.28 billion in fiscal 2025. The share count fell 6.22 percent over the past year.

One cost needs its own line. stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay to employees in the company's own shares, issued over the life of each award. It was $1.58 billion in the nine months of fiscal 2026 and $1.83 billion in fiscal 2025, about 7.7 percent of that year's revenue. This system asks for SBC below 5 percent of revenue for a core holding. Adobe misses that bar today, and that single fact keeps it out of the core bucket (chapter 7).

The net cash position, worked out

The number

Adobe holds about $4.04 billion more in cash and short-term investments than it owes on its notes, as of the end of August 2026.

How it was calculated

Take the balance sheet lines from the filing for the quarter ended August 28, 2026: cash and cash equivalents of $4.36 billion, short-term investments of $1.28 billion, notes payable of $1.60 billion. Cash plus investments minus debt: 4,359 + 1,280 − 1,597 = 4,042, in millions.

The inputs

The cash, investment, and debt lines come from Adobe's balance sheet in the 10-Q for the quarter ended August 28, 2026, retrieved 2026-10-08. The stockanalysis.com page shows a different, broader debt figure that includes leases, which is why its net-cash number differs from this one.

The range

Between about $3.70 billion and $4.10 billion. The low end assumes the pending roughly $340 million Topaz Labs deal closes in cash within the next two quarters.

If the number is off

If the true figure is materially different, the cause is one of two things: a debt line that was not counted (for example, a new bond issue), or the Topaz deal closing sooner than expected. Neither would change the direction of the story, which is a cash surplus, not a borrowing burden.

The 200-day moving average is the average over the last year of trading; it stands at $257.55 and is still falling, having been $285.15 three months ago. The price is below both. A base is the flat zone a stock builds after a long decline, while its sellers run out. The stock is in a base (Stage 1) and has not entered Stage 2, which is the advance that follows a broken base. The lowest close in the last sixty-three days was $210.88.

The pivot is the level the price must close above to leave the base; here it is the 50-day moving average at $257.99, and the price sits 3.41 ATRs below it. A breakout is a close above the pivot. What could happen next

First, where the stock stands on value. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by trailing earnings per share; at 13.45 it is low for a business with these margins. On forward, or next-year, earnings the multiple is 8.97.

Two more yardsticks. The price-to-earnings-to-growth (PEG) ratio divides that multiple by the growth rate; at 0.62, the stock is cheap by that measure. return on invested capital (ROIC) is the profit earned on the capital tied up in the business; at 57.22 percent it is very high.

earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is a cash-profit measure that adds back non-cash charges. Enterprise value divided by EBITDA is 9.54, and debt divided by EBITDA is 0.68, a light load on a very profitable business.

The free cash flow yield, worked out

The number

About 11 percent: for every dollar paid today, trailing free cash flow returns about 11 cents a year.

How it was calculated

Take one and divide it by the price-to-free-cash-flow multiple of 8.82. That gives 0.113, or 11.3 percent.

The inputs

The multiple of 8.82 and the trailing free cash flow of $10.59 billion come from stockanalysis.com, retrieved 2026-10-08. The cash flow here is the non-GAAP, company-style measure.

The range

Between 9 and 13 percent, depending on how much of the reported cash flow is treated as real once stock-based compensation is counted as a real cost (chapter 6).

If the number is off

The yield flatters if cash flow falls: a slower buyback program, a large acquisition, or a heavier capital program would each cut it. If trailing cash flow fell to $8 billion, the yield would be near 8.5 percent, no longer standout.

Three outcomes over the next twelve months, with the odds assigned and the reason for each.

Bull (30 percent). AI-driven demand keeps outpacing the current 13 percent growth, the top of the fiscal 2026 target ($26.63 billion) is met or raised, and the stock re-rates toward the top of the analyst range. Price: about $330. Why 30 percent: AI-first ARR grew 150 percent, but it is still small inside a $27.50 billion total, so a full re-rating is a step, not a certainty.

Base (45 percent). The target is met at its midpoint (revenue $26.60 billion, GAAP EPS $18.14). Buybacks keep shrinking the share count by about 5 percent a year. The consensus estimate is the average of the 39 analysts covering the stock; it stands at a $275.06 price target with a Hold rating. Price: about $270. Why 45 percent: this is the company's own published plan, and Adobe has a long record of landing near the middle of its range.

Bear (25 percent). Figma and other AI-native tools take share in creative, ARR growth slows below 8 percent, and the leadership transition stumbles. The stock falls toward the low analyst target of $195. Why 25 percent: the stock has already fallen 36 percent over the year, which prices in some of the fear, but the competitive threat is real and the $1 billion Figma fee shows how expensive these battles are.

The expected outcome is the probability-weighted average: 0.30 × $330 + 0.45 × $270 + 0.25 × $195 = $269, about 11.7 percent above the $241.05 price, before costs and tax. That is the return the numbers support. Whether the path gets there is a timing question, and the momentum gate exists to answer it. Right now it says wait.

Why these estimates might be wrong

The fiscal 2026 target. The weakest assumption is that demand holds through a normal business slowdown, and that Figma's push as a public company does not take share quickly. Falsifying evidence: quarterly ARR growth below 8 percent, or a guidance cut at any of the four earnings. When we will know: each quarter, starting with the fourth-quarter and full-year report, expected around January 2027.

The free cash flow yield. The weakest assumption is the non-GAAP treatment of equity pay. dilution is the shrinking of each owner's slice as new shares are issued or vest. If the stock-based compensation ratio climbs from 7.7 percent toward 9 percent of revenue, the real cash available to owners falls, and the yield overstates. When we will know: each quarterly filing reports the number for that period.

The net cash figure. The weakest assumption is that no new debt is issued. The balance sheet was a cash surplus through fiscal 2025 and into fiscal 2026. A large acquisition could change that; the pending Topaz deal at roughly $340 million is small. When we will know: the next balance sheet, in the filing for the December quarter.

The transition. The weakest assumption is that the CEO change and the interim CFO arrangement run smoothly. Falsifying evidence: a delay in the new CEO starting, a key-departure announcement, or a tone change in the first earnings call under new leadership. When we will know: the start date is announced publicly, and the first earnings under the new CEO will be the clearest test.

What we would do, and when

Decision: WAIT FOR SUPPORT. No core position, no tactical position, today.

The core screen asks four questions. Adobe passes three: return on invested capital of 57.22 percent against a 12 percent bar; free cash flow margin of 41.45 percent against a 12 percent bar; and a balance sheet that is a cash surplus rather than a heavy borrower. It fails one: stock-based compensation at 7.7 percent of revenue against a 5 percent bar. That single failure closes the core bucket, and this system does not override it.

The tactical screen is the momentum gate, and it returned NO_MOMENTUM: the price is below both moving averages, the 200-day line is still falling, and the stock sits 3.41 ATRs from support, far outside the entry ladder, which allows no buy beyond 1.2 ATR.

There is a deeper reason to wait. The reward-to-risk ratio is the upside of a position in dollars divided by its downside in dollars. Enter near $260 after a breakout, and set a stop loss, which is the pre-set price that ends the trade if the market goes against it, below the $210.88 low. The downside is about $49. The base-case upside to $270 is only $10. The asymmetry — upside over downside — works out to about 0.2 to 1. The system requires at least 3.5 to 1 for a tactical position. The trade does not meet the standard at today's price, whatever its business merits. That is why the gate says: allocated only on a confirmed breakout of the base pivot or a retest that holds, never before.

The entry zone is the price band where a buy order would sit. For a future tactical entry it would be $258 to $265, just above the pivot, and only at the size the system allows once a retest holds.

Each of the lines that follows names a price or event that would change the decision.

Trigger to act: a daily close above $258 (the pivot) on above-average volume, with the 200-day moving average flat or rising. Re-run the gate. If a retest of the pivot holds, place limit orders in the $258 to $265 entry zone, sized so the reward-to-risk from a stop below the new, higher base low is at least 3.5 to 1. The position cap is 15 percent of the market portfolio.

Trigger to abandon: a close below $210.88, the lowest close of the last sixty-three days. The base has broken; the name returns to the watchlist and no position is opened.

Trigger to re-check the core screen: four consecutive quarters with stock-based compensation below 5 percent of revenue, as reported in the quarterly filings.

Trigger to exit after entry: a close below the stop, set below the then-current base low, ends the position in full in the same session. No averaging down.

If none of these happens, the correct action is no action. A business can be excellent and the entry still wrong. This system is built to never confuse the two.
