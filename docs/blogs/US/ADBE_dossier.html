1. What the company does

Adobe makes software that people pay for by the year. A designer uses Photoshop or Premiere to edit pictures and video. An office worker uses Acrobat to read, sign and search PDF files. A marketing team uses Adobe Experience Manager and Adobe Experience Platform to build websites and decide which customer sees which message [4]. Adobe now groups its customers into two sets. Creative & Marketing Professionals bought $4.65 billion of subscriptions in the quarter ended August 28, 2026. Business Professionals & Consumers, who mostly use Acrobat and Express, bought $1.91 billion [4][5].

The money comes from subscriptions. Subscription revenue was $6.58 billion of $6.76 billion in total revenue in that quarter, or 97 percent [4][5]. The two customer groups account for $6.56 billion of it, and a further $26 million is subscription revenue that Adobe counts in neither group [4]. A subscription is a yearly or monthly fee, so most of next quarter's revenue is already contracted. Adobe tracks this with total annual recurring revenue (ARR), the yearly value of all live subscription contracts. ARR was $27.50 billion at the end of August 2026, up 11.2 percent from a year earlier, and that figure includes the Semrush search-marketing business bought in April 2026 [4][6]. Remaining performance obligations (RPO), the signed contracts not yet turned into revenue, were $22.16 billion, up 8 percent [4].

Adobe also gives its software away for free to attract users. It says more than 1 billion people use its products each month, and that more than 100 million use the free versions of its creative tools [12]. The bet is that some of these users will pay for premium plans or AI credits later. That bet is also the main reason net new recurring revenue is soft today, a point Chapters 2 and 6 take up in detail [12][13].

Sales are spread across the world. The Americas were 59 percent of third-quarter revenue, Europe, the Middle East and Africa 27 percent, and Asia-Pacific 14 percent [4]. Adobe employed 31,360 people at the end of fiscal 2025 [1]. Leadership is in transition. Shantanu Narayen, chief executive for eighteen years, hands the job to Anil Chakravarthy on December 1, 2026, and the finance chief left in June, so an interim finance chief is in place [6][7][8][11].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ARR (annual recurring revenue) is the yearly value of live subscriptions. Net new ARR is the change in ARR from one period to the next.
  • RPO (remaining performance obligations) are signed contracts not yet recognised as revenue.
  • MAU (monthly active users) counts distinct people who use a product in a month, including free users.
  • Freemium means a free version that draws users in, with paid plans for extra features.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is cash from operations minus spending on equipment. SBC (stock-based compensation) is pay given to staff in shares.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by shares. GAAP is the audited accounting standard. Non-GAAP EPS removes items such as SBC and the cost of acquired intangibles.
  • P/E is the price paid per dollar of yearly profit. Reverse DCF works backward from today's price to the growth the price assumes.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money invested in the business.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing. RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 meter of recent price momentum.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Adobe's competitive position rests on three things: the files and habits professionals already use, deep links into corporate workflows, and a very large user base. Acrobat opens more than 400 billion PDFs a year, and Adobe reports more than 1 billion monthly users across its products [12]. Creative teams share work in Adobe formats, train new hires on Adobe tools, and plug Adobe into brand-approval and asset systems. Those habits are costly to change, which is why revenue has kept growing: subscription revenue rose 14 percent in the quarter to August 28, 2026 [5].

The hard question is whether generative AI (software that makes images, video and text from a short instruction) weakens Adobe's seat economics, meaning the fee Adobe collects for each person who uses its tools. The evidence is mixed and we state both sides. Against Adobe: an analyst on the third-quarter call said net new ARR fell 36 to 37 percent from a year earlier, and management answered by pointing to its freemium strategy rather than disputing the figure [12]. Wells Fargo puts the organic fall at more than 40 percent and cut its rating to Underweight on October 12, 2026 [20]. In December 2025 management had called net new ARR a record, so the third-quarter fall is a sharp turn [14]. An Investor's Business Daily headline said the stock sank to a seven-year low after the June report [26]. Management also said it deferred planned Creative Cloud price changes, and that about half of the hit to its ARR target came from that choice [13]. HSBC cut its rating on October 13, 2026 after a developer released seven free, AI-built imitations of Adobe apps, which are still in a very early alpha state [22][23].

For Adobe: the damage so far is in the pace of additions, not in a fall in paying users. Adobe does not publish seat counts in the filings we read, so a drop in seats cannot be ruled out, but reported subscription revenue grew 14 percent and the enterprise products Experience Manager, GenStudio and Experience Platform each grew ARR by more than 20 percent [5][12]. AI-first ARR, which counts products built around AI such as the Firefly app, passed $650 million and grew more than 150 percent [5][12]. The whole software group is also under pressure: Intuit is down 48.8 percent over 52 weeks and Figma down 53.3 percent, so part of Adobe's fall is a sector move [28][30]. Our reading is that AI is hurting Adobe's pricing power at the low end first, with students and freelancers, while large companies are slower to switch.

AI also carries a cost. Adobe's gross margin is still high at 89.25 percent over the last twelve months [15]. But cost of subscription revenue rose 24 percent in the third quarter, and hosting and data-center costs, which include the computing power for AI, supplied 22 of those 24 points [4]. Adobe's own 10-K lists rising competition from companies that offer generative and agentic AI as a risk to renewals, upsell and pricing [1]. What this means for an owner: the franchise is intact, but it has less room to raise prices, and the next two quarters will show whether the AI products can make up for that.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

A good test of management is whether it hit the targets it set a year ago. For fiscal 2025, Adobe's December 2024 targets were revenue of $23.30 billion to $23.55 billion, non-GAAP EPS of $20.20 to $20.50, and Digital Media ARR growth of 11.0 percent [10]. The results were revenue of $23.77 billion, non-GAAP EPS of $20.94, and Digital Media ARR growth of 11.5 percent [9]. Every target was met or beaten.

Fiscal 2026 is a mixed record. The December 2025 targets were revenue of $25.90 billion to $26.10 billion, non-GAAP EPS of $23.30 to $23.50 and ARR growth of 10.2 percent, all excluding Semrush [9]. By September 2026 the revenue target was $26.576 billion to $26.626 billion and the EPS target $24.45 to $24.50 [5]. That looks like a beat, but the ARR target stayed at 10.2 percent after Semrush, worth about $0.48 billion, was added [6]. Taking Semrush out, the organic ARR target fell from 10.2 percent to about 8.3 percent (our arithmetic). Management said freemium and deferred pricing caused it [13].

Capital allocation was active. Adobe paid $11.28 billion for buybacks in fiscal 2025, $6.82 billion in the first nine months of fiscal 2026, and its board added a new $25 billion authority in April 2026, with $24.55 billion left [1][4]. It bought Semrush for $1.87 billion in cash and agreed to buy Topaz Labs for about $340 million [4]. In fiscal 2024 it also paid a $1.0 billion termination fee after a planned deal was abandoned [1]. The filing index shows the leadership notices came in March, June and September 2026 [31]. Leadership turnover is the weak spot: the finance chief left on June 15, 2026, the chief executive changes on December 1, 2026, and the head of the creative business stepped down on September 27, 2026 [6][11].

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter puts five years of audited results next to the Layer 1 tests. Fiscal years end in late November or early December. The last column is the trailing twelve months to August 28, 2026, which we built by adding the last quarter of fiscal 2025 to the first nine months of fiscal 2026 [1][4]. Revenue grew from $15.79 billion in fiscal 2021 to $23.77 billion in fiscal 2025, a three-year compound rate of 10.5 percent from fiscal 2022 [1][3]. Net income grew 14.5 percent a year over those three years, and diluted EPS grew 18.2 percent a year because the share count fell [1][3].

Profit margins are high and stable. Gross margin ranged from 87.7 to 89.3 percent across the five years. GAAP operating margin dipped to 31.3 percent in fiscal 2024 because of the $1.0 billion fee, then recovered to 36.6 percent in fiscal 2025 and 35.7 percent over the trailing twelve months [1][2][4]. Cash conversion is a strength. Free cash flow was $9.85 billion in fiscal 2025 and $10.59 billion over the trailing twelve months, which is 40.8 percent of revenue [1][4][15]. Pay in shares costs about 8 percent of revenue, which is real money even though it is not a cash cost.

Adobe has used its cash mostly to buy back shares. It repurchased $11.28 billion of stock in fiscal 2025 and $6.82 billion in the first nine months of fiscal 2026, at an average of about $261 a share [1][4]. That is 91 percent of free cash flow in the nine months. Shares outstanding fell from 475 million at the end of fiscal 2021 to 391 million on August 28, 2026, and to 389.2 million on September 18, 2026 [3][4]. The cost is a balance sheet that has moved from a net cash position of $4.21 billion in fiscal 2023 to net debt of $0.72 billion today. Debt is small against cash earnings, but $1.35 billion of notes fall due in February and April 2027 and Adobe says it intends to refinance them [4].

If the number is off

Each point of margin is worth about $260 million a year, or roughly 2.4 percent of today's free cash flow. A margin drop of 5 points would cut free cash flow by about $1.3 billion and the yield on today's market value from 10.3 percent to about 9.0 percent, which would weaken the cash-based case but not break it.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one weak assumption. This chapter names it, builds the strongest case against us, and says when the evidence arrives. The next dated event is Adobe's fiscal fourth-quarter report, expected on December 9, 2026 but not yet confirmed by the company [15][25]. The new chief executive starts on December 1, 2026, so that report will also be his first, and it should include targets for fiscal 2027, as the December 2024 report did for fiscal 2025 [10][11].

Fatal flaw 1: AI turns paying seats into free users

The reverse DCF and the base target both assume that cash flow does not fall for long. If AI tools make creative work cheap enough that students, freelancers and small studios stop paying, Adobe's seat income could shrink faster than its AI products add. An open-source suite of seven apps that mimic Adobe's came out in October 2026, and its builder said it would match Adobe's features within a month [23]. It is in a very early alpha state, so today it is a warning and not a threat [23][24]. What would show it is real: net new ARR below $0.40 billion in the fourth quarter, or management cutting its own ARR target again.

Fatal flaw 2: the fiscal 2027 reset

A new chief executive and an interim finance chief both have reasons to set low targets that are easy to beat later. Wells Fargo expects fiscal 2027 ARR growth of about 7 percent against a consensus above 8 percent, expects net new ARR to turn negative, and has lowered its margin forecasts [21]. If fiscal 2027 EPS comes in at $25 instead of $27.64, our bear case becomes the base case. The weakest assumption here is that Adobe can add users for free and still grow EPS 13 percent. We would know in December.

Fatal flaw 3: the cash flow is flattered, and the buyback is not free

Free cash flow of $10.59 billion excludes $2.07 billion of share pay, and Adobe has spent 91 percent of its nine-month free cash flow on buybacks, mostly by letting net cash turn into net debt [4]. Insiders sold $50.8 million of shares and bought $1.9 million in the past 12 months [22]. If a falling share price makes buybacks less effective and the ARR slowdown continues, the main support for EPS growth weakens.
