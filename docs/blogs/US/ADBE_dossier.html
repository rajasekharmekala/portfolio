1. What the company does

Adobe sells software by subscription. People and companies pay a monthly or yearly fee to use its creative apps, its document tools such as Acrobat, and its marketing software.[1] In fiscal 2025, the year that ended November 28, 2025, subscriptions brought in $22.9 billion of the $23.8 billion in revenue, or 96 percent.[1] The rest came from one-time product sales of $325 million and services of $540 million.[1] The $22.9 billion is the subscription line of the income statement. The $22.80 billion in the customer-group table that follows is Adobe's separate customer-group measure of subscription revenue, which is about $0.1 billion lower, and the filings we read do not reconcile the two line by line.[6][1]

Adobe now reports its subscription business in two customer groups. Business Professionals & Consumers uses tools such as Acrobat and Adobe Express to read, edit and create documents and content, and it brought in $6.50 billion of subscription revenue in fiscal 2025.[6][1] Creative & Marketing Professionals uses the Creative Cloud apps, the Firefly image-generation models and the Adobe Experience Platform, and it brought in $16.30 billion.[6][1] Until fiscal 2025, Adobe reported by product segment instead: Digital Media made $17.65 billion and Digital Experience made $5.86 billion.[6]

Customers are spread across the world. The Americas supplied $14.12 billion of fiscal 2025 revenue, or 59 percent, Europe, the Middle East and Africa supplied $6.29 billion, and Asia-Pacific supplied $3.36 billion.[1] Currency matters at the margin: Q3 revenue grew 13 percent as reported and 12 percent at constant currency.[3]

The economics are unusually good. Gross margin is the share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of serving customers, and Adobe's was 89.3 percent over the last twelve months.[3][1] Customers often pay up front, so Adobe held $7.1 billion of deferred revenue (cash received for service not yet delivered) in the latest quarter.[3] Remaining performance obligations (RPO), meaning signed contracts not yet recognized as revenue, were $22.16 billion, and about 67 percent of that is due to be recognized within twelve months.[2] Those two figures explain why Adobe's revenue is predictable: most of next year's sales are already under contract or paid for.

Artificial intelligence is both the opportunity and the threat. Adobe builds its own Firefly models and also lets customers use models from other companies inside its apps.[1] It says AI-first ARR, a new category, grew more than 150 percent in the latest quarter, and that its tools now reach one billion monthly active users, helped by free tiers.[3] Management describes the plan as growing the number of users through a freemium strategy, meaning free entry-level products that lead to paid upgrades.[3] Adobe also returns cash to owners through a buyback, which is the company repurchasing its own shares, and it bought back $11.3 billion of stock in fiscal 2025.[1]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • SBC stands for stock-based compensation, which is pay in shares. It is a real cost to owners because it creates new shares, and the report subtracts it to show a stricter version of cash flow.
  • FCF is free cash flow, the cash from operations minus the cash spent on equipment. It is the money an owner could take out of the business.
  • EPS is earnings per share, the company's profit divided across all its shares.
  • ROIC is return on invested capital, the after-tax profit earned on each dollar put into the business.
  • EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges.
  • Dilution is the shrinking of each existing share's slice when a company issues more shares, for example to pay employees.
  • Reverse DCF means working backwards from today's price to the growth in cash flow that the price assumes for the next ten years.
  • Non-GAAP figures exclude items such as stock pay and the amortization of acquired intangibles, so they are higher than the audited GAAP figures.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence of a durable business is how much of each sale Adobe keeps and how steadily sales arrive. Gross margin of 89.3 percent means pricing power has not been competed away.[3][1] Free cash flow was 40.8 percent of revenue over the last twelve months, which means roughly 41 cents of every sales dollar reaches the owners before stock pay.[2][1] Total ARR reached $27.50 billion at the end of the latest quarter, up 11.2 percent, and RPO stood at $22.16 billion.[2] A business with these numbers is hard to dislodge quickly, because customers' files, templates and team workflows sit inside Adobe's apps. That switching-cost argument is our reading of the evidence, and the filings do not measure it directly.

Demand has been improving, not fading. Quarterly revenue growth went from 10 percent in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2025 to 12 percent, 13 percent and 13 percent in the next three quarters.[6][5][4][3] Subscription revenue in the two customer groups grew 14 percent in the latest quarter, up from 12 percent in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2025.[3][6] Adobe has beaten its own revenue and profit targets in each of the last four quarters (the table in Chapter 2B shows the detail), and Zacks counted four beats of the Street's earnings estimate in a row.[31] On September 10, 2026 Adobe raised its full-year EPS target to $24.45 to $24.50, above the $24.36 that analysts expected.[30][22]

The threat is that AI lowers the cost of making good-enough images, video and documents. Adobe's own annual report lists competition from AI-first creative tools, web- and mobile-first design platforms, and creative features built into other apps, and it says the market has limited barriers to entry and customers that are sensitive to price.[1] Some pressure already shows in the numbers. Adobe's targeted non-GAAP operating margin (the share of sales left after all running costs, with stock pay excluded) is about 45 percent for fiscal 2026, against 46.2 percent in fiscal 2025.[3][6] RPO grew only 8.4 percent over the year, from $20.44 billion to $22.16 billion, while subscription revenue grew 14 percent.[7][2] Adobe also kept its full-year ARR growth target at 10.2 percent after it bought Semrush, which brought about $480 million of ARR, so organic growth in the target is lower than at the start of the year (Chapter 2B shows the arithmetic).[6][4]

Management is changing at the same time. The chief financial officer left on June 15, 2026, and the chief executive officer, Shantanu Narayen, will hand over to Anil Chakravarthy on December 1, 2026 and become executive chair.[4][9] Chakravarthy has run Adobe's marketing-software business and its sales organization since 2020, which gives continuity, but it is also a first-time test for the new team on the next earnings date.[9] Executive pay is tied in part to relative total stockholder return over 2026 to 2028 and to a Net New Sales goal, so the people running Adobe are paid for beating other stocks, not just for growing sales.[13] We looked for a published figure for net revenue retention (NRR, the share of last year's customer revenue still paying this year) in the releases and filings we read and did not find one, so the Street and we are inferring customer loyalty from ARR and RPO instead.

What this means for an owner: the business quality is high and visible in the cash flow, but the evidence of strength is mostly in the past and present, while the market is pricing the future. The number

Adobe's return on invested capital (ROIC) is about 58 percent: it earns roughly 58 cents of after-tax operating profit per year for each dollar of capital in the business.

How it was calculated

We took twelve-month operating income of $9,271 million, applied the twelve-month tax rate of 21.5 percent to get after-tax profit of $7,276 million, and divided by invested capital of $12,488 million (debt plus equity minus cash and short-term investments).

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate rests on a weakest assumption. This chapter names it, gives the evidence that would prove it wrong, and says when we will know. It then sets out the strongest case against our own conclusion, which is the bear case in this book, and the one claim on which the verdict hangs.

Reverse DCF: the weakest assumption is the 18x exit multiple

The reverse DCF assumes that in year ten the market still pays 18 times free cash flow for Adobe. If AI has worn down Adobe's moat by then, the multiple could be much lower, and the growth needed to justify today's price would be higher, not lower: at 12 times the figure is -2.5 percent a year, still weak. The evidence that would show this assumption too generous is a falling Adobe ARR growth rate in each of the next two reports. We will know the first data point on the next earnings date.[19][20]

Central target: the weakest assumption is that free cash flow after stock pay grows at all

Our $331 target needs free cash flow after stock pay to grow about 2 percent a year and the multiple to recover to about 12 times. If growth is zero, the same model gives about $285, which is the level at which the Street's average target of $283.90 already sits.[18] The evidence that would prove this assumption wrong is a fiscal 2027 revenue target midpoint below $28.5 billion, which would be growth of about 7 percent or less from the $26.6 billion midpoint for fiscal 2026. The Street currently expects $29.04 billion.[14][3]

Quality figures: the weakest assumption is that stock pay stays near 8 percent of revenue

ROIC and free cash flow margin look excellent, but they ignore that Adobe pays employees about $2.1 billion a year in shares.[2][1] If AI talent costs push that above 10 percent of revenue, Adobe would lose the SBC exception and fail the Layer 1 financial floor outright.
