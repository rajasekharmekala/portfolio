1. What the company does

DigitalOcean sells cloud computing, which means renting servers, storage and software over the internet by the hour or the month instead of selling machines. The company began as a simple service for developers and small software firms, and it still reports more than 680,000 customers.[6] Its description of itself has changed, though. It now calls itself an AI-native cloud for running trained AI models, a job called inference, and for software that acts on its own. The AI side began with the purchase of Paperspace, which launched the company's AI and machine learning products.[1] The watchlist description of this name as a developer cloud for small businesses is therefore half right: the small-customer roots are real, but the growth now comes from a much smaller group of large and AI-focused customers.

The company earns money mostly from usage, so a customer's bill rises as it runs more work. Management describes the model as largely consumption-based, which lets it move capacity to wherever demand is strongest.[11] Two measures show the scale. Annual recurring revenue (ARR), which DigitalOcean calculates as the latest quarter's revenue multiplied by four, was $1,125 million in June 2026, up 29 percent from $875 million a year earlier.[1] Remaining performance obligations (RPO), the value of signed contracts not yet turned into revenue, reached $894 million with a weighted life of 3.7 years, up from $71 million a year earlier.[1][6] AI customer ARR was $234 million, up 212 percent from $75 million.[1] That figure counts all spending by customers who use at least one AI product, including their ordinary cloud spending, so it is not the same thing as renting graphics chips.[6] Management says 85 percent of it comes from inference and core cloud services rather than bare metal, which is raw chip rental.[6]

The costs sit mostly in data centers. DigitalOcean rents data center space and power, buys servers, and pays for the depreciation of those servers as they wear out. Gross margin, which is revenue minus the direct cost of providing the service, as a share of revenue, fell to 55 percent in the June 2026 quarter from 60 percent a year earlier, because new data centers were paid for before their revenue arrived.[1] On the other side, the company raised list prices on several generations of its graphics-chip fleets by about 30 percent, which is a sign that customers are accepting higher prices.[11] Revenue is global: 47 percent from North America, 22 percent from Europe, 22 percent from Asia and 9 percent from the rest of the world.[1] The 25 largest customers made up about 20 percent of revenue in the June quarter, up from 9 percent a year earlier, so concentration is rising from a very low level.[1]

Capacity and funding explain most of the risk. The company runs 20 data centers and has about 155 megawatts of capacity committed, most of it due to be online by the end of 2027.[11][6] It paid for the growth in 2026 by selling shares: 11.9 million at $77 in March, raising $887.9 million, and 12.5 million at $117.54 in July, which raised about $1.47 billion. The quarterly report says the proceeds were used to repurchase $472 million of convertible note principal; at the $39.17 conversion price those notes were worth about $1.42 billion in shares, close to the amount raised, but the price actually paid is not in the record.[1] It has also signed leases for data center space that have not started yet, with $2.76 billion of fixed payments over an average of 11.2 years, which do not appear on the balance sheet today.[1] Those leases are why the cash flow story in chapter 3 matters more than the growth story.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Annual recurring revenue (ARR) is the latest quarter's revenue multiplied by four. DigitalOcean calculates it this way, so it moves with usage, not with signed contracts.[1]
  • Remaining performance obligations (RPO) are signed contracts whose revenue has not yet been recognized.[1]
  • Net dollar retention (NDR) is what current customers spend now compared with what the same customers spent twelve months ago. Above 100 percent means existing customers are spending more.[2]
  • Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit before financing costs, taxes and the wear on equipment. This report uses operating income plus depreciation and amortization, because the company's own adjusted version also removes stock pay.[1]
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operations minus spending on equipment and capitalized software. A second reading adds equipment bought through financing arrangements.[1]
  • Stock-based compensation (SBC) is the cost of paying employees in shares, shown as an expense but paid in new shares instead of cash.[1]
  • Return on invested capital (ROIC) is operating profit after tax divided by the money invested in the business (equity plus debt plus finance leases, less cash).[25]
  • Net debt is debt and finance lease balances minus cash.[1]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence for the business is pricing and demand. Revenue growth doubled from 14 percent in the June 2025 quarter to 29 percent in June 2026, and management says it is delivering new data centers on time or early.[11][6] The company says demand is well in excess of capacity, and it raised list prices on several graphics-chip fleets by about 30 percent while revenue kept growing.[11] Backlog supports the claim: signed commitments reached $894 million, up more than twelve times in a year, and the first nine-figure annual commitments came from AI-native customers.[6] Net dollar retention (NDR) rose to a three-year high of 102 percent, which means existing customers are now spending slightly more than a year ago.[11] It had been 101 percent in 2023, 98 percent in 2024 and 100 percent in 2025.[3][2]

The weakest evidence is cash and margin quality. Gross margin has fallen for two quarters in a row on a year-over-year basis: 56 percent against 61 percent in the March quarter, and 55 percent against 60 percent in June.[5][1] Free cash flow looks strong at 17 percent of sales over the last twelve months, but that figure leaves out $178 million of servers acquired under financing arrangements in the same period, which the company repays over time.[1][2] The 2026 free cash flow guide was cut from 15 to 17 percent of sales in February to 11 to 13 percent in August, even though revenue guidance, which is the company's own forecast for the period, rose, so growth is being bought with spending.[8][6] Management also said it will stop highlighting NDR as a key metric, because growth now depends on the highest-spending and AI customers, which removes one of the few retention figures investors could track.[11]

Competition comes from three directions. Large cloud providers sell to big companies and frontier AI labs; management says they optimize for frontier labs and large enterprises.[11] AI-focused rental clouds, called neoclouds, sell raw graphics-chip capacity. CoreWeave grew revenue 112 percent to $2,575 million in the June quarter, but it had a small operating loss and raised more than $10 billion of debt and convertible bonds, which shows how much more capital that model needs.[21] The third group is smaller developer-cloud and edge peers such as Akamai, whose cloud infrastructure services revenue grew 39 percent to $99 million, and Cloudflare and Fastly, which grow 36 percent and 23 percent with higher gross margins.[24][22][23] DigitalOcean's claim is that it sits between the two: more software than a neocloud, more affordable than a large cloud. The 85 percent of AI customer ARR that is not bare metal is the best evidence for the claim.[6]

The sector now has a doubt attached to it that the company cannot fully answer. On 20 October, a South Korean neocloud, Elice, fell about 33 percent on its first trading day.[18] In mid-October, Gartner forecast that more than 80 percent of neocloud providers could cease independent operation by 2030 and said AI revenue may not cover planned infrastructure spending.[17] A Gartner forecaster told CNBC that, at the moment, there is more compute capacity than demand, and that tokens are being given away in free products.[16] That sits against DigitalOcean's statement that demand exceeds its capacity. We weight the company's statement for 2026, because capacity is allocated to customers before the data centers open, and we weight Gartner's view for 2027 and 2028, when the new supply arrives and the 155 megawatts must be filled.[11][16] The next chapter turns that argument into numbers that can be checked.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 has hard red lines and compounder floors. A hard red line is a solvency or structure test that rejects the name outright: debt of 4.0 times EBITDA or more with interest cover below 2.0 times, a Stage 4 chart, or an accounting or going-concern problem. Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend on the Weinstein scale, in which the price sits below a falling 50-day and a falling 200-day average. Compounder floors are return on invested capital (ROIC) of 12 percent or more, free cash flow margin of 12 percent or more, net debt to EBITDA below 3.0 times, and stock pay (SBC) below 8 percent of revenue. A base is a sideways floor that a price builds before a move. A breakout is a move above a base on heavy volume. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend. A name that misses a floor can still qualify as a turnaround if it has rising signals and the entry price is low enough, using asymmetry, which is the reward-to-risk ratio: the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop loss, the price at which the idea is wrong. The floor for this bucket is 2.5 to 1. Entry is planned through a limit order, which only fills at a set price or better, placed in an entry zone, the band of prices where buying is planned.[27]

Weakest assumption of the Reverse DCF: that reported free cash flow is the right starting point

Weakest assumption of the twelve-month values: that demand keeps filling new capacity at current prices

Our base value needs the revenue guide to hold and cash growth to continue. DigitalOcean raised prices on several graphics-chip fleets by about 30 percent, and the evidence that would show this wrong is price cuts or falling utilization once the new 155 megawatts open in 2027 and 2028.[11][6] Gartner's forecaster said there is more compute capacity than demand today, and the Gartner research put cumulative AI revenue at $3.6 trillion through 2030 against $8.5 trillion needed for a 12 percent return on infrastructure spending.[16][17][19] CNBC reported that Anthropic's revenue growth slowed from 140 percent to 45 percent between two quarters, and that CoreWeave and Nebius fell 25 percent in the week to 16 October. Weakest assumption of the probabilities: that financing stays open

The company plans to settle $312.3 million of 2026 convertible notes due on 1 December 2026 by drawing on a revolving credit line, using cash, or generating cash.
