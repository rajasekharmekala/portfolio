Before you read

DigitalOcean rents cloud computing to start-ups and developers, and it now sells chip capacity to artificial intelligence firms[1][2]. Its sales grew 29% in the June 2026 quarter, up from 14% a year earlier[3][7]. Our verdict is REJECTED: the company misses our floors for return on capital, share pay and trend, and the price of $98.69 already assumes about 22% yearly cash-flow growth for ten years[35][2]. The stock fell 9.85% on 2026-10-16 in a sector-wide scare over borrowed money for AI computers, and we found no company news that day[24][15]. 1. What the company does

DigitalOcean sells rented computing power over the internet. Think of it as an electricity supplier for software: a developer starts a virtual server, which the company calls a Droplet, uses it for as long as needed, and pays for what was used each month. The same customer can add managed databases, container software (Kubernetes), file storage and a service that deploys applications[1]. In Q2 2026 the company averaged about 21,700 customers spending more than $500 a month, which it calls Digital Native Enterprise customers, and it says it has more than 680,000 customers in total[2][3].

The newer business is artificial intelligence. The company rents graphics chips (GPUs) by the hour and sells a platform for running trained AI models, which the industry calls inference. Inference means answering questions with a model that already exists; training means building the model. DigitalOcean positions itself for inference and for AI agents, which are programs that carry out tasks on their own[3]. The company reports ARR (annual recurring revenue), which it calculates as the latest quarter's revenue multiplied by four. ARR was $1,125 million at 2026-06-30, up from $875 million a year earlier, and AI Customer ARR was $234 million, up from $75 million[2]. Management says 85% of AI Customer ARR comes from inference and ordinary cloud products rather than from renting whole GPU servers[3].

Who pays is shifting toward larger customers. Customers whose bills run above $100,000 a year, which the company calls $100K+ customers, grew in number by only 9% in Q2 2026, yet their revenue grew 98% and now makes up 35% of the total[3]. Customers with $1 million or more of annual run rate numbered 45, up from 26[2]. The top 25 customers made up about 20% of revenue in Q2 2026 against about 9% a year earlier[2]. That is faster growth, but it also means a handful of AI-focused customers now matter a great deal.

To serve them the company buys servers and rents data-centre space. It pays for servers with cash, with finance leases and with equipment financing. It has committed to about 155 megawatts of data-centre capacity, with more being pursued[3]. As of 2026-06-30 it had $2,759.3 million of future lease payments, spread over an average of 11.2 years, for space whose leases have not yet started[2]. Those promises are the main reason debt and cash flow need a careful reading in Chapter 3. An IDC estimate quoted in the annual report puts the market for cloud services used by firms with fewer than 500 employees at about $138 billion in 2025 and $251 billion in 2028[1].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ARR is annual recurring revenue, the latest quarter's sales multiplied by four.
  • RPO is remaining performance obligations, meaning signed contracts not yet recognised as revenue.
  • EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization; adjusted EBITDA also adds back stock pay.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after the business pays its running costs and buys equipment and software. The company's adjusted version leaves out equipment bought under leases and financing deals.
  • Cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates before financing decisions.
  • SBC is stock-based compensation, the cost of paying staff in shares.
  • EPS is earnings per share, the company's profit divided across all its shares.
  • Net debt is borrowings plus finance-lease obligations, minus cash.
  • Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of running the cloud, as a share of revenue. Operating margin is revenue minus all running costs, as a share of revenue.
  • Guidance is the company's own published forecast for the coming period. Dilution is the rise in the share count that shrinks each share's slice of the company.
  • RSI is the relative strength index, a 0 to 100 meter of how fast the price has moved. Below 30 is called oversold. ATR is the average true range, the typical daily price swing in dollars.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on the Weinstein scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend.
  • A base is a sideways stretch of trading that a price builds before its next move. A pivot is the price at the top of that stretch, which a breakout must clear.
  • The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the ratio of potential gain to potential loss: (target - price) / (price - stop).
  • Reverse DCF is the method of working backwards from today's price to the yearly growth in cash flow that the price assumes.
  • NDR is net dollar retention: this year's revenue from last year's customers as a share of last year's revenue from them.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

DigitalOcean competes on simplicity and predictable pricing. Its annual report names Amazon, Microsoft, Google, IBM, Alibaba and Oracle as the large rivals, and OVHcloud, Akamai (Linode), Hetzner and Vultr as the smaller ones. For AI it also names CoreWeave and Lambda Labs[1]. It does not have the scale of the big three, so its case rests on serving customers who find the large clouds too complicated or too costly, and on being a full cloud rather than only a GPU landlord.

The evidence for strength is the growth path. Revenue growth rose from 14% in Q2 2025 to 16%, 18%, 22% and then 29% in Q2 2026[7][6][5][4][3]. Million-plus-dollar customers produced $259 million of ARR, up 214%[3]. Signed commitments (RPO) rose to $894 million from $71 million, and the average contract length moved from 1.6 years to more than three[3]. Management says it signed its first nine-figure annual commitments in the quarter. Longer contracts make revenue stickier, which is the best sign yet that AI customers see the platform as more than spare capacity.

The evidence for weakness is in the margins. The gross margin, which is revenue minus the direct cost of running the cloud as a share of revenue, was 55.0% in Q2 2026 against 59.9% a year earlier (154,662 / 281,184 and 130,945 / 218,700, in thousands)[2]. Operating income fell to $29.4 million from $35.6 million while revenue rose 29%, so the operating margin, which is revenue minus all running costs as a share of revenue, fell to 10.4% from 16.3%[2]. Depreciation on new GPU servers and the cost of new data-centre space sit in cost of revenue, so faster growth is currently buying lower margins. Stock pay was $32.7 million, or 11.6% of revenue, against 9.6% a year earlier[2].

Our judgement is that the moat is narrow and improving but untested. Renting a virtual server is easy to switch away from. Managed databases, Kubernetes and the new AI platform are harder to leave, and multi-year contracts add stickiness. Net dollar retention was 100% for the full year 2025 (101% in the fourth quarter alone), so on average existing customers neither grew nor shrank[1]. Gartner, quoted by CNBC, expects more than 80% of neocloud providers to stop operating independently by 2030 because of cost pressure and weak differentiation[23]. DigitalOcean argues that it differs from bare-metal GPU neoclouds because it sells a full platform, but that claim has not yet been tested in a downturn.

Who owns the stock and who is selling

Access Industries, the investment group controlled by Len Blavatnik, reported 18,653,451 shares, or 15.86% of the company, in a Schedule 13D amendment dated 2026-08-06[14]. Insiders own about 0.96% of the shares and sold 22,641 shares worth $2,589,317 in the last quarter. The chief financial officer sold 10,000 shares at $105.73 on 2026-09-03 under a pre-arranged Rule 10b5-1 trading plan[19]. GuruFocus reports $573.8 million of insider sales over twelve months and no insider buying[21]. We could not itemise that total, and it probably includes the large holder's sales, so we treat the individual executive sales as small and pre-arranged, and we read the absence of insider buying as a neutral-to-negative signal. Short interest is 9.27% of shares outstanding[18].

What this means for an owner

The business is growing faster than it ever has, and the product mix is moving toward higher-value AI and database services. The price of that growth is lower margins, more dilution (the rise in the share count that shrinks each share's slice of the company) and more commitments to pay for capacity. Chapter 2B tests those trade-offs with company-specific measures, and Chapter 3 puts numbers on them.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Layer 1 of our process asks two questions. First, static levels: does the company clear fixed floors for return on capital, free cash flow margin, debt and share-based pay? Second, trajectory: are those measures improving fast enough to justify a miss? The same layer tests whether the price is in a confirmed uptrend, because a tactical name must be in Stage 2, the confirmed-uptrend phase of the Weinstein scale, while Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. It also tests asymmetry, which is the ratio of potential gain to potential loss, measured as (target - price) / (price - stop). The five-year tables give the history, and the Layer 1 table at the end of the chapter gives the verdict.

Revenue has grown every year, from $428.6 million in 2021 to $901.4 million in 2025, and was $1,011.1 million over the twelve months to 2026-06-30 (901.4 - 429.4 + 539.1)[11][1][2]. The profit line has improved more: operating income moved from a loss of $11.2 million in 2021 to a gain of $157.0 million in 2025[10][1]. Net income of $259.3 million in 2025 is flattered by a $52.6 million tax benefit and a $48.1 million gain on retiring debt, so operating income is the cleaner guide[1].

Cash flow from operations, the cash the business generates before financing, also grew each year, to $309.6 million in 2025. Spending on servers and software rose too, and from 2025 the company began buying equipment under financing arrangements that do not pass through its capital-spending line: $126.8 million in 2025 and $178.4 million in the last twelve months[1][2]. Because that financed equipment is real spending that will be repaid with interest, we show free cash flow both ways in the second table.

Debt has changed form more than size. For four years the company carried $1.46 billion to $1.49 billion of borrowings, almost all of it 0% convertible notes due in 2026. In 2025 and 2026 it replaced most of them with a term loan, new 2030 notes, equity sales and finance leases[11][10][1][2]. The share count tells the other half of the story: 91.9 million shares at the end of 2025 and 117.6 million by 2026-07-30, a 27.9% rise in seven months[1][18].

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate above rests on a few assumptions. This chapter names the weakest one for each, states what evidence would show it wrong and says when that evidence will arrive. It also gives the red-team bear case, the strongest honest argument against our own conclusion, and the one claim on which the whole verdict hinges.

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF: which cash flow counts

The 21.8% answer uses the company's adjusted free cash flow, which excludes equipment bought under financing. If investors accept that definition, the price is demanding but not extreme. If they count the financed equipment, free cash flow is about zero and no growth rate solves the model. Evidence that would show our approach too strict: financed equipment purchases falling while revenue keeps rising, so that cash after all purchases turns clearly positive. A change of one point in the growth rate moves the value by about $7, so a modest error in the path changes the answer a lot. A second test changes only the starting cash flow. We do not combine this with the growth cuts. The modeled value is a present-value estimate and not cash available to shareholders. Evidence that would show it too low: an adjusted free cash flow margin guided above 13%, a backlog above $894 million with more than $366 million due within a year, and Q4 revenue growth above the 35% that management has promised. All of these arrive with the Q3 results, expected 2026-11-04 and not confirmed by the company.

Weakest assumption in the leverage estimate: the cash balance and the lease build

Net debt of $276.2 million uses the cash balance at 2026-06-30 and assumes the July deal was cash-neutral apart from unpublished fees. Q3 capital spending, the draw on the new equipment facility and the $312.3 million of notes due on 2026-12-01 will all move it[2][13]. If the company repays those notes by drawing on its revolving credit line, as it says it plans to, net debt does not change but borrowings rise. The Q3 balance sheet will show it.

Red-team bear case: why the loss could be larger than our bear value

The sector scare is about whether AI capacity can pay for itself. CNBC quotes Apollo's chief economist asking what happens if the payoff arrives slower than expected, and reports that Anthropic's third-quarter revenue grew 45% from the second quarter, a step down from earlier in the year[23]. The ten-year Treasury yield is 5.10%[25], and DigitalOcean's new equipment facility costs a swap rate plus 2.75%[13]. The company's nine-figure commitments come from AI-native companies, and the top 25 customers are already 20% of revenue[2][3]. If one or two of them pause, quarterly revenue could miss the guide. A stock priced at 11.7 times revenue would then be repriced. The July deal also shows the cost of rising stock prices: the company paid 3.1 times par to retire notes because the shares had risen[12].

Red-team bull case: why rejecting could be a mistake

The company has beaten its own revenue, margin and earnings guide in each of the last five quarters, and analysts' 2026 earnings estimates have risen 18.7% in 90 days[3][16]. Net debt is now small, interest cover is 4.75 times and the revolving credit line has $406.7 million available[2]. The enterprise value is 6.5 times 2027 consensus revenue, and Oppenheimer's checks suggest enterprise customers are adding AI use cases[27]. The stock fell 23% in three sessions with no negative company news that we found, and some of the neocloud selling may have little to do with DigitalOcean's own finances, although the $2.76 billion of unstarted leases cut against that. A buyer who accepts the Street's revenue forecasts and ignores the cash definition would reach a very different value. Our answer is that revenue multiples hide the financing cost, and our rule requires 3.5 to 1 reward to risk, which this price does not offer.
