1. What the company does

DigitalOcean rents out computing power over the internet. A customer who needs a server, storage space or a database clicks a few buttons and pays for what it uses, instead of buying machines and renting a room for them. The company was incorporated in 2012 and sells from Broomfield, Colorado.[16] It began as a simple, low-priced cloud for individual developers and small software firms. Today it calls itself an AI-native cloud built for inference, which is the stage where a trained AI model answers real requests, and for agentic software, which means programs that take actions on their own.[1] It counts more than 680,000 customers, but the group that matters is far smaller: about 21,700 customers each spend more than $500 a month, and they produce 67 percent of revenue, up from about 59 percent a year ago.[2]

Most customers pay for what they use, so a bill rises when a customer runs more work. The filings describe the model as largely consumption-based, with most customers on month-to-month terms and a growing number signing minimum-spend commitments.[2] Two measures show the size of the business. Annual recurring revenue (ARR) is simply the latest quarter's revenue multiplied by four. It was $1,125 million in June 2026 against $875 million a year earlier, up 29 percent.[1][30] It is a run rate, not a contract. Signed contracts show up in a second measure, remaining performance obligations (RPO). RPO was $894 million, up from $71 million a year earlier. About $366 million of it should become revenue within 12 months. The weighted life of the contracts is 3.7 years.[1][2] At the end of 2025, by comparison, RPO was $134 million with a life of 2.0 years.[5]

The growth story is AI. AI customer ARR was $234 million, up 212 percent from $75 million.[1][2] The company defines it as the yearly rate of all spending by customers who use at least one AI product, including their ordinary cloud spending, so it is wider than the money earned from renting graphics chips.[1] Management says 85 percent of it comes from inference and core cloud services rather than bare metal, which is raw chip rental with no software on top. It adds that inference services grew almost 800 percent and are now over 70 percent of AI customer ARR.[1][22] That split matters because bare-metal rental is the business of the so-called neoclouds such as CoreWeave, whose customers are few and whose contracts are long and fixed.[5][26]

The costs sit in data centers. DigitalOcean leases space and power, buys servers, and writes off the cost of those servers over several years. In the June quarter, cost of revenue rose 44 percent to $126.5 million while revenue rose 29 percent. Depreciation on new data-center equipment rose $17.2 million, and co-location (rented data-center space and power) rose $12.4 million.[2] That is why gross margin fell to 55 percent from 60 percent. Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of running the service, as a share of revenue.[1][2] The company plans new facilities in Memphis, Richmond and Kansas City in 2026 and has committed to about 155 megawatts of capacity, most of it online by the end of 2027.[1][5][22] It had also agreed, at 30 June, to $2,759.3 million of fixed payments for data-center space that has not yet started, over an average of 11.2 years, and $281.6 million of server leases that began in July.[2]

The company paid for this growth with new shares and new borrowing. In March 2026 it sold 11,948,052 shares at $77.00 and received $887.9 million, which it used to repay $500.0 million of a term loan.[2][4] In July it sold 12,543,915 shares at $117.54. It spent about $1.474 billion buying back $471.8 million of its 0 percent convertible notes due 2030.[2][13] A convertible note is a loan that the lender can swap for shares at a fixed price, here $39.17. Those notes were worth about 12.05 million shares and the new issue was 12.54 million. That is roughly half a million more shares, a rise in the share count known as dilution, which management says it will offset by buying back about 500,000 shares.[2][22] On 10 September it signed an equipment financing facility of up to $725 million with MUFG. The rate is the term SOFR swap rate plus 2.75 percent. Servers can be bought on credit and paid for over about four years.[10][11] Revenue is global. In the June quarter 47 percent came from North America, 22 percent from Europe, 22 percent from Asia and 9 percent from the rest of the world. United States customers rose to 41 percent of revenue from 31 percent a year earlier.[2]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Annual recurring revenue (ARR) is the latest quarter's revenue multiplied by four, so it moves with usage, not with signed contracts.
  • Remaining performance obligations (RPO) are signed contracts whose revenue has not yet been recognized.
  • Net dollar retention (NDR) is what current customers spend now compared with what the same customers spent twelve months ago. Above 100 percent means existing customers are spending more.
  • Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit before financing costs, taxes and the wear on equipment. This report uses operating income plus depreciation and amortization, because the company's own adjusted version also removes stock pay.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operations minus spending on equipment and capitalized software. The company's adjusted version excludes servers bought through financing arrangements, and this report shows both.
  • Stock-based compensation (SBC) is the cost of paying employees in shares, shown as an expense but paid in new shares instead of cash.
  • Return on invested capital (ROIC) is operating profit after tax divided by the money invested in the business (equity plus debt plus finance leases, less cash).
  • Convertible note: a loan that the lender may swap for shares at a fixed price. A capped call is a purchased option that offsets part of the dilution when that swap happens.
  • A conviction tier is one of our four levels of confidence in a stock, and each level caps the share of net worth that one stock may hold. TIER_4 is the lowest, with a ceiling of 2 percent.
  • The risk budget is the most of net worth we accept losing if the stop price is hit. For TIER_4 it is 35 basis points, which is 0.35 percent. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent.
  • Inference: the stage where a trained AI model answers requests. Bare metal: renting a physical server with no software layer on top.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest evidence for the business is demand and pricing. Revenue growth doubled from 14 percent in the June 2025 quarter to 29 percent in June 2026. The company guides to 32 to 34 percent for the September quarter and at least 35 percent by the fourth quarter.[1][22] Management says demand is well in excess of capacity. It also says it raised list prices on several graphics-chip fleets by about 30 percent. The effect on June-quarter net new ARR was modest, and the rise is already in the 2026 guide.[22] Signed backlog supports the claim. RPO of $894 million is more than twelve times its level a year ago, and the first nine-figure annual commitments came from AI-native customers.[1][12] The company has also beaten its own guidance, which is its published forecast for a period, again and again. June-quarter revenue of $281.2 million compared with a guide of $272 to $274 million. Earnings per share (EPS) of $0.45 compared with a guide of $0.20 to $0.23. The 2026 revenue outlook has been raised three times, from 21 percent growth in February to about 30 percent in August.[1][3][6]

The weakest evidence is margin, cash quality and dilution. Gross margin fell to 56 percent in March and 55 percent in June, from 61 and 60 percent a year earlier. The company pays for new data centers before their revenue arrives.[2] Free cash flow looks healthy at 17.3 percent of sales over the last twelve months. That figure leaves out $178.4 million of servers acquired under financing arrangements in the same period. The company repays those over time.[2][5] The 2026 adjusted free cash flow guide was cut from 15 to 17 percent of sales in February to 9 to 12 percent in May, and then lifted to 11 to 13 percent in August, which is still below where it began.[3][6][1] Management also said it will stop highlighting net dollar retention just as it reached a three-year high of 102 percent. Growth now depends on the highest-spending and AI customers, it said.[22]

Competition comes from three directions. Large cloud providers (Amazon, Microsoft, Google) sell to big companies; smaller clouds such as OVHcloud, Akamai's Linode, Hetzner and Vultr sell to small customers; and AI-focused infrastructure firms such as CoreWeave and Lambda rent graphics chips.[5] CoreWeave grew revenue 112 percent to $2,575 million in the June quarter. It posted an operating loss and paid $640 million of net interest. It has raised more than $10 billion of unsecured debt and convertible bonds, which shows how much more capital that model needs.[26] Akamai's cloud infrastructure services grew 39 percent to $99 million. The company says it has signed multi-year contracts worth over $2.8 billion this year, so an old rival is now chasing the same AI customers.[27] DigitalOcean's claim is that it sits between the giants and the chip renters. It offers more software than a neocloud and is simpler and cheaper than a hyperscaler. Management says its incremental revenue per megawatt should rise as more customers add core cloud services. It says its older, non-AI business earned more than $22 million of ARR per megawatt.[22]

The sector now carries a doubt that the company cannot fully answer. On 14 October, Gartner warned that 80 percent of neoclouds could disappear by 2030. It said over $3.5 trillion of AI infrastructure spending could be exposed to impairment. A hot inflation report the same day also pushed borrowing costs higher.[23] DigitalOcean fell 11.86 percent that day to $111.05 and a further 10.3 percent on 16 October to $98.17, in a sell-off that hit every AI data-center and cloud name.[21][24] Funding stress was visible in CoreWeave, whose bond yield a widely shared chart put at 15 percent, and borrowing costs were named as a driver of the sell-off.[23][25] We weight management's statement for 2026, because capacity is allocated to customers before a data center opens. We weight Gartner's view for 2027 and 2028, when the new supply arrives and the 155 megawatts must be filled at a price that covers their cost. What this means for an owner: the business is real, growing faster each quarter and backed by signed commitments, but it is being built with other people's money, and the stock price has so far paid for that growth in advance. The competitive position is good enough to own at a lower price and not strong enough to ignore the price.

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 compares the company with the standards in our scoring file and then asks whether the direction of travel is improving. The three archetypes are a compounder (high quality, steady growth), a turnaround or value inflection (misses a static standard but is cheap and improving) and a tactical breakout. A breakout is a move above a flat trading range on heavy volume, backed by a dated catalyst. A name that passes none of them cannot receive new money. A holding that already exists is judged against its stop and its upside, not only against the entry rule. Every estimate in this report has a weakest assumption. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when we will see that evidence. The red-team bear case against our trim is simple. The company has beaten guidance for five quarters. Demand exceeds capacity, and the 2027 growth target is more than 50 percent. The stock is 43 percent below its high. An investor who sells 42 shares after a 6.89 percent bounce may be selling near a low. Our answer is that the price assumes success. Flaw one: the funding market closes

The business is built with financing. The company has signed up to $725 million of equipment financing at the swap rate plus 2.75 percent. It wants to add $300 million more. Evidence that this flaw is playing out: a second equipment facility priced above the swap rate plus 3 percent, a cut in the 2027 growth target, or a fall in the 2026 cash guide below 11 percent after financing. Flaw two: gross margin keeps falling

Two terms drive the sizing. A conviction tier is one of our four levels of confidence in a stock, and each level caps the share of net worth that one stock may hold. For TIER_4 it is 35 basis points, which is 0.35 percent, and a basis point is one hundredth of a percent.

The verdict is TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING: reduce the holding to a small stub and keep the stub. Three numbers drive it. Reward-to-risk is minus 0.50 at the price, against a floor of 2.5.
