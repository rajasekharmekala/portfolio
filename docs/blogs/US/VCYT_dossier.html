1. What the company does

Veracyte runs cancer tests in its own laboratories. A doctor sends a small piece of a patient's tumor, and the lab reads the activity of thousands of genes in it. A computer model turns that reading into a score. The score tells the doctor how likely the cancer is to spread or return, and so whether the patient needs strong treatment or can safely skip it. The two big products are Decipher Prostate and Afirma, a thyroid test. The company also sells Prosigna for breast cancer, Decipher Bladder, and a new test called TrueMRD that looks for traces of bladder cancer after surgery. It had 755 employees at the end of 2025, with labs in South San Francisco and San Diego.[1]

Patients do not pay the bill directly; insurers do. In 2025, Medicare's traditional program paid 34 percent of testing revenue, Medicare Advantage plans paid 18 percent, private insurers paid 47 percent and Medicaid paid 1 percent.[1] In the first half of 2026 Medicare alone was 33 percent of all revenue and UnitedHealthcare was 13 percent.[4] That means two payers decide almost half of the sales. The company books revenue when it delivers the report, and it carries the risk that the insurer pays late or pays less than expected.[4] This is why the price paid per test (the average selling price, or ASP) matters as much as the number of tests.

The numbers per test are easy to follow. In the second quarter of 2026 the lab reported 48,389 testing reports and $145.7 million of testing revenue, so each report brought in about $3,010. That figure includes about $4.5 million of money collected from older claims, which the CFO called prior period collections; without them the average was about $2,900.[5][31] Gross margin, which is revenue minus the direct cost of running the tests, was 72.2 percent under accounting rules and 74.9 percent after the usual adjustments.[5] A lab like this has high fixed costs, so extra tests add profit quickly. That is why profit grew much faster than sales over the last three years.

Sales are concentrated. Decipher Prostate earned $310.7 million and Afirma $172.9 million in 2025, which together are about 94 percent of the $517.1 million total.[7] The rest is small: a few other tests, product kits sold abroad, and drug-company projects. A French subsidiary, Veracyte SAS, went into liquidation, and the company says that is why drug-company revenue is falling.[5] Three events in the last six months widen the product list. Prosigna and TrueMRD launched in June 2026, Medicare agreed to cover Prosigna for a defined group of breast cancer patients on 2026-10-16, and the company bought Convergent Genomics on 2026-09-14 for $150 million in cash to add a urine test for bladder cancer.[5][29][13] Convergent's first test is not expected on the market until late 2028, so it costs money before it earns any.[14]

What makes this a growth story and what could stop it

Growth has three sources: more tests ordered, a better price per test, and new products. Test volume grew 19 percent in 2025 and 14 percent in the second quarter of 2026, so the first source is slowing but still strong.[7][5] Price per test rose mostly because the billing team won more payments from insurers, and management calls that gain sustained (about 3 percent excluding collections). Only about $4.5 million of the quarter was one-time prior-period collections that will not repeat.[31] The third source, new products, is real but small until at least 2027. The risk to this picture is that fewer tests are ordered in low-risk prostate cancer, a group where doctors still ask for more proof; management cut its full-year Decipher volume outlook by about 1,000 tests on 2026-07-30 for that reason.[31]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Laboratory developed test (LDT): a test designed and run inside one lab, not sold as a kit. Veracyte's U.S. tests are LDTs.
  • MolDX: a Medicare program run by Palmetto GBA that decides whether Medicare covers a genetic test.
  • PAMA and the Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule: the 2014 law that makes Medicare pay for lab tests at the median price that private insurers pay. The schedule is reset every few years.
  • Average selling price (ASP): testing revenue divided by tests reported.
  • Prior period collections: money collected in a quarter for tests reported in earlier quarters. It lifts the quarter's price per test but does not repeat.
  • Minimal residual disease (MRD): tiny traces of cancer that remain after treatment, found with a blood or urine test.
  • Adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization): the company's own profit measure that adds back interest, taxes, depreciation, share-based pay and some one-off items.
  • Net operating loss (NOL): past losses that a company can use to reduce its future tax bill.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest part of the case is the evidence behind Decipher. The 2025 annual report says Decipher Prostate is the only gene-based test in the prostate cancer guidelines of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN, the group whose guidelines most U.S. doctors follow), backed by more than 115 published studies, and covered by Medicare and insurers that represent more than 215 million members.[1] A new analysis from the STAMPEDE trial that shows Decipher can predict who benefits from adding the drug abiraterone was released on 2026-10-23, and the company says earlier STAMPEDE work has been folded into the NCCN 2027 guidelines.[30] Insurance coverage tends to follow guideline inclusion, so each new study is not just science: it widens the group of patients for whom the test is paid.

Afirma has a simple medical logic. When a thyroid biopsy is unclear, doctors used to remove part of the thyroid, even though 70 to 80 percent of those nodules turned out to be harmless. Afirma reads the genes in the sample to pick out the harmless ones so that those patients avoid surgery.[1] Afirma revenue grew 18 percent in the second quarter of 2026, and a new version of the lab process (the v2 transcriptome) cut the share of samples that gave no result, adding about four points to volume growth.[5][31] The company is also suing Sonic Healthcare USA over three patents tied to the rival ThyroSeq test, which shows it is willing to defend the franchise, although the outcome is not yet known.[4]

The competitors are credible. Decipher faces Myriad Genetics and MDxHealth in prostate cancer, and the annual report also names image-based artificial intelligence tools such as ArteraAI as possible future rivals. In bladder cancer MRD, the company names Natera as its main competitor.[1] Natera is far larger: it reported $752.8 million of revenue in the second quarter of 2026 against Veracyte's $150.3 million and processed about 296,700 cancer tests in that quarter, though it lost $67.0 million.[24] Veracyte's total was about 51,000 tests.[5] Size gives Natera a large sales force and data set, so Veracyte's TrueMRD launch is an entry into a crowded and well-funded field, not a clear-field opportunity.

The structural weakness is reimbursement. Medicare is about a third of revenue, so a lower Medicare price would reach the profit line almost one for one, because the lab's costs do not fall when the price does. Under PAMA, Medicare's new rates are set from a 2025 survey of private-insurer prices and can start on 2027-01-01.[1] CMS published preliminary 2027 rates on 2026-09-21. For Decipher Prostate (code 81542) the preliminary figure equals the current $3,873, and for Afirma (code 81546) it equals the current $3,600; for the Prosigna code the preliminary rate rises 3.8 percent.[19] The final rates are due in November 2026, and by law no test's price may fall more than 15 percent in a year.[18] We treat this as good news that is not yet final.

How the moat compares with the peer set

The peer table in Chapter 3 shows the pattern. Castle Biosciences, which sells skin cancer and esophagus tests, has a 75 percent gross margin but reported a small net loss of $2.1 million.[23] Natera and Guardant grow faster (38 and 44 percent) but both lose money, and Guardant burned $69.5 million of free cash in the quarter. Myriad's revenue fell 11 percent and it suspended its profit guidance after what it called payer friction.[24][25][26] Veracyte and Castle are the two that pair double-digit growth with positive operating cash flow (Castle's was $15.2 million), and Veracyte's adjusted EBITDA margin of 29.2 percent is more than twice Castle's 12.0 percent, which is the best sign that its moat is real. The Medicare payment table adds a hint of how that moat is priced: Decipher keeps its $3,873 rate in the preliminary 2027 schedule while the rate for a 70-gene breast test is preliminarily cut by 20.6 percent before the 15 percent cap applies.[19][36]

Management quality is average to good. The board has eight independent directors out of nine, and 97.3 percent of shareholders backed the 2025 pay plan.[15] The annual bonus is tied to testing revenue and adjusted EBITDA margin, and the company says those targets were achieved at 118 percent for 2025.[15] One governance mark against it: in June 2026, 21.1 million votes opposed adding 3.5 million shares to the employee equity plan, which passed with 52.1 million votes in favor.[16] That is roughly 29 percent of the votes cast for or against, and it points to the dilution issue we test in Chapter 3.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

On forecasting, management has a clear record of promising less than it delivers. For 2025 it first guided testing revenue of $470 to $480 million and an adjusted EBITDA margin of about 21.6 percent.[12] The margin outlook was lifted in May, August and November.[11][10][9] It then raised part of the outlook at each later report, and the year closed at $493.2 million of testing revenue, 2.7 percent above the top of the first range, with a 27.6 percent margin, six points higher than first promised.[7] The preliminary figures given on 2026-01-11 ($515 to $517 million) matched the final $517.1 million.[8][7] For investors this record matters in two ways: it lowers the chance of a nasty surprise in the numbers, and it means guidance is a floor more than a midpoint.

On capital allocation, the record is mixed. The 2021 purchase of Decipher Biosciences for $574.4 million in cash and about $147 million of stock is now the main profit engine, with $310.7 million of 2025 revenue.[2][7] The 2021 purchase of HalioDx, a French drug-services business, for $162.4 million in cash was less successful: the company wrote down $68.3 million in 2023, mainly HalioDx-related intangibles and a license, and in 2025 its French subsidiary went through a restructuring that brought a $20.5 million write-down and a $6.7 million loss when the unit stopped being consolidated.[2][1][5] The new $150 million Convergent deal follows the same pattern of buying technology before it has revenue. The company has no debt and no share buyback, and the CEO said on the second-quarter call that investing in the business and acquisitions come before returning cash.[31][4]

Share issuance is the other thing to watch. Shares outstanding rose from 71.1 million at the end of 2021 to 79.4 million at the end of 2025, and to 80.4 million on 2026-07-27.[3][1][4] Staff stock awards cost $43.6 million in 2025, and in June 2026 shareholders approved 3.5 million more shares for the equity plan, with 21.1 million votes against.[1][16] Insiders are also selling small amounts: two directors filed notices on 2026-10-27 to sell 14,850 and 7,500 shares, together about 0.03 percent of the company, and another director's 2026-10-19 sale was made under a pre-arranged plan.[20][21][22] None of this is alarming alone, but it is why the stock-based pay test in Chapter 3 is a real test and not a formality. Management also changed in March 2026, when the company hired Kevin Haas, who had been chief technology officer at the rival Myriad Genetics, as its chief development and technology officer.[17]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate rests on one assumption that, if wrong, breaks it. This chapter names the weakest one for each estimate, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when we will see that evidence. It then sets out three ways the investment could fail badly.

Failure 1: Decipher growth fades faster than the guidance implies

The base case needs Decipher, 60 percent of revenue, to keep growing near 17 to 20 percent. The management comment on 2026-07-30 shows where the cracks might be: it cut its full-year volume outlook by about 1,000 tests, mostly in low-risk prostate cancer, and it said the 2025 NCCN update showed that doctors need more evidence before using genomic tests in low-risk patients.[31] The new evidence that could fix that has 'initial readouts as early as 2027'.[31] If low-risk growth stalls in 2026, Decipher volume growth could drop from 17 percent to the low teens, and price gains cannot keep making up the difference because part of the second-quarter price gain was one-time collections.[31]

What the first sign would look like: Decipher test volume in the third quarter growing at less than 15 percent. A year earlier the lab ran about 26,700 Decipher tests, so 15 percent is about 30,700 tests and 12 percent is about 29,900.[9] Our ladder for Decipher volume is one rule, used in the primer and in Chapters 6 and 7. The Layer 2 scorecard keeps its own, separate scale for total testing volume. At 15 percent or more the thesis is intact. From 12 to 15 percent we make no new purchase and re-underwrite in full. Below 12 percent we sell. A second trim of the full-year volume outlook counts the same as a reading below 15 percent. At 12 percent volume growth and no price gain, revenue growth falls to the low double digits, the margin stops expanding and the 25 times multiple is hard to defend.

Failure 2: Medicare changes the price

Medicare pays about a third of revenue, and it sets the price from a survey of what private insurers paid in the first half of 2025. The preliminary 2027 figures leave Decipher at $3,873 and Afirma at $3,600, but CMS calls them preliminary, the comment period was 30 days, and final rates are due in November 2026.[19][18] The average preliminary change across all codes is about 16 percent lower, and the cap that limits any one test to a 15 percent cut a year applies to 2027 through 2029.[18] The risk is not that Decipher's price falls in 2027, but that later survey rounds or a change to how the schedule treats these tests cut it in 2028 or 2029, after the point when the market has stopped watching.

A second payer risk is concentration. UnitedHealthcare is 13 percent of revenue, and a change to its coverage policy would be about as damaging as a Medicare cut.[4] Myriad's second quarter shows how fast this can go wrong: revenue per test fell 9 percent and the company suspended its profit guidance.[26]

Failure 3: a gap on the earnings report, and earnings that look better than they are

The shares have moved between 8 and 28 percent after each of the last four reports, so a position held through the print risks a gap that the stop cannot stop. A fall of 22.5 percent from a $44.00 entry would leave the stock at about $34.10, which is $5.90 below the $40.00 stop.[35] The stop is therefore not a cap on the loss. The position-sizing rule in Chapter 7 takes this into account by starting at one percent of the portfolio.

The quality of earnings also needs watching. Accounting profit gets a boost from the low tax rate and from prior-period collections; non-GAAP EPS excludes $48.5 million of stock pay; and a future release of the tax valuation allowance could create a large one-time gain that makes the stock look cheaper than it is.[1][31][5] A fourth issue is acquisition spending. The company has just paid $150 million for a test that cannot launch until late 2028, and write-downs tied to its 2021 HalioDx purchase and the French operations came to $88.9 million across 2023 and 2025.[14][2][1][5]

7. What we would do, and when

The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT. The rule is the portfolio's routing rule. A Stage 2 stock that is more than 1.6 average true ranges above support is not bought at market. Veracyte is 2.05 ranges above its 20-day average and 4.93 above the ledger's base-low anchor. At the $50.28 close the reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is only 0.63 to 1. The plan is a limit order, which fills only at your price or better. It sits in the entry zone from $43.25 to $44.00. The top of the zone is just above the 50-day average of $43.86, and the bottom is a quarter of an average true range below it. We set the top at $44.00 for a reason. With the $56.75 base target and the $40.00 stop it gives 3.19 to 1. With 2028 margin one point lower, so a $54.81 target, it still gives 2.70 to 1. The lowest target that gives 2.5 to 1 at $44.00 is $54.00. If the stock does not fall to the zone, there is no trade.

This setup is fragile and we say how. If 2028 EPS is $0.20 lower, the value is $51.75 and the ratio at $44.00 is 1.94 to 1, below the floor. At a 20 times multiple it is 0.35 to 1. If the market quotes full-tax EPS and keeps 25 times, it is 0.58 to 1. The order is therefore a conditional setup, not a standing purchase authority. Before any entry after the 2026-11-04 report, we re-run the work. That means fresh 2028 EPS, a fresh multiple, a fresh stop from the new swing low and average true range, and a fresh zone. A new entry needs 2.5 to 1 at the new zone high, and it must still pass after a one-point margin shortfall. If it fails either test, we wait.

The earnings date shapes the order, and it is not live today. A fill just before the 2026-11-04 report would carry a gap like July's 22.5 percent drop. So we allow a pre-report starter only after the pullback has held. That means a close between $43.25 and $44.00, so the 50-day average was tested and not broken, on volume below the 20-day average, with a fresh run of the gate and the valuation check that still gives 2.5 to 1. If any of these is missing, there is no order until the post-report re-run. A starter is 1.0 percent of the portfolio, which is half of the 2.0 percent cap for this tier. A 22.5 percent gap from $44.00 would cost about 0.23 percent of the portfolio at that size. That is inside the 35-basis-point risk budget for the tier. It is a calculation, not a cap: a gap can open below the stop, and the stop does not guarantee the $4.00 of risk.[35][38] After the report we re-underwrite, as described above, and the position can grow to the full 2.0 percent only if the re-run passes.

Why not buy at $50.28, and why the small tier? The margin of safety is thin, and two binary events fall in the next few weeks. The base target of $56.75 is only 12.9 percent above the close. The stop of $40.00 is 20.4 percent below it. The three scenarios average $55.99. That is a good business at a fair price, not a mispriced one. The business passes every hard line, and Layer 2 scores 3.5 of 5.0, so it is worth waiting for. But the entry cushion is narrow, the tax treatment of future EPS is unresolved, and the chart fails all three momentum tests. That is why we use TIER_4, which carries a 35-basis-point risk budget, instead of TIER_3. The stop sits 9.1 percent below $44.00. That gives a raw weight of 3.8 percent, which the tier cap lowers to 2.0 percent. Once held, the let-winners-run rule applies. There is no mechanical trim for a high RSI. The stop trails under the rising 21-day or 50-day average. An appreciated winner may reach 22 percent of the portfolio.

Its stop ignores the 20-day swing low of $41.22, which the tactical rule requires us to respect. Its target is the 52-week high, not a value built from earnings. At the top of the seed band, $46.51, the reward-to-risk against our target and our stop is only 1.57 to 1. That is below the 2.5 floor. So we keep the idea but move the zone down to the 50-day average.
