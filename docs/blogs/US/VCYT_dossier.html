Before you read

Veracyte runs laboratories that read the genes in a cancer sample and report a score that helps doctors choose treatment[6]. It is profitable, holds more cash than it owes, and grew revenue 16% in 2025[15][20]. Our advice is to wait: at $50.28 the price already assumes steady growth, and a margin of safety appears only near $41.25 to $42.00, and only if pay in shares is not counted as a cost (on the stricter view the ratio at $42.00 is -0.61:1)[1][34]. We would be wrong if the November 4 results and the November Medicare fee decision show strong growth and no fee cut, because the shares could rise without a pullback[31][27]. We would also be wrong if Medicare cuts test fees by the legal maximum of 15% a year[27].

1. What the company does

Veracyte sells information, not products. A doctor who has taken a tissue sample from a patient with prostate, thyroid, breast or bladder cancer sends it to one of Veracyte's certified laboratories in California or Texas. The laboratory measures which genes are switched on in the sample and returns a score that helps the doctor choose treatment, for example whether a man with prostate cancer needs hormone therapy on top of radiation, or whether a thyroid nodule that looks uncertain under the microscope is almost certainly harmless.[6] The payer is not the doctor. Medicare, private health insurers and patients' health plans pay Veracyte for each reported result.[7]

The money comes mostly from two tests. In the second quarter of 2026 the company reported $150.3 million of revenue, of which $145.7 million (97%) was testing. Decipher Prostate earned $91.9 million on about 29,700 tests and Afirma, the thyroid test, earned $51.2 million on about 18,600 tests.[13] The average payment per test was $3,010, which includes $4.5 million collected for tests reported in earlier periods; without that catch-up it was about $2,900.[21] Volume is therefore only half of the story. The price an insurer actually pays per test, and how quickly the laboratory collects it, decide how much revenue a test volume turns into.

Who pays matters as much as the list price. In 2025 traditional Medicare paid for 34% of testing revenue, Medicare Advantage plans (run by private insurers) for 18%, private insurers for 47% and Medicaid for 1%.[7] Medicare does not set these prices by negotiation. Under a 2014 law called the Protecting Access to Medicare Act, or PAMA, it sets each rate from the median of what private insurers pay, and a new round of rates is scheduled to apply from January 1, 2027 to December 31, 2029.[7] Medicare's final 2027 fee schedule is due in November 2026, which makes it a dated event for this company, discussed in Chapter 6.[27]

Three newer products are the growth plan. Prosigna, a breast cancer recurrence test, went on sale as a laboratory-run test in June 2026. TrueMRD, a blood test that looks for traces of bladder cancer after surgery, launched in the same quarter. On September 14, 2026 the company paid $150 million in cash for Convergent Genomics, whose urine test is aimed at earlier-stage bladder cancer and is not expected to reach the market until late 2028.[13][22] Management left Prosigna and TrueMRD out of its 2026 revenue forecast, so any sales from them would be extra.[21] The company employs 755 people and, as of June 30, 2026, listed no borrowings among its liabilities.[9][13]

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest part of the competitive position is evidence. Veracyte's tests are used because published trials show they change treatment, and treatment guidelines and insurers follow trials. By the company's account, Decipher Prostate is the only genomic test used across the full course of prostate cancer. It is backed by more than 115 peer-reviewed studies. It is also the only gene expression test listed in the 2026 prostate cancer treatment guidelines of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN).[6] Medicare and commercial insurers covering more than 215 million people pay for it.[6] New evidence keeps arriving. On October 23, 2026 the company reported a trial analysis showing that Decipher can predict who benefits from adding abiraterone to hormone therapy in very high-risk disease. It said earlier analysis from the same trial is already in the 2027 guideline for high-risk prostate cancer.[30] Evidence, guideline listing and coverage reinforce each other. A rival needs all three, and that takes years of trials to build.

The result shows in the growth rate. Decipher revenue was $310.7 million in 2025, up 27%, on about 102,000 tests, also up 27%. In the second quarter of 2026 it grew 20% on 17% more tests.[15][13][5] Afirma is a smaller and steadier franchise. About 650,000 US patients a year have a thyroid needle biopsy. Between 70% and 80% of the nodules that look uncertain under the microscope turn out to be benign. A test that can say "benign" lets the patient skip surgery.[6] Afirma revenue was $172.9 million in 2025, up 9%. It then grew 18% in the second quarter of 2026 on 10% more tests.[15][13] Two helpers lifted that quarter: a lower share of samples returning no result, and collections of earlier-period payments.[21] Management has told investors that these helpers make 2027 a harder comparison for Afirma.[21]

Competition differs by test. In prostate the named rivals are Myriad Genetics and MDxHealth. The 10-K also flags artificial-intelligence pathology companies such as ArteraAI as possible entrants. On the second-quarter call, asked about AI-based testing, management said it still thinks molecular and AI testing are complementary and that it is not really seeing any difference in share.[8][21] In thyroid the rival is ThyroSeq, sold by Sonic Healthcare. Veracyte has sued Sonic for patent infringement, and the jury trial is set for the first quarter of 2027. On July 21, 2026 the patent office granted a re-examination of one of the five asserted patents, so the case can cut either way.[20] In breast cancer the 10-K states that Exact Sciences holds a substantial majority of the market.[8] An analyst on the call asked what share of the existing Oncotype market Prosigna could contest.[21] Prosigna therefore starts with a small share of a market that the 10-K says Exact Sciences dominates. In bladder-cancer blood monitoring the 10-K names Natera as the main rival.[8] Natera reported $752.8 million of revenue in the quarter in which Veracyte reported $150.3 million, and ran 57.2% more cancer tests than a year earlier.[42][13]

The weaknesses are concentration and the payer. Decipher and Afirma are 95% of revenue, so a setback in either is felt at once.[13] Traditional Medicare pays 34% of testing revenue directly under its fee schedule. Medicare Advantage plans add 18%. The PAMA reset therefore matters more than any single competitor.[7] The preliminary 2027 schedule from CMS (the Medicare agency) was posted on September 21, 2026. It cuts the average test rate by about 16%. Molecular pathology codes are down 22%, genomic sequencing codes are down 23%, and the proprietary laboratory analysis group is down 2.4%. A legal cap limits any single test to a 15% cut a year from 2027 to 2029. Final rates are due in November.[7][27] The sources we fetched do not say which group each Veracyte test falls in. The effect on Veracyte is therefore unknown until the final table is published. The company's own acquisition record is a third weakness. It recorded impairment charges of $68.3 million in 2023 and $20.5 million in 2025 on acquired businesses, about $89 million in all. The $150 million Convergent purchase deserves scepticism until its product ships.[2][22]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

The second half of Layer 2 asks a plain question: did management do what it said it would do? On operating guidance the record is very good. In February 2025 the company guided to 2025 testing revenue of $470 million to $480 million and an adjusted EBITDA margin of about 21.6%; it delivered $493.2 million and 27.6%.[19][15] It raised parts of its outlook at every report after February 2025 and ended above the top of its final range for total revenue ($506 million to $510 million against $517.1 million actual).[17][15] In 2026 it began with a range of $570 million to $582 million and 25%, raised it in May and again in July to $590 million to $596 million and above 26%, and has so far earned 30.0% in the first half.[16][14][13]

The record is less clean on two other promises. First, a cut of about 1,000 tests to the 2026 Decipher low-risk volume expectation, announced on the July call, was followed by a 22.5% one-day share price fall, so the market treats volume misses harshly even when revenue guidance rises.[21][39] Second, capital allocation: the company paid $574.4 million in cash for Decipher Biosciences and $162.4 million for HalioDx in 2021, then recorded impairment charges of $68.3 million in 2023 and $20.5 million in 2025 on acquired businesses.[11][2] The $150 million Convergent purchase repeats the pattern of paying cash for a platform whose first test will not be sold until late 2028, and no purchases of its own shares appear in the 2023 to 2025 cash flow statements.[22][4] On pay, 97.3% of votes supported the 2025 executive pay plan and about 97% (71.3 million for, 1.9 million against) did so at the June 2026 meeting, and the annual incentive metrics, testing revenue and adjusted EBITDA margin, were achieved at 118%, which is consistent with the delivered results.[25][24]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue has grown from $219.5 million in 2021 to $517.1 million in 2025, a rate of about 24% a year over four years and 20% a year over the last three (from $296.5 million in 2022).[11][2] That growth came with large losses until 2023, because 2021 was the year the company bought Decipher Biosciences and HalioDx for $736.8 million in cash and then had to pay for the people and laboratories that came with them.[11] Operating income turned from a loss of $85.8 million in 2023 to a profit of $16.1 million in 2024 and $57.8 million in 2025.[2]

Reported profit needs two corrections. GAAP net income of $66.4 million in 2025 includes a $15.3 million gain from lowering the estimated cost of acquisition earn-outs, and the first half of 2026 includes a $4.2 million settlement of escrow claims.[4][20] The cleaner measure is adjusted EBITDA, which grew from $45.4 million (12.6% of revenue) in 2023 to $91.9 million (20.6%) in 2024 and $142.5 million (27.6%) in 2025, but which excludes $43.6 million of pay in shares in 2025.[19][15][4] We show both and lean on cash.

Cash is the strongest part of the record. Cash flow from operations rose from $44.2 million in 2023 to $75.1 million in 2024 and $136.3 million in 2025; after $9.7 million of equipment spending, free cash flow was $126.6 million, a 24.5% margin.[4] Over the three years 2023 to 2025 the company generated $255.6 million of operating cash against $279.8 million of adjusted EBITDA, a 91% conversion.[4][19][15] Over the twelve months to June 30, 2026 free cash flow was $166.0 million, a 29.5% margin.[34] The company holds no borrowings, so there is no interest bill to cover and no debt to refinance.[13]

The balance sheet carries one risk: purchased intangibles. At June 30, 2026 goodwill of $767.2 million and acquired intangible assets of $82.7 million together equal $849.9 million, or 57% of the $1,486.4 million of total assets.[13] Goodwill is not cash. If a purchased business disappoints, the company writes it down, which is what happened in 2023 and 2025. After the $150 million Convergent payment, cash and short-term investments of $485.2 million would fall to about $335.2 million, before counting the cash generated since June 30.[13][23]

5. What could happen next

A share price is a bet on the future, and it is possible to run the bet backwards. Given today's price and today's cash flow, a calculation called a reverse discounted cash flow, or reverse DCF, finds the yearly growth rate that the price already assumes for the next ten years. It uses a 10% required return and a final-year value of 18 times that year's free cash flow. The question it answers is not whether the company is good. It asks whether the price leaves room for a good outcome to be rewarded.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on one weakest assumption. The evidence that would show it wrong arrives on known dates. For the reverse DCF the weakest assumption is the starting cash flow. The $166.0 million includes $4.5 million of catch-up collections in the second quarter alone and a tax charge of only 1.3%. It also adds back $48.5 million of share pay. It is measured before the $150 million Convergent payment and before that business's running costs. The Q3 report on November 4, 2026 is the first test of it.

For the twelve-month value the weakest assumption is ten years of 10% growth. That is a claim that Decipher growth fades gently and not abruptly. The evidence is Decipher volume against the forecast of 31,000 tests in the third quarter and 33,000 in the fourth. It is also the November 2026 final Medicare fee table and the first 2027 outlook, expected with fourth-quarter results in February 2027. The second weakest assumption is the treatment of share pay. Our values use the company-standard lens, which leaves share pay out of cash flow. The owner lens, which charges it, gives a value of about $40, and that changes the answer. For the probabilities the weakest assumption is that a 30% bear weight is enough. The stock moved +24.9% and -22.5% on its last two reports. For return on invested capital the weakest assumption is the tax rate. The evidence is a possible release of part of the tax reserve, which management says could come within 12 months of the second-quarter filing.

Fatal flaw 1: Medicare cuts the price of the core tests

The legal cap allows Medicare to cut a test's rate by up to 15% a year from 2027 to 2029. The September 21 preliminary schedule showed an average cut of about 16%. Genomic sequencing codes were down 23% and molecular pathology codes down 22%. The proprietary laboratory analysis group was down only 2.4%.[27] Traditional Medicare paid 34% of testing revenue in 2025.[7] Apply a 15% cut to 34% of the $579 million midpoint of the 2026 testing revenue forecast. That removes about $29.5 million of revenue, or 17.5% of twelve-month adjusted EBITDA of $168.8 million. Nearly all of it would fall to profit, because the laboratory cost per test does not fall.[13] After the 21% tax charge in our base cash flow, it lowers the base cash flow from $143.2 million to $119.9 million. The stress table shows that a one-year cut of this size leaves the company-standard value at about $49 and the zone-top ratio at 2.32:1, still above the floor. A cut repeated for the full three years the law allows would be larger, and we do not model it. This is an upper bound for one year. It assumes both main tests are cut at the cap. The sources we fetched do not show the preliminary rates for Veracyte's own codes, so the real effect is unknown until the final table in November. The same schedule carries a warning. Prosigna's older kit-based code fell from $3,443 to $900 under the first PAMA round. The final price of the new Prosigna laboratory test is therefore not secure, even though management guided to a low end of about $2,500.[28][21]

Fatal flaw 2: Decipher growth fades faster than price gains can offset

Decipher is 61% of revenue. Its 2026 revenue growth of about 20% rests on 17% volume growth plus price gains. Management has already cut its low-risk volume expectation by about 1,000 tests.[21][13] Low-risk prostate cancer is only about 20% penetrated and growing in single digits. The reason is that the 2025 guideline update took active surveillance testing out of the guidelines for every test on the market. Management says new evidence could restore it, but the earliest readouts are in 2027 and 2028 and may not be positive.[21] The company also faces artificial-intelligence pathology entrants named in its 10-K.[8] Suppose Decipher grew 10% instead of about 15% in 2027, from a base of about $373 million. Revenue would be roughly $18.6 million lower. That is close to 11% of twelve-month adjusted EBITDA if it all fell to profit.[13] The price gains that have offset volume are partly one-off. Of the second-quarter average price, $4.5 million came from earlier-period collections.[21]

Fatal flaw 3: the new tests cost more than they earn, and the acquisition record repeats

Launch spending is rising while launch revenue is excluded from the forecast. The Prosigna sales team grows from about 10 to 15 people. Research and development expense in the second quarter was $29.4 million against $16.3 million a year earlier. Part of that increase is a reclassification of costs from administration. Management also said Prosigna volume would be a slight gross margin headwind in the second half, because revenue is recognised only as cash is collected.[21][13] Convergent adds running costs. The company says they were included in its 2026 adjusted EBITDA forecast of more than 26%. Its first test is not expected to be sold until late 2028.[22] The company has recorded about $89 million of impairment charges across 2023 and 2025.[2] Goodwill and acquired intangibles are $849.9 million, or 57% of assets.[13] Event risk sits on top. Beta is 2.0 and 12.4% of the float is sold short. A disappointing report can move the stock more than 20% in a day, as it did on July 31.[39]

Position size follows the risk-unit rule. The weight is the lesser of the tier maximum and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. The tiers run from TIER_1 (7% to 10%, 100 basis points of risk) through TIER_2 (4% to 6.5%, 75), TIER_3 (2% to 3.5%, 50) and TIER_4 (1% to 2%, 35) to TIER_0 for rejects. Once a position is held, the let-winners-run rule applies. We do not trim only because the relative strength index passes 70 or the weight passes 15%. We raise the stop behind the rising 21-day exponential average or the 50-day moving average. We let the weight grow toward the 22% ceiling while the trend and the fundamentals hold.
