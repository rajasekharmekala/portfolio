Molina Healthcare runs health plans paid for by state and federal governments, and it covers about 4.9 million people.[1][4] We reject the stock at $209.15 for now, because our central value of $177.60 is below the price and trailing interest coverage is under our 2.0 times red line.[21][1][4] The business may be turning: management calls 2026 the low point, draft January rates average about 7 percent, and 2027 earnings per share (EPS) could top $9.50 against at least $5.25 for 2026.[3][1] We are wrong if Medicaid margins recover faster than we assume, since each point of medical care ratio is worth about $5 a share.[3] We would look again near $178.00 to $192.90 only if our central value first rises above about $242, or after the February 2027 results (a date we estimate).

1. What the company does

Molina Healthcare, Inc. is a health insurer for people who get coverage through government programs. It runs plans under Medicaid (state programs for low-income families and for people with disabilities), Medicare (federal coverage, where Molina focuses on people who qualify for both programs) and the Marketplace (the state exchanges created by the Affordable Care Act, where most buyers receive federal subsidies). At 30 September 2026 it served about 4.9 million members: 4,372,000 in Medicaid, 222,000 in Medicare and 267,000 in Marketplace.[1] A year earlier it served 5,628,000, so membership has fallen by about 14 percent.[1] At the end of 2025 it operated in 21 states.[4]

The customers are state Medicaid agencies and the federal government.[4] They pay a fixed amount each month for every enrolled member, which is called a capitation payment, and Molina then pays that member's doctors, hospitals, pharmacies and care-home bills. The company says it typically receives the payment monthly, in advance of paying the claims.[2] That timing explains why Molina can hold about $8.6 billion of cash and investments (cash of $4,479 million plus investments of $4,100 million at 30 September 2026) while earning very little profit, and why most of that money is not free to spend: it backs unpaid claims of $4,988 million and the capital that state regulators require.[1]

The key number in this business is the medical care ratio (MCR), which is the share of premium revenue paid out as medical costs. In the first nine months of 2026 premium revenue was $30,873 million and medical care costs were $28,429 million, so $92.08 of every $100 of premium went to care.[1] The rest, about $7.92, had to cover administrative cost, interest and tax and leave a profit. On guided 2026 premium of about $42 billion, one point of MCR is about $420 million of pre-tax profit (our calculation).[1] That is why small changes in medical cost move earnings so much: the 2026 adjusted EPS guide of at least $5.25 is small next to $42 billion of annual revenue.[1]

Margins depend on two growth rates: the rate update states grant and the growth in cost per member, called medical cost trend. States set rates from past claims data, so rates lag cost. When trend runs ahead of rates for several years, the MCR rises. Management gave Medicaid trend as 6.5 percent in 2024, 7.5 percent in 2025 and 5.5 percent in 2026, and its 2026 view is a rate increase of 4.5 percent against that 5.5 percent trend.[3] Management says this imbalance between rates and trend is what squeezed Medicaid margins, while consolidated profit fell from $1,179 million in 2024 to $472 million in 2025 and to a trailing twelve-month net loss of $48 million (our calculation from filed figures).[3][4][1]

Contracts, wins and acquisitions

Growth comes from winning state contracts and buying plans. The Florida children's medical services contract started on 1 October 2026 and management says it took on 115,000 members at a run rate of $6 billion of revenue.[3] Two big awards are late: the Georgia contract was meant to start on 1 July 2025, and both it and the Texas STAR and CHIP contract are now expected in late 2027 or in 2028 because court cases continue.[2] Illinois awarded Molina a Medicaid managed care contract in June 2026 with a planned start of 1 January 2027.[2][17]

Two acquisitions matter. Bright Health Medicare in California closed on 1 January 2024 and added about 109,000 members, and Molina paid $295 million in cash for it in 2024.[5][4] ConnectiCare in Connecticut closed on 1 February 2025 for $350 million in cash and added about 140,000 members across Marketplace, Medicare and some commercial products.[4] In its 2025 report the company said ConnectiCare supplied 4 percent of revenue and 1 percent of net income, and that higher MCRs on that business pushed up the 2025 Marketplace MCR.[4] The integration is therefore still a small contributor and not yet a profit driver.

Three product decisions shape 2027. Molina will stop selling its traditional Medicare Advantage prescription drug product (MAPD, about $1 billion of annual premium), which lost about $1.00 a share in 2026, and it will focus Medicare on about $5 billion of dual-eligible plans.[8][1] It booked a $93 million non-cash impairment on the intangible assets tied to that product.[15] It is also cutting Marketplace to seven states for 2027, which management expects to remove about $1 billion of Marketplace premium.[3][2]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Capitation: a fixed monthly payment per member from a state or federal payer, out of which Molina pays all of that member's medical bills.[2]
  • Medical cost trend: the year-over-year growth in medical cost per member, from higher use of care and higher prices.
  • Rate update: a change in the capitation amount a state pays, set by state actuaries under federal actuarial-soundness rules.
  • Redetermination: a state check that a person still qualifies for Medicaid. Federal law will require checks every six months for most expansion adults from 1 January 2027.[27]
  • Dual-eligible: a person who qualifies for both Medicare and Medicaid, the group Molina is making its Medicare focus.
  • Embedded earnings: management's name for profit it expects from contracts already won and acquisitions already made, which excludes any recovery in margins on its older business.[3]
  • Days in claims payable: a measure of how many days of medical costs are held as unpaid-claims reserves. It was 44 at 30 September 2026.[1]
  • Statutory capital: the minimum capital state regulators require each health plan to hold, about $3.0 billion across Molina's plans at 30 September 2026.[2]
  • Parent company cash: cash and investments held by the holding company outside the regulated plans. It was $608 million at 30 September 2026.[2]
  • Reverse DCF: a calculation that starts from today's price and solves for the annual growth rate a business must deliver for ten years to justify it.
  • Reward-to-risk is the planned gain to the target divided by the planned loss to the stop, so 2.5 means $2.50 of gain for each $1.00 of risk.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest part of the business is how it wins and keeps contracts. In its 2025 report Molina said it had a 90 percent win rate when its Medicaid contracts went out for re-bid, worth $14 billion of retained revenue, and an 80 percent win rate on new contracts, worth $20 billion of premium.[4] State Medicaid contracts usually run three to five years and either side can end them with or without cause, so the record has to be earned again in every cycle.[4] The awards of 2025 and 2026 (Florida children's care, Illinois, and the Georgia and Texas awards still in court) show that the sales side still works.[2]

The second strength is cost. The administrative expense ratio (G&A) was 6.4 percent of revenue in the third quarter of 2026 and about 6.4 percent is guided for the year, and Centene, a large Medicaid peer, reported a selling and administrative ratio of 7.0 percent in its second quarter.[1][3][23] The third strength is reserves: the company released $352 million of earlier-year claim reserves in the first nine months of 2026, against $124 million a year earlier, and days in claims payable were 44.[1] Those are signs that past estimates were cautious, which is what a reader wants from an insurer in a bad cost year.

The weak side is price-taking. States set Medicaid rates, so Molina cannot raise prices when costs rise; it can only ask, and states often answer after the loss has happened. Management itself says most of its states are slightly underfunded.[3] Margins are thin: the pre-tax margin was 0.5 percent in the third quarter of 2026 and 1.1 percent adjusted for the nine months.[1] Medicaid is about 75 percent of premium, so one program and one set of buyers carries most of the company.[4] The balance sheet carries $3,771 million of senior notes rated BB- by S&P and Ba2 by Moody's, which are below investment grade.[2]

Evidence from 2025 shows how fast the picture can change. In February 2025 the company gave guidance, which is its own published forecast, of adjusted EPS of at least $24.50. The year ended at $11.03. The fourth quarter alone was an adjusted loss of $2.75 a share, which included about $2.00 of unfavorable retroactive premium items in California Medicaid.[14][8] Medicare and Marketplace added to the damage, and Marketplace cost was far above the risk adjustment revenue received for it.[11][8] A business that mispriced two of its three segments in one year does not have a durable pricing edge today; it has a good contracting machine that is currently underpaid.

Policy, subsidy and rate risks

Federal law passed in July 2025 (called OBBBA in company filings) requires Medicaid expansion adults to show 80 hours a month of work, study or volunteering from 1 January 2027, and covers roughly 18.5 million enrollees according to KFF Health News.[28][2] The same law requires eligibility checks every six months for most expansion adults from renewals on or after 1 January 2027.[27] Molina's filing expects the enrollment loss to cut premium by 2 to 3 percent a year through 2029, mainly in expansion, and CEO Joe Zubretsky says 80 percent of Molina's states are leaning toward policies that keep people enrolled.[2][3] Management still plans for a 6 percent fall in Medicaid members in 2027.[3]

The law also limits state provider taxes and caps some provider payments from 2028, and Molina says the effect is uncertain and may take 5 to 10 years to show.[2] The enhanced premium tax credits that made Marketplace coverage cheap ran through 2025 and then lapsed.[4] KFF reports that national Marketplace enrollment fell by nearly three million people between 2025 and 2026.[29] Molina's own Marketplace membership fell from 655,000 at the end of 2025 to 267,000 at 30 September 2026, mostly by choice, because it repriced and shrank the book.[1] CMS released its 2026-2027 rate guide in February 2026 for states to use in setting managed care rates, and management says states are adding the high-cost years to the claims data they use and, in six states, adding explicit allowances for work-requirement effects.[30][3]

3. The numbers, in plain words

Revenue growth is the steady part of this record. Total revenue rose from $27,771 million in 2021 to $45,426 million in 2025, which is 13.1 percent a year over four years and 12.4 percent a year over the three years from 2022 to 2025.[6][4] Profit is the unsteady part. Net income reached $1,179 million in 2024, fell to $472 million in 2025 and was a net loss of $48 million over the twelve months to 30 September 2026 (our calculation: nine months of 2026 plus 2025 minus nine months of 2025).[4][1] The three-year compound change in net income from 2022 to 2025 is -15.8 percent a year.

The cause is visible in the medical care ratio. It sat between 88.0 and 89.1 percent from 2021 to 2024, then rose to 91.7 percent in 2025 and 92.7 percent over the latest twelve months (our calculation from filed premium and medical costs).[6][4][1] Because premium is about $42 billion, each point is about $420 million. Premium revenue has also stopped growing: it fell 3.5 percent in the third quarter of 2026 as Marketplace premium dropped 46 percent and Medicaid and Medicare were roughly flat to higher on rate updates.[1]

Cash flow does not follow profit. Cash flow from operations (the cash the business itself generates before financing) was $2,119 million in 2021, $644 million in 2024 and an outflow of $535 million in 2025, which the company links to Medicaid risk corridor settlements, tax payment timing and weaker second-half results.[6][4][8] In the first nine months of 2026 it was an inflow of $629 million against an outflow of $237 million a year earlier, which the company ties to smaller government receivable and payable settlements.[1] Free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operations less capital expenditure, and it never reached 12 percent of revenue in five years (the peak was 7.4 percent in 2021). Our scoring rules mark the FCF margin floor as not applicable to insurers, so we do not fail the name on it. The consequence is that we lean on earnings, return on equity, coverage and statutory capital instead, and we treat the ledger's cash-flow reverse DCF with care (Chapter 5).

The balance sheet is where trailing profit and debt meet. Debt rose from $2,180 million in 2023 to $3,771 million in September 2026 as the company issued $740 million of notes in 2024 and $838 million in 2025 (the $850 million 6.500% notes due 2031 were sold in November 2025), while it also spent $1.0 billion on buybacks in each of 2024 and 2025.[6][4][16][2] Interest expense was $213 million over the latest twelve months against operating income of $172 million (our calculation), which is why the lenders cut the covenant floor to 1.75 times for 2026.[1][4][15]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that could fail. For the reverse DCF it is the choice of base, because trough earnings of $270 million make the required growth look high and normal earnings make it look low. For the $9.00 estimate of 2027 EPS it is that Florida and Marketplace reach breakeven, which management itself calls an early view.[3] For the $12.00 estimate of 2028 EPS it is that legacy Medicaid margin improves by 0.6 point in 2028, after rates and trend were not in balance for three years.[3] This is the main hinge of the central case: without that recovery, FY2028E EPS is $9.00 and the value is $133.20, which is 36.31 percent below the close. For the probability-weighted value it is the three probabilities, which are our judgment.

The bear case, in plain words, is that cost trend and the acuity shift (a sicker mix of members as healthier people leave) rise again while states raise rates too slowly, so the 2026 trough is not the trough. In that case 2028 EPS is about $8.00, the multiple falls to the low end of its range and the value is about $90.00, which is 57 percent below the close.

Fatal flaw 1: trend and acuity shift outrun rates again

Medicaid trend was 6.5 percent in 2024, 7.5 percent in 2025 and 5.5 percent in 2026 on management's figures, and rate updates lagged each year.[3] Management's 2027 view is rates of about 6 percent against trend of 5.5 to 6 percent, which would keep legacy Medicaid MCR flat or slightly better.[3] If trend is 6 percent and rates come in at 5 percent, the legacy MCR rises by about 0.9 point (our calculation), which is worth about $4.50 a share on management's sensitivity, and most of the room between our $9.00 and the $9.50 floor is gone. Work requirements add a second risk, because the members who leave first tend to be healthier, which raises the cost per remaining member; management assumes only 1 point of that for 2027 and expects the effect to be small.[3]

Fatal flaw 2: the new contracts start worse than planned

The Florida children's medical services contract adds about $1.5 billion of revenue in the fourth quarter at a 99 percent MCR and a 5 percent pre-tax loss, and management needs to find about 500 basis points to reach breakeven in 2027, of which 200 are one-time reserves it expects to disappear.[3] Georgia and Texas have already slipped to late 2027 or 2028 and are in court, so the $9 a share of embedded earnings depends on dates the company does not control.[2][3] On $6 billion of annual revenue each point of Florida pre-tax margin is about $0.87 a share (our calculation at the 25.5 percent statutory tax rate), so a 5 percent loss for all of 2027 instead of breakeven would cost about $4.30 a share.

Fatal flaw 3: coverage and capital tighten before earnings recover
