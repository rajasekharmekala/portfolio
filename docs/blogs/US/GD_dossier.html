Chapter 1

General Dynamics Corporation is not a conventional industrial conglomerate. It is a 127-year-old defense and aerospace franchise whose corporate DNA was forged underwater [7]. Its lineage begins in February 1899, when Isaac L. Rice and John Philip Holland founded the Electric Boat Company in Groton, Connecticut, to build the first practical submersible [7]. When the U.S. Navy accepted the USS Holland (SS-1) in 1900, Electric Boat became the core supplier of America's undersea fleet [7]. That sovereign relationship held through two World Wars, the Cold War, and the nuclear deterrence era [7]. Over the early twentieth century, Electric Boat mastered diesel-electric propulsion, hull metallurgy, and undersea weapons integration, creating a specialized industrial base in New England.

In 1952, Electric Boat acquired Canadian aircraft maker Canadair and reincorporated as General Dynamics Corporation [7]. Two years later, it launched the USS Nautilus (SSN-571), the world's first nuclear-powered submarine [7]. That milestone built a technical and regulatory moat in naval nuclear propulsion that rivals still cannot replicate [7]. Under Chairman John Jay Hopkins, GD expanded into a sprawling Cold War prime contractor by acquiring Convair. It built iconic platforms including the Atlas ICBM, the B-58 Hustler, the F-16 Fighting Falcon, and the M1 Abrams tank [7]. By the late 1980s, however, capital was spread too thinly across dozens of programs with uneven returns.

That sprawling conglomerate model nearly broke when the Berlin Wall fell. Facing the post-Cold War "Peace Dividend" and steep budget cuts, CEO William Anders executed a disciplined restructuring. Rather than clinging to subscale lines, Anders divested the tactical aircraft division (including the F-16) to Lockheed Martin in 1993 [7]. He also sold the missile business to Hughes Aircraft and space systems to Martin Marietta [7]. Management returned proceeds via special dividends and buybacks, and kept cash for a focused roll-up. Every new acquisition targeted businesses where General Dynamics held #1 or #2 market share.

Starting in 1995, General Dynamics rebuilt around four high-barrier pillars [7]. It acquired Bath Iron Works (1995) in Maine for DDG-51 destroyers and NASSCO (1998) in San Diego for Navy auxiliary ships [7]. In 1999, it bought Gulfstream Aerospace in Savannah, Georgia, for ultra-long-range business jets [7]. Two decades later, in 2018, CEO Phebe Novakovic led the $9.7 billion debt-funded acquisition of CSRA [9]. Merging CSRA with GDIT created a top-tier federal IT, cloud, and mission-support platform [9]. Today, GD employs 117,000 people and generated $52.55 billion in FY2025 revenue ($42.14 billion in 9M 2026) [1][2][3]. The portfolio balances long-cycle sovereign defense programs against high-margin commercial aviation.

Historical cycles show a clear pattern: earnings and cash flow rarely collapse across all four segments at once. Commercial aerospace and sovereign defense follow different clocks. During the 2008–2013 GFC and sequestration cycle, business-jet demand froze and Pentagon outlays flattened. Yet the 2012 Gulfstream G650 launch and shipyard cost cuts kept returns on equity near 20%+ [9]. During 2020–2023, supply-chain snarls and FAA scrutiny delayed the flagship Gulfstream G700, tying up inventory. FAA certification in March 2024 and G800 certification in April 2025 then triggered a multi-year delivery and margin inflection [6].

Chapter 3

General Dynamics runs two distinct contracting engines under one corporate roof [1]. Across Marine Systems, Combat Systems, and Technologies, the main buyers are the U.S. government and NATO allies. Procurement follows the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR), multi-year appropriations, and Foreign Military Sales (FMS). Per the FY2025 Form 10-K, U.S. government revenue split 51% fixed-price, 44% cost-reimbursement, and 5% time-and-materials [1]. Cost-reimbursement deals (common on early Columbia-class engineering and GDIT tasks) shield against overruns, though margins cap near 7%–9.5% [1]. Fixed-price lots (mature Virginia-class blocks, Abrams/Piranha vehicles, and Gulfstream jets) let GD keep productivity gains—or absorb rework if suppliers slip [1][10].

In Aerospace, Gulfstream sells direct through a factory sales force to corporations, ultra-high-net-worth buyers, and sovereign fleets [6]. That direct model bypasses third-party dealers and captures full retail pricing [6]. Customers wire non-refundable milestone deposits during the 18-to-36-month build cycle. Those deposits finance work-in-process inventory and convert to revenue upon FAA/EASA certification and delivery [1][6]. Afterward, Jet Aviation's global FBO and MRO network captures recurring revenue from inspections, cabin refits, and fuel.

The largest operational friction in GD's unit economics is sub-tier supply-chain synchronization [10]. Electric Boat relies on hundreds of sole-source nuclear suppliers for castings, valves, and propulsion parts. When one valve or hull section arrives late, shipbuilders face costly out-of-sequence work. Workers install surrounding systems and later tear back into compartments, compressing margins from 9%+ toward 7.0%–7.9% [1][10]. In Aerospace, Gulfstream relies on partners like Honeywell for propulsion and avionics. Engine bottlenecks or certification delays temporarily trap cash in inventory ($9,232 million at year-end 2025) [1][6].
