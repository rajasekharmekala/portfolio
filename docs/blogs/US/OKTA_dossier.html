Before you read

Okta sells the software that decides who, or what, is allowed to log in to a company's systems, and it has become a cash-rich, steadily profitable business with about $3.1 billion of yearly sales.[1][10] The stock has risen from $62.66 to $232.13 in a year, and at that price the market is paying for far more than the company's cash flows can support once the cost of paying staff in shares is counted.[24] This report concludes REJECTED: the business passes the cash and debt floors, but it fails the share-pay floor, the capital-return floor and the reward-to-risk floor, and every honest valuation we built lands below today's price. The conclusion would be wrong if growth re-accelerates well beyond the 10 to 11 percent the company guided to, or if share-based pay falls toward the 5 percent floor much faster than the trend shows. Chapter 6 says exactly what to watch and when.

What the company does

Every company that lets employees, partners or customers log in to its software has to answer one question thousands of times a second: is this person really who they claim to be, and are they allowed in? Okta answers that question as a service. A company pays Okta a yearly subscription, and Okta's cloud software handles the login screen, the password-free sign-in options, the second check on a phone, and the list of which person may open which application. Okta calls this identity, and it compares to the front-desk security of a large office building: one desk, one badge system, every door.[6]

The company sells two broad products that share one engine. The workforce product, usually bought by the technology and security teams of large employers, controls how staff reach their tools. The customer identity product, built on the Auth0 platform, is bought by software developers who want to add secure sign-in to their own apps, so the end customer of a bank or a retailer logs in through Okta without ever knowing it.[6] Revenue is almost entirely subscription: $793 million of the $805 million booked in the quarter ended 31 July 2026 was subscription, and only $12 million was professional services, a line the company is deliberately handing to partners.[10][20]

Customers sign contracts, usually for one to three years, and pay mostly up front each year. That is why the company reports contracted but not yet recognised revenue as a headline number, and why cash arrives ahead of the revenue it is later counted as. At 31 July 2026 that contracted backlog stood at $4,858 million, and 5,255 customers each spent more than $100,000 a year.[16] The company reports that about two-thirds of its revenue sits in repeat customers who stay and add seats, which is the engine of the story.

The new element in the pitch is artificial intelligence. Okta's annual report describes a possible long-term role as the independent control point for what it calls non-human identities, meaning the accounts that AI software agents will need in order to act inside company systems.[7] The same filing says adoption is in its early stages. That caution matters, because the share price has moved as if the opportunity were already in the sales figures, and chapter 5 tests that assumption with numbers.

What this means for an owner: you own a profitable, subscription-based security software business whose cash flow is real but whose growth is in the low double digits, so the price you pay matters far more than the story. The terms below are used in the rest of the book. Each is explained once here, in plain words.

ARR (annual recurring revenue) is the yearly value of subscriptions in force. Okta does not report it, so this book uses contracted backlog instead.

RPO (remaining performance obligations) is contracted subscription revenue not yet recognised. It is the closest published measure of what customers have already promised to pay.

NRR (net revenue retention) is how much a group of customers spends this year compared with the same group a year ago, counting upgrades and cancellations. Okta calls it the dollar-based net retention rate. A figure of 110 percent or more is healthy for software.

TAM (total addressable market) is the most the company could sell if every possible buyer bought. It is a ceiling, not a forecast.

SBC (stock-based compensation) is pay given to staff as company shares. It costs the existing owners because their slice of the company shrinks. The gate used in this book wants it below 5 percent of yearly revenue.

dilution is that shrinking slice: new shares issued to staff mean each old share owns less of the company.

FCF (free cash flow) is the cash a company generates from operations minus what it spends on equipment and capitalised software. It is the cash left for owners, before counting the cost of share-based pay.

free cash flow margin is that cash divided by revenue.

cash flow from operations is the cash the day-to-day business produces before any investing.

capital expenditure is money spent on long-lived equipment and software the company builds for itself.

ROIC (return on invested capital) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business. It shows whether the company earns more than its capital costs. The gate wants at least 12 percent.

EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a rough measure of operating earnings before accounting charges.

EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares.

net debt is borrowings minus cash. A negative number means the company holds more cash than debt.

gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of delivering the product, as a share of revenue.

operating margin is profit from running the business, before interest and tax, as a share of revenue.

price-to-earnings is the share price divided by earnings per share; it is how many years of profit you pay for.

guidance is the sales and profit range the company's own management forecasts for the next quarter and year.

buyback is when a company uses cash to purchase its own shares, which reduces the share count.

consensus estimate is the average forecast of the analysts who cover the stock.

working capital is the short-term money tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers.

Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest argument for Okta is its position. It is the largest independent identity vendor, which means it sells to companies that do not want their login system owned by the same vendor that sells their email, cloud or security tools. The annual report names the risk that large technology companies will increasingly offer identity as part of a bundle, and says some competitors have greater financial, technical and sales resources.[6] Independence is therefore both the moat and the exposure: customers who value neutrality stay, and customers who value one-bill convenience can leave.

The financial evidence supports a durable franchise, not a fast-growing one. Dollar-based net retention was 111 percent in the year to January 2024, 107 percent a year later, 106 percent in January 2026 and 107 percent in July 2026.[4][16] That path means existing customers still expand, but by only about seven cents on the dollar a year, below the 110 percent mark the scorecard treats as healthy. Revenue growth has narrowed too, from 17 percent in fiscal 2025 to 12 percent in fiscal 2026 and 11 percent in the latest quarter.[1][10]

Offsetting that, backlog is growing faster than sales. Total contracted backlog rose 17 percent and the part due in the next twelve months rose 14 percent in the July quarter, up from 12 percent in the April quarter.[18][21] That is an early sign that new products, led by the governance product, are adding to the base. Management's own forecast, however, calls for next-quarter near-term backlog growth of 11 to 12 percent and revenue growth of about 10 percent, which means the company itself is not guiding to an acceleration.[19] It describes this as a prudent approach, so there is room for a beat, but a forecast is the only hard number we have.

The momentum gate: both tests fail

The momentum gate has two tests. Test A asks whether the stock is in momentum now, which needs the structure above, a rising 200-day line, outperformance over 63 sessions, and a price within 1.2 ATR of support. Test B asks whether the stock is entering momentum from a base. What could happen next

A valuation is a story with numbers. We rely on a discounted cash flow, which values a company by adding up the cash it is expected to produce over a decade and a terminal period, then converting that future cash into today's dollars at a rate that reflects risk. Two rules from the underwriting framework apply. A target must be anchored to a method, not a guess, and the anchor used here is the discounted cash flow. The other method named in the framework, forward earnings multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, is N/A: no source gives a verified three-year median, and because earnings were negative for most of that window a median is not meaningful. Estimate 2: reward-to-risk at today's price

The number

The reward-to-risk ratio at $232.13 is about −2.2 to 1, which means the base-case target sits below the entry. The floor for a tactical momentum name is +3.5 to 1.[29]

How it was calculated

Reward-to-risk is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop. The stop loss is the price at which you sell if you are wrong. The rule for a tactical position is below the lower of the swing low and the 20- or 50-day lines, minus half an ATR: the lowest of $170.22, $204.01 and $173.11 is $170.22, minus 0.5 × $9.42 = $4.71, so the stop is $165.51. The risk is $232.13 − $165.51 = $66.62, which is 28.7 percent of the price. The reward is $88 − $232.13 = −$144. The ratio is −144 ÷ 66.62 = −2.2.

Why these estimates might be wrong

An estimate is only as good as its weakest assumption, so each one gets a challenge here. The bear case below is an honest attempt to argue against our own REJECTED verdict, and then against the quality of the business, in the order of how much each point could change the answer.

The red-team case against our verdict

The strongest argument against rejecting Okta is that the market may be right about growth. The stock has risen 148 percent in a year, the data vendor shows nine analyst upgrades since March, and backlog growth accelerated from 12 percent in the April quarter to 14 percent in July.[21][25] If AI agents multiply the number of identities that every company must manage, Okta's independent position could turn the current 11 percent growth into the mid-teens, and the bull case, not the base case, would be the right anchor. The company's own prepared remarks describe accelerating contract value in both of its businesses.[18]

The reply is that the filings do not show it yet. The annual report says adoption is in early stages, retention is still 107 percent, the near-term guide is 10 percent growth, and professional services are being shifted to partners, which costs about one point of growth.[7][16][20] A bull-case value of $146 requires 13 percent growth, a figure not reached since fiscal 2025, and even the cash-flow version that ignores share pay needs the best margin path in our range to reach $205. Paying $232 therefore requires a view that goes past anything in our bull case.

Three fatal-flaw scenarios

Each scenario is a way the investment loses money even if the business stays healthy. The first is valuation compression. If the stock trades at 5 times sales, still a premium multiple, the equity value is about $15.4 billion, or about $84 per share on 184 million shares plus cash, which is down 64 percent from here. The second is growth that stalls at 10 percent while share pay stays near 15 percent: the free cash flow figure of $961 million then converts to only about $460 million of owner cash, and the stock would have no earnings support below $100. The third is a reversal in the tactical trend. A stock that has risen 35 percent in a month, on 0.65 times average volume, with Bernstein downgrading it to market perform on 17 September, can lose its 20-day line in a week, and the stop at $165.51 is 28.7 percent below the close.[25]

The single claim everything hinges on

The whole REJECTED verdict hinges on one claim: that the stock is paying for growth the filings do not show. If management shows net retention above 110 percent and near-term backlog growth in the mid-teens, that claim weakens, and a pullback-based entry would then deserve a fresh look. If growth comes in at the guide or below, the claim is confirmed.

What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED for the TACTICAL_MOMENTUM bucket. It is not a view that Okta is a bad company. The rule that triggers it is Layer 1: three floors fail, share pay at 16.4 percent of revenue against a ceiling of 5 percent, ROIC of 3.9 percent against 12 percent, and a reward-to-risk ratio of −2.2 against 3.5.[29] Any one of them would have rejected the name. The momentum gate also returns NO_MOMENTUM, so there is no allocation on either measure.

What would change this: a revisit needs all of the following together. Share pay below 5 percent of revenue is not realistic in under three years, so the first reconsideration trigger is a price at which the discounted cash flow in chapter 5 gives reward-to-risk above 3.5, which at today's inputs means a price near or below $60; or growth sustained above 13 percent with net retention above 110 percent for two reports, which would raise the bull case to the base case.

Layer 3: entry posture, if the name were ever re-opened

The posture is NO_BUY (OVEREXTENDED), the same band as a WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT stance: the price is 3.0 ATR above the nearest support and 11.1 ATR above the base, and RSI is above 72. A buyer would need a price at or below $215.32 even to be inside the limit-order band, and the stock's valuation fails there as well. We therefore set no entry zone, no position size and no target. For completeness, the structural stop for a tactical position would be $165.51, and the levels that matter on the chart are $204.01, $173.11, $170.22 and $127.60.

The chart would be reconsidered if the price closes within 1.2 ATR of the 20-day line (at or below $215.32) on rising volume while the valuation conditions above are also met; a close below $170.22 would end the Stage 2 trend and make any tactical case moot.
