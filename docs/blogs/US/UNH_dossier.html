1. What the company does

UnitedHealth Group sells health insurance and also owns a large set of businesses that deliver and manage care. It has two parts. UnitedHealthcare sells insurance to employers, to people over 65 through Medicare Advantage, and to low-income people through state Medicaid programs. Optum runs clinics, sells data and software, and manages prescription drug benefits. In the third quarter of 2026, UnitedHealthcare served 48.3 million people and booked $86.3 billion of revenue, while Optum booked $66.9 billion.[5] The two figures add to more than the company total of $112.7 billion because the parts sell to each other, and those internal sales are removed once from the total.[6]

Most of the money comes from insurance premiums. The company says premiums from risk-based products are nearly 80% of its total revenue.[7] Risk-based means the company collects a fixed monthly payment and then pays whatever the care costs. If members use more care than planned, profit falls. If they use less, profit rises. The key gauge is the medical care ratio (MCR), the share of premium dollars paid out as medical claims. It was 88.4% in the third quarter of 2026 and 89.9% a year earlier.[8] A lower ratio means more profit. In chapter 2 we estimate that one point of ratio is worth about $3.17 of earnings per share.

The largest single buyer is the US government. Under Medicare Advantage, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) pays a fixed monthly amount for each member, and the amount depends on where the member lives, age and health status.[9] UnitedHealthcare served 8.4 million people through Medicare Advantage at the end of 2025.[9] By September 30, 2026 the figure was 7.605 million, because the company is shrinking membership and exiting unprofitable contracts.[10][11] Medicare & Retirement plus Community & State (mostly Medicaid) made up $66.1 billion of the quarter's $112.7 billion of revenue, which is about 58.7% by our arithmetic.[12]

Optum earns money in three ways. Optum Health runs clinics and contracts in which it is paid a fixed amount per patient to manage that patient's care. Optum Insight sells data, analytics and technology to insurers and hospitals. Optum Rx is a pharmacy benefit manager (PBM): it manages drug coverage for employers, insurers and governments. In the third quarter these three produced revenue of $22.9 billion, $5.5 billion and $39.9 billion, and operating earnings of $735 million, $1.279 billion and $1.718 billion.[12][13]

Profit fell sharply in 2025, and 2026 has been a repair year. Earnings from operations dropped from $32.3 billion in 2024 to $19.0 billion in 2025, as medical costs rose faster than premiums.[14][15] Management responded with higher prices, benefit changes and smaller membership. UnitedHealthcare served 48.3 million people at the end of September 2026, down from 50.1 million a year earlier.[10] Optum Health revenue fell 10% because it served fewer value-based care patients, meaning patients whose care is paid for by outcome rather than by visit.[16] The price of the repair is flat sales: revenue was $112.7 billion in the third quarter against $113.2 billion a year before.[17]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Medical care ratio (MCR) — the share of premium dollars the company pays out as medical claims. Lower means more profit.
  • Risk-based — the company is paid a fixed amount per person and carries the cost if care costs more than planned.
  • Medicare Advantage — private health plans for people over 65, paid for by the federal government through CMS.
  • Star Ratings — a 1-to-5-star quality score from CMS; plans rated 4 stars or more earn bonus payments.
  • Optum — the care, data and pharmacy half of the company; it sells to UnitedHealthcare and to outside customers.
  • Value-based care — care paid for by patient outcome and cost rather than by each visit.
  • Adjusted earnings per share — profit per share after removing items management calls one-off, such as sale gains and restructuring.
  • Reverse DCF — a way to read the price backwards: it finds the yearly growth rate in cash flow that today's price already assumes.
  • Weinstein stage — a four-step description of a chart. Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend, Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend, and Stage 3 is a topping or pullback phase after an advance.
  • EPS (earnings per share) — the company's profit divided across all its shares.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) — the cash left after running the business and buying equipment; the free cash flow margin is that cash as a share of revenue.
  • Cash flow from operations — the cash the core business collects before investing and financing.
  • Operating margin — operating profit as a share of revenue.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) — a rough measure of operating cash profit; net debt is debt minus cash and short-term investments.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) — after-tax operating profit as a share of the money invested in the business.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) — pay given to employees in shares.
  • Buyback — the company buying its own shares, which shrinks the share count.
  • Asymmetry (reward-to-risk) — the possible gain to the target divided by the possible loss to the exit level.
  • Stop loss, or exit level — the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut.
  • Entry zone — the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Limit order — an order that fills only at your price or better and never chases the price.
  • RSI (relative strength index) — a 0-to-100 meter of how fast the price has been rising.
  • ATR (average true range) — the typical daily price swing in dollars.
  • Base, pivot and breakout — a base is a sideways stretch the price builds before a move, the pivot is the top of that stretch, and a breakout is a close above the pivot on strong volume.
  • Consensus estimate — the average of analysts' forecasts.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

UnitedHealth's first strength is scale. It had more than 390,000 employees at the end of 2025, and nearly 165,000 of them were clinical professionals.[18] It insured 48.3 million people in September 2026, and Optum says it cares for more than 120 million patients and consumers.[5] Scale helps in insurance because costs are spread across many members, which makes results more predictable. A very large buyer can also negotiate lower prices from hospitals and drug makers.

The evidence is in the margins. In a StockAnalysis.com snapshot of S&P Global data dated October 10, 2026, UnitedHealth's trailing operating margin was 5.35%. That is above Elevance Health at 3.79%, Cigna at 3.90%, CVS Health at 2.95%, Humana at 2.30% and Centene at 1.86%.[19][20][21][22][23][24] Its return on invested capital was 11.37% on the same snapshot, behind only Cigna's 13.39% among the five peers.[25] These are third-party figures taken before the third-quarter release, and they are used here only to compare companies on one basis.

The second strength is integration. UnitedHealthcare pays for care, and Optum provides care, data and drugs. That lets the company steer members toward lower-cost settings. The chief financial officer told CNBC that acute hospital admissions among members fell 15% year over year through the third quarter, while home health admissions rose 10%.[26] Integration also adds risk, because regulators and the public judge the two halves as one company.

The business is also thin. Operating margin was 5.32% over the last twelve months, by our arithmetic from the filings, and risk-based premiums run above $350 billion a year.[17][27] A one-point rise in the medical care ratio would cost about $3.5 billion before tax. After the guided tax rate of about 18.5% and 902 million diluted shares, that is about $3.17 per share.[28][29] The 2025 record shows how fast this can turn. The adjusted medical care ratio rose from 85.5% in 2024 to 88.9% in 2025, and operating margin fell from 8.1% to 4.2%.[15][14]

Rates, rules and courts decide a large part of the outcome

Government payment rules drive a large share of revenue. For 2027, CMS finalized an average increase in Medicare Advantage payments of 2.48%, worth over $13 billion, or 4.98% once expected growth in diagnosis scores is counted.[30] The agency's first proposal, in its Advance Notice, had been only 0.09%.[30] Legal risk is open. The US Department of Justice (DOJ) has pursued a False Claims Act case about Medicare risk adjustment since 2017, and a special master recommended judgment for the company in March 2025, which the DOJ asked the court to reject in April 2025.[31] In July 2025 the company said it was cooperating with civil and criminal DOJ investigations of its Medicare billing.[32] The Internal Revenue Service also sent notices of proposed adjustment in March 2026 for the 2017 through 2020 tax years.[33]

None of these matters has produced a finding or a restatement, which is why we did not treat them as a hard red line in chapter 3. They are open, and an adverse result could be large. That is the main reason this report keeps any future position small and sets a wide exit level.

How much of the reported profit can be trusted

Insurers report profit using estimates of claims that have happened but not yet been billed. The auditor named these estimates a critical audit matter, which means they involve especially difficult judgments.[34] The auditor, Deloitte & Touche LLP, gave an unqualified opinion on March 2, 2026.[35] In 2026 the company recorded favorable reserve development of $860 million in the second quarter and $610 million in the third, mostly tied to 2026 dates of service.[36][8] That lifts reported profit and is not a sign of lower costs by itself. Goodwill and other intangible assets are $131 billion, which is 42% of total assets.[37] A write-down would follow if acquired businesses underperform.

How we treat an insurer's balance sheet

The Layer 1 rule that net debt divided by EBITDA must be below 3.0 times excludes banks and other financial institutions. For a bank, deposits and borrowing are raw material, so a debt ratio says little. UnitedHealth is not a bank. The SEC classifies it as a hospital and medical service plan (code 6324), meaning a health insurer.[70] Its debt is ordinary borrowing at the parent company, serviced from the cash its regulated insurance subsidiaries send up and from Optum. So we do not exclude it. We run the rule two ways. The first subtracts all cash and short-term investments from debt. The second uses gross debt, because much of that cash and the investment portfolio sits in regulated subsidiaries and backs unpaid claims. Where the two disagree, we use the stricter gross figure. We add insurer checks: regulatory capital, the company's debt-to-capital ratio, interest cover and credit ratings.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report rests on assumptions that can fail. This chapter names the weakest assumption in each estimate, builds the strongest honest case against our own conclusion, picks the single claim that matters most, and says when we will know. The aim is to say in advance what evidence would prove us wrong.

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF: trailing cash flow is a fair starting point

The reverse DCF starts from $22,826 million of free cash flow. That figure includes timing benefits. The company said 2025 operating cash flow was higher than anticipated because of the timing of cash flows, including payments received in 2025 that were expected in 2026.[48] If true free cash flow is $18 billion to $20 billion, the implied growth rises to 7.2% to 5.8%. That moves the number but does not settle the real weakness, which is that ten-year cash flow growth and a one-year earnings target are different measures. The bridge in chapter 5 is an assumption, not evidence about growth after 2029. What would show us wrong is a fall in nine-month cash conversion, or evidence that growth fades faster than the bridge assumes.

Weakest assumption in the 2027 earnings estimate: the improvement is lasting

Our base case needs the medical care ratio to hold near 88% without help from reserve releases. The company recorded $860 million of favorable development in the second quarter and $610 million in the third.[36][8] By our arithmetic those two items alone are worth about $1.33 per share. The chief financial officer also said costs 'continue to be elevated versus historical norms', and that the No Surprises Act dispute process adds at least one percentage point to medical costs.[90] Commercial cost trends are running above the company's initial 11% expectation.[91] If that trend worsens, the bear case applies.

Weakest assumption in the target multiple: three year-end data points describe the future

The 19.42 multiple is the median of only three year-end readings, taken from a secondary data provider.[75] A 15.66 multiple is the stock's 52-week low price divided by 2025 adjusted earnings, so the market has paid about that much recently. Peers trade at a median of 14.04 times forward earnings, and a re-rating toward that level would cut the base target by about a quarter.[20][22] What would show us wrong is a sector-wide fall in managed-care multiples after the first 2027 outlooks arrive.

The red-team bear case, in its strongest honest form

A careful skeptic would say the 2026 recovery is mostly repricing, benefit cuts and shrinking membership, not lower underlying costs. Medicare Advantage people served fell from 8.435 million to 7.605 million in a year, a drop of 9.8%, and revenue is flat.[10][17] Reserve releases flatter the medical care ratio. Quality bonus payments are at risk: TD Cowen estimates that lower 2028 Star Ratings could cost about $13 per member per month, or $1.5 billion of revenue.[45] The DOJ False Claims Act case, the civil and criminal investigations of Medicare billing, and the IRS notices for 2017 to 2020 are open, and the outcome could be large.[31][32][33] Credit ratings carry negative outlooks.[74] In that world, earnings per share stall near $17 to $18, the stock trades at a trough multiple, and the price falls to about $271, which is 32.5% below today.
