1. What the company does

UnitedHealth Group makes money in two ways that feed each other. UnitedHealthcare is the insurance arm. Employers, individuals and government programs pay it a fixed monthly premium, and it pays the doctors and hospitals when members get care. At September 30, 2026 it covered 48.3 million medical members, including 7.6 million in Medicare Advantage, the private-plan version of Medicare for people over 65[5]. When members use less care than the premium assumed, the insurer keeps the difference. When they use more, it loses money.

Optum is the second arm, and it sells to almost everyone in the health system. Optum Health employs and contracts with doctors and runs clinics, often under value-based care, where the group gets a fixed payment per patient and keeps the savings if it keeps patients healthy. Optum Insight sells software, data and payment-processing services to hospitals and insurers. Optum Rx is a pharmacy benefit manager (PBM), a company that negotiates drug prices and runs prescription claims for employers and health plans. In the third quarter of 2026 Optum booked $66.9 billion of revenue and $3.7 billion of operating earnings[5].

Revenue is huge and thin-margined. The company booked $447.6 billion of revenue in 2025, with 78.7% of it ($352.2 billion) coming from premiums[1]. Because Optum sells to UnitedHealthcare and to rivals, about $168.0 billion of the group's 2025 revenue was between its own arms and was removed when the results were added together[1]. The profit pool is therefore a small slice of a large number. A one-point change in the medical care ratio (medical costs divided by premium revenue) moves profit by roughly $3.5 billion before tax, so small changes in how much care members use decide the year.

Three facts about its customers shape everything that follows. First, government programs (Medicare, Medicaid and the military) are a large part of the premium pool, so rules written in Washington change profit. Second, the company is shrinking some insurance books on purpose: Medicare Advantage membership fell by 935,000 since the end of 2025 as it raised prices and exited unprofitable contracts[5][8]. Third, the pharmacy arm is moving to a fee-based model, with a pledge to pass 100% of manufacturer rebates to clients by January 1, 2028[7], which lowers one source of PBM profit.

How a dollar of premium flows through the company

A member's premium goes to UnitedHealthcare first. About 88 cents of each premium dollar went to medical bills in the third quarter of 2026 (the medical care ratio was 88.4%) and about 13 cents went to running costs (the operating cost ratio was 12.8%)[5]. The sum is slightly above 100 cents because investment income and the non-premium revenue streams cover the difference. The company's profit is the gap between those costs and what it collects.

The cash side runs ahead of earnings in some years and behind in others. Insurers collect premiums first and pay claims later, so they hold a float of unpaid claims; medical costs payable stood at $39.4 billion at September 30, 2026[5]. Timing of government payments can move cash by billions from one year to the next, which matters when reading cash flow in Chapter 3.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Medical care ratio (MCR): medical costs divided by premium revenue. Lower is better for the insurer.
  • Medicare Advantage (MA): private health plans that Medicare pays a fixed monthly amount per senior.
  • Star Ratings: a 1-to-5 quality score from the federal Medicare agency (CMS). Plans rated 4 stars or higher earn quality bonus payments.
  • Value-based care: doctors are paid per patient rather than per visit, and share in savings when patients stay healthier.
  • PBM: a pharmacy benefit manager, the middleman that negotiates drug prices and processes prescription claims.
  • Risk adjustment: a Medicare formula that pays plans more for sicker members. Audits of how plans record diagnoses are a recurring legal risk.
  • Reverse DCF: a calculation that starts from today's price and solves for the cash-flow growth that price already assumes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

UnitedHealth's strongest advantage is scale that rivals cannot copy quickly. It serves 48.3 million medical members and Optum cares for more than 120 million patients and consumers[7]. A larger membership gives it more pricing data, more bargaining power with hospitals and a bigger pool over which to spread technology spending. Revenue of $447.6 billion in 2025 was about 2.2 times that of Elevance Health, the largest pure health insurer after UnitedHealth in the peer set, whose trailing revenue is $201.8 billion[1][30].

The second advantage is that the group is both payer and provider. Optum Health, Optum Rx and Optum Insight give UnitedHealthcare data and care-management tools that smaller insurers do not have. The company points to results from this model: acute hospital admissions among members fell 15% year over year through the third quarter of 2026 while home health admissions rose 10%[8]. That is management's claim, and the filing does not audit it, so we treat it as evidence of direction rather than proof.

The weakness is that the business is a bet on medical costs, and costs have turned against insurers. The medical care ratio rose from 83.2% in 2023 to 85.5% in 2024 and 89.1% in 2025[1]. Earnings from operations fell from $32.3 billion in 2024 to $19.0 billion in 2025[1]. In 2025 the company's own forecast of $29.50 to $30.00 of adjusted earnings per share missed by almost half, and it suspended guidance in May 2025 while its chief executive left[16][18].

The recovery since then is real but not finished. The 2026 ratio is 83.9% in the first quarter, 86.7% in the second and 88.4% in the third[12][11][5]. It is better than a year ago in each case, but it rises through the year as members use more care in the second half, and management says costs are still above historical norms, particularly in commercial insurance, specialty drugs and inpatient care[8][9]. Moat strength is therefore proven in scale and weaker in cost control.

Regulatory and legal exposure

Regulation is the single largest outside force on this stock. In April 2026 the Medicare agency finalized a 2.48% average payment increase for Medicare Advantage in 2027, worth over $13 billion, far above the 0.09% it first proposed[21][22]. That is helpful. The same announcement stops counting diagnoses that are not tied to a patient visit (called unlinked chart review records), and CMS expects the hit to be larger for plans that rely on them[21].

The legal overhang is harder to size. UnitedHealth said on July 24, 2025 that it was complying with formal criminal and civil requests from the Department of Justice about its Medicare practices[20]. A separate civil case, begun in 2011 and joined by the Justice Department in 2017, claims improper risk-adjustment submissions; a court-appointed Special Master recommended judgment for the company in March 2025 and the Justice Department asked the court to reject that report in April 2025[4]. The 10-Q says the company cannot reasonably estimate the outcome of the civil case. The company also received IRS proposals on March 6, 2026 that would raise taxable income for 2017 through 2020[4]. We treat these as a risk to the multiple and not as a solvency or fraud finding, because the filings we read show no conviction, no judgment against the company and no going-concern doubt.

Credit strength

Lenders still see an investment-grade borrower with a warning label. As of June 30, 2026 senior unsecured debt was rated A2 by Moody's, A+ by S&P Global and A by Fitch, each with a negative outlook, and A- with a stable outlook by A.M. Best[4]. The negative outlooks mean the agencies are waiting to see whether margin recovery holds. This ties to Chapter 3, where leverage falls but cash conversion carries timing noise. Ratings also set the cost of borrowing and the room to keep funding buybacks. Interest expense was $2,880 million in the first nine months of 2026 against $3,028 million a year earlier[5], so the cost of debt has stopped rising. A downgrade would not threaten solvency at 1.6 times EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), but it would signal that the margin recovery is failing, so it would trigger a review of this report.

Red-team bear case: the 2026 repair is cost shifting, not cost control

A bear would say the 2026 improvement came from raising prices, cutting benefits and shrinking the books, which can be done once, and that members left after those changes. UnitedHealthcare's membership fell from 50.1 million to 48.3 million and Medicare Advantage by 935,000 since year-end[5]. Commercial trends are still above 11% and the No Surprises Act arbitration process adds at least 100 basis points to total medical costs[9][8]. The CFO said he did not want the improved ratio to mislead anyone about medical cost trends[8].

If so, the medical care ratio rises 1.5 points above the level behind consensus, 2027 earnings per share fall to about $18.55 ($23.23 less 1.5 x $3.12) and the multiple drops toward Elevance's 15.3 forward P/E[30]. That produces our $288 bear target, 23.5% below the $376.28 buy zone high and 18.0% below the $351.20 stop. A stop is a trading instruction and not a guaranteed loss cap. A legal or Medicare headline can open the stock below it, so the loss could be well above the planned 6.7%. Chapter 7 puts numbers on that gap and sizes the first purchase for it.

Fatal flaw 1: a criminal or civil outcome in the Medicare probes

The Department of Justice has made formal criminal and civil requests, and the older False Claims Act case is unresolved[20][4]. A large settlement, a corporate integrity agreement that restricts risk-adjustment coding, or exclusion from a program would be severe. The company's response is that independent audits and a Special Master report support it[20]. The probability of a company-ending outcome is low, but a multi-billion-dollar payment or a hit to Medicare Advantage revenue is plausible. It would show up in the date of any filing or announcement, which no one can predict. Because the timing is unknown, the report sizes the position for the possibility and does not try to forecast the date. Investors who cannot accept an open legal risk should skip this name.

Fatal flaw 2: Medicare payment rules turn against the model

The 2027 rate is favorable at 2.48%, but it is a policy decision, not a law, and CMS is removing diagnoses that are not tied to a patient visit[21]. UnitedHealth is exiting about 140 counties and has just 3 of 15 five-star contracts[24]. If a future rate notice uses the 2023-2024 data model that CMS declined to adopt for 2027, risk scores and payments would fall[22]. Star-rating cuts would reduce bonus payments on top of that. The 2027 rate was set in April 2026, so the risk is to the 2028 notice and later, which means the next rate announcement is the date to watch. A buyer should see this as a slow-moving risk that shows up first in the plan's benefit cuts and membership losses.

Fatal flaw 3: the pharmacy and care-delivery arms lose profit pools

Optum Rx has committed to pass 100% of manufacturer drug rebate discounts to clients by January 1, 2028 and has launched a fee-based pharmacy care model[7]. That lowers one profit source, and adjusted scripts fell to 393 million from 414 million as membership shrank[5].
