1. What the company does

UnitedHealth Group earns money in two linked ways. UnitedHealthcare is the insurance arm. Employers, individuals and government programs pay it a fixed monthly premium, and it pays the doctors and hospitals when members get care. At September 30, 2026 it served 48.3 million medical members, including 7.6 million in Medicare Advantage (MA), the private-plan version of Medicare, in which the government pays a private insurer a fixed monthly amount for each senior[6]. When members use less care than the premium assumed, the insurer keeps the difference. When they use more, it loses money, so one ratio, the share of premiums spent on medical bills, decides most of the year.

Optum is the second arm, and it sells to almost everyone in the health system. Optum Health employs and contracts with doctors and runs clinics, often under value-based care, an arrangement in which a provider gets one fixed payment per patient and keeps the savings if the patient stays healthy. Optum Insight sells software, data and payment services to hospitals and insurers. Optum Rx is a pharmacy benefit manager (PBM), the middleman that negotiates drug prices and runs prescription claims for employers and health plans. In the third quarter of 2026 Optum booked $66.9 billion of revenue and $3.7 billion of operating earnings[5][6].

The revenue figure is huge and the profit margin is thin. The company booked $447.6 billion of revenue in 2025, and 78.7% of it ($352.2 billion) was premiums[1]. Because Optum sells to UnitedHealthcare as well as to rivals, $168.0 billion of 2025 revenue was sales between the company's own arms[1]. Those sales are removed when the results are added together. In the third quarter the removal was $41.9 billion in all: $40.5 billion of sales between the company's arms and $1.4 billion eliminated inside Optum[6]. On revenue this large, a one-point change in the share of premiums spent on care moves profit by about $3.5 billion before tax, so small shifts in how much care members use decide the year.

Three facts about the customers shape the rest of this report. First, government programs (Medicare, Medicaid and the military) are a large part of the premium pool, so rules written in Washington change profit. Second, the company is shrinking some insurance books on purpose: medical membership fell from 50.1 million a year ago to 48.3 million, and Medicare Advantage membership fell by 935,000 since the end of 2025 as the company raised prices and left unprofitable contracts[6][8]. Third, Optum Rx has committed to pass 100% of manufacturer rebate discounts to clients by January 1, 2028, which removes one source of pharmacy profit[6].

How a dollar of premium flows through the company

A member's premium goes to UnitedHealthcare first. In the third quarter of 2026 about 88 cents of each premium dollar went to medical bills (the medical care ratio, or MCR, was 88.4%) and about 13 cents went to running costs (the operating cost ratio was 12.8%)[5]. The two add to more than a dollar because the company also earns investment income and revenue that is not premium. The gap between what comes in and what goes out is the profit.

Insurers collect premiums first and pay claims later, so they hold a float of unpaid claims. Medical costs payable stood at $39.4 billion at September 30, 2026[6]. Days claims payable, the number of days of claims the company has not yet paid, was 47.0, level with June and above the 46.2 of a year earlier[5]. Timing of government payments can move cash by billions from one year to the next, which matters when reading cash flow in Chapter 3. What this means for an owner: profit depends on predicting medical costs a year ahead, and the next chapter asks how well the company has done that.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Medical care ratio (MCR): medical costs divided by premium revenue. Lower is better for the insurer.
  • Medicare Advantage (MA): private health plans that Medicare pays a fixed monthly amount per senior.
  • Star Ratings: a 1-to-5 quality score from the federal Medicare agency (CMS). Plans rated 4 stars or higher earn quality bonus payments.
  • Value-based care: doctors are paid per patient rather than per visit, and share in savings when patients stay healthier.
  • PBM: a pharmacy benefit manager, the middleman that negotiates drug prices and processes prescription claims.
  • Risk adjustment: a Medicare formula that pays plans more for sicker members. Audits of how plans record diagnoses are a recurring legal risk.
  • Reserve development: the change in the estimate of unpaid claims from earlier periods. Favorable development lowers the current period's medical costs.
  • Reverse DCF: a calculation that starts from today's price and solves for the cash-flow growth that price already assumes.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

UnitedHealth's strongest advantage is scale that rivals cannot copy quickly. It serves 48.3 million medical members and Optum cared for more than 120 million patients and consumers[5]. A bigger membership gives more pricing data, more bargaining power with hospitals and a larger revenue pool over which to spread technology spending. Revenue of $447.6 billion in 2025 was about twice Elevance Health's trailing revenue of $201.8 billion[1][31].

The second advantage is that the group is both payer and provider. Optum Health, Optum Rx and Optum Insight hand UnitedHealthcare data and care-management tools that smaller insurers lack. The chief financial officer says acute hospital admissions among members fell 15% year over year through the third quarter while home health admissions rose 10%[8]. That is a management claim that the filings do not audit, so we treat it as evidence of direction and not as proof.

The weakness is that the business is a bet on medical costs, and costs turned against insurers. The medical care ratio was 82.6% in 2021, 82.0% in 2022 and 83.2% in 2023, then rose to 85.5% in 2024 and 89.1% in 2025[2][1]. Earnings from operations fell from $32.4 billion in 2023 to $19.0 billion in 2025[1]. In January 2025 the company still guided to adjusted profit of $29.50 to $30.00 per share[15], and in May 2025 it suspended that outlook and changed its chief executive[14]. A moat that did not stop a 46% fall in net profit from 2023 to 2025 is a scale moat and not a cost-control moat.

The 2026 recovery is real, but its quality needs a second look. The ratio was 83.9% in the first quarter, 86.7% in the second and 88.4% in the third, each below the 84.8%, 89.4% and 89.9% of a year earlier[10][9][5]. Net favorable reserve development, which lowers medical costs by releasing money set aside for claims, was $860 million in the second quarter and $610 million in the third, against $80 million in the third quarter of 2025[9][5][12]. Taking the $610 million out, the third-quarter ratio is 89.1% against 90.0% a year earlier, so the underlying improvement is about 0.8 points and not the 1.5 points on the headline[6]. Management also says costs remain above historical norms, and the chief financial officer said he did not want the lower ratio to mislead anyone about medical cost trends[8].

Regulatory and legal exposure

Regulation is the largest outside force on this stock. In April 2026 the Medicare agency finalized a 2.48% average payment increase for Medicare Advantage in 2027, worth more than $13 billion and far above the 0.09% it first proposed[18][17]. With the expected trend in risk scores (population changes and coding practices) counted, the agency puts the increase at 4.98%[17]. The same notice stops counting diagnoses taken from chart reviews that are not tied to a patient visit, which hurts plans that lean on them[18]. Insurers themselves project that Medicare Advantage membership will fall from 36.3 million to 34 million in 2027, which would be the first decline since at least 2007 by one count[21].

The legal overhang is harder to size. On July 24, 2025 the company said it was complying with formal criminal and civil requests from the Department of Justice about its Medicare practices[16]. A separate civil case, started by a whistleblower in 2011 and joined by the Department in 2017, claims improper risk-adjustment submissions. A court-appointed Special Master recommended judgment for the company in March 2025, the Department asked the court to reject that report in April 2025, and the company says it cannot estimate the outcome[4]. The Senate Judiciary Committee's majority staff published a 105-page report on January 12, 2026 that accuses the company of turning risk adjustment into a profit strategy[22]. We found no report in primary or trade sources of a charge, settlement or court ruling in the Department's matters after the 2025 statement, and the latest periodic filing, the second-quarter 10-Q, still lists no estimate of loss[4]. One legal-marketing website says the probe expanded in February 2026; we could not corroborate that and do not rely on it.

The list has grown at the edges. Massachusetts sued a UnitedHealthcare unit on May 29, 2026 over more than $100 million of alleged Medicaid overbilling[26]. The Texas Attorney General served civil investigative demands in early October 2026 over coverage denials[25]. On September 30, 2026 a federal judge let part of a CalPERS securities suit proceed, on claims about the 2024 portfolio refinement deals, granting the motion in part[24]. A shareholder suit against board members was amended on August 7, 2026[23], and the Internal Revenue Service sent proposed adjustments on March 6, 2026 for transactions in tax years 2017 through 2020[4]. None of these is a finding of wrongdoing. Together they say the stock carries event risk that a profit forecast does not capture.

Stars, membership and Medicare footprint

Quality scores decide bonus payments. The company reports that at least 69% of its Medicare Advantage members are in plans rated four stars or higher for 2027, in line with its expectations, and it expects to approach 75% by 2028 against an industry level near 70%[6][7]. A third-party tally lists three five-star contracts out of 15 nationally[20][19]. The same tally shows the company exiting about 140 counties and Wyoming for 2027 and raising published plan premiums about 9%[20]. That is deliberate repricing for margin, and it points to another year of shrinking Medicare Advantage membership before any growth.

Credit strength

Lenders still see an investment-grade borrower with a warning label. As of June 30, 2026 senior unsecured debt was rated A2 by Moody's, A+ by S&P Global and A by Fitch, each with a negative outlook, and A- with a stable outlook by A.M. Best[4]. Negative outlooks mean the agencies are waiting to see whether the margin recovery holds. Interest expense was $2,880 million in the first nine months of 2026 against $3,028 million a year earlier[6], so the cost of debt has stopped rising. A downgrade would not threaten solvency at a debt load of 1.6 times EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, which is operating profit plus depreciation and amortization), but it would signal a failing recovery and would trigger a review of this report.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's record splits into two periods. Before May 2025 it missed badly, and since July 2025 it has met or beaten each earnings number it set. The January 2025 plan called for adjusted profit of $29.50 to $30.00 per share and operating cash flow of $32 billion to $33 billion[15]. The company delivered $16.35 and $19.7 billion[11]. A guidance record is judged this way: guidance is the company's own published forecast for the coming period, and the test is whether the actual number lands on the right side of it.

The reset worked. In July 2025 the company re-established its outlook at no less than $16.00, raised the floor to $16.25 in October and finished at $16.35[13][12][11]. For 2026 it opened at more than $17.75 in January and then raised the floor to more than $18.25 in April, a range of $19.50 to $20.00 in July and at least $20.50 in October[11][10][9][5]. It also lifted its planned buyback — company repurchases of its own shares — from about $2.5 billion to at least $5.0 billion and had spent $5.0 billion by September[9][6].

A skeptic should note two things. First, initial guidance in a recovery year is easy to beat, so three raises show caution as much as skill. Second, the company has no multi-year record of hitting its 13% to 16% yearly growth target, which management repeated on the October call[7], and the July medical care ratio outlook of 88.1% implies a fourth-quarter ratio above 93% (our computation, assuming fourth-quarter premiums near the third quarter's $87.1 billion), which looks cautious next to the 86.3% nine-month figure[9][6]. Our rating is partial credit: credible since mid-2025, unproven over a full cycle.

3. The numbers, in plain words

This chapter reads the audited numbers in plain words. A static level is where a measure stands today. Trajectory is its rate of change. A company can miss a level and still qualify if the direction is clearly up, which is the logic of the turnaround archetype used in this report, and a company can pass a level while the direction is down, which is a warning.

Over five years revenue grew from $287.6 billion to $447.6 billion, but profit peaked in 2023 and then fell sharply as medical costs rose[2][1]. The tables use GAAP, the audited accounting standard, unless marked adjusted. Earnings per share (EPS) is the company's profit divided across its shares; adjusted EPS leaves out intangible amortization and one-time items, so it runs higher than the GAAP figure ($16.35 adjusted against $13.23 GAAP in 2025[11]). An insurer reports no gross profit line, so we derive it as revenue minus medical costs minus cost of products sold. Gross margin is revenue minus direct costs as a share of revenue.

Four more terms recur. Free cash flow (FCF) is operating cash flow minus capital spending, the cash left for owners. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay delivered in shares. EBITDA, short for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, is operating profit plus depreciation and amortization. Return on invested capital (ROIC) is the profit earned on each dollar tied up in the business. Net debt is debt minus cash and short-term investments.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one weak point. This chapter names that point, builds the strongest bear case against our own conclusion, lists three ways the thesis could fail outright, and picks the single claim that, if false, breaks everything. It also says when each test resolves.

The Reverse DCF is weakest on its starting cash flow, because trailing cash includes timing gains that management said it expected to reverse. The earnings estimate is weakest on the medical care ratio, because part of the 2026 improvement is a reserve release and because the ratio rose in each quarter of 2026. The price target is weakest on the multiple, because the three-year median rests on only three year-end readings. The probabilities are weakest on the legal tail, because no one outside the Justice Department knows the outcome.

Red-team bear case: the 2026 repair is repricing and reserve release, not cost control

A bear would say the 2026 improvement came from raising prices, cutting benefits, shrinking the books and releasing reserves, and that each of these can be done once. Medical membership fell from 50.1 million to 48.3 million and Medicare Advantage by 935,000 since year-end[6]. Net favorable reserve development was $860 million in the second quarter and $610 million in the third, against $80 million a year earlier[9][5][12]. Commercial trend is still above 11%, and the dispute process under the No Surprises Act adds at least one point to total medical costs[7][8]. The chief financial officer said he did not want the lower ratio to mislead anyone about cost trends[8].

If so, the medical care ratio rises 1.5 points above the level behind consensus, 2027 profit per share falls to about $18.61 ($23.29 less 1.5 x $3.12) and the multiple drops toward Elevance's 14.67[27][31]. That gives our $288 bear target, 23.4% below the $375.85 zone high and 18.1% below the $351.56 stop. A stop is a trading instruction and not a guaranteed loss cap: a legal or Medicare headline can open the stock below it, so the loss could be well above the planned 6.5%. Chapter 7 puts numbers on that gap and sizes the first purchase for it.

Fatal flaw 1: a criminal or civil outcome in the Medicare probes

The Department of Justice has made formal criminal and civil requests, and the older False Claims Act case is unresolved[16][4]. A large settlement, a corporate integrity agreement that restricts risk-adjustment coding, or exclusion from a program would be severe. The company's reply is that independent audits and the Special Master's report support it[16]. A company-ending outcome is unlikely, but a multi-billion-dollar payment or a hit to Medicare Advantage revenue is plausible. The timing is unknown, so the report sizes the position for the possibility and does not try to forecast the date. Investors who cannot accept an open legal risk should skip this name.

Fatal flaw 2: Medicare payment rules and enrollment turn against the model

The 2027 payment rate is favorable at 2.48%, but it is a policy decision and not a law, and the agency is removing diagnoses that are not tied to a patient visit[18][17]. The company is leaving about 140 counties and raised published premiums about 9%[20], and insurers expect industry Medicare Advantage enrollment to fall to 34 million[21]. If a later rate notice uses the newer risk model that the agency declined to adopt for 2027, risk scores and payments would fall[18]. Star-rating slippage would cut bonus payments on top. The next test dates are the 2028 rate notices, which follow the same January-to-April calendar, and the open enrollment period that ends December 7[21].

Fatal flaw 3: the care and pharmacy arms lose profit pools

Optum Rx has committed to pass 100% of manufacturer rebate discounts to clients by January 1, 2028, and adjusted scripts fell to 393 million from 414 million as membership shrank[6]. Optum Health earns 3.2% against a 6% to 8% target, its revenue fell 10%, and management warned that its fourth-quarter result reflects normal seasonality plus added investment[6][7]. If these arms stall, the group margin cannot reach the level that justifies the target. A new risk is the pace of reinvestment: management said it will accelerate spending on technology and policy changes in 2027, which is a headwind to the 13% growth story[7].
