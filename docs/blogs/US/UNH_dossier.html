Chapter 1

From Minneapolis HMO Roots to the Dual-Engine Architecture (1977–2017)

UnitedHealth Group Incorporated traces its roots to 1974, when Richard T. Burke founded Charter Med Incorporated in Minnetonka, Minnesota. The firm managed Physicians Health Plan, an early independent-practice-association health maintenance organization (HMO) [1]. The company reorganized as United HealthCare Corporation in 1977 and went public on October 17, 1984 [1]. Over its first two decades, it proved that managed care could pool actuarial risk across employers. At the same time, it negotiated discounted fee schedules with fragmented hospital and physician networks. In the 1990s and 2000s, the company scaled fast through disciplined regional acquisitions. Key deals included MetraHealth in 1995, AmeriChoice in 2002 (building its Medicaid base), and Oxford Health Plans in 2004 (anchoring the Northeast commercial market). It also bought PacifiCare Health Systems in 2005 and Sierra Health Services in 2008 to cement Western Medicare Advantage density [1], [2].

The defining strategic pivot occurred under Stephen J. Hemsley, who served as Chief Executive Officer from 2006 to 2017 [7]. Pure-play health insurance faces strict caps under the 2010 Affordable Care Act. Statutory Medical Loss Ratio (MLR) floors require insurers to spend 80% to 85% of premium dollars on medical care and quality programs [1]. Seeing that limit, Hemsley grouped the company's non-regulated health services, pharmacy benefit management (PBM), and data-analytics units under one brand—Optum—in 2011 [2]. Pairing a regulated insurance payer (UnitedHealthcare) with an unregulated care, data, and pharmacy platform (Optum) built an internal hedge. When medical care use rose, Optum Health clinics and Optum Insight analytics captured a share of that spend. When drug prices climbed, Optum Rx scaled prescription volume and rebate administration. In 2015, the roughly $12.8 billion purchase of Catamaran Corporation lifted Optum Rx into the top tier of U.S. PBMs alongside CVS Caremark and Express Scripts [2], [13].

Capital-Heavy Expansion, Vertical Integration, and the 2024–2025 Shock Cycle

Between 2017 and 2021 under CEO David Wichmann, and from February 2021 to May 2025 under CEO Andrew Witty, UnitedHealth Group doubled down on vertical integration. The company expanded aggressively across home health, outpatient clinics, and claims networks [2], [7]. Consolidated revenue grew from $184.84 billion in 2016 to $371.62 billion in 2023. Over that same span, earnings from operations compounded from $12.93 billion to $32.36 billion [24]. To keep growing at nearly $400 billion in scale, management leaned heavily on large, complex acquisitions. It bought DaVita Medical Group in 2019 and closed the roughly $13 billion Change Healthcare deal in October 2022 after defeating a Department of Justice (DOJ) antitrust suit. It then bought home-health leader LHC Group for $5.4 billion in February 2023 and agreed to buy Amedisys for $3.3 billion in 2023. That Amedisys deal finally cleared via a December 2025 DOJ consent decree requiring 164 facility divestitures and a $1.1 million HSR civil penalty [12], [18].

That multi-decade streak of steady margin stability broke sharply across 2024 and 2025. Three converging operational and regulatory shocks hit the business at once. First, in February 2024, the BlackCat/ALPHV ransomware attack on Change Healthcare crippled U.S. claims clearinghouse pipes. UnitedHealth Group paid a $22 million ransom, sent billions in interest-free provider liquidity advances, and booked $2.3 billion to $2.45 billion in direct response and disruption costs [14], [18]. Second, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) phased in its Version 28 (V28) Hierarchical Condition Category (HCC) risk-score model from 2024 to full 100% adoption in 2026. That shift cut Medicare Advantage risk scores just as post-pandemic senior outpatient, orthopedic, and behavioral care surged [9], [16]. Third, rapid growth in Optum Health's fully capitated value-based care (VBC) membership outpaced clinical risk-coding and medical-cost controls.

The 2025 Leadership Reset and 2026 Turnaround Execution

By spring 2025, Medicare Advantage mispricing, high senior care use, and Optum Health contract losses forced management to pull and reset its full-year 2025 outlook [16]. On May 13, 2025, Andrew Witty stepped down as CEO for personal reasons and moved to senior adviser. Board Chairman Stephen J. Hemsley returned as Chief Executive Officer [7]. The board also named Tim Noel as CEO of UnitedHealthcare in January 2025 and Dr. Patrick Conway as CEO of Optum in May 2025. On September 2, 2025, veteran managed-care CFO Wayne S. DeVeydt (formerly CFO of Elevance Health) replaced John Rex [8]. In FY2025, revenue reached $447,567 million (+12% YoY), but operating earnings fell to $18,964 million (a 4.24% margin). The Medical Care Ratio climbed to 89.1%, and Optum Health posted a $(278) million operating loss [3], [18].

Under Hemsley and DeVeydt, 2026 is a margin-repair and balance-sheet-repair year, not a top-line land grab. UnitedHealthcare shed about 1.8 million lower-margin lives across Medicare Advantage, Medicaid redeterminations, and risk-bearing commercial and individual plans. As a result, Q3 2026 revenue dipped 0.4% YoY to $112.7 billion [4]. Still, operating discipline returned rapidly across the core business. Nine-month 2026 operating earnings hit $23,544 million, already beating all of FY2025 by 24%. Q3 2026 MCR improved 150 basis points YoY to 88.4%, aided by $610 million in favorable prior-period reserve development for 2026 service dates. Management raised full-year 2026 adjusted EPS guidance to at least $20.50 [4], [19]. Yet major structural overhangs remain active. DOJ civil and criminal probes into Medicare Advantage risk billing and Optum Rx continue, alongside FTC and bipartisan PBM scrutiny. UnitedHealthcare's 2027 Star Ratings also fell to 68%–71% of MA members in 4+ star plans, down from ~81% for 2026, keeping the turnaround a multi-year execution test [15], [17].

Cycle Lesson: Scale Amplifies Both Flywheel Leverage and Underwriting Mistakes

For nearly twenty years (2006–2023), UnitedHealth Group's dual-engine model smoothed out earnings swings. Optum's fee-based services offset seasonal shifts in insurance claims. In 2024–2025, however, Optum Health moved deeper into fully capitated value-based care. That shift meant both UnitedHealthcare and Optum Health were short the exact same risk: senior care use and CMS V28 risk-score cuts.

When an insurer-provider group takes full capitation risk on both sides, vertical integration magnifies pricing mistakes. Those losses persist until annual CMS bids and provider contracts reset.

Anatomy of the 2024–2025 Margin Drawdown: Three Converging Mechanisms

To see why FY2025 broke UnitedHealth Group's long operating streak, analysts must separate one-off shocks from core pricing errors. The February 2024 Change Healthcare cyberattack was far more than an IT outage. Change Healthcare ran the largest electronic data interchange (EDI) claims switch in the United States. When it went down, cash flow stopped for tens of thousands of small clinics and rural hospitals [14]. UnitedHealth Group stepped in as an emergency lender, sending billions in interest-free advances. It absorbed $2.3 billion to $2.45 billion in direct response, recovery, and disruption costs across 2024–2025 [14]. At the same time, pausing prior-authorization and care-review edits during the outage hid rising hospital and outpatient claim trends.

By the time claims data normalized in late 2024 and early 2025, CMS's Version 28 (V28) transition had begun cutting high-weight diagnosis codes from Medicare Advantage pay formulas [9]. CMS phased in V28 across 2024–2026 right as senior demand for outpatient orthopedics, cardiac procedures, and behavioral health jumped. As a result, UnitedHealthcare's 2025 Medicare Advantage bids fell materially short of actual medical cost trends across core markets [16]. That pricing gap hit Optum Health's 4.7 million value-based care patients directly. Under global capitation contracts, clinics receive a fixed share of the CMS premium, turning an internal profit hedge into a $(278) million FY2025 operating loss [3], [22].

UnitedHealthcare: Three Insurance Engines with Concrete End-to-End Examples

1. Employer & Individual ($79.2B FY2025 revenue): Serves large national corporations, mid-sized firms, small businesses, and ACA individual-exchange buyers. Concrete Example: Consider a Fortune 500 manufacturing employer with 35,000 U.S. workers facing 8% annual medical inflation and high specialty-drug spend. Instead of buying fully insured coverage, the employer hires UnitedHealthcare under an Administrative Services Only (ASO) self-funded contract. UnitedHealthcare provides access to its national Choice Plus PPO network, delivering hospital discounts of 40%–55% off billed charges. It also processes employee claims and layers on its Surest copay-only digital plan design. The employer pays a fixed Per-Employee-Per-Month (PEPM) administrative fee plus shared-savings fees on out-of-network claims, while keeping the medical risk.

2. Medicare & Retirement ($171.3B FY2025 revenue): UnitedHealthcare's largest revenue engine, delivering Medicare Advantage (Part C), Medicare Supplement (Medigap), and Part D drug plans to seniors. Concrete Example: A 71-year-old retiree in Maricopa County, Arizona, has Type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure. She enrolls in an AARP-branded UnitedHealthcare Medicare Advantage PPO plan with a $0 monthly plan premium beyond her standard Part B premium. CMS pays UnitedHealthcare a monthly capitated base rate adjusted by her county benchmark, her HCC risk score under CMS V28, and the plan's Star Rating bonus (a 5% bonus for 4+ star plans). In return, UnitedHealthcare covers her hospital, physician, Part D drug, dental, and vision benefits. The plan also deploys in-home HouseCalls nurse-practitioner visits to close care gaps and document chronic conditions.

3. Community & State ($94.4B FY2025 revenue): Contracts with state Medicaid agencies to manage care for low-income families, pregnant women, and dual-eligible (Medicare + Medicaid) adults with complex needs. Concrete Example: A state Medicaid agency awards UnitedHealthcare a multi-year contract covering 450,000 members, including high-need dual-eligible adults. A 58-year-old member with heart failure and unstable housing gets a dedicated care coordinator. The coordinator books non-emergency rides to heart clinic visits and links the member to local food aid, preventing $18,000 emergency-room readmissions. The state pays UnitedHealthcare an actuarially certified Per-Member-Per-Month (PMPM) capitation rate, subject to state MLR corridors (typically 85%–89%).

Optum: Three Health-Services Engines with Concrete End-to-End Examples

4. Optum Health ($101.2B FY2025 revenue; $(278)M FY2025 operating loss): Operates primary, specialty, urgent, ambulatory-surgery (SCA Health), behavioral, and home-health (LHC Group, Amedisys) care delivery networks. The unit serves ~100+ million consumers overall and ~4.7 million patients in fully accountable value-based care models [22]. Concrete Example: A 76-year-old Medicare Advantage member in Florida is attributed to an Optum Care primary-care clinic under a global capitation contract. Instead of billing fee-for-service per visit, Optum Health receives ~80%–85% of the member's CMS monthly premium up front. When the patient gets a knee replacement at an Optum-owned Surgical Care Affiliates (SCA) surgery center ($12,000 episode cost vs. $28,000 in a hospital ward) and recovers at home with an LHC Group physical therapist instead of a skilled nursing facility, Optum Health captures the clinical savings. However, if senior inpatient and outpatient care use exceeds the capitated budget, as happened in 2025, Optum Health absorbs the loss directly.

5. Optum Insight ($19.2B FY2025 revenue; $2,624M FY2025 operating earnings): Provides revenue-cycle management, claims clearinghouse links (via Change Healthcare), payment-integrity auditing, and AI clinical analytics to hospitals, health systems, and payers. The unit is backed by a $31B–$33B contract backlog [3], [18]. Concrete Example: A 12-hospital regional health system struggles with 11% claim-denial rates and 52 days in accounts receivable. It signs a 7-year managed revenue-cycle and analytics contract with Optum Insight. Optum Insight embeds its clearinghouse EDI pipes and AI prior-authorization and coding software inside the hospital's EHR workflow. The tools catch missing clinical notes before claims leave the building and audit payer underpayments. The health system pays Optum Insight a multi-year SaaS subscription plus a percentage of net cash collected above baseline.

6. Optum Rx ($151.3B FY2025 revenue; $7,193M FY2025 operating earnings): Processes over 1.52 billion adjusted prescriptions annually (>400 million per quarter) through retail pharmacy networks, Genoa behavioral pharmacies, and Optum Specialty/Home Delivery [3], [18]. Concrete Example: A large municipal school district with 25,000 covered lives contracts with Optum Rx to manage its pharmacy benefit. When a teacher is prescribed a $1,000/month GLP-1 or $6,000/month immunology biologic, Optum Rx routes the script through its formulary engine and negotiates drugmaker rebates. Under its 2025–2026 transparent pass-through contracts, it passes 100% of those rebates back to the client [23]. Optum Specialty Pharmacy then ships the biologic via cold-chain home delivery with nurse adherence coaching, earning per-claim admin fees, dispensing margins, and specialty-pharmacy spread.

Intersegment Economics and the 2026 Portfolio Pruning

Reading UnitedHealth Group's segment numbers requires tracking the $167.9 billion intersegment elimination line in FY2025 [18]. UnitedHealthcare buys pharmacy management from Optum Rx, claims analytics from Optum Insight, and clinical care from Optum Health. As a result, roughly 62% of Optum's $270.6 billion gross revenue comes from sister UnitedHealthcare plans [3], [18]. Even so, Optum also serves over 100 outside health plans, thousands of hospitals, and large self-insured employers directly. That external client base gives Optum real commercial validation beyond its captive UnitedHealthcare book [4].

In 2026, management pruned both platforms to restore unit profits. At UnitedHealthcare, domestic membership fell by ~1.8 million lives YoY to 48.3 million at Q3 2026. That drop reflects planned Medicare Advantage county exits, ACA individual-exchange repricing, and ongoing state Medicaid redeterminations [4]. At Optum Health, management sold its remaining South American units and exited losing third-party value-based care contracts where coding math failed hurdle rates. Capital now focuses on core U.S. clinics, SCA Health surgery centers, and home-health integration after the December 2025 Amedisys close [12], [22].

Chapter 3

Go-to-Market Channels: Brokers, Employer RFPs, and Annual CMS Bidding

UnitedHealth Group's customer acquisition engine runs across three distinct sales funnels. In Employer & Individual, national benefits consultants (Mercer, Aon, Willis Towers Watson) and regional brokers run most mid-market and Fortune 500 RFPs. They grade network discount depth, administrative PEPM fees, and specialty-pharmacy integration [1], [2]. Because switching insurers disrupts employee doctor ties and HR payroll feeds, large-employer retention routinely exceeds 95%. In Medicare & Retirement, growth turns on the annual June CMS bid cycle. Actuaries project county medical cost trends 12 to 18 months ahead and factor in CMS's Final Rate Notice (+5.06% net average for CY2026; +2.48% net average for CY2027) [9], [10]. They then set supplemental benefits (dental, vision, Part B givebacks) to attract seniors during the October–December Annual Enrollment Period. Sales flow through captive agents, e-brokers, and the flagship AARP co-branding license. In Community & State, multi-year state Medicaid RFP awards grant regional contracts based on clinical quality, behavioral integration, and local provider adequacy.

Claims Float, Pharmacy Supply Chain, and Working-Capital Mechanics

Structurally, UnitedHealthcare enjoys negative working capital on its insurance book. CMS and commercial employers remit monthly premiums at the start of each coverage month. By contrast, medical claims take weeks to be incurred, submitted by hospitals, adjudicated, and paid. Measured by Days Claims Payable (DCP)—medical claims payable divided by average daily medical costs—UNH held 44.1 days at Q4 2025. Management rebuilt DCP to 48.6 days at Q1 2026 and held 47.0 days at both Q2 and Q3 2026 [3], [4], [5], [6]. That ~47-day claims float supports a massive investable cash and short-term bond portfolio. That portfolio produced $3,920 million in consolidated investment and other income in FY2025 [18]. However, the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) Medicare Part D redesign altered pharmacy cash-flow timing in 2025. Combined with billions in interest-free provider loans after the 2024 Change Healthcare attack, operating cash flow fell from $29.1 billion in 2023 to $24.2 billion in 2024 and $19.7 billion in FY2025 [14], [18].

On the pharmacy supply chain side, Optum Rx acts as the clearinghouse and buying pool between drugmakers, wholesale distributors, retail pharmacy chains, and plan sponsors. Historically, PBMs earned a mix of administrative fees, retail spread pricing, and retained manufacturer rebates. Facing intense FTC and bipartisan legislative scrutiny in 2025–2026 [13], Optum Rx sped up its shift toward transparent cost-plus and 100% rebate pass-through contracts. This move cuts reliance on opaque spread pricing across commercial accounts. Instead, Optum Rx leans on Optum Specialty Pharmacy dispensing volume, biosimilar adoption (such as Humira biosimilars), and per-claim service fees to defend its ~4.75% operating margin [18], [23].

Medicare Advantage Bid Mechanics & PBM Rebate Economics

In Medicare & Retirement ($171.3B FY2025 revenue), pricing discipline follows the annual CMS benchmark-and-bid auction [3], [9]. Each county carries a CMS benchmark rate adjusted by the health plan's Star Rating. Plans at 4.0+ stars earn a 5% quality bonus to their benchmark plus higher rebate retention [15]. Each June, UnitedHealthcare submits an actuarial bid showing its expected cost to deliver standard Part A and Part B benefits for an average-risk senior. When the bid falls below the county benchmark, CMS returns part of the gap as a rebate. The plan must return that rebate to enrollees via lower Part B premiums, $0 drug deductibles, or supplemental dental and vision benefits [9], [10]. If actual medical use exceeds the June bid, the insurer absorbs 100% of the cost overrun until the next calendar year.

In Optum Rx ($151.3B FY2025 revenue), unit economics are shifting from spread and retained-rebate models toward transparent fee structures [18], [23]. Under legacy PBM contracts, Optum Rx negotiated volume rebates from brand-name drugmakers, passing most to clients while keeping a small slice. Amid FTC enforcement and bipartisan PBM bills in 2025–2026 [13], Optum Rx rolled out 100% rebate pass-through and transparent cost-plus pharmacy pricing [23]. It protects its ~$7.2 billion operating profit pool through per-claim admin fees, mail-order dispensing efficiency, and high-margin specialty fulfillment for complex biologics.
