1. What the company does

Berkley collects premiums from businesses that want protection against accidents, lawsuits, property damage and injured workers, and it pays claims when those events happen. It runs two segments: Insurance, which made up 88.0% of net premiums written in 2025, and Reinsurance & Monoline Excess, which made up the other 12.0%.[4] Reinsurance means insuring other insurers. Monoline excess means covering only the top layer of a single type of risk. The company describes itself as among the largest commercial lines writers in the United States, and its chief executive says the 60 specialty businesses give it greater stability because the price cycles for different lines have decoupled.[1]

The money comes from two places. The first is underwriting profit, which is premiums earned minus claims and operating costs. In 2025 Berkley earned $12,446.9 million of premiums and reported a combined ratio of 90.7%, which means it kept about 9.3 cents of every premium dollar after claims and costs (our calculation).[4] The second is investment income. Premiums arrive before claims are paid, so the company holds and invests that cash, called float. Net investment income was $1,429.1 million in 2025 and $1,206.6 million in the first nine months of 2026, up 10.6% on the same period a year earlier (our calculation).[4][1] At September 30, 2026 the investment portfolio was $33.95 billion, with fixed-maturity holdings averaging an AA- rating and a duration of 3.3 years.[1]

The table of net premiums written shows what Berkley actually sells. Other liability, a line that includes umbrella and excess liability cover, is the largest line at $3,500.2 million for the nine months, and Berkley is now deliberately shifting it toward smaller accounts where competition is thinner.[1][2] Auto, workers' compensation and professional liability follow. In reinsurance, casualty premiums fell sharply because management says the ceding commissions on offer (the fee a reinsurer pays to the insurer that hands over the business) do not make economic sense.[2] Note that the lines are not equally easy to write: property is the line where prices are falling fastest, while casualty liability is where prices are rising and where claims have been surprising on the high side.[25][4]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Gross and net premiums written are the premiums on policies sold in the period, before and after paying reinsurers to take part of the risk. Net is what Berkley keeps.
  • Premiums earned are the part of written premiums that has been used up as time passes, because a one-year policy is earned evenly over twelve months.
  • Loss ratio is claims and claim-handling costs as a share of premiums earned. Expense ratio is commissions and running costs as a share of premiums earned. Combined ratio is the two added together. Below 100% means an underwriting profit.
  • Accident-year combined ratio excluding catastrophes measures only the policies written this year and leaves out big storms and old-year reserve changes, so it shows the current pricing quality.
  • Reserves are the balance-sheet estimate of claims already incurred but not yet paid. Prior-year development is the change in that estimate for older years: favorable means reserves were too high, adverse means too low.
  • Catastrophe losses are claims from large events such as hurricanes, wildfires and severe storms.
  • Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding. Return on equity (ROE) is a year's profit divided by shareholders' equity; Berkley's convention divides by equity at the start of the year.
  • Operating income is net income without investment gains and losses and currency gains and losses, which the company uses to show the underlying business.
  • Rate change is the average price change on renewing policies for the same amount of insured exposure; retention is the share of expiring premium that renews.
  • Unrealized loss is the drop in market value of bonds still held. It lowers book value now but reverses if the bonds are held to maturity at par.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business starts with consistency. The combined ratio has stayed between 89.3% and 90.7% in every year from 2022 to 2025, so the company has earned an underwriting profit through a period of inflation, social-inflation lawsuits and heavy catastrophe years.[7][5][4] Return on equity, measured on beginning-of-year equity, was 20.8%, 20.5%, 23.6% and 21.2% in those four years (our calculation).[7][5][4][8] The A.M. Best rating agency rates 33 of the group's insurance subsidiaries A+ (Superior), the second-highest of 15 grades.[4] Customers also stay: renewal retention has sat at about 80% to 81%, which management says shows the book is not churning.[2][9]

The second strength is the investment tailwind. The domestic bond portfolio is just under $24 billion and yields about 4.7%, while new money is going in at 80 to 100 basis points more, a basis point being one hundredth of a percentage point.[2] The portfolio shortens by close to a year every year as bonds mature, and its 3.3-year duration is still shorter than the roughly four-year life of its loss reserves, so management can add duration and lock in current yields.[2][1] Net investment income rose 9.2% in the third quarter, and the core portfolio income rose 12.0%.[1] This is why profit can keep rising even as underwriting margins narrow.

The weak points are pricing and claims. Berkley's average renewal rate increase excluding workers' compensation, a measure of how much it can raise prices, was 7.6% in 2025, 5.6% for the first half of 2026, 3.8% in the second quarter and about 4.0% in the third quarter.[4][9][1] Broker data show a softer market overall: Marsh reported global commercial rates down 5% in the third quarter, the ninth straight quarterly decline, with property down 10% and casualty up 1%.[25] Management itself says commercial property "is going to get uglier before it gets prettier" and expects January 1 reinsurance renewals to be challenging.[2] Reserves are the other worry: net favorable prior-year development was only $3.2 million in 2025 on $12.4 billion of earned premium, while the Insurance segment alone developed adversely by $44 million, mostly in other liability and auto liability tied to social inflation.[4]

Pricing by line of business: where the cycle is turning

A price cycle is the repeating swing between periods when insurers can raise prices and periods when competition pushes them down. Berkley says the cycles for its different product lines have decoupled.[1] The broker data agree. In the second quarter the Council of Insurance Agents and Brokers found premiums down an average 2.0% (1.2% in the first quarter), commercial property down 6.3%, but umbrella up 5.3% and commercial auto up 4.5%.[26] Chubb reported North America commercial property pricing down 4.2% and casualty up 5.7% in the third quarter, and its North America commercial premiums fell 2.7%.[27] Berkley's own mix leans toward casualty (our reading of the premium table), which is the part of the market still firming, and it is writing less property reinsurance, so it is better placed than a pure property writer.[2][1]

Workers' compensation is the soft spot with a possible turn: management is "pleased to see the bottoming out and incremental firming in California" while the rest of the country lags, and directors and officers liability is in the process of bottoming.[2] Commercial auto remains the problem line, because the CEO said in January that early-2025 signs of improvement "proved to be a mirage" and hoped for a bottom by the end of 2026, and in October the auto line was again the main source of adverse reserve development.[11][2] What this means for an owner: pricing power is fading in property and reinsurance, holding in casualty, and still short of the claims trend in auto, so margins are more likely to narrow slowly than to widen from here.[2]

The 10-K and 10-Q measure these percentages excluding shares held in a deferred-compensation trust, so the figures are not strictly comparable with the 15% target.[14][4][16][9]

This is a support because MSI's shares are voted as the Berkley family recommends, apart from limited exceptions, which concentrates voting control with the family, and because the board accepted an MSI designee, Andrew Carrier, who was elected on June 3, 2026.[15][4][13] It is a risk for three reasons. The family can determine how MSI's shares vote even on an extraordinary transaction, which the 10-K itself lists as a barrier to an unsolicited takeover, so minority holders may not receive a takeover premium.[4] Mr. Berkley, Jr. now holds the Chairman, Chief Executive and President roles together. MSI also trades with Berkley: in 2025 Berkley ceded $70 million of written premiums to MSI or its affiliates and assumed $12 million from them, which the 10-K describes as arm's-length.[4] We treat the arrangement as neutral for the valuation and positive for stability, and we have no evidence of an accounting or governance failure.

3. The numbers, in plain words

An insurer's accounts do not read like a normal company's, so the usual shortcuts need translating. Gross margin and earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) have no clear meaning when the main cost is claims that arrive years after the sale. Free cash flow (FCF), meaning the cash left after running a business and buying equipment, is also misleading here. Berkley's cash flow from operations, the cash a business makes before financing, was $3,582.6 million in 2025, but $1,766.9 million of it came from growth in claim reserves and $321.7 million from unearned premiums, which is money held for policyholders.[4] Net debt, meaning debt minus cash, has the same problem because much of the cash belongs to future claims. We therefore use return on equity, the combined ratio, book value growth, capital returned and the debt share of total capital, and we say so where a standard test is not applicable.

Over five years the numbers show a business that has compounded. Total revenue rose from $9,455.5 million in 2021 to $14,707.9 million in 2025, which is 11.7% a year over four years and 9.6% a year over the three years to 2025 (our calculation).[6][4] Net income to common stockholders rose from $1,022.5 million to $1,779.4 million, and diluted earnings per share (EPS), meaning profit divided by shares, rose from $2.44 to $4.45 after adjusting 2021 for the July 2024 three-for-two share split (our calculation).[6][5][4] Growth in gross premiums written slowed each year since 2022: 11.3%, 8.9%, 9.6% and 6.3%, then 3.3% for the first nine months of 2026 (our calculation).[7][5][4][1]

The sources of profit changed. Net investment income more than doubled, from $671.6 million to $1,429.1 million, an increase of $757.4 million that equals 76% of the $997.6 million rise in pre-tax income over the four years (our calculation).[6][4] The combined ratio did the opposite: it crept up from 89.3% in 2022 to 90.7% in 2025 and was 90.5% for nine months of 2026.[7][4][1] In plain terms, underwriting margins have been flat to slightly lower since 2022 while interest income has supplied the growth, which is why the reinvestment of a $24 billion bond book at higher yields is central to the thesis.[2]

Debt and capital are conservative. Debt fell from 33% of total capital at the end of 2021 to 23% at the end of 2025 and 22% at June 30, 2026. No money is drawn on the $300 million credit line, which now runs to June 2031. The next large bond maturity after $7 million due in 2026 is $250 million in 2037.[8][4][9][17] Dilution, meaning a rise in share count from issuing new shares, is absent: diluted shares were 391.3 million in the third quarter of 2026 against 400.2 million a year earlier.[1] Book value per share has grown at 14.8% a year over three years (our calculation), but it was almost flat in 2026 ($25.72 to $25.87) because rising interest rates created after-tax unrealized investment losses of $928 million.[7][6][5][4][1]

Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

For banks and insurers the process replaces ROIC with return on equity or on tangible equity, which strips out goodwill, and treats the free cash flow and net debt tests as not applicable. Hard red lines can reject a stock outright: a distressed balance sheet, a Stage 4 breakdown in the price, or an accounting or governance failure.[4] Several planning terms appear in the table. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the potential gain divided by the potential loss from a planned entry to the stop loss. The stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong and we sell. The entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy. A breakout is a move above a recent high on rising volume. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend phase, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend phase. A base is a flat stretch of trading before a move, and its pivot is the top of that stretch. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could fail, and this chapter names the weakest one for each, says what evidence would show it is wrong, and gives the date by which we will know. The bear case is the story in which the assumptions fail together. Prices in commercial insurance keep softening, reserves for old accident years need to be topped up, the exit multiple compresses to 12.5 times, and 2027 earnings per share come in near $4.10 instead of $4.86. That bear case is worth $51.25 a share, 30.1% below the close, and we give it a 30% probability.[2][4]

Flaw 1: reserves turn out too thin for the claims trend

Reserves are management's estimate of claims still to be paid, and they stood at $23.6 billion at September 30, 2026, up from $22.2 billion at the end of 2025.[1] The cushion is small: net favorable development was only $3.2 million in 2025 and $2.7 million in the first half of 2026, while the Insurance segment developed adversely by $44 million in 2025, concentrated in other liability for accident years 2017 through 2022 and auto liability for 2021 through 2023.[4][9] The CEO said in October that the adverse development was primarily commercial auto and its spill into excess and umbrella cover, and that it is premature to declare victory on claims trend.[2]

If reserves had to rise by 3%, the charge would be about $708 million before tax, $545 million after a 23% tax, or $1.39 a share, which is 29% of our $4.86 central earnings estimate (our calculation). That would be a one-time hit to earnings and book value, and it would also force higher loss assumptions on new business, which lifts the combined ratio in later years. This is the flaw most likely to cut the multiple, because a reserve problem tells investors the 20% return on equity was overstated.

Flaw 2: the price cycle turns faster than investment income can offset it

Marsh reports that global commercial rates fell for a ninth straight quarter, with property down 10%, and Berkley's own average rate increase excluding workers' compensation has fallen from 7.6% in 2025 to about 4.0%.[25][4][1] In January the CEO said that competition in property catastrophe reinsurance, where Berkley's own main treaty saw a 19% risk-adjusted rate decrease, seemed to be spilling over into casualty. In October he said the January 1 reinsurance renewals would likely prove challenging.[11][2] Berkley buys more reinsurance than it sells, which is a partial hedge for property, but the hedge does nothing for casualty.[2]

Each one-point rise in the combined ratio costs about $127 million before tax, or $0.25 a share, on $12.7 billion of annual earned premiums (our calculation). Reinvesting the bond book at higher yields adds roughly $0.43 a share a year once fully phased in on our numbers, so investment income offsets roughly 1.7 points of margin erosion, not the 3 points in our bear case. If casualty rates stop rising while loss costs keep climbing, the offset is not enough.

Flaw 3: ownership changes or a rate shock hit the multiple
