1. What the company does

Berkley sells insurance to businesses. A customer pays a premium, and Berkley promises to pay covered losses, such as a lawsuit against a contractor or a fire at a warehouse. The company is a group of about 60 separately run specialty businesses: 53 were built inside the company and seven were bought.[11] Each one focuses on a niche, a type of customer or a type of risk, and prices that niche on its own. In the nine months to 2026-09-30 the group wrote $9.87 billion of net premiums written, which is premium kept after paying reinsurers (insurers of insurers) to take part of the risk. Of that, 89.2% came from the Insurance segment and 10.8% from Reinsurance & Monoline Excess, the unit that insures other insurers and writes excess policies that sit above a primary policy.[1]

Berkley has three sources of profit. The first is underwriting profit, the premium left after claims and running costs. The yardstick is the combined ratio, the share of each premium dollar paid out in claims and costs; below 100% is a profit. Berkley's combined ratio was 90.8% in the third quarter of 2026, so about nine cents of every premium dollar was left before tax and before any investment income.[1] The second source is investment income. Berkley holds premiums until claims are paid, and it earned $383.5 million on those assets in the quarter, against $296 million of underwriting profit. Investments therefore produced about 56% of the two combined.[3][1] The third source is fees and non-insurance businesses, which brought in $421.8 million of revenue in nine months against $383.4 million of matching expenses, a small contribution.[1]

The central fact about an insurer is that it is paid first and pays later. Liability claims can take years to settle, so Berkley carries reserves for losses and loss expenses, its own estimate of claims it still owes, of $23.6 billion at 2026-09-30. That is about 2.5 times its $9.6 billion of shareholder equity.[1] Premiums not yet earned add to the pool: the unearned premium balance was $6.7 billion at the end of 2025, so the company holds roughly $30 billion of other people's money.[11] That pool, called float, is why investment income is large. It is also the risk: reserves are estimates, and if claims for past years cost more than estimated, the extra cost reduces current profit. The portfolio is $34.0 billion of net invested assets, 76.6% of it in fixed-maturity (bond) securities, with an average rating of AA- and an average duration of 3.3 years, a little shorter than the life of the reserves, which the chief executive put at just under four years.[1][3]

Ownership matters here more than at most large companies. The Berkley family, the Estate of William R. Berkley and related trusts reported 95,557,324 shares, or 25.67%, on 2026-07-21. Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance (MSI), a Japanese insurer, reported 58,780,450 shares, or 15.8%, as of 2026-06-04.[16] Under agreements made in 2025, MSI's shares are voted as the Berkley family recommends except in limited cases, so roughly 41% of the votes sit with one group.[17][11] The founder and executive chairman, William R. Berkley, died on 2026-06-09 at age 80, and his son W. Robert Berkley, Jr., already chief executive and president, became chairman.[18] This ownership structure makes a takeover unlikely, and the annual report says so as a risk to holders who might want a bid.[11]

Who buys, and why they stay

Berkley's customers are businesses, usually reached through independent agents and wholesale brokers rather than directly. The chief executive said on the third-quarter call that the renewal retention ratio, the share of expiring policies that renew, "just doesn't move" and sits at 80%, so the book is not churning.[3] He also said the group is shifting toward smaller accounts by design, because larger accounts attract more price competition and the company can handle small accounts efficiently with technology.[3] Alternative routes, such as selling direct to customers or at the point of sale, exist but are not a cornerstone of distribution.[3]

How the groups are run and how technology fits

Each of the 60 businesses sets its own prices and appetite, which lets the group grow where margins are good and shrink where they are not. The chief financial officer pointed out that some units are growing while others shrink at the same time, so the mix can move the accident-year loss ratio by tens of basis points in a quarter.[3] A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. Management also said technology has made intake of new business more than 20% more efficient and that more than half of claims settle for $5,000 or less, which points to less manual handling over time.[3]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 meter of how fast a price has been moving; readings near 30 are called oversold and near 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical daily price swing in dollars; we use it to measure distances in units that fit the stock.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend on a four-stage trend scale, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. The ledger gives the Stage 2 label when the price is above both long averages, so the label can sit beside a failed momentum gate, as it does for Berkley.
  • A base is a sideways range that a price builds before a move; the pivot is the top level of that range, and a breakout is a move above the pivot on strong volume.
  • A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better; the entry zone is the band of prices where we plan to buy.
  • Reward-to-risk is the possible gain divided by the possible loss, (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop); the same idea is also called asymmetry.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is a company's profit divided across all its shares.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before financing and accounting adjustments; net debt is interest-bearing debt minus cash.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is the profit earned on each dollar invested in a business; for an insurer we use return on equity instead.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is the cash left after running the business and buying assets.
  • Combined ratio: claims plus costs as a share of premiums earned; under 100% means an underwriting profit.
  • Accident-year combined ratio excluding catastrophes: the same ratio for claims from the current year only, before storm and other catastrophe losses and before changes to old reserves. It is the cleanest read of underlying profit.
  • Catastrophe losses: losses from large events such as hurricanes and wildfires.
  • Net premiums written and earned: written premium is what was sold in the period; earned premium is the part of it that has been covered so far.
  • Reserves: the insurer's estimate of claims it still has to pay; prior-year development is the change in that estimate for older years, adverse if it rises.
  • Rate increase: the average change in price on renewing policies, before changes in the amount of coverage.
  • Float: premiums and reserves the insurer holds before paying claims, which it invests.
  • Book value per share (BVPS): shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding; return on equity (ROE) is a year's profit divided by equity.
  • Operating income: Berkley's profit measure that leaves out investment gains and losses and currency gains and losses.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Berkley's edge is consistent underwriting across many niches, and the record supports it. The calendar-year combined ratio, the full-year version of the profit yardstick, was 89.6% in 2021, 89.3% in 2022, 89.7% in 2023, 90.3% in 2024 and 90.7% in 2025, and it was 90.5% for the first nine months of 2026.[12][7][1] Every one of those years was an underwriting profit. The cleaner measure, the accident-year combined ratio excluding catastrophes, has sat in a narrow band of 87.6% to 88.4% across the seven quarterly releases we recorded between 2024 and 2026, and it was 88.1% in the third quarter of 2026.[10][9][8][7][6][4][1] The drift is real but small: the reported ratio has risen about one point since 2022 as catastrophe losses grew from $195 million in 2023 to $336 million in 2025 and rate gains faded.

Returns on equity back this up. Net income to common stockholders divided by average equity was 20.6% in 2022, 19.5% in 2023, 22.2% in 2024 and 19.7% in 2025, our calculation from the annual reports. The company's own ratios use beginning-of-year equity and were 21.2% in 2025 and 23.6% in 2024, so ours are lower in years when equity grew.[13][12][11] The company's own annualized operating return was 20.2% for the first nine months of 2026, against a stated target of 15%.[1][6] The proxy statement records an average annual gain in book value per share, with dividends included, of 16.7% since 1973, and says 93% of the chief executive's pay and 84% of other named executives' pay is performance-based.[15] Executives also do not receive shares from vested restricted stock units until they leave, which keeps their holdings tied to long-term results.[15] Financial strength is high: 33 insurance subsidiaries carry A.M. Best's A+ (Superior) rating, the second highest of 15 grades.[11]

The investment portfolio is now a second engine, and it is accelerating. Net investment income was $383.5 million in the third quarter, up 9.2% on the year, and income from the core portfolio of bonds, stocks, cash and real estate rose 12.0%.[1] The domestic bond portfolio is just under $24 billion with a book yield of about 4.7%, and management said new money is invested 80 to 100 basis points higher.[3] Left alone, the bond portfolio shortens by just under a year each year as bonds mature, so the higher rates feed through gradually. If the whole $24 billion repriced 0.9 percentage points higher, pre-tax income would rise by about $216 million a year, or roughly $0.42 per share after tax, our illustration.[3] That is a tailwind, not a promise, because it depends on rates staying high.

The weak points are price, old claims and ownership. Price first: average rate increases excluding workers' compensation, which lifts the price of expiring policies, were 8.4% in the third quarter of 2024, 7.6% for 2025, 7.2% in the first quarter of 2026, 3.8% in the second quarter and 4.0% in the third.[10][7][6][5][1] Consolidated net premiums written grew only 1.3% in the third quarter, because Reinsurance & Monoline Excess shrank 11.6% as management declined business it thought was priced too low.[1][3] Old claims second: the quarterly filing shows adverse development in the Insurance segment in other liability and auto liability, with the other liability part concentrated in accident years 2019 to 2023.[14] Ownership third, covered in Chapter 1: a single block of about 41% of votes limits outside influence and, since the founder's death, adds some uncertainty about how the family will act over time.

Where the edge is thinner than the headline

Berkley does not have the lowest combined ratio. Its third-quarter combined ratio of 90.8% compares with 82.5% at Arch, 84.1% for Chubb's property and casualty business, 86.2% at RLI and 86.8% at Travelers, and only Cincinnati Financial, at 90.9%, is close.[1][31][29][33][30][32] The definitions differ a little between companies, so the gap is a guide, not a precise number. It means Berkley's high return on equity rests on its investment income and on holding about 2.5 times as much reserve as equity, more than on a unique underwriting margin. That structure is fine while interest rates and reserves behave, and it is the reason the next two chapters look at investment income and reserve development so closely.

Capital return is real but now price-sensitive

Berkley returned $970.5 million to shareholders in 2025, including $567.6 million of special dividends, and $728.6 million in the first nine months of 2026.[7][1] In the third quarter, though, it bought back only $21.1 million of stock and paid no special dividend, after $302.4 million of buybacks in the first quarter and $111.5 million in the second.[1][6][4] The chief executive said the company does not buy a set amount regardless of price, that it still holds a ten-figure amount of excess capital, and that analysts should not read too much into one quarter.[3] The average price paid was about $67.29 a share over nine months and $68.17 in the third quarter, both below today's $71.63 price, which suggests a pullback toward the mid-$60s would meet a price-aware buyer (our reading).[1]

Ownership, the founder and key-person risk

William R. Berkley founded the company in 1967 and died on 2026-06-09. His son, who has run the company as chief executive, now holds the chairman's seat as well.[18] The estate's shares are part of the 25.67% family position, so what the estate does with them is an open question we cannot see in the filings.[16] MSI's 15.8% stake is above the 15% its agreement provided for, so its buying looks complete, but MSI's future intentions are not disclosed.[16][11] A seller of a large block, whether the estate or MSI, would be a risk to the share price even if it did not change the business. We treat this as a governance and supply risk, not a business flaw.

2B. Company specifics, operating KPIs, and management walk-the-talk

Layer 2 of our process asks which three to five numbers decide whether this particular business is healthy, and scores each one PASS (1.0 point), WATCH (0.5) or FAIL (0.0). An insurer is not judged on factory output or software subscriptions. It is judged on whether its policies make money, whether its reserves are honest, whether it can keep raising prices faster than claims costs rise, what its investments earn, and what it returns on its equity. We chose five numbers on that basis. The rule is that a company qualifies when the weighted score is at least 3.0 out of 5.0 and at most one number is a FAIL.

Berkley scores two PASS, three WATCH and no FAIL, or 3.5 out of 5.0, which qualifies as PASS_WITH_WATCH. That is the same count we recorded in the 2026-10-23 report, and we reached it again from the new evidence, not by copying it. The business is healthy. The three WATCH grades are all about direction, not level: reserves for old casualty years keep producing small adverse items, price increases have slowed from 7.6% to about 4%, and book value per share has not grown for a year.[14][1][7]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management's promises over the past two years were mostly specific and mostly kept. The pattern is that Berkley says what it will do about price, growth and capital, then does it, even when the result looks worse on one measure. In the second quarter of 2026 the chief executive said the company would keep its "foot on the rate pedal" but would not press as hard, and the rate increase that quarter was 3.8%, in line with what he had said.[5] That is the right way to judge a promise: it was met, and the lower rate was the price of the promise.

Two items deserve a harder look. The first is the pledge to exceed a 15% return on equity "for the foreseeable future", made in April 2026; nine months of operating return at 20.2% are well above it, but only a short time has passed.[6][1] The second is capital return. The company spent $970.5 million on dividends and buybacks in 2025 and $728.6 million so far in 2026, but third-quarter buying was light and the chief executive explained that the company does not buy mechanically.[7][1][3] We do not count that as a broken promise, because the company never promised a fixed amount.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of results show premiums and investment income growing together. Total revenues rose from $11.17 billion in 2022 to $14.71 billion in 2025. That is 9.6% a year over those three years (our calculation).[12][11] Net income to common stockholders rose from $1.38 billion to $1.78 billion, or 8.8% a year over the same three years. Measured from 2021, when revenue was $9.46 billion and net income $1.02 billion, the four-year rates are 11.7% and 14.9%. Two things drove that. Net premiums earned grew from $8.11 billion to $12.45 billion. Net investment income more than doubled, from $672 million to $1,429 million, as interest rates rose.[12][11] The second driver should repeat more slowly. The earned yield on investments was 4.6% in 2024 and 4.5% in 2025. So the recent gain came from a larger asset base more than from higher yields.[11]

Per-share figures are better than totals because Berkley has bought back stock. Diluted EPS was $4.45 in 2025. It was $3.60 in the first nine months of 2026, against $3.32 a year earlier.[11][1] Earlier years need care. Berkley split its stock 3-for-2 in July 2024. We divide the EPS as filed for 2021 and 2022 by 1.5 to compare them (our adjustment).[11][12] The share count helps too. Diluted shares averaged 393.0 million in nine months of 2026, against 400.3 million a year earlier. That is a fall of 1.8%.[1]

Cash flow needs a caution. Operating cash flow was $3.58 billion in 2025. After $170 million spent on property and equipment, free cash flow was $3.41 billion, or 23.2% of revenue.[11] For an insurer, that figure is mostly growth in the float. Reserves for losses rose $1.77 billion in 2025, and unearned premiums rose $0.32 billion.[11] This is money held for policyholders until claims are paid. It is not profit free to distribute. So we do not use FCF margin as a gate for an insurer, and our scoring rules mark it not applicable. Operating cash flow was $2.20 billion in nine months of 2026, against $2.59 billion a year earlier. The chief executive said a very large tax payment and a few one-time items explain the third-quarter dip.[1][3]

The balance sheet is conservative for its size. Total debt was $2.84 billion at 2026-09-30. That is 22.8% of debt plus common equity, down from 32.9% in 2021.[1][13] Pre-tax income covered interest about 19.5 times over the last twelve months (our calculation). The revolving credit facility was extended in June 2026 to June 9, 2031.[1][19] The weaker spot is equity. Common equity of $9.61 billion is below the year-end figure of $9.70 billion, even after profits. After-tax unrealized investment losses now stand at $928 million, up from $125 million at the end of 2025. And $729 million went back to shareholders in the nine months.[1]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on an assumption that could be wrong. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate. It builds the strongest honest case against owning the stock, called the red-team case. And it says what evidence would show us we are wrong, and when that evidence will arrive. We rank the three scenarios below by how much damage they could do to the central value of $68.28.

Fatal flaw 1: older casualty claims need more reserves

Reserves for unpaid claims are the largest number on the balance sheet. Net of reinsurance they were $19.7 billion at 2026-06-30.[14] The company has reported adverse development on older claims in other liability and auto liability. For other liability, the affected accident years run from 2019 to 2023. The drivers were umbrella and excess claims and social inflation, which is the rise in claim size from litigation and jury awards.[14] In 2025 the Insurance segment's adverse development was $44 million.[11] The third-quarter call pointed to commercial auto as the main source of another round. An analyst put it at about $15 million in Insurance, offset in reinsurance.[3] Those amounts are small. But they come from the lines where errors are largest and slowest to surface.

The arithmetic shows the exposure. One percent of net reserves is $197 million before tax, or about $0.39 per share after tax. A 5% shortfall would be $987 million before tax. That is about $1.94 per share, which is 7.5% of book value per share and 7.9% of common equity.[14][1] Our bear case is a combined ratio 2.0 points worse, split about 1 point of reserve charge and about 1 point of price squeeze. The reserve half is about $127 million, or 0.64% of net reserves. If the true shortfall were near 5%, the stock would fall below our $54.90 bear value. We cannot call that likely. But it is the pattern that has hurt other liability insurers.

Fatal flaw 2: prices fall behind claims costs

Berkley's accident-year combined ratio has held near 88%. Much of the credit goes to price increases of 7% to 8% that outran claims costs in 2024 and 2025. Rate increases excluding workers' compensation have since fallen to 3.8% in the second quarter and 4.0% in the third.[7][5][1] Management says loss trends are "starting to quiet down" outside auto. It also called it too early to declare victory. It described property as heading for a worse market and reinsurance as already competitive.[3] Each point of combined ratio is $127 million before tax and $0.25 per share. So three points is $0.75, or 15.4% of 2027 consensus earnings.[1][22]

Two things soften the risk. Berkley buys more reinsurance than it sells, which hedges the reinsurance market. And liability lines have historically held prices longer than property, according to the chief executive.[3] Two things make it worse. The improvement in the Insurance segment's accident-year loss ratio came mainly from non-catastrophe property results. The chief executive confirmed that the chief financial officer was managing expectations, so the level may not repeat.[3] And the January 1 reinsurance renewals, which management said it awaits with concern, could push competition into primary insurance.[3]

Fatal flaw 3: the investment engine stalls or marks hurt

Investments made about 56% of the third-quarter total of underwriting profit plus investment income. A conviction tier ranks our confidence, and so the risk allowed. Budgets run from 100 bps for TIER_1 to 35 bps for TIER_4. At $65.90 with a $64.75 stop, the distance is 1.75%. The 35 bps budget alone would allow 20.0%, so the 2.0% ceiling binds. The let-winners-run rule says not to sell a rising stock only because its RSI passes 70 or because its weight crosses 15%. That holds while the business and estimates hold. The weight may rise to 22% while the stop trails the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average. A 2.0% position will not reach those limits, so the rule is stated for completeness.[22]
