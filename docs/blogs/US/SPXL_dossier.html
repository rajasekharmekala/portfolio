1. What the company does

SPXL is the Direxion Daily S&P 500 Bull 3X ETF. An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a basket that trades on a stock exchange like a share. Rafferty Asset Management runs it, and its stated goal is 300% of the daily performance of the S&P 500 Index, before fees and expenses[1]. If the index rises 1% in a day, the fund aims to rise 3%. On October 16, 2026 the S&P 500 fell 1.09% and SPXL fell 3.39%, about 0.1 point more than three times the index move[30][6]. The fund began trading on November 5, 2008 and was called the Direxion Daily S&P 500 Bull 3X Shares until the February 27, 2026 prospectus, which uses the new name “ETF”[2][1]. The objective did not change with the name. It covers one trading day only, and the prospectus says the fund does not seek that result for any other period[1].

The fund owns stocks and swaps. A swap is a contract with a bank in which the fund receives the index’s gain or loss in return for a financing charge. In the Direxion holdings file dated October 16 (its prices are those of October 15) the fund had 24,750,001 shares outstanding and net assets of about $7.178 billion, which is our arithmetic from the file’s weights and agrees with the $7.18 billion on a data page[3][6]. Of that, $5.04 billion, or 70.2% of net assets, sat in 502 S&P 500 stocks. Seven swap legs carried $16.49 billion of index exposure, or 229.7% of net assets, and about $2.09 billion, or 29.1%, was held in cash and Treasury funds[3]. The stocks and swaps add up to 3.0 times net assets. At the close each day Rafferty rebalances so that exposure returns to 300% of net assets[1]. A simple picture is cruise control that is reset every evening to three times the speed reached that day. That reset is what makes the fund useful for a one-day bet and costly for a long hold.

The people who pay the fund are traders who want a short, amplified bet on the whole US market. Direxion charges a management fee of 0.75% a year. With other expenses and the fees of funds it holds, the gross total is 0.95%, and a contract waiver through September 1, 2027 brings the net figure to 0.84%[1][2]. The annual report put the actual expense ratio for the year to October 31, 2025 at 0.81%, and the adviser earned $17.5 million of net advisory fees in the six months to April 30, 2026[4][5]. Those are not the full cost. The fund borrows about two dollars of market exposure for every dollar of net assets, and the overnight bank rate (SOFR) was 3.89% on October 15[29]. Our own measurement from prices is larger: over the last 250 trading days SPXL returned 34.43% while three times the index’s daily moves, compounded, would have given 44.46%, a gap of 10.04 points, or about 6.95% a year[13][14]. That gap covers fees, financing net of the dividends the fund receives, and tracking gaps, and price data alone cannot split them.

The engine behind the fund is the S&P 500, an index of 503 stocks at December 31, 2025, weighted by the value of the shares the public can trade and rebalanced quarterly[1]. It covers roughly 83% of the total value of US public companies, which Wikipedia puts at $70.3 trillion at August 12, 2026 for the index[27]. It is top-heavy: the ten largest stocks were 38.9% of its value at August 31, 2026, and information technology was 38.03% of it at June 30[27][2]. This book treats SPXL as the framework requires for leveraged funds. It studies the index, the price trend and the decay risk, and it measures the framework’s company tests on the index, not on a business the fund does not have. The positions replay of 2026-10-16 holds 3 shares at Schwab, so this is a current holding and the verdict has two parts: what to do with new money and what to do with the shares you own[34].

The measures this book uses

Several measures come up again in later chapters, so here they are in plain words. Earnings per share (EPS) is a company’s profit divided by its number of shares, and for an index it is the profit of all members, scaled to the index. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash a business has left after paying for its equipment. Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money put into the business. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is operating profit before borrowing costs, taxes and the wear on equipment, and net debt is debt minus cash. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given to staff in shares, and price-to-earnings is the price paid for each dollar of yearly earnings.

The chart terms are these. The relative strength index (RSI) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has been moving, and below 30 is called oversold. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing in dollars. The 20-day moving average is the average closing price of the last 20 sessions, the 50-day moving average is the same over 50 sessions, and the 200-day moving average is the same over 200. A base is a flat floor that a price builds before a move, a pivot is the top of that floor, and a breakout is a close above it. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. A retest is a pullback that tests a broken level from the other side.

The decision terms are these. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is cut. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better and does not chase. An entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the gain you could make divided by the loss you would take: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). A measured move is a target projected from the height of a base. The framework’s fixed floors live in a configuration file, and a floor is a number a candidate must clear.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Leveraged ETF — a fund that aims to return a multiple (here 3 times) of one day’s move in something else.
  • Daily reset — the fund rebuilds its 3x exposure at every close, so gains and losses compound day after day.
  • Swap — a contract with a bank that pays the fund the index’s gain or loss in return for a financing fee.
  • Counterparty — the bank on the other side of a swap; if it fails to pay, the fund can lose what it is owed.
  • Volatility decay — the slow loss a daily-reset fund suffers when the index swings up and down, even if the index ends where it began.
  • Net asset value (NAV) — the value of everything the fund owns, per share, calculated at the close.
  • SOFR — the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, the overnight bank borrowing rate that sets the fund’s financing cost.
  • S&P 500 — the index of 503 large US stocks that SPXL follows; SPY is the exchange-traded fund the ledger uses as its benchmark for it.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A fund has no brand or factory, so its strength is how well it does the one job it promises and how it compares with rivals doing the same job. On both counts SPXL is sound. Three large funds offer 3x daily exposure to the S&P 500 or a close cousin: SPXL ($7.18 billion of assets, 0.84% net expenses), ProShares UltraPro S&P500 or UPRO ($5.61 billion, 0.88%) and, for a lower multiple, ProShares Ultra S&P 500 or SSO at 2x ($9.06 billion, 0.84%)[6][9][10]. On October 16, 2026 the S&P 500 proxy SPY fell 1.11%, so a perfect 3x fund would have fallen 3.33%. SPXL fell 3.39% and UPRO fell 3.36%, so the two funds are interchangeable on tracking[13][15][14]. SPXL’s edge is trading volume and a 0.04-point cost advantage over UPRO. Over the last 63 sessions SPXL traded about $455 million a day against about $287 million for UPRO and $214 million for SSO (our arithmetic from prices and volumes)[13][15][16]. A net expense gap of 0.04 points is too narrow to decide anything.

The product is cheap for what it is. A net expense ratio of 0.84% compares with 0.09% for the plain S&P 500 fund SPY, so the 3x wrapper costs about 0.75 points a year more in fees alone[2][8]. For that price the buyer gets a fund with a record that runs back to November 2008, a regulated structure, daily creation and redemption in blocks of 50,000 shares and a market price that stayed within three cents of net asset value on October 15 ($290.00 against $290.03)[1][2]. The weakness is not the fee. It is the daily reset and the financing cost, which the next subsections measure.

The seed’s theme tags called the fund a mega-cap compounder with a broad-market earnings story. That describes the index, not the fund. The index earned as-reported profit of $298.03 per share over the twelve months to June 2026, up from $250.20 at the end of 2025, and the fund captures that growth only when it arrives smoothly[20][24]. A fund that is reset every day turns the index’s zigzags into a steady cost, so an owner needs the index to climb in a fairly straight line, or to climb far enough to pay for the zigzags.

Strength: the fund does what it says each day

The strongest evidence is the daily match. On October 12 to 16, 2026 SPY moved -0.06%, -0.01%, -1.08%, +0.28% and -1.11%, so a perfect 3x fund would have moved -0.18%, -0.03%, -3.24%, +0.84% and -3.33%. SPXL moved -0.20%, +0.02%, -3.29%, +0.82% and -3.39%[13][14]. Over the last 250 trading days the average daily gap from three times SPY was -0.03 percentage points, the average absolute gap was 0.07 points, and the worst gap was 0.78 points on December 19, 2025. Over two years the worst gap was 3.94 points on April 9, 2025, the day SPY rose 10.50% and SPXL rose 27.56%[13][14]. These are our measurements from closing prices. The market price also stays close to value: the issuer’s page gives a net asset value of $290.03 and a close of $290.00 on October 15, a 0.01% discount[2].

Weakness: the daily reset costs more when the index is volatile

The prospectus states the weakness plainly. Over a period longer than one day, the fund’s return is the result of each day’s compounded return, and the fund will lose money if the index is flat[1]. Its table shows a one-year loss of 3.0% if the index goes nowhere at 10% volatility, 17.1% at 25% volatility and 95% at 100% volatility[1]. The rule of thumb behind the table is simple. For a 3x fund the yearly loss from compounding is about three times the square of the volatility: 3 times 0.25 squared is 0.1875, and e to the minus 0.1875 is 0.829, which is the 17.1% loss. FINRA warned in 2009 that daily-reset funds typically are unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them for longer than one trading session[32].

The S&P 500’s volatility is moderate at the moment. Measured from SPY’s daily closes it is 10.5% a year over the last 20 sessions, 11.1% over 63, 12.7% over 250 and 16.5% over 500, which includes the April 2025 shock[14]. The prospectus puts the five-year figure at 16.96% and the highest calendar year at 24.19%[1]. Real prices show what that does. Over 250 sessions SPY rose 14.88%, which is 44.64% when simply tripled. Compounding each day’s tripled return gives 44.46%, a drag of only 0.18 points, because the year trended upward with few reversals. SPXL actually returned 34.43%, another 10.04 points lower, which is fees, financing and tracking gaps[13][14]. Chapter 3 turns this into a break-even number.

What a 3x fund does in a drawdown

A drawdown is the fall from a peak to the next low. A 3x fund falls more than three times as far over a long decline, because each day’s loss is taken from a smaller balance, and it recovers slowly because gains also start from a smaller balance. The clearest case in our price data is the winter of 2024-25. SPXL closed at $189.22 on December 6, 2024 and at $96.11 on April 8, 2025, a fall of 49.21%. The S&P 500 proxy SPY fell 19.00% from its February 19, 2025 close to the same April 8 low[13][14]. SPXL took until August 12, 2025 to regain its December 6 close, while the S&P 500 index regained its December 6 level on June 24, 2025[13][19]. On April 4, 2025 the fund lost 18.03% in one session while SPY lost 5.85%[13][14].

The prospectus shows the extreme case. SPXL’s worst quarter was -60.54% (the quarter ended March 31, 2020) and its best was +60.90% (the quarter ended June 30, 2020)[1]. It also warns that the fund can lose its entire value in a single day if the index falls more than 33%[1]. For an owner of 3 shares worth $840.51, a repeat of the 2025 drawdown of 49.21% would take the position to about $427, a loss of about $414, which is 0.5% of net worth (our arithmetic on $840.51 and $82,699.61)[34]. The same fund rose 27.56% on April 9, 2025, so the drawdown also contained the fund’s best day. That symmetry is why we manage the position with a stop and not with a forecast.

Direxion promised a 3x daily result, a cost cap and an unchanged index, and it warned that the fund should not be expected to give 3x for longer periods. On the first promise it delivered, as the tracking numbers show. 3. The numbers, in plain words

A fund has no sales or profit of its own, so the numbers that matter come in groups. The first group is the fund’s own facts: its size, its price against value and its cost. The second is the S&P 500’s five-year record of returns, earnings, price and yield. The third is the fund’s record against the index, and the fourth is the volatility and drag measured from real prices. We then show how a position fits the portfolio, test the index and the fund against the framework’s Layer 1 floors, and compare the peers.

The financial tests apply to the index because a fund has no operations. The index’s return on invested capital, free cash flow margin, net debt to EBITDA and stock pay as a share of revenue are not published as aggregates in any source we could fetch, and S&P Dow Jones Indices’ own pages refused our requests, so those four floors appear as N/A with the reason and are not scored as passes or fails. What can be measured is as-reported earnings per share and the price paid for them, which the five-year table shows. We use the tactical asymmetry floor of 3.5 to 1 for the leveraged satellite bucket, because the configuration lists no separate floor for it and the fund is held for a three-month trading window, as the Direxion prospectus itself assumes by stating only a daily goal[1].

A note on method. We use SPY, the S&P 500 exchange-traded fund that the ledger uses as its benchmark, as the stand-in for the index when we measure tracking, volatility and drag, because its daily closes line up with the fund’s on every date. The raw S&P 500 index series from the same data source disagrees with SPY on some days, for example June 26, 2026, when it shows -0.05% against SPY’s -0.72%, so we use it only for record-close and recovery dates[14][19]. Volatility is the standard deviation of daily close-to-close changes times the square root of 252. Drag is three times the index’s simple return minus the return from compounding three times each day’s move. Costs and gaps are that compounded return minus the fund’s actual price return. Our price series leaves out the fund’s distributions of $1.45 over the year[2].

The number

Over three months the S&P 500 must rise about 1.0% (to roughly 7,736, or SPY near $771) for SPXL to finish flat, if the index’s volatility stays near 12.7% a year and the fund’s holding costs stay near 6.95% a year.

How it was calculated

A daily-reset 3x fund is worth about its starting value times the index price ratio cubed, times a decay factor of e to the power of minus 3 times volatility squared times the years, times one minus the yearly cost times the years. With volatility of 12.75% and 0.25 years the decay factor is 0.9879, and the cost factor is 1 - 0.0695 x 0.25 = 0.9826. Setting the product to one gives a price ratio of the cube root of 1 / (0.9879 x 0.9826), which is 1.0100. This is the standard approximation and our simplification, not a number from the fund.

The inputs

Volatility of 12.75% from 250 daily closes of SPY[14]. A yearly cost of 6.95%, measured as the gap between SPXL’s 250-day price return and the daily-compounded 3x return on SPY, which is 0.9305 of the compounded figure[13][14]. The start prices are $280.17 for SPXL and $763.25 for SPY[13][14].

The range

At 10% volatility the break-even is about +0.84%, at the prospectus’s five-year figure of 16.96% it is about +1.31%, and at 25% it is about +2.17%. Over a full year the hurdle is about +4.1% at 12.7% volatility, +5.4% at 16.96% and +9.0% at 25%.

If the number is off

If volatility rises toward 25% the hurdle roughly doubles and a rising index can still leave the fund lower. If costs fall with lower short-term rates the hurdle eases, but volatility matters more than cost for a 3x fund.

Each test is a short list of yes or no conditions, and the fund must pass at least one test to be allocated at market. One point needs care for this fund. Relative strength in the gate compares the fund with SPY, which is the fund’s own underlying. For an ordinary stock, beating SPY over 63 days means leadership. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, the evidence that would show it wrong and when we will know. It then gives three fatal-flaw scenarios, the strongest case against our own conclusion and the single claim the conclusion depends on.

Estimates 1 to 3 in Chapter 3 and the scenario values in Chapter 5 all assume that the index’s volatility stays near 12.7% and that the fund’s cost stays near 6.95% a year. Neither is stable. SPY’s volatility was 10.5% over the last 20 sessions, 12.7% over 250 and 16.5% over 500, and the prospectus’s five-year figure is 16.96%[14][1]. At 25% the three-month break-even rises from +1.0% to +2.2% and the base case falls from $280.53 to about $271. The cost estimate comes from one year of prices and includes financing, which is quoted as a spread over the overnight bank rate that stood at 3.89% on October 15[29]. The evidence that would show us wrong is 63-session volatility above 20% or a visible jump in the daily gap, and we will know within 20 trading days.

The scenario prices of $834.14, $771.18 and $729.10 come from chart levels in the ledger and the data page, not from a valuation, and the 15%, 55% and 30% weights are a judgement informed by odds, not a forecast[33]. The reverse DCF rests on as-reported earnings as a stand-in for free cash flow, and as-reported earnings include one-off items[20]. If the September-quarter figure is below the 12-month figure of $298.03, the implied growth rate would rise and the valuation cross-check would look worse. We will know when S&P publishes the September-quarter result.

Fatal flaw 1: a gap through the stop

The $273.01 stop is $7.16 below the close, which is 0.92 of one normal day’s swing. SPXL moved more than 3% on 51 of the last 250 sessions and more than 5% on 12. Its worst day in that window was -7.89% on June 5, 2026, and its worst in two years was -18.03% on April 4, 2025[13]. A stop can be jumped, either overnight or inside one session, and then the loss is larger than planned. On the April 4 move the 3 shares would have lost about $152 in one day, which is 0.18% of net worth. Treat the stop as a line where the idea is wrong, not as a guarantee of the exit price.

Fatal flaw 2: a valuation and AI-spending unwind

The index’s cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio is 40.54, close to the 44.19 peak of December 1999, the ten largest stocks are 38.9% of its value, and technology is 38.03% of it[23][27][2]. In the week to October 16 chips fell 11.41%, the technology sector fell about 7.7% and the index fell 1.95%[31]. A slow grind lower with high volatility is the worst case for a 3x fund: the prospectus’s table loses 17.1% a year in a flat index at 25% volatility and 57.6% in an index that falls 20%[1]. In 2000 to 2002 the index’s price fell 10.14%, 13.04% and 23.37% in three straight years[27]. A three-year version of that slide would take a 3x fund close to zero. Nothing says it will happen, and the stop is how we limit the harm if it starts.

Fatal flaw 3: rates, financing and the banks behind the swaps

The fund borrows about two dollars of exposure for every dollar of net assets, so each extra point on the overnight rate adds about two points a year to its cost. SOFR is 3.89%, the 10-year Treasury yield ended the week at 5.098% and September inflation was 3.7%, so a rate shock is not remote[29][31]. Separately, seven swap legs carry $16.49 billion of exposure, and the prospectus says a swap bank may close a swap after a dramatic intraday move and that the fund may then be unable to enter another[3][1]. Issuers can also close or change funds, and the net expense cap runs only to September 1, 2027[2][1]. Nothing in the files we read says any of this is imminent, and we cannot put a probability on it. It makes the downside worse than the formula in Chapter 5 shows.

Our decision for SPXL is REJECTED for new money, and it is a decision about adding, not a sell signal. You hold 3 shares at Schwab, so there are two questions: whether to add and whether to sell[34]. The reward-to-risk ratio is 0.05 to 1 against a floor of 3.5 to 1, and Layer 2 scores 3 of 5 against a bar of 4. Any one of those three would be enough for a rejection.

The rule that decides the second question is the exit rule for a holding: sell when support breaks (a close below the stop or a Stage 4 breakdown), when two operating KPIs fail, or when the thesis is impaired. None is met. The close of $280.17 is above the $273.01 stop, the stage is 3 and not 4, no Layer 2 KPI fails, and the index’s earnings are rising. The support ladder alone would allow a full-size entry. The close is 0.42 ATR above the $276.92 swing low, inside the FULL_SIZE band, which is any distance under 0.6 ATR[33]. A 3x fund is a short-term holding, so the tighter rule applies: the 20-day swing low of $276.92 less half of one ATR ($3.905) gives a stop of $273.01. The whole-share arithmetic matters here, because 3 shares do not divide into the usual trim fractions. The stop instruction is simple: if SPXL closes below $273.01, sell all 3 shares at the next session’s open. A market order is acceptable for 3 shares of a fund that trades about 1.6 million shares a day[13].
