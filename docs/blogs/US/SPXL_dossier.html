1. What the company does

SPXL is a fund, not a company. It is the Direxion Daily S&P 500 Bull 3X ETF, an exchange-traded fund (a basket product whose shares trade on a stock exchange all day, like a share). Its stated aim is to deliver 300% of the daily performance of the S&P 500 Index, before fees and costs.[1] Rafferty Asset Management runs it for the Direxion brand, and it began trading on November 5, 2008.[12][2] The fund's name changed in 2026 from Direxion Daily S&P 500 Bull 3X Shares to Direxion Daily S&P 500 Bull 3X ETF, and the watchlist record still carries the old name.[1] Nothing in the structure changed with the name.

The word daily is the whole story. At the close of every trading day the manager resets the fund so that its exposure to the index is again three times its net assets.[1] If the index rose that day, the fund has more money and must add exposure. If the index fell, it must cut exposure. Over any stretch longer than a day, the result is each day's gain or loss multiplied together, and that product will very likely differ from three times the index's return over the same stretch.[1] The prospectus says the fund would lose 17.1% over a year in which the index went nowhere but swung with a yearly volatility of 25%. Volatility here means the typical size of daily price swings, stated as a yearly percentage. At a volatility of 100% the loss in a flat year is 95.0%.[1]

The fund gets its 300% exposure mainly from swaps. A swap is a private contract in which a bank pays the fund the return of the index and the fund pays the bank a financing charge. The holdings file dated October 28, 2026 shows seven swap lines worth 228.86% of net assets, plus stock lines worth 71.14%, for 300.0% in total (our sums of the file's weights).[3] The filing for July 31, 2026 names the seven banks on the other side: Barclays, BNP Paribas, Citibank, Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, Bank of America (formerly Merrill Lynch) and UBS.[6] Each swap pays a financing rate that the filing describes as one-month SOFR plus a spread, and the rates it reports run from 4.43% to 4.55%.[6] SOFR, the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, is the overnight US dollar borrowing rate, and it stood at 3.95% on October 27, 2026.[29]

A buyer pays in three ways. The first is the stated fee: 0.95% gross and 0.84% after the sponsor's waiver, which the sponsor has promised to keep through September 1, 2027.[1][2] The second is the financing charge on the two extra dollars of exposure borrowed for each dollar invested, which is the larger cost today. The third is volatility decay, the slow loss a daily-reset fund suffers when the index swings up and down, which Chapter 3 measures. In return the fund paid $1.45 of distributions per share over the last four quarters (our sum of $0.31028, $0.52306, $0.44927 and $0.17186).[2] FINRA, the brokerage industry regulator, has said that funds reset daily typically are unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them for longer than one trading session.[25] We use SPXL only as a short-term trading satellite, and this report judges it that way.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Leveraged ETF: a fund that aims for a multiple, here three times, of one day's move in an index.
  • Daily reset: the fund restores its 300% exposure at each close, so gains and losses compound along the path.
  • Swap: a private contract that pays one side the return of an asset in exchange for a fee; the other side is the counterparty.
  • Volatility decay: the loss a daily-reset fund suffers when the index rises and falls repeatedly, even if it ends where it started.
  • NAV: net asset value, the per-share value of everything the fund owns minus what it owes.
  • ATR (average true range) is the typical size of one day's price swing in dollars, used here to place stops and measure distance to support.
  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast the price has moved; under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is a company's profit divided across all of its shares.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a profit measure struck before financing and accounting charges.
  • net debt is the interest-bearing debt a company owes minus the cash it holds.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is the after-tax operating profit earned on each dollar put into a business.
  • FCF (free cash flow) is the cash left after running a business and paying for its equipment.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying employees in shares.
  • price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio is the price paid for each dollar of a company's yearly earnings; the forward version uses next year's expected earnings.
  • stop loss is the price at which we admit a plan is wrong and sell.
  • limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better and does not chase the market.
  • entry zone is the band of prices where we would be willing to place a buy order.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop).
  • base is a sideways stretch of trading that a price builds before its next move.
  • pivot is the top of a base, the price level that must be cleared.
  • breakout is a move of the price above a base or pivot on rising trading volume.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend of a four-stage price cycle; Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase between them.
  • guidance is a company's own published forecast for the coming period.
  • consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts for a number.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A fund like SPXL has no moat of its own. Its competitive position is a matter of price, size and safety against other funds that sell the same exposure. Its closest rival is ProShares UltraPro S&P500 (UPRO), which also targets three times the daily S&P 500 move. UPRO charges 0.88% against SPXL's 0.84% after waiver, and it holds $5.30 billion on a data-site page.[13][2] The two-times version, ProShares Ultra S&P 500 (SSO), also charges 0.84% and holds $8.52 billion.[14] The Nasdaq-100 version, ProShares UltraPro QQQ (TQQQ), charges 0.78% and holds $34.42 billion, but it follows a different and more volatile index.[16] The plain index fund SPY charges 0.09% and holds $814.8 billion, which shows what the leverage costs: about nine times the fee for a 3x product.[15][17]

Size and trading depth are the second test. The holdings file shows 24,700,001 shares outstanding, which at the October 27 NAV of $286.31 is about $7.07 billion (our calculation).[3][2] A data site shows $6.84 billion on October 28, about 3% lower, which looks like a lag in that site's figure, so we use the file.[12] Net assets were $6,076,194,773 at the October 31, 2025 year end, $5,929,370,932 on April 30, 2026 and $6,725,757,504 on July 31, 2026.[4][5][6] On the October 28 session the fund traded 1,103,157 shares, 0.68 times its 20-day average, which is about $319 million of stock in one day (our calculation from the session ledger).[39] Direxion closed ten small funds in April 2026 because they could not attract assets, and SPXL is far from that group.[10]

Safety is the third test, and this cycle it rests on newer evidence than the last one. The July 31, 2026 filing, found through the EDGAR filing list, lists seven swaps with a combined notional amount of $15.14 billion, which is 225.2% of the fund's $6.73 billion of net assets (our sums).[6][7] The banks owed the fund $880.2 million of unrealized gains on those swaps, which is 13.1% of net assets (our sum), and the largest single amount was $194.3 million owed by J.P. Morgan, or 2.9% of net assets.[6] If a bank failed to pay, the fund could lose what that bank owes it, and the prospectus lists counterparty credit risk because swaps are placed with a limited number of dealers.[1] The prospectus also says a counterparty may be allowed to close a swap at once after a dramatic intraday index move, and that the fund could then fail to meet its objective.[1] The peer funds spread their swaps wider: UPRO shows nine named banks among its top ten lines and TQQQ ten, against seven for SPXL.[13][16][6] The names are as of July 31, and the holdings file codes the seven swap lines without naming the banks, so we cannot name today's banks.[3]

The underlying index is strong in profit and fragile in price. The S&P 500 holds about 503 companies, and technology made up 39.71% of the index at September 30, 2026.[17][2] The ten largest companies add up to 39.06% of the index (our sum of the published weights).[2] Look through the fund to those companies and the quality is high: the index earns an aggregate ROIC of 14.56% against a 28-year average of 9.10%, its aggregate net debt is 1.97 times EBITDA with interest covered 10.33 times, and SBC is 1.34% of revenue.[35][34][38] Analysts expect third-quarter earnings growth of 29.5% and, for calendar 2026, 33.1%.[18] The price paid for those earnings is not low: the forward P/E is 18.7 on FactSet's measure and 20.44 on the SPDR fund page's measure.[18][17] Index rules have been steady, because S&P Dow Jones Indices kept the S&P 500 eligibility screens unchanged after a megacap consultation in June 2026.[24]

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Three managements matter. Rafferty Asset Management, the fund's adviser, promises a cost ceiling and a daily target, and it has met both. S&P Dow Jones Indices, which owns the S&P 500, promises stable rules and has changed little in the past year. The companies inside the index give guidance (a company's own published forecast for the coming period), and FactSet counted 72 of the 116 companies that gave third-quarter earnings guidance as positive, or 62%, against a 5-year average of 40%.[20] By October 23, 24% of the index had reported, 83% of those had beaten the earnings estimate, and growth was 29.5% against 26.7% expected on June 30.[18][20] The beats were smaller than usual, 2.1% in aggregate against a 5-year average of 7.5%, so delivery is real but not lavish.[18]

The number

We measure SPXL's all-in cost drag at 9.93% a year, or 1.72% over the 44 sessions to December 31, 2026.

How it was calculated

Over the last 252 sessions we compared the fund's actual log return with the log return of an ideal daily 3x of SPY, and annualised the gap. The ideal path has no fees or financing, so the gap is the cost.

The inputs

Adjusted daily closes of SPXL and SPY.[31][32] The floor for comparison is two times the 12-month average SOFR of 3.71% plus the 0.84% net fee, which is 8.26% a year.[29][1] The 6-month measure is 9.76% and the 3-month measure is 10.22%.

The range

Between 9.8% and 10.2% a year across the three windows, or between 1.7% and 1.8% of value over the 44 sessions. If SOFR rose 0.25 points after a December rate rise, the floor would rise by 0.50 points a year.

If the number is off

Each extra point of annual drag costs about 0.17% of value over the holding window, so an error of one point moves our base price by about $0.50. The cost is small against a 7.7% stop distance but large against a 1.1% weighted edge.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate rests on a weakest assumption, and the useful question is what evidence would show it wrong and when. The base estimate of $293.48 rests on index volatility of 15% and an index return of 1.5%. The weighted price of $292.14 rests on the probabilities. The stress estimate rests on a P/E of 16.0 and volatility of 30%. The bear case that follows is the strongest honest argument against holding the fund at all.

The bear case is that a 3x fund in a narrow uptrend at the edge of a rate-rise cycle is a coin flip with a thin edge. The index is only 1.19% under its high, breadth is 50.3% of stocks above their 50-day averages, the earnings multiple is not cheap on the SPDR page's measure, the 10-year yield is 4.99% and the financing cost is 3.95%.[39][15][30][29] In that setting a nine-week hold has a weighted gain of about 1%, a bad tail of about -33% and a stress tail of about -41%. The fund's own prospectus says an investor could lose the full principal in one day if the index falls more than 33%.[1]

Fatal flaw 1: volatility jumps and the reset eats the position

If index volatility rises from today's 9.97% (20 sessions) to 25% and stays there, the prospectus table says the fund loses 17.1% in a year even with a flat index.[1] In 2020 SPY's volatility reached 33.4% and SPXL fell 76.86% from February 19 to March 23, and in 2022 it fell 63.80% from January 3 to October 12 (our calculation).[31][32] The evidence would be 20-session SPY volatility above 20%, and we would know within days because the figure updates every session.

Fatal flaw 2: a one-day gap goes through the stop

A stop is a price that triggers a sale, and a gap is a move from one close to the next open that skips the stop. SPXL's worst single day was -33.87% on March 16, 2020, and the worst day since 2021 was -18.03% on April 4, 2025 (our calculation).[31] On the 6.1 shares that are the most an owner should keep ($1,762.59), a fall of 18.03% costs $317.73, or 36.0 basis points of net worth, about the 35 basis point budget for the lowest tier and just over it. On the 6.6209 shares held today the same gap would cost $344.86, or 39.1 basis points, the figure in the Chapter 7 table. A move of 33.87% would cost $596.99, or 67.7 basis points. The stop at $266.70 cannot prevent either loss, and its nominal loss figure assumes a fill at the stop price, which a gap or a next-open fill can exceed. The evidence is simply a big index gap, and it cannot be foreseen.

Fatal flaw 3: the Fed raises rates in December and the multiple compresses

On October 28 the Federal Open Market Committee held its range at 3-3/4 to 4 percent by a vote of 10 to 2, and both dissenters wanted a quarter-point rise.[27] The market had put less than a 10% chance on a rise at this meeting, but futures now point to a strong possibility of one in December.[28] A rise would lift the financing cost the fund pays on two thirds of its exposure and would pressure the multiple. The evidence would be fed funds futures pricing, the 10-year yield above 5.2% and the SPY close under its 50-day average, and we will know at the December 8-9 meeting.[26]

Fatal flaw 4: counterparty or closure failure

The fund could lose swap gains if a bank failed, and a closure would force a sale at a date the sponsor picks. On July 31, 2026 the fund was owed $880.2 million across seven banks, with 2.9% of net assets owed by the largest, J.P. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, was defined in Chapter 1. Our house floor for a tactical idea is 2.5. Here the base target is $293.48 and the stop is $266.70, so the ratio is 0.20 at the $288.95 close, which means a buyer risks $22.25 to gain $4.53. Portfolio heat is the share of net worth we would lose if every stop were hit, counted in basis points (a basis point, or bp, is 0.01%). A conviction tier gives each idea a risk budget in bp and a size range. TIER_0 means zero size, TIER_4 is the smallest real position (1% to 2% of net worth with a 35 bp budget) and TIER_1 is the largest. The risk-unit formula is: weight = risk budget divided by the stop distance, capped by the tier maximum. We choose TIER_4 because the gain-to-loss floor fails at the close and the fund decays, and the tier would only rise to TIER_3 at a price where the floor passes. TIER_4 sets the size of any re-entry.

Which stop we use needs a reason. Three rules give three levels. The tactical rule takes the lower of the 20-day swing low ($274.42) and the 20-day average ($288.96), which is $274.42, and subtracts half an ATR ($3.86), giving $270.56. The Stage-2 rule takes the lower of the swing low and the 50-day average ($286.70) and subtracts one full ATR ($7.72), giving $266.70. The base-low rule, for a fund still building a base, takes the 63-day base low of $258.44 and subtracts one ATR, giving $250.72. We use the Stage-2 level because Test A now passes, so the fund is treated as in an uptrend and not in a base, and it sits 4.7% above the 200-day average of $254.84. The three levels match S&P 500 levels near 756, 752 and 737, because SPXL at $266.70 is a fall of 7.70% and the cube root of 0.9230 is 0.9736 (our calculation, ignoring a few days of decay). The 20-day swing low of SPY is 758.68 and its 63-day low is 737.68, so the stop is a little under the first and well above the second.
