1. What the company does

SPXL is a fund, not a company. It is the Direxion Daily S&P 500 Bull 3X ETF, an exchange-traded fund (a basket product whose shares trade on a stock exchange all day, like a share). Its stated aim is to deliver 300% of the daily performance of the S&P 500 Index, before fees and costs.[1] Rafferty Asset Management runs it for the Direxion brand, and it began trading on November 5, 2008.[1][3] In 2026 the fund's name changed from Direxion Daily S&P 500 Bull 3X Shares to Direxion Daily S&P 500 Bull 3X ETF; the watchlist record still carries the old name.[1] Nothing in the structure changed with the name.

The word daily is the whole story. At the close of every trading day the manager resets the fund so that its exposure to the index is again three times its net assets.[1] If the index rose that day, the fund has more money and must add exposure. If the index fell, it must cut exposure. Over any stretch longer than a day, the result is each day's gain or loss multiplied together, and that product will very likely differ from three times the index's return over the same stretch.[1] The prospectus says the fund will lose money when the index is flat over a year with volatility of 25% (volatility is the typical size of daily price swings, stated as a yearly percentage): the loss is 17.1%. At 100% volatility the loss is 95%.[1]

The fund gets its 300% exposure mainly from swaps. A swap is a private contract in which a bank pays the fund the return of the index and the fund pays the bank a financing charge. At April 30, 2026, swap contracts equalled 229.6% of net assets and common stocks equalled 70.4%, for 300.0% in total.[6] At the October 31, 2025 year end, seven banks were on the other side of those swaps: BNP Paribas, Goldman Sachs, Bank of America Merrill Lynch, J.P. Morgan, UBS Securities, Citibank and Barclays.[5] Each contract charged the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR, the overnight US bank funding rate, 3.90% on October 22, 2026) plus a spread of 0.63% to 0.80%.[5][24]

A buyer pays in three ways. The first is the stated fee: 0.95% gross, 0.84% after the sponsor's waiver, which the sponsor has promised to keep through September 1, 2027.[1][2] The second is the financing charge on the two extra dollars of exposure borrowed for each dollar invested, which is the larger cost today. The third is volatility decay, the slow loss a daily-reset fund suffers when the index swings up and down, which Chapter 3 measures. In return the fund paid $1.45 of distributions per share over the last four quarters.[2] FINRA, the brokerage industry regulator, has said that funds reset daily typically are unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them longer than one trading session.[22] We use SPXL only as a short-term trading satellite, and this report judges it that way.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Leveraged ETF: a fund that aims for a multiple, here three times, of one day's move in an index.
  • Daily reset: the fund restores its 300% exposure at each close, so gains and losses compound along the path.
  • Swap: a private contract that pays one side the return of an asset in exchange for a fee; the other side is the counterparty.
  • Volatility decay: the loss a daily-reset fund suffers when the index rises and falls repeatedly, even if it ends where it started.
  • SOFR: the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, the overnight US dollar borrowing rate that swap financing is priced from.
  • NAV: net asset value, the per-share value of everything the fund owns minus what it owes.
  • ATR: average true range, the typical size of one day's price swing in dollars, used here to place stops.
  • RSI: relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast the price has moved; under 30 is called oversold.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A fund like SPXL has no moat of its own. Its competitive position is a matter of price, size and safety against other funds that sell the same exposure. Its closest rival is ProShares UltraPro S&P500 (UPRO), which also targets three times the daily S&P 500 move. UPRO charges 0.88% against SPXL's 0.84% after waiver, and it holds $5.59 billion on its data-site page.[12][1] The two-times version, ProShares Ultra S&P 500 (SSO), also charges 0.84% and holds $8.84 billion.[13] The plain index fund SPY charges 0.09% and holds $820.42 billion, which shows what the leverage costs: about nine times the fee for a 3x product.[14]

Size and trading depth are the second test. SPXL had 24,800,001 shares outstanding on October 23, 2026, which at the $275.63 close is about $6.84 billion (our calculation).[4] Net assets were $6,076,194,773 at the October 31, 2025 year end and $5,929,370,932 on April 30, 2026.[5][6] A data site shows $7.20 billion for the same fund, and we do not use that number because it does not match shares times price.[11] On the session of October 23 the fund traded 1,743,100 shares, 1.07 times its 20-day average, which is about $480 million of stock in one day (our calculation from the session ledger). Direxion closed ten small funds in April 2026 because they could not attract assets, and SPXL is far from that group.[9]

Safety is the third test. At the October 31, 2025 year end the fund's swaps had a notional amount of $12,198,803,268 across seven banks, and the fund was owed $1,483,795,459 of unrealized gains on them, which is 24.4% of its $6,076,194,773 net assets (our calculation).[5] The largest single bank exposure was Citibank, with $440,138,607 owed, or 7.2% of net assets.[5] If a bank failed to pay, the fund could lose what that bank owes it. Seven banks and $4,393,837,301 of securities set aside as collateral help limit the loss from a single failure, but the prospectus still lists counterparty credit risk as a risk because swaps are placed with a limited number of dealers.[5][1] The names are as of the year end, and the newer report does not carry the swap schedule, so we cannot name today's banks.[6][28]

The underlying index is strong in profit and fragile in price. The S&P 500 held 503 companies at the end of 2025, and technology made up 39.71% of the index at September 30, 2026.[1][2] The ten largest companies add up to 39.06% (our sum of the published weights).[2] Analysts expect third-quarter earnings growth of 29.0% and, for calendar 2026, 32.8%.[17] The price paid for those earnings is not low: the forward price-to-earnings ratio (P/E, the price paid for a dollar of expected next-twelve-month earnings) is 19.0 on FactSet's measure and 20.33 on the SPDR fund page.[17][16] Index rules have been steady. S&P Dow Jones Indices kept the S&P 500 eligibility screens unchanged after a megacap consultation in June 2026.[21]

Two managements matter. Rafferty Asset Management, the fund's adviser, promises a cost ceiling and a daily target, and it has met both. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate rests on a weakest assumption, and the useful question is what evidence would show it wrong and when. The base estimate of $279.26 rests on index volatility of 15% and an index return of 1.5%. The weighted price of $274.75 rests on the probabilities. The stress estimate rests on a P/E of 16.0 and volatility of 30%. The bear case that follows is the strongest honest argument against holding the fund at all.

The bear case is that a 3x fund in a correction is a coin flip with a negative edge. The index is under its 50-day average, breadth is weak (49.6% of stocks above their 50-day average), the earnings multiple is not cheap and the financing cost is 3.90%. In that setting a ten-week hold has a weighted outcome of about zero, a bad tail of about -30% and a stress tail of about -44%. The fund's own prospectus says an investor could lose the full principal in one day if the index falls more than 33%.

Fatal flaw 1: volatility jumps and the reset eats the position

If index volatility rises from today's 9.75% (20 sessions) to 25% and stays there, the prospectus table says the fund loses 17.1% in a year even with a flat index.[1] In 2020 SPY's volatility reached 33.4% and SPXL fell 76.86% from peak to trough in 23 trading days, and in 2022 it fell 63.80% from January 3 to October 12 (our calculation).[26][27] The evidence would be 20-session SPY volatility above 20%, and we would know within days because the figure updates every session.

Fatal flaw 2: a one-day gap goes through the stop

A stop is a price that triggers a sale, and a gap is a move from one close to the next open that skips the stop. SPXL's worst single day was -33.87% on March 16, 2020, and the worst day since 2021 was -18.03% on April 4, 2025 (our calculation).[26] On the 5.9 shares we keep after the first sale ($1,626.22), a fall of 18.03% costs $293.22, or 35.6 basis points of net worth, which is in line with the 35 basis point budget for the lowest tier (it would be 39.9 on the current holding). A move of 33.87% would cost $550.80, or 66.9 basis points. The stop at $241.83 cannot prevent either loss. The evidence is simply a big index gap, and it cannot be foreseen.

Fatal flaw 3: counterparty or closure failure

The fund could lose swap gains if a bank failed, and a closure would force a sale at a date the sponsor picks. At the 2025 year end the fund was owed $1,483,795,459 across seven banks, with 7.2% of net assets owed by the largest, Citibank.[5] The sponsor closed ten smaller funds in April 2026.[9] For SPXL the signal would be a falling share count or a closure notice, and we would learn of it from a sponsor press release or an SEC supplement. The probability is low, and the loss in a closure is mainly timing.

Some plain definitions come first. A stop loss is the price at which we admit the plan is wrong and sell. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, measured from a given buy price; the formula is (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). Our house floor for a tactical idea is 2.5. Here the base target is $279.26 and the stop is $241.83, so the ratio is 0.11 at the $275.63 close. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and it does not chase. The entry zone is the band of prices where we would be willing to place one. For SPXL that band is $250.00 to $252.50, and a later paragraph works out its ratio for a stated date.

Portfolio heat and tiers set the size. Portfolio heat is the share of net worth we would lose if every stop were hit, counted in basis points (a basis point, or bp, is 0.01%). A conviction tier gives each idea a risk budget in bp and a size range. TIER_0 means zero size, TIER_4 is the smallest real position (1% to 2% of net worth with a 35 bp budget) and TIER_1 is the largest. The risk-unit formula is: weight = risk budget divided by the stop distance, capped by the tier maximum. For SPXL the stop is 4.23% under $252.50, so the formula gives 8.3% and the TIER_4 cap of 2.0% decides. We choose TIER_4 because the gain-to-loss floor fails at the close, the gate fails and the fund decays. The head figure of 2.0% is therefore the ceiling. We hold 2.22%, so the first step is to sell the excess.

Which stop we use needs a reason. The tactical rule takes the lower of the 20-day swing low ($274.42) and the 20-day average ($288.57), which is $274.42, and subtracts half an ATR ($4.03). That gives $270.40, only 0.65 ATR under the price, so one ordinary day could reach it. The core rule takes the 63-day base low of $249.88 and subtracts one full ATR ($8.05), which gives $241.83. We use the core level as the final stop, because it sits under SPXL's own 200-day average of $254.01. We use the tactical level as a first, partial exit. The two instruments are linked by the 3x formula. SPXL at $241.83 is a fall of 12.26%, and the cube root of 0.8774 is 0.9573, so it matches an S&P 500 fall of about 4.3%, or SPY near $727.50 (our calculation, ignoring a few days of decay). That is almost exactly SPY's 200-day average of $727.01, and just under its 63-day low of $729.10. The gain-to-loss ratio at the close is 0.11 on the core stop and 0.69 on the tactical stop. Both are under the 2.5 floor. The stop is a structural invalidation level, meaning a price whose break shows the setup has failed, and CMP means current market price, here the $275.63 close. The simple moving average (SMA) is the plain average of closing prices over a set number of days, and the exponential moving average (EMA) weights recent days more. They are the levels we would trail a winner with, but we do not trail this fund, for the reason given later in this chapter.

The entry zone needs a dated calculation, because a target that starts from today's price cannot be reused at a lower entry. Suppose SPXL reaches $252.50 on Friday, November 20, 2026, with 41 days left to December 31. Charging the same 15% volatility decay and cost drag over the 28 days from today to that entry, the 3x formula puts the S&P 500 about 2.5% lower, with SPY near $741.25. If SPY then regains the base-case level of $771.37 by December 31, the fund is worth about $279.26 (our calculation, with the same decay and cost over the last 41 days), which is the base-case target again, and the ratio on the $241.83 stop is 2.51 at $252.50 and 3.58 at $250.00. If the index only drifts up 1.5% pro rata (0.9% over 41 days), the target is about $254.49 and the ratio is 0.19 at $252.50. So the floor is met only if the dip is followed by a recovery to the base level, which we cannot count on. The zone is therefore a trigger for a fresh underwriting run that rebuilds the target from the actual entry date, index level and remaining days. It is not an order to place today.
