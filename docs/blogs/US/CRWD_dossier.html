Before you read

CrowdStrike sells cloud-based cybersecurity software on yearly subscriptions to large organizations. We reject the stock as a buy at its 2026-10-16 close of $227.39. The business is speeding up, but stock pay costs 23% of revenue, return on invested capital is negative, and the chart is in a Stage 3 pullback.[2][1][26] Our central 12-month value of $210 is below the price, which already assumes about 33% yearly growth in free cash flow, the cash left after paying for operations and equipment, for ten years.[1][6] We would look again if the stock regains an uptrend above $286.99, falls to about $177, or cuts stock pay below 10% of revenue.

1. What the company does

CrowdStrike sells security software that protects the computers, cloud servers, user accounts and data of large organizations from hackers. The product is the Falcon platform, which the company describes as a cloud-native, single-sensor platform.[3] A sensor is a small program installed on each device. Customers then add modules, which are separately priced features such as cloud protection, identity protection and next-generation SIEM (a tool that gathers security logs so analysts can search them).[2] Each module is priced by the unit that fits it, for example per device, per user, or per gigabyte of daily data.[2]

Customers sign subscriptions that typically last one to three years, and most cannot be cancelled early.[2] CrowdStrike generally bills at the start of the term, so cash arrives before the revenue is recognized, and the billed-but-not-yet-earned balance (called deferred revenue) was $4.84 billion on 2026-07-31.[2] Subscriptions made up $4.56 billion of the $4.81 billion in revenue for the year ended 2026-01-31, and the rest came from services such as incident response.[3] About 65% of second-quarter revenue came from the United States.[2]

The number the company and its investors watch most is annual recurring revenue (ARR), the yearly value of the subscription contracts in force at a date. ARR reached $5.84 billion on 2026-07-31, up 25% from a year earlier, and $332.8 million of it was net new ARR, meaning the net amount added in the quarter after cancellations.[1] Revenue follows ARR with a lag, because a contract signed this quarter is recognized evenly over its term.

The company pushes a licensing model called Falcon Flex. A customer commits a budget up front and then draws it down across many products over time.[2] Accounts that use Flex carried $2.29 billion of ARR at the end of the second quarter, up 101% from a year earlier, and management says customers who convert from a standard subscription to Flex raise their ARR by more than 40% on average.[1][16] Flex matters because it makes the next module easier to buy, which is how a security vendor grows inside a customer it already has.

Most of what CrowdStrike spends goes to selling and research, not to running the software. On a reported basis, sales and marketing took 35% of second-quarter revenue and research and development took 30%.[1] Gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of delivering the product. Subscription gross margin was 78%, or 81% once stock pay and a few other items are removed.[1] That gap between reported and adjusted figures is a theme of this book, because the adjustments are largely the cost of paying staff in shares.

One event still shapes how customers buy. On 2024-07-19 CrowdStrike released a configuration update to its Falcon sensor that crashed a number of customers' Windows systems; the company said it was not a cyberattack.[15] Afterward it offered customer commitment packages, which have included discounts, extra modules, flexible payment terms or longer subscription periods, and it says these packages cause some contraction and lower upsell dollars.[2] Chapters 2 and 2B check whether that damage is healing.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ARR (annual recurring revenue): the yearly value of subscriptions that renew. Net new ARR is the amount added in a period.
  • Net revenue retention (NRR): the share of last year's customer ARR still there this year, including upgrades and cancellations. Gross retention counts only customers lost, so it cannot exceed 100%.
  • Free cash flow (FCF): cash from operations minus spending on equipment and software. CrowdStrike's own definition does not subtract stock pay.
  • Stock-based compensation (SBC): the cost of paying employees in shares. It uses no cash today but makes each existing share worth a smaller slice of the company.
  • Falcon Flex: a commit-up-front, draw-down-over-time licensing model that spans many products.
  • Rule of 40: revenue growth plus free cash flow margin; a software company at or above 40 is generally seen as healthy.
  • Reverse DCF: working backward from today's price to the growth rate in cash flow that the price needs to be fair.
  • Stage 3: the Weinstein chart stage that follows an uptrend, when the price stalls and falls back toward its 50-day average.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The clearest strength is that growth is speeding up at a large size. ARR growth was 22%, 20%, 23%, 24%, 24% and 25% in the six quarters ended 2025-04-30 through 2026-07-31.[10][9][8][6][7][1] Net new ARR of $332.8 million in the latest quarter was a record and 51% higher than a year earlier, and management raised its full-year net new ARR growth outlook to about 34% from 22.5% at the start of the year.[1][16] Revenue of $1.47 billion grew 26%, which means the faster sales of the past year are now reaching the income statement.[1]

Customers are also buying more of the platform. 51% of subscription customers use six or more modules, 35% use seven or more, and 26% use eight or more, up from 50%, 34% and 24% at the start of the year.[1][6] Gross retention, the share of last year's ARR that did not leave with departing customers, was 97% at 2026-01-31, and net revenue retention (NRR) was 115%.[17] Contracts are signed for one to three years, and the signed backlog of remaining performance obligations (RPO) was $10.7 billion on 2026-07-31, about 1.8 times ARR.[2][1] Together these give a reason to expect revenue to keep growing over the next year even if sales slow.

Net retention has not returned to its older level, though. It was 125% at 2023-01-31, 119% at 2024-01-31, 112% at 2025-01-31 and 115% at 2026-01-31.[4][3] The company says both net and gross retention improved again in the second quarter but gave no percentages, so the latest numbers are eight months old.[2][16] The cause is partly deliberate: the post-outage commitment packages lengthen terms and trim upsell dollars, and the company says they will keep doing so.[2]

The weakest part of the business is what shareholders pay in stock. CrowdStrike recorded $1.10 billion of stock-based compensation (SBC) in the year ended 2026-01-31, which was 22.8% of revenue.[3] In the first half of fiscal 2027 it was $674.6 million, or 23.6% of revenue.[2][1] Paying in shares creates dilution, which is the rise in share count that shrinks each existing share's slice of the company. Profit measured under the official accounting rules (GAAP) is therefore close to zero: the second quarter showed a $5.3 million net profit and a $33.2 million operating loss.[1] The headline 25% operating margin management quotes leaves out stock pay and several other items. Operating margin is the share of revenue left after running costs.[16][1]

Competition arrives from several sides. CrowdStrike's own filing lists network security vendors adding endpoint and cloud products, identity security vendors, cloud security vendors, and others.[3] Microsoft says its Defender for Endpoint product competes with endpoint security providers.[32] Palo Alto Networks reported 34% revenue growth in its latest quarter against CrowdStrike's 26%, and Fortinet reported 26% growth with a 34% GAAP operating margin.[19] On 2026-10-13 a report said OpenAI was in early talks to buy the security research firm Trail of Bits, which raised a new worry that AI developers could build security products themselves.[20] CrowdStrike's own view is that no competitor has a true platform offering equivalent to Falcon.[3] We treat that as the company's claim, not as a fact we have verified.

The July 2024 outage still has loose ends. A passenger class action was dismissed, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed the dismissal on 2026-05-20; a securities class action ended in a final judgment on 2026-01-28; and the derivative suits (brought by shareholders on the company's behalf) were dismissed in March and April 2026.[2][3] Delta Air Lines' suit, filed 2024-10-25 in Fulton County, Georgia, survived a motion to dismiss in part and is in discovery, seeking unspecified damages.[2] The company has also received requests for information from the US Department of Justice and the Securities and Exchange Commission about how it recognizes revenue and reports ARR for transactions with certain customers.[2] These are open questions, not findings. Net outage-related costs were a $14.5 million credit in the second quarter against $35.7 million of expense a year earlier.[1]

What this means for an owner: the operating engine looks strong and is improving, and customers face real switching costs once the sensor is installed. But the engine is paid for with heavy stock pay, and the competitors are large companies that can bundle security with other products they already sell. That is why the next chapter scores the operating numbers on their own, and why Chapter 3 then tests whether the cost to owners is acceptable.

Debt has never been a problem. The company has had $750 million of senior notes since January 2021, carried at about $0.74 billion. Lenders often compare net debt with EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), a rough measure of operating profit. That cushion is why the solvency tests pass easily. Layer 1 underwriting: static levels, trajectory (rate of change), and archetype fit

Layer 1 tests static levels, which are where the numbers stand today. It also tests trajectory, which is the direction they are moving. The first group of tests are red lines for solvency, trend and governance. Failing a red line rejects a stock outright. The second group are floors for quality of earnings, such as the return on invested capital, cash generation and stock pay. Failing a floor means the stock must qualify under another archetype, and each archetype is tested after the table.

The third group are the technical floor and the asymmetry (reward-to-risk) floor. Asymmetry is the ratio of what we could gain to what we could lose from today's price. We calculate it as (central target minus price) divided by (price minus stop). The technical floor asks for Stage 2, the confirmed uptrend, and rejects Stage 4, the confirmed downtrend.

The target must also be anchored. One way is forward earnings per share (EPS) times the three-year median price-to-earnings multiple, which is the price paid for each dollar of earnings. The alternatives are a DCF or a measured move, which is the height of a recent range projected above its top. The tactical archetype is built for a breakout, a move above a prior high on strong volume.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Weakest assumption in the reverse DCF: house multiples

The 10% discount rate and the 18 times terminal multiple are house rules, not market facts. A company still growing 25% at year ten might deserve more than 18 times free cash flow. Moving the multiple from 12 to 25 times changes the implied growth between 37.2% and 29.1% a year. Moving the discount rate to 8% gives 30.4%. The company guides to 29% to 31% for the third quarter.[1][16] That will be known on the third-quarter report.

Weakest assumption in the central value: a two-turn fall in the multiple

We assume the sales multiple falls from 31.0 to 29 times, but nothing forces it to. The stock earned its multiple as net new ARR growth accelerated to 51% year over year and management raised its full-year net new ARR growth outlook from 22.5% to about 34%.[1][16] The 2026-10-16 fall came on a news report and a weak market, not on a bad quarter.[16][19] Suppose the December report shows net new ARR above the $343 to $347 million outlook and net retention above 115%.
