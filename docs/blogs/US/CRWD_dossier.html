1. What the company does

CrowdStrike Holdings, Inc. is a cybersecurity company based in Austin, Texas. It sells the Falcon platform, a cloud-based security platform that guards laptops and servers, cloud workloads, user identities and company data through a single lightweight sensor[3]. Customers can add modules, the platform's separate security tools, without adding more sensors to each device. The platform delivered 32 cloud modules at January 31, 2026 and delivers 33 today[3]. The fiscal year (FY) ends on January 31, so FY26 ended on January 31, 2026. The company says this single-sensor design separates it from older rivals, but that is the company's own claim, and Chapter 2 tests it against the numbers[3].

Customers pay mainly through subscriptions. Subscription revenue was 95% of total revenue in fiscal 2026 and fiscal 2025, and professional services such as incident response made up the other 5%[3]. Customers typically sign contracts of more than one year, and the company generally bills at the start of the term, so cash arrives before the revenue is recorded[2][3]. At January 31, 2026 the balance sheet carried $3,421.1 million of current deferred revenue plus $1,332.4 million of longer-term deferred revenue, and the company reported about $4.2 billion of signed contracts not yet billed[3]. This is why free cash flow can run ahead of reported profit.

The commercial model that matters most now is Falcon Flex, the company's flexible subscription offering, which management calls a key driver of platform consolidation[13]. Ending ARR from accounts on Flex passed $2.29 billion at July 31, 2026, up 101% from a year earlier[1]. Management says customers who convert from a standard subscription to Flex see average ending ARR rise by more than 40%, and that the company added more than 935 Flex accounts in the quarter[13]. These are management figures, not audited ones.

Sales run through the company's own sellers, partners, resellers and cloud marketplaces. In the second quarter, deal value passed through the Amazon, Google and Microsoft marketplaces reached $600 million, up more than 30% from a year earlier[13]. About 65% of second-quarter revenue came from the United States and 35% from other countries[13]. Incident response work also acts as a sales channel, because many customers who hire the team later buy the subscription[3].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • ARR (annual recurring revenue) is the yearly value of customer subscriptions as of a given date, assuming expiring contracts renew on the same terms.
  • Net new ARR is the ARR added in a period after subtracting lost or reduced subscriptions; it is the best single read on growth that has not yet reached revenue.
  • NRR (net revenue retention), also called the net retention rate, compares ARR from last year's customers with what the same customers pay now; above 100% means existing customers spend more over time.
  • Gross retention is the share of last year's ARR that was not lost to customers who left; it ignores any extra spending by customers who stayed.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operations minus spending on equipment and capitalized software; CrowdStrike also nets small deferred-compensation items.
  • Stock-based compensation (SBC) is the cost of paying staff in shares; it is a real cost because new shares dilute existing owners.
  • Reverse DCF works backwards from today's price to find the yearly growth in cash flow that the price needs, using a 10% discount rate and an 18-times exit multiple.
  • Breakout is a move of the price above the top of its recent trading range, usually on rising volume.
  • Pivot is the top of a base, the price level a breakout must clear; a base is a sideways trading range that comes before a move.
  • Stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold; it caps the loss before it grows.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The case for the business is real. ARR growth, which fell to 20% at July 31, 2025, has risen to 23%, 24%, 24% and now 25% at July 31, 2026[2][21][7][20]. Net new ARR, the amount added in a quarter, was $221.1 million in the second quarter of fiscal 2026 and $332.8 million in the second quarter of fiscal 2027, a rise of about 51%[2]. Management says net new ARR from new customers was a record and that both net and gross retention improved from the prior quarter[13].

The company's own risk filing lists the kinds of rivals it meets: older antivirus vendors, other endpoint providers, network security vendors adding endpoint tools, cloud security vendors, identity security vendors and service firms[3]. It does not name them in that passage, and it states that it does not believe any competitor has a platform equal to Falcon[3]. Treat that as marketing until peers' numbers say otherwise. In the peer table in Chapter 3, Palo Alto Networks, Zscaler, Fortinet and SentinelOne all report revenue growth between 21% and 34%, so CrowdStrike's 26% revenue growth sits in the middle of that group, not above it[22][23][24][25].

Several things make the position harder to attack. Customers sign contracts of more than one year. The Falcon platform sells many modules through one sensor, and 51% of subscription customers use six or more modules, 35% use seven or more and 26% use eight or more[1]. The more modules a customer runs, the more work it takes to switch away. That module share has crept up slowly from 49% at October 2025[12], so the pull toward more modules is steady, not explosive.

The weak points are cost, trust and price. On a GAAP basis (the audited accounting basis) the company lost $293.3 million from operations in fiscal 2026 on $4,812.0 million of revenue, and stock awards alone cost $1,096.7 million, more than the whole loss[3]. Trust took a hit on July 19, 2024, when a Falcon content update crashed certain Windows systems. The next subsection sets out what the filings say is still open. Price is the third weak point, covered in Chapters 5 and 6.

The July 2024 outage: customer credits and legal cases still open

After the outage the company offered customer commitment packages, which included discounts, extra modules, services, flexible payment terms or longer subscription periods. The company says these packages led to more contraction, through longer terms and smaller upsells[6][2]. Management said in March 2026 that accounts that took these deals now show gross and net retention above the company average[14]. That is a management statement that outside parties cannot yet check.

On the legal side, the passenger class action over flight disruptions was dismissed in June 2025, the Fifth Circuit affirmed the dismissal on May 20, 2026, and rehearing was denied on June 15, 2026[2]. Shareholder derivative suits in Texas and Delaware were ordered dismissed between March and April 2026[2]. Delta Air Lines sued in October 2024; a motion to dismiss was granted in part and denied in part in May 2025, and discovery continues[2]. The company has also received requests for information from the US Department of Justice and the Securities and Exchange Commission about how it recognises revenue and reports ARR for transactions with certain customers, among other matters[2].

The company says it cannot estimate the amount or range of any loss[2]. Costs booked so far are small: the net accrued balance was $13.1 million at July 31, 2026, and net expenses were $3.6 million in the first half of fiscal 2027[2]. The risk is not today's cost. The risk is that a regulator questions how ARR, the metric investors watch most, is counted. We keep this in view in Chapter 6.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 has a weakest assumption. This chapter names each, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when we will know. It also gives the strongest case against our own verdict, because a rejection is only worth holding if its best opposing argument has been heard.

The case against our verdict, put as strongly as we can: CrowdStrike is a Stage 2 leader that beat the S&P 500 by 40.18 points over 63 days, with net new ARR accelerating and a record pipeline for Q3[13]. Twelve of the 20 latest analyst target changes were increases[26]. A growth stock priced at 142.6 times current-year FCF can stay expensive for years, and holding the multiple would give $308.8. Our answer is that even the bull case gives a gain of $60 a share against a possible loss of $147, and the stock award and legal issues are not priced in the chart.

Fatal flaw 1: the demand surge fades

Our base case needs net new ARR to stay near the guided pace. The fiscal 2027 outlook of about $1.35 billion means the second half must add about $761 million, against about $595 million in the second half of FY26, a rise of 28%[13][7][2]. If growth falls short, FCF would follow within two to four quarters, and the multiple would likely fall at the same time. A fade is the most likely route to our bear case.

Fatal flaw 2: regulators question how ARR is counted

The company has received requests from the US Department of Justice and the Securities and Exchange Commission about its recognition of revenue and its reporting of ARR for transactions with certain customers[2]. ARR is the metric that drives every growth claim in this report, and the company defines it using assumptions about renewals[1]. No finding has been disclosed. But the company says it cannot estimate any loss, and the timing of any outcome is unknown. A restatement or enforcement action would hit the price far more than the dollar cost would suggest.
