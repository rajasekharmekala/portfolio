1. What the company does

ProShares UltraPro Dow30 trades on NYSE Arca under the ticker UDOW and is run by ProShare Advisors. Its stated aim is to deliver, before fees and expenses, three times the daily performance of the Dow Jones Industrial Average[1]. ProShares calls it the only ETF that targets three times the daily return of the Dow[3][5]. It began trading on February 9, 2010[6]. You pay for it through a total annual operating expense of 0.94%: a 0.75% management fee plus 0.19% of other costs[1]. The fund also pays the financing cost of its borrowed exposure, which the prospectus says lowers returns but does not list in the fee table[1].

The fund reaches three times the exposure with derivatives rather than by buying three dollars of stock for every dollar of cash. On May 31, 2026 it held stocks worth 54% of net assets, swap contracts worth 235% and index futures worth 11%, for 300% in total[2]. A swap is a contract in which a bank pays the fund the index return and the fund pays a financing charge. On 2026-10-23 six banks stood on the other side: Societe Generale, Bank of America, BNP Paribas, Barclays, Citibank and UBS. Their swaps carried $2.00 billion of exposure and a Dow futures contract added $138.8 million[4]. That adds up to about 3.0 times the fund's net assets of $874.4 million (our calculation from the daily holdings file)[4].

The fund resets every day. The prospectus says it rebalances each day so that its exposure matches the target, buying more exposure after a rise and cutting it after a fall[1]. Because of that reset, three times the daily move is a promise about one day only. Over longer spans the return can be higher or lower than three times the index, and the prospectus says the gap may be significant[1]. Regulators say the same: FINRA calls daily-reset leveraged ETFs typically unsuitable for retail investors who hold them longer than one trading session[27].

The index is the Dow Jones Industrial Average, a list of 30 large companies chosen without fixed quantitative rules and weighted by share price, not by company size[1][12]. S&P Dow Jones Indices decides the membership: Alphabet replaced Verizon on June 29, 2026, and Nvidia and Sherwin-Williams replaced Intel and Dow Inc. on November 8, 2024[13][14][15]. You hold 10 shares bought for $510.85 that are worth $672.60 at the $67.26 close, a gain of $161.75 and 0.82% of your $82,368.77 net worth, all in the Schwab account. The options book holds no UDOW contracts.

How a daily reset works in plain numbers

Take an index that rises 10% on day one and falls 10% on day two. It ends at 0.99 of its start, a 1.0% loss. A fund targeting three times each day gains 30% and then loses 30%, so it ends at 1.3 times 0.7, which is 0.91, a 9.0% loss. Three times the index's 1.0% loss would have been 3.0%, so the reset cost the fund six extra points in two days (our calculation). Choppy markets create this gap, and strong one-way markets reverse it: a steady climb lets the fund gain more than three times.

That is the whole idea behind the decay figures later in this report. The loss from choppiness grows with the square of volatility, so doubling the Dow's wobble roughly quadruples the cost. It is why we set a fixed holding limit instead of a long-term hold.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • The relative strength index (RSI) is a 0-to-100 meter of how fast a price has been moving. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • The average true range (ATR) is the typical size of one day's price swing, in dollars. UDOW's ATR of $2.06 is about 3.1% of its price.
  • Earnings per share (EPS) is a company's profit divided by its share count. For the Dow, an index-level EPS growth estimate is a weighted view of its 30 members.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash a business has left after paying to run itself and to buy equipment. A fund has none of its own, so no reverse DCF (a calculation that works backwards from price to implied growth) applies.
  • Price-to-earnings is the price paid for each dollar of yearly profit. A reading of 19.34 on next year's profit means buyers pay $19.34 per $1 of expected earnings.
  • A base is a sideways stretch of trading that a price builds before its next big move, like a floor under a staircase.
  • A pivot is the top of that sideways stretch, the price a move must clear to count as a real advance.
  • A breakout is a close above the pivot on strong trading volume.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend phase of the Weinstein scale and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend phase; Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase in between.
  • A stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong and the position is sold, so the loss is capped.
  • The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is (target price minus entry price) divided by (entry price minus stop price). At 2.5, a trade aims to make $2.50 for every $1 it can lose.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A leveraged fund competes on three things: how closely it delivers its multiple, how little it costs, and how safe the plumbing is. On the first, UDOW is the only fund offering three times the Dow, so it has no direct rival[3][5]. Its nearest substitutes are DDM, ProShares' own two-times Dow fund, and the plain DIA fund that holds the Dow stocks at one times[18][16]. A holder who wants more Dow exposure than DIA gives can borrow on margin, buy DDM, or buy UDOW and hold fewer shares.

On cost, UDOW charges a total annual expense of 0.94%, compared with 0.95% for DDM, 0.88% for UPRO (three times the S&P 500) and 0.94% before waivers for TQQQ (three times the Nasdaq-100), which is 0.78% after waivers[1][18][20][22]. DIA's gross expense ratio is 0.16%[16]. The extra 0.78 points over DIA buys the three-times target, not a better Dow. UDOW's size is respectable but smaller than its S&P 500 and Nasdaq cousins: $826.5 million of net assets at the May 31 year-end, against $5.60 billion for UPRO and $39.75 billion for TQQQ[2][21][23].

The plumbing looks sound. Six large banks hold the swaps, and no one bank carries more than 18.5% of the swap exposure (our calculation from the holdings file)[4]. The prospectus warns that a swap contract may let the bank close the trade at once, even during the day, if the index moves sharply and the fund's net assets drop[1]. In a fast crash that risk is real, but it has not shown up in tracking: over 252 sessions the fund's daily moves lined up with three times DIA's at a slope of 0.993 and a correlation of 0.999 (Chapter 3).

What the Dow brings to the structure

The Dow supplies the quality, and it is mixed. The 30 stocks are blue-chip names, yet the index is price-weighted, so it is not a map of the American economy by size. The SPDR Dow ETF reports a forward price-to-earnings ratio of 19.34 and a trailing 21.84 as of 2026-10-22, with an estimated 15.00% yearly growth in earnings per share over three to five years and an index dividend yield of 1.49%[16]. Financials are the heaviest sector at 26.28% of DIA, followed by information technology at 19.05% and industrials at 16.02% (as of 2026-09-30)[17].

The Dow also trailed the broad market lately. In the year to May 31, 2026 it returned 22.71%, against 29.78% for the S&P 500[2]. A three-times fund multiplies that gap, so UDOW's year to May 31, 2026 return of 58.50% sits far below UPRO's 88.11% and TQQQ's 142.72%[2][21][23]. Holding UDOW is therefore a bet on blue-chip financials and industrials, not on the leaders of the market's current advance.

What this means for an owner

The structure is sound and the cost is fair for its type, but the business of this fund is rented volatility. Its edge is convenience. Its weakness is that you pay a daily cost for it, and the Dow has lagged the S&P 500 over five years, at 10.18% a year against 14.14%[2]. That is why the verdict in Chapter 7 is a hold with a fixed end date and no new money, not a long-term holding.

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that can fail. The strongest honest bear case is simple.
