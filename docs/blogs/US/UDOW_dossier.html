1. What the company does

UDOW is the ProShares UltraPro Dow30 fund. An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a basket that trades on a stock exchange like a share. ProShare Advisors runs UDOW, and its stated goal is to deliver three times (3x) the daily percentage change of the Dow Jones Industrial Average, before fees and expenses[2]. If the Dow rises 1% in a day, the fund aims to rise 3%. If the Dow falls, the fund aims to fall three times as much. On October 16, 2026 the Dow closed at 52,002.41, down 0.34%, so a perfect 3x fund would have fallen 1.02%. UDOW closed at $66.56, down 1.20%, a shortfall of about 0.18 points against the target[11][10][9]. The fund began trading on February 11, 2010. It split its shares 2 for 1 on November 20, 2025, which halved the price per share and doubled the share count, so every price in this book is split-adjusted[6][5]. It is the only ETF that offers 3x daily exposure to the Dow[1].

The fund builds its exposure with three tools. At May 31, 2026 its market exposure was 300% of net assets: 54% in Dow stocks held directly, 235% in total-return swaps and 11% in futures on the Dow[4]. A total-return swap is a contract with a bank: the fund receives the index's gain or loss on an agreed amount and pays the bank a financing rate. UDOW had six swaps, each with a different bank, covering $1,938.2 million of notional amount in all (the face value on which the swap payments are calculated), and the rate the fund paid ran from 4.42% to 4.72%[5]. The rest of the fund's assets sit in short-term US Treasury bills, and $119.3 million of its securities were set aside at May 31 as collateral for the swaps[5]. Each evening the fund resets: it rebuilds its exposure to three times its net assets at that day's close. A simple picture: a driver whose cruise control is reset every evening to three times the speed reached that day. That reset is what makes the fund useful for a one-day bet and costly for a long hold.

The people who pay the fund are traders who want a short, amplified bet on blue-chip stocks. ProShare Advisors charges a management fee of 0.75% a year, and other expenses add 0.19%, so the stated total is 0.94%. A contract caps total expenses at 0.95% through September 30, 2027, and the actual ratio for the year to May 31, 2026 was 0.94%[2][4]. The annual report lists $122 of costs for its hypothetical $10,000 investment, which is more than 0.94% of $10,000 ($94), probably because the investment grew during a 58.5% year, but the annual report does not state the reason[4]. The advisory fees came to $5,546,691 for the year[4]. Two costs sit outside that table. The prospectus says the fund also pays transaction and financing costs that are not in the fee table[2]. Our own measurement from prices is larger: over the last 250 trading days the fund's price return was about 6.8% a year below what a perfect daily 3x rule would have produced. That gap covers fees, swap financing and tracking gaps, and it is our arithmetic from the price history[10][11]. Against it the fund paid four quarterly distributions, $0.69 a share in total, a yield of 1.04%, which are not in the price figures[8].

The engine behind the fund is the Dow Jones Industrial Average, a list of 30 large US companies, kept by S&P Dow Jones Indices. The index covers every industry except transportation and utilities, and a committee picks the members: stock selection is not governed by quantitative rules, and a company is typically added only if it has an excellent reputation, shows sustained growth and interests many investors[24][2]. Most well-known indexes, such as the S&P 500, weight each company by its market value, the share price times the number of shares. A company worth $5 trillion counts far more than one worth $80 billion. The Dow does not work that way. It is price-weighted, which means it adds up the 30 share prices and divides by a number called the divisor, 0.16824816528350 since June 29, 2026[25]. A stock with a high share price counts more, whatever the size of the company. We checked the arithmetic: the 30 closing prices on October 16 add up to $8,749.31, and dividing by the divisor gives 52,002.41, the index close[33][25][11].

The result is a lopsided index. Goldman Sachs, at $881.26 a share, is 10.07% of the Dow although it is worth only $266.8 billion, 0.91% of the 30 companies' combined value of $29.19 trillion. NVIDIA, at $214.13, is 2.45% of the Dow although it is worth $5.17 trillion, 17.71% of the combined value[33][19]. Every $1 move in any member shifts the index by 5.9436 points[25]. A 10% fall in Goldman Sachs would take 523.8 points off the Dow, or 1.01%, and a 10% fall in NVIDIA would take off 127.3 points, or 0.24%.

For a 3x fund this has three consequences. First, UDOW is a bet on a few high-priced names: the three highest-priced members (Goldman Sachs, Caterpillar and Microsoft) are 24.47% of the index and the ten highest-priced are 54.76%, while the ten lowest-priced together are 15.31%[33]. Second, the index leans toward financials, which are 26.6% of it, followed by technology at 19.1% and industrials at 14.8%[27]. Third, the weights drift with share prices, and the committee's choices matter: Verizon, a low-priced stock, was only about half of one percent of the index when it left[26]. A 3x fund multiplies all of this by three each day, so a 10% fall in Goldman Sachs alone would cost the fund about 3.0%.

The measures this book uses

Several measures come up in later chapters, so here they are in plain words. Earnings per share (EPS) is a company's profit divided by its number of shares, and free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after paying for equipment. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is operating profit before borrowing costs, taxes and the wear on equipment. Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money put into the business. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given to staff in shares. Net debt is debt minus cash, and the price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share. A consensus estimate is the average of analysts' forecasts. A basis point is one hundredth of one percent, so 50 basis points is 0.5%. Beta is how many percent a holding tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%. A percentile ranks an outcome among many: the 10th percentile of three-month moves is a fall that only 10% of the periods we measured exceeded.

The chart terms are these. The relative strength index (RSI) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has been moving, and below 30 is called oversold. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing in dollars. A 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days, and the 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average work the same way over longer spans. A base is a flat floor that a price builds before a move, the pivot is the top of that floor, and a breakout is a move above it. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend, Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase after it, and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend. A stop loss is the price at which the idea is wrong and the position is sold. The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned. Asymmetry is the same as reward-to-risk: the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Leveraged ETF — a fund that aims to return a multiple (here 3 times) of one day's move in something else.
  • Daily reset — the fund rebuilds its 3x exposure at every close, so gains and losses compound day after day.
  • Swap — a contract with a bank that pays the fund the index's gain or loss in return for a financing fee.
  • Counterparty — the bank on the other side of a swap; if it fails to pay, the fund can lose the gain it is owed.
  • Volatility drag — the slow loss a daily-reset fund suffers when the index swings up and down, even if the index ends where it began.
  • Price-weighted — an index where each member counts in proportion to its share price, not its company size.
  • Divisor — the number the sum of the 30 prices is divided by to give the index level; it changes when a member splits or is replaced.
  • Net asset value (NAV) — the value of everything the fund owns, per share, calculated at the close.
  • Dow — the Dow Jones Industrial Average, the index UDOW follows.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A fund has no brand or factory, so its strength is how well it does the one job it promises and how it compares with rivals doing the same job. On both counts UDOW is sound and unremarkable. It is the only ETF that offers 3x daily exposure to the Dow[1]. The closest rival is its own sibling, DDM, which offers 2x the Dow with $526.07 million of assets and an expense ratio of 0.95%[20]. Investors who want 3x exposure to other indexes have UPRO and SPXL (S&P 500) and TQQQ (Nasdaq-100), which are much larger[21][22][23]. UDOW's edge is that it is the only way to hold a leveraged Dow in a single ticker, not that it is cheaper or larger.

The strongest evidence is the daily match. In the five sessions of October 12 to 16 the Dow moved +0.38%, -0.35%, -0.09%, +1.08% and -0.34%, so a perfect 3x fund would have moved +1.14%, -1.05%, -0.27%, +3.24% and -1.02%. UDOW moved +1.10%, -1.03%, -0.35%, +3.19% and -1.20%[10][11][9]. The daily gaps were -0.04, +0.02, -0.08, -0.05 and -0.18 points. About 54% of the fund's exposure was held in Dow stocks directly, which reduces how much depends on swaps[4].

Weakness: the daily reset eats value when the index swings

The prospectus states the weakness plainly. Over a period longer than one day, the fund's return can differ a lot from three times the index's return, and the fund can lose money when the index is flat. Its table shows a fund losing 3.0% over a year if the index goes nowhere at 10% volatility, 17.1% at 25% volatility and 52.8% at 50% volatility[2]. FINRA warned in 2009 that leveraged and inverse funds that reset daily typically are unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them for longer than one trading session, and in October 2021 the SEC chair directed his staff to study the risks of complex exchange-traded products[30][31].

The Dow's volatility, which is how much it swings from day to day, is moderate: over the same 250 sessions the S&P 500's was 12.8% and the Nasdaq-100's 19.8%[16][17]. Measured from daily closes the Dow's is 12.7% a year over the last 250 trading days, 9.3% over the last 20 and 15.0% over the last 500[11]. The index's five-year volatility to May 31, 2026 was 14.79%, and its highest one-year reading in that span was 17.52%[2]. Real prices show what that does. From October 17, 2025 to October 16, 2026 the Dow rose 12.58% and UDOW rose 26.84%. Simple tripling of the index's gain would give 37.75%. Compounding each day's tripled return gives 36.05%, which is 1.69 points of drag. The fund's actual result was another 9.21 points below that, the cost of fees, financing and tracking gaps. The fund kept 71% of the tripled gain[10][11].

Over two years the picture is worse. From October 17, 2024 to October 16, 2026 the Dow rose 20.27%, simple tripling gives 60.80% and UDOW returned 30.75%, which is 51% of the tripled gain[10][11]. The issuer's longer record says the same: to May 31, 2026 the fund returned 13.58% a year over five years against 10.18% for the Dow, which is 1.33 times and not 3 times, and 23.54% a year over ten years against 13.48%, which is 1.75 times[4]. The daily promise is kept, but the promise does not carry over to long holds.

What a 3x fund does in a drawdown

A drawdown is a fall from a peak to a low. A 3x fund falls three times as far on a single day and, over many days, a little less than three times as far, because each day's loss is taken from a smaller base. The prospectus warns that if the index approaches a 33% loss at any point in a day, you could lose your entire investment, and that a swap counterparty may close out its swap with the fund immediately, including during the day, if the index makes a dramatic move[2]. The worst quarter in the fund's record, the three months to March 31, 2020, was a loss of 66.36%[2].

The recent record shows the same shape. From the December 4, 2024 peak to the April 8, 2025 low the Dow fell 16.37% and UDOW fell 45.10%, 2.76 times as far[10][11]. A fall of 45% needs a gain of 82% to recover. Chapter 3 turns this into a table of rules of thumb for today's price of $66.56.

3. The numbers, in plain words

A fund has no sales or profit of its own, so the numbers that matter come in groups. The first is the fund's own facts: its size, its price and its cost. The second is the index's path over five years and the combined financial statements of its 30 companies, which drive the fund's results. Then come the portfolio fit, the framework's Layer 1 tests and the peers. The Dow's own history is in calendar years, and the members' statements are in the trailing twelve months to the latest quarter each has reported, which for most is June 2026.

The financial tests apply to the Dow, because a fund has no operations. We treat the index as the sum of one share of each member, which is how a price-weighted index works. For each member we take its figure per share, from the data page's own total and share count, and add the 30 results. This ties out: the 30 prices add up to the index close at the divisor, the earnings per share add up to a price-to-earnings of 20.92, and the forward figures give 19.34, which match the DIA data page to the cent[33][18][25]. Four members are financial firms whose cash flow and debt are not comparable (Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, American Express and Travelers), so the cash flow, debt and return tests use the other 26. Visa is kept, because it is a payments company with ordinary cash flow.

We use the tactical asymmetry floor of 3.5 to 1 for the leveraged satellite bucket, because the configuration lists no separate floor for it. Return on invested capital is the vendor's figure for each member, and we report a price-weighted average and the median, because the invested capital of each member is not on the page. The averages are therefore a guide to the group, not an exact measure of the index.

The number

Over three months the Dow must rise about 0.98% (from 52,002.41 to about 52,512) for UDOW to finish flat, if the Dow's volatility stays near 12.7% a year and the fund's holding costs stay near 6.8% a year.

How it was calculated

A daily-reset 3x fund is worth about its starting value times the index's price ratio cubed, times a decay factor of e to the power of minus three times volatility squared times the years, times one minus the yearly cost times the years. With 12.7% volatility and 0.25 years the decay factor is 0.9880, and the cost factor is 1 - 0.0677 x 0.25 = 0.9831. Setting the product to one gives a price ratio cubed of 1 / (0.9880 x 0.9831), so the ratio is 1.0098. This is the standard approximation and our simplification, not a number from the fund.

The inputs

Volatility of 12.7% from 250 daily closes of the Dow[11]. A yearly cost of 6.8%, measured as the gap between UDOW's 250-day price return and the daily-compounded 3x return on the Dow[10][11]. The start level of 52,002.41[11].

The range

At the 20-day volatility of 9.3% the break-even is 0.79%; at the 500-day volatility of 15.0% it is 1.14%; at 20% it is 1.58%, at 30% 2.86% and at 50% 7.06%. Decay alone over three months is 1.20% today and 6.53% at 30% volatility.

If volatility rises toward 30% the hurdle climbs from 1% to almost 3% and a rising Dow can still leave the fund lower. If costs fall, as they would if short-term interest rates fell, the hurdle shrinks only slightly, so volatility matters more than cost.

Our variant perception

Our view differs from the market's on the vehicle, not on the index. Analysts are constructive: the members' mean price targets add up to 14.9% above today's prices, and 21 of 30 members had their 2026 estimates raised in the last 60 days[33][32]. We do not dispute that earnings are rising. Our difference is that a healthy index does not make a good 3x fund today. The fund's chart is in Stage 3, its 50-day average sits 4.1% above the price and falling, and holding it costs about 6.8% a year before any decay.

The second difference is the cause of the recent pressure. The week's weakness was in chips, neoclouds and technology, which are about a fifth of the Dow by price, and the Dow gained 0.67% as money moved to consumer staples (+5.02% for the week) and health care (+3.45%)[28]. That is the Dow behaving as a defensive index. The risk we weight more than the market does is the rate path: September inflation was 3.7%, the 10-year yield is above the Dow's earnings yield, and futures price an 89% chance of a hike by December[28]. A 3x fund on an index with a 26.6% financial share can lose a quarter of its value in a sharp reversal, so patience for a chart confirmation is cheaper than guessing the turn.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, the evidence that would show it wrong and when we will know. It then gives three fatal-flaw scenarios, the strongest case against our own conclusion and the single claim the conclusion depends on.

Volatility and cost. We assumed the Dow's volatility of 12.7% a year and a holding cost of 6.8% a year. Neither is stable. The Dow's volatility was 9.3% over the last 20 sessions, 12.7% over 250 and 15.0% over 500, all measured from daily closes[11]. At 30% volatility the three-month break-even rises from +0.98% to +2.86% and decay alone takes 6.5% of value in three months. The cost estimate comes from one year of prices and includes swap financing, which is quoted as a rate over short-term interest rates and ran from 4.42% to 4.72%[5]. A rate rise, which futures price at 17% for October and 89% for December, would raise the cost[28]. The evidence that would show us wrong is realized volatility above 20% or below 8% for a month, or a visible jump in the daily gap. We will know within 20 trading days.

Scenario prices and weights. The levels of 54,349, 53,035 and 49,657 come from the record close, an earnings carry and a statistical fall, not from a valuation, and the 25%, 50% and 25% weights are a convention, not a forecast[11]. The members' analyst targets point to a Dow near 59,731, a real counterweight[33]. The evidence that would show our weights wrong is the September-quarter reports. A margin above 12.0% and a further rise in revisions would raise the bull weight; falling estimates would raise the bear weight.

Fatal flaw 1: a gap through the stop

The $60.49 stop is $6.07 below the close, 9.1% or 3.04 times one normal day's swing. The fund moved more than 5% in a day on 11 of the last 250 sessions, and its worst day was -6.50% on 2026-07-29[10]. A stop can be jumped, either overnight or inside one session, and then the loss is larger than planned. The Dow's worst day in our two-year record was -5.50% on April 4, 2025, which a 3x fund turns into about -16.5%, and a 10% Dow fall in a day, nearly twice that, would cost the fund 30%[11]. Treat the stop as a line where the idea is wrong, not as a guarantee of the exit price.

Fatal flaw 2: a counterparty or fund event

Six banks owe the fund swap gains of $104.5 million, 12.6% of net assets, and the amount beyond collateral was $11.14 million at May 31[5]. Each swap can be ended at will by either party without penalty, and the prospectus says a counterparty may close a swap immediately, including during the day, after a dramatic index move[5][2]. If the fund could not replace a swap it might not reach its objective. Issuers also close funds, and the fee waiver runs only to September 30, 2027[2]. Nothing in the filings says any of this is imminent, and we cannot put a probability on it. It makes the downside worse than the formula in Chapter 5 shows.

Fatal flaw 3: the Dow's price-weighted concentration meets the rate path

Goldman Sachs and Caterpillar together are 18.42% of the Dow by price[33]. A 20% fall in just those two would take 3.68% off the Dow and about 11.1% off the fund, before anything else moves. Financials are 26.6% of the index[27]. With the 10-year yield at 5.098%, above the Dow's trailing earnings yield, inflation at 3.7% and WTI crude at $101.41 on October 16, a rate shock could hit financials and industrials together while the defensive members fall too[28]. This is the scenario in which a low-volatility index stops behaving like one, and the 3x fund compounds the loss.

Basket method. The Dow's combined figures are the sum, over the 30 members, of each member's trailing total divided by its shares outstanding, which is one share of each. The method ties out (prices to the index close at the divisor, earnings per share to the price-to-earnings of 20.92 and 19.34 on the DIA data page). ROIC is a price-weighted average of vendor figures, not capital-weighted. The free cash flow margin a year ago uses today's share counts and the 21 members whose income-statement page is quarterly. Members that have not yet reported the September quarter are on June or July trailing data. The scenario formula is the standard 3x approximation and ignores gaps and counterparty events.
