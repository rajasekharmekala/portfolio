1. What the company does

ProShares UltraPro Dow30 trades on NYSE Arca under the ticker UDOW and is run by ProShare Advisors. Its stated aim is to deliver, before fees and expenses, three times the daily performance of the Dow Jones Industrial Average[1]. ProShares calls it the only ETF that targets three times the daily return of the Dow[3][5]. It began trading on February 9, 2010[6]. You pay for it through a total annual operating expense of 0.94%: a 0.75% management fee plus 0.19% of other costs[1]. The fund also pays the financing cost of its borrowed exposure. The prospectus says that cost lowers returns but does not list it in the fee table[1].

The fund reaches three times the exposure with derivatives, not by buying three dollars of stock for every dollar of cash. On May 31, 2026 it held stocks worth 54% of net assets, swap contracts worth 235% and index futures worth 11%, for 300% in total[2]. A swap is a contract in which a bank pays the fund the index return and the fund pays the bank a financing charge. On 2026-10-27 six banks stood on the other side: Societe Generale, BNP Paribas, Bank of America, Barclays, Citibank and UBS. Their swaps carried $1.995 billion of exposure, a December Dow futures contract added $140.9 million, and 30 stocks added $472.1 million[4]. That is $2.607 billion in all. Net assets come to about $869.0 million if we add the market values listed in the holdings file, and about $879.7 million if we multiply the 12,950,000 shares outstanding by the $67.93 close of 2026-10-27, so exposure is 2.96 to 3.00 times net assets (our calculation)[4][8][27].

The fund resets every day. The prospectus says it rebalances each day so that its exposure matches the target[1]. Because of that reset, three times the daily move is a promise about one day only. Over longer spans the return can be higher or lower than three times the index, and the prospectus says the gap may be significant[1]. The brokers' regulator, FINRA, says leveraged funds that reset daily are typically unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them for longer than one trading session[35].

The index is the Dow Jones Industrial Average, a list of 30 large companies chosen without fixed quantitative rules and weighted by share price, not by company size[1][15]. S&P Dow Jones Indices decides the membership. Alphabet replaced Verizon effective June 29, 2026, and NVIDIA and Sherwin-Williams replaced Intel and Dow Inc. on November 8, 2024[16][17]. How a daily reset works in plain numbers

Take an index that rises 10% on day one and falls 10% on day two. It ends at 0.99 of its start, a 1.0% loss. A fund targeting three times each day gains 30% and then loses 30%, so it ends at 1.3 times 0.7, which is 0.91, a 9.0% loss. Three times the index's 1.0% loss would have been 3.0%, so the reset cost the fund six extra points in two days (our calculation). Choppy markets create this gap, and strong one-way markets reverse it: a steady climb lets the fund gain more than three times.

That is the whole idea behind the decay figures later in this report. The loss from choppiness grows with the square of volatility, so doubling the Dow's wobble roughly quadruples the cost. The prospectus shows it: a flat index at 10% yearly volatility costs the fund 3.0% in a year, and at 25% it costs 17.1%[1]. It is why we set a fixed holding limit instead of a long-term hold.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • The relative strength index (RSI) is a 0-to-100 meter of how fast a price has been moving. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • The average true range (ATR) is the typical size of one day's price swing, in dollars. UDOW's ATR of $1.89 is about 2.8% of its price.
  • Earnings per share (EPS) is a company's profit divided by its share count. For the Dow, an index-level figure is a weighted view of its 30 members.
  • Price-to-earnings is the price paid for each dollar of yearly profit. A reading of 19.10 on next year's profit means buyers pay $19.10 for each $1 of expected earnings.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash a business has left after paying to run itself and to buy equipment. A fund has none of its own, so a reverse DCF (a calculation that works backwards from a price to the growth it assumes) is not defined for the fund itself.
  • Return on invested capital (ROIC) is the profit earned on each dollar a business has put to work. It is a test written for companies, so it is not defined for a fund.
  • Stock-based compensation (SBC) is the cost of paying employees in shares. It is also a company test and is not defined for a fund.
  • Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is profit before financing and accounting charges. It is the profit measure used in the debt test in Layer 1.
  • Net debt is interest-bearing debt minus the cash held. The fund borrows through swaps rather than loans, so the test is not defined for it.
  • A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days. The 20-day, 50-day and 200-day averages are slow lines that a price tends to bounce off, and a falling average means recent prices are lower than older ones.
  • A base is a sideways stretch of trading that a price builds before its next big move, like a floor under a staircase.
  • A pivot is the top of that sideways stretch, the price a move must clear to count as a real advance.
  • A breakout is a close above the pivot on strong trading volume.
  • Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend phase of the Weinstein scale and Stage 4 is the confirmed downtrend phase; Stage 3 is the topping or pullback phase in between.
  • A stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong and the position is sold, so the loss is capped.
  • A limit order is an order that only fills at your price or better. It does not chase the market.
  • The entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the target price minus the entry price, divided by the entry price minus the stop price. At 2.5, a trade aims to make $2.50 for every $1 it can lose.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

A leveraged fund competes on three things: how closely it delivers its multiple, how little it costs, and how safe the plumbing is. On the first, UDOW is the only fund offering three times the Dow, so it has no direct rival[3][5]. Its nearest substitutes are DDM, ProShares' own two-times Dow fund, and the plain DIA fund that holds the Dow stocks at one times[21][18]. A holder who wants more Dow exposure than DIA gives can borrow on margin, buy DDM, or buy UDOW and hold fewer shares.

On cost, UDOW charges a total annual expense of 0.94%, compared with 0.95% for DDM, 0.88% for UPRO (three times the S&P 500) and 0.94% before waivers for TQQQ (three times the Nasdaq-100), which is 0.78% after waivers[1][21][23][25]. DIA's gross expense ratio is 0.16%, and its 30-day median bid-ask spread is 0.01%[18]. The extra 0.78 points over DIA buys the three-times target, not a better Dow. UDOW's size is respectable but smaller than its S&P 500 and Nasdaq cousins: $826.5 million of net assets at the May 31 year-end, against $523.4 million for DDM, $5.60 billion for UPRO and $39.75 billion for TQQQ[2][22][24][26]. Money has also left the fund: it saw net outflows of $185.69 million in the 12 months to May 2026[6].

The plumbing looks sound. Six large banks hold the swaps, and no one bank carries more than 18.6% of the swap exposure (our calculation from the holdings file)[4]. The prospectus warns that a swap contract may let the bank close the trade at once, even during the day, if the index has a dramatic move that cuts the fund's net assets[1]. In a fast crash that risk is real. It has not shown up in tracking: over 252 sessions the fund's daily moves lined up with three times DIA's at a slope of 0.993 and a correlation of 0.999 (Chapter 3). The fund trades close to its net asset value, at a discount of 0.04% on 2026-10-26[9].

The Dow supplies the underlying quality, and it is mixed. The 30 stocks are blue-chip names, yet the index is price-weighted, so it is not a map of the American economy by size. The SPDR Dow ETF reports a forward price-to-earnings ratio of 19.10 and a trailing 21.16 as of 2026-10-27, with an estimated 13.96% yearly growth in earnings per share over three to five years and an index dividend yield of 1.50%[18]. Financials are the heaviest sector at 26.28% of DIA, followed by information technology at 19.05% and industrials at 16.02% (as of 2026-09-30)[19].

What the Dow has delivered against the market

The Dow trailed the broad market lately. In the year to May 31, 2026 it returned 22.71%, against 29.78% for the S&P 500[2]. A three-times fund multiplies that gap, so UDOW's return for the year of 58.50% sits far below UPRO's 88.11% and TQQQ's 142.72%[2][24][26]. Over five years the Dow returned 10.18% a year against 14.14% for the S&P 500[2]. Holding UDOW is therefore a bet on blue-chip financials and industrials, not on the leaders of the market's current advance.

What this means for an owner: the structure is sound and the cost is fair for its type, but the business of this fund is rented volatility. Its edge is convenience. Its weakness is that you pay a daily cost for it. That is why the verdict in Chapter 7 is a partial sale now, with a capped hold on the rest to a fixed end date and no new money, not a long-term holding.

5. What could happen next

A leveraged fund held for about three months does not behave like one held for a year, so the decision here uses scenarios that end on the day we would sell. That day is 2027-01-22, 58 trading days after today's close, or 58/252 of a year (our count of weekdays after 2026-10-28, less Thanksgiving, Christmas, New Year's Day and Martin Luther King Day). We model the Dow first, then convert each Dow path into a UDOW path with the daily-reset formula. UDOW's growth factor for a year equals (1 + Dow price return) cubed, times exp(-3 x volatility squared), times a cost factor of 0.9354 calibrated to the last twelve months (Chapter 3). For the holding window we raise that annual factor to the power 58/252, which assumes the annual pace and the volatility hold evenly through the window. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that can fail. For the central price of $70.33 it is that the Dow's annual pace and its 14.79% volatility hold evenly through the 58 sessions and that the cost factor stays near 0.9354. The evidence is DIA's 63-day realised volatility, which stood at 10.59% on 2026-10-28 and which we read at every weekly review through 2027-01-22[28]. Carry matters less than the horizon: a 3% or 9% annual cost drag moves the central price only between $70.92 and $69.89, but on a twelve-month view the same 9% drag would cut the central price from $76.98 to about $74.89. For the probability-weighted price, the weakest part is the 25% weight on the bull case, which needs the Dow to repeat its best recent pace while it is in Stage 3. For the bear price, the weakest assumption is that the fall is smooth: real crashes come as gaps, and a gap can pass through a stop. For the look-through reverse DCF, the weakest input is price to cash flow, which is operating cash flow and not free cash flow; the evidence will come as the 30 members report, with the last of them, American Express, due about 2027-01-22 (an aggregator estimate)[34]. For the sale-plan value in the last two estimates of Chapter 5, the weakest assumption is the probability split: the break-even bear probabilities of 27% for the old plan and 33% for the kept shares sit close to our 30%, so a small change in that judgment flips the sign. The evidence will be DIA's path and volatility through the weekly reviews.

The strongest honest bear case is simple. The Dow is in a pullback with a falling 50-day average, UDOW trails the S&P 500 by 5.01 points over 63 sessions, the Dow trades at 19.10 times forward earnings, and the fund quietly costs about 6.5% a year in carry before any volatility drag. The $173.75 gain on this position equals the loss from a 25% fall in UDOW, which is about an 8% fall in the Dow. The position is small, but the instrument turns a modest Dow decline into a large one[18][36].

If the Dow's yearly volatility rises from about 12% to 25% and the index goes nowhere, the prospectus table shows a flat index costing the fund 17.1% in a year before fees and financing[1]. In 2020, with volatility of 36.6%, the Dow rose 7.26% while UDOW fell 17.43% (our calculation)[27][28]. This would break the central case and the probability weights.
