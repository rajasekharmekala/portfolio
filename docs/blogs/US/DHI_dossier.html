1. What the company does

DHI builds and sells houses. It has been the largest US homebuilder by number of homes closed every year since 2002, and it operates in 126 markets across 36 states[1]. In the twelve months to June 30, 2026 it closed 84,655 homes, and across its 47-year history it has closed 1.3 million[1]. Most buyers are people buying a first home: 65% of the customers of its mortgage arm in the June quarter were first-time buyers, and the average closing price was $362,000, about $155,000 or 30% below the average price of a new home in the country[15]. That low price is the company's chosen niche, and it explains why demand moves so sharply with mortgage rates. A buyer stretching to afford a $362,000 house feels a one-point change in the loan rate in the monthly payment at once. This report updates our earlier report on DHI, written on 2026-10-23, which the chapters below call our 2026-10-23 report.

The money comes from the gap between the price of a finished house and what it cost to make. A house has three big costs: the lot, the construction (builders call it stick and brick), and the incentives given to close the sale, such as paying down the buyer's mortgage rate. In the June 2026 quarter DHI took in $8,681.9 million from home sales and spent $6,884.6 million on them, so its gross margin — the share of revenue left after direct costs — on home sales was 20.7%, down from 21.8% a year earlier, when it took in $8,561.0 million against $6,691.6 million of cost[1][10]. Per square foot, revenue fell 3% year on year, stick and brick costs fell 5%, and lot costs rose 5%[15]. Selling, general and administrative costs took 8.3% of homebuilding revenue, up from 7.8%[15]. After all of this the company earned $904.9 million, or $3.20 for each diluted share[1].

Land is the second half of the model. DHI owned or controlled 568,500 lots at June 30, 2026. Only 22% (126,600 lots) were owned. The other 78% (441,900 lots) were controlled through purchase contracts, which means the company holds a right to buy lots later and does not carry them on its balance sheet today[1]. A year earlier it owned 145,900 lots, so owned lots fell 13.2%[1][10]. Forestar, a lot developer that DHI majority-owns, supplies many of the finished lots. In the first nine months of fiscal 2026, 67% of homes closed stood on lots developed by Forestar or by third parties[1]. This holds down the cash tied up in land, but the business is not asset-light: at June 30, 2026 inventory was $26,686.9 million of $36,511.3 million in total assets[1].

The cycle runs from order to backlog to closing, and customers pay at closing. Backlog is homes under contract that have not yet closed: 15,983 homes worth $6,179.4 million at June 30, 2026, up 13.6% in homes and 15.8% in value from 14,075 homes and $5,338.1 million a year earlier[1]. Of every ten orders placed in the June quarter, two were cancelled, against about 1.7 in the year-earlier quarter[1]. DHI also steers demand with mortgage rate buydowns, which are payments that lower the buyer's loan rate for a period: the average buydown was 1.6% of the price, and buyers in the backlog held a 4.9% mortgage against a market rate of about 6.5% at June 30[15]. The market rate has since moved up, to 7.32% in the Freddie Mac survey of October 22, 2026, against 6.19% a year earlier[25].

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPS (earnings per share): net profit divided by the number of shares. It rises when profit rises or when the company buys back shares.
  • FCF (free cash flow): cash from operations minus spending on equipment and buildings. It is the cash left for owners.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital): after-tax operating profit divided by the debt and equity used to earn it.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization): a rough measure of operating cash earnings used to judge debt.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation): the cost of paying staff in shares, which dilutes owners.
  • Backlog: homes under contract that have not yet closed. It is the revenue already signed.
  • Buydown: a payment by the builder that lowers a buyer's mortgage rate for a period.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

DHI's first advantage is scale. It closed 84,655 homes in the twelve months to June 30, 2026, more than any other US builder, in 126 markets[1]. Scale gives it buying power with suppliers and trade crews: in the June quarter its stick and brick cost per square foot fell 5% from a year earlier, and the median time from starting a house to closing it improved by about three weeks[15]. Faster builds mean less money sits in unfinished houses. Its second advantage is a flexible land position. With 78% of lots controlled by contract and owned lots down 13.2% in a year, DHI can walk away from a lot deal if demand weakens[1][10].

The third advantage is the balance sheet. Net debt to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization is 1.15 times, or 0.79 times without the debt that funds mortgage loans, and the company's debt-to-capital ratio was 23.0% at June 30, 2026 against a stated long-term target of about 20%[1]. Liquidity was $6.1 billion, and $600 million of homebuilding senior notes mature within twelve months[1]. Credit-rating agency pages could not be fetched (HTTP 403 or an empty page), so agency ratings are N/A in this report. The evidence for solvency is the filed leverage, not a rating.

Where DHI lags is returns and margin. Its return on invested capital is 10.4% by our calculation (Chapter 3) and its return on equity is 12.8%[1], against 31.41% and 28.30% at NVR, 13.46% and 13.86% at PulteGroup and 11.68% and 14.39% at Toll Brothers, each pair given as return on invested capital and then return on equity on the data provider's definitions[37][36][38]. Only Lennar (5.18% and 5.96%) and KB Home (4.17% and 5.91%) are lower[35][39]. DHI's home sales gross margin of 20.7% sits above Lennar (15.8%), KB Home (16.5%) and NVR (18.3%) but below Toll Brothers (23.9%) and PulteGroup (24.8%)[31][34][32][33][30]. Scale has not bought the best returns, because the company's price point is the lowest and its selling costs are not shrinking with its sales.

Growth has stalled. Revenue was $33,480.0 million in fiscal 2022 and is $33,350.0 million over the twelve months to June 2026, and the company said on the July call that it has not seen growth on its top line for a few years[4][15]. It also said that opening about 30 markets in five years has cost it efficiency in overhead. Selling, general and administrative costs rose to 8.3% of homebuilding revenue from 7.8%, which is about 8% higher as a share of revenue, while orders were flat. Consolidated SG&A dollars rose 5.0% ($991.2 million against $944.3 million) on homes closed up 4%[15][1]. Management expects overhead leverage to return when revenue growth resumes, which is a promise about the future and not yet a result.

What the housing market is doing now

The market is weak, with the latest weekly mortgage rate slightly lower. The average 30-year mortgage rate was 7.32% in the Freddie Mac survey of October 22, 2026, down from 7.40% the week before but above the 6.19% of a year earlier[25]. The Mortgage Bankers Association put its own 30-year rate at 7.37% for the week ending October 23, with the purchase index 18% below the same week a year earlier[29]. Builder confidence, measured by the National Association of Home Builders index, fell three points to 29 in October, the lowest since June 2012; 43% of builders cut prices that month and 65% used incentives[26].

The central bank is not helping. It raised its policy rate by a quarter point in September, and on October 28, 2026, after our ledger date, it left the range at 3-3/4 to 4 percent on a 10 to 2 vote in which both dissenters wanted a further increase[27][28]. The 10-year Treasury yield, which mortgage rates track, was 5.05% on that day[16]. Peers' latest reports, from August to October, show the effect on orders: PulteGroup fell 6%, Lennar 9%, NVR 7% and KB Home 12%, while Toll Brothers, which sells luxury homes, rose 5%[30][31][32][34][33]. DHI's own June-quarter orders were flat, and the September-quarter orders arrive on October 29.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Five fiscal years (October to September) show a business that grew fast to 2022, then gave back margin and profit while sales stayed flat. Revenue rose from $27,774.2 million in fiscal 2021 to a peak of $36,801.4 million in fiscal 2024 and fell to $34,250.4 million in fiscal 2025. Over the twelve months to June 30, 2026 it was $33,350.0 million[4][3][1]. Net income attributable to DHI fell from $5,857.5 million in fiscal 2022 to $3,585.2 million in fiscal 2025 and $3,052.9 million over the latest twelve months, and diluted earnings per share fell from $16.51 to $11.57 and then to $10.49[4][3][1].

Two numbers explain most of the fall. Revenue less cost of sales, which is the company's closest line to gross profit because DHI does not report one, shrank from 31.4% of revenue in fiscal 2022 to 23.7% in fiscal 2025 and 22.6% over the latest twelve months, as incentives and lot costs rose while prices softened[4][3][1]. Selling, general and administrative costs also rose in dollars, from $2,933.7 million in fiscal 2022 to $3,692.0 million in fiscal 2025, while revenue was flat[4][3]. The three-year compound growth rate from fiscal 2022 to fiscal 2025 was +0.76% a year for revenue, -15.09% for net income and -11.18% for diluted earnings per share. Measured from fiscal 2023 to the latest twelve months the rates are -2.21% for revenue, -14.82% for net income and -9.5% for diluted earnings per share.

The market's view, in the consensus numbers, is that earnings per share rise 6.9% in fiscal 2027, from $10.43 to $11.15, on revenue 2.7% higher at $33.65 billion[16]. Our difference from the market is about price and multiple, not about the business. At the same time the cash flow reverse DCF says the price assumes no growth, so the market is not paying for a recovery either. We therefore see a fairly priced stock with falling estimates: neither the premium multiple nor the zero-growth cash flow price is a bargain, and the rule for this book is to buy a turnaround only when the reward-to-risk clears 2.5 to 1. Where the signals conflict, we weight the multiple test over the cash flow test, for three reasons. Second, the 18 times exit multiple on cash flow is generous for a cyclical business whose own cash flow yield is 7.7%. The cash flow test still matters as the argument for patience, because it says a lot of bad news is already in the price, and it is the reason this report is not a rejection. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is most likely to be wrong. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong, and gives the date the evidence arrives. It then argues against our own verdict as strongly as the facts allow.

Fatal flaw 1: mortgage rates stay at or above 7.3% and orders break

The base case assumes orders stay flat to slightly down. The 30-year rate was 7.32% on October 22, up from 6.19% a year earlier, the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate in September, and on October 28 two of twelve voters wanted a further increase[25][28][27]. If rates stay there, DHI's incentives have to grow to hold orders, and each point of incentive comes straight out of margin. Orders fell 6% to 12% at four of the five peers in their latest quarters (Toll Brothers rose 5%), the Mortgage Bankers Association purchase index is 18% below last year, and 43% of builders cut prices in October[30][31][32][34][29][26]. In that case the low estimate of $9.18 would look like the middle, not the floor, and the stock would trade through the $128.33 stop.

Fatal flaw 2: the margin slides because land costs rise faster than prices

In the June quarter DHI's revenue per square foot fell 3% while lot costs rose 5%, and only a 5% fall in stick and brick costs held the gross margin at 20.7%[15]. Management said on the July call that it is more challenging to get significant further improvement as it gets closer to an optimal state[15]. Our own inference is that if construction costs stop falling, the margin goes back to about 20% as in the September 2025 quarter[6], and fiscal 2027 earnings land near the low estimate. The September-quarter guide of 20.5% to 21.0% was given on July 21, before the later rise in rates[15].

Fatal flaw 3: the buyback outruns the cash

DHI plans at least $3.25 billion of repurchases and about $0.5 billion of dividends in fiscal 2026, which is $3.75 billion against a cash flow guide of at least $3.0 billion, and debt rose by $1,147.1 million in nine months[11][1]. If the September quarter's cash flow falls short of the $2,119.2 million it needs, or if lenders tighten, the company would slow repurchases, and the share count shrinkage that supports earnings per share would stop. In our reading the consensus path leans on the buyback, because net income is roughly flat in fiscal 2027.

The red-team case against our verdict

The red-team case against our verdict is the strongest honest argument for buying. DHI is the largest builder with 84,655 closings a year, owns only 22% of its lots, has net debt of 1.15 times EBITDA and returns almost all its cash to owners: twelve-month buybacks were $2,914.7 million, an 8.4% buyback yield on the provider's figures, plus $506.7 million of dividends[1][20].
