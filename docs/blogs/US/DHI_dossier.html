Before you read

DHI, known as America's Builder, is the largest US homebuilder and sells mostly entry-level homes.[3][11] The portfolio holds no DHI shares, so this report treats the shares as a new idea and audits five January 2027 call options on their own. The verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT: the business is sound, but at $147.48 the reward is only 0.17 dollars for each dollar of risk, while a zone of $130.40 to $134.60 offers 2.55 to 1 or better, and the first purchase is held below the 3.5% formula size shown in the key figures. The 6.25% jump on 2026-10-22 followed lower interest rates, not company news, and results arrive on October 29, 2026.[27][20][11] The case fails if mortgage rates stay near 7.3% while orders and margins slip, or if the price closes below $128.27.

1. What the company does

DHI builds and sells houses. It has been the largest US homebuilder by number of homes closed every year since 2002, and it operates in 126 markets across 36 states.[3] Homebuilding makes up 92% of its revenue, and the stock trades on the New York Stock Exchange.[3] The company has closed 1.3 million homes in its 47-year history.[11] Most of its buyers are people buying their first home: 65% of the customers of its mortgage arm in the latest quarter were first-time buyers.[12] The average closing price was $362,000, about $155,000 or 30% below the average price of a new home in the country.[12] That low price point is the company's chosen niche, and it is why demand moves so sharply with mortgage rates: a buyer stretching to afford a $362,000 house feels a one-point change in the loan rate in the monthly payment right away.

The money comes from the gap between the price of a finished house and what it cost to make. A house has three big costs: the lot, the construction (builders call it stick and brick), and the incentives given to close the sale, such as paying down the buyer's mortgage rate. Gross margin on home sales is the share of price left after direct costs, and in the June 2026 quarter it was 20.7%, down from 21.8% a year earlier, because the average price fell and incentives rose.[2] Selling, general and administrative costs took 8.3% of revenue, up from 7.8%.[12] Year over year, construction costs per square foot fell 5%, lot costs rose 5%, and revenue per square foot fell 3%.[12] The company still earned $904.9 million, or $3.20 per diluted share, in that quarter.[1]

Land is the second half of the model. DHI owned or controlled 568,500 lots at June 30, 2026. Only 22% were owned; the other 78% were controlled through purchase contracts, which means the company holds a right to buy lots later and does not carry them on its balance sheet today.[1][2] Forestar, a lot developer that DHI majority-owns, supplies many of the finished lots. In the first nine months of fiscal 2026, 67% of homes closed stood on lots developed by Forestar or by third parties.[1] This matters because it holds down the cash tied up in land. It does not make the company 'asset-light': at June 30, 2026, inventory was $26,686.9 million of $36,511.3 million in total assets.[1] Inventory turned 0.96 times in the past twelve months.[13]

DHI also runs three smaller businesses. In the June quarter the rental unit (single-family and apartment communities built to sell to investors) earned $31.0 million before tax on $266.1 million of revenue. Forestar earned $48.7 million on $407.0 million. Financial services (mortgage lending, title and insurance for buyers) earned $70.3 million on $220.7 million, a 31.9% pre-tax margin.[1] Customers pay at closing, so the cycle runs from order to backlog to closing. At June 30, 2026, the backlog was 15,983 homes worth $6,179.4 million, up from 14,075 homes worth $5,338.1 million a year earlier.[1] Of every ten orders placed in the quarter, two were cancelled, up from about 1.7 in the year-earlier quarter.[1]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • EPS (earnings per share): net profit divided by the number of shares; it rises when profit rises or when the company buys back shares.
  • FCF (free cash flow): cash from operations minus spending on equipment and buildings; it is the cash left for owners.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital): after-tax operating profit divided by the debt and equity used to earn it.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization): a rough measure of operating cash earnings used to judge debt.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation): the cost of paying staff in shares, which dilutes owners.
  • Gross margin: the share of a house's price left after the direct cost of building and selling it.
  • Guidance is management's official forecast of its own results.
  • Backlog: homes under contract that have not yet closed.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

DHI's first advantage is scale. It closed 84,655 homes in the twelve months to June 30, 2026, which is more than any other US builder, and it works in 126 markets.[11][3] Scale gives it buying power with suppliers and trade crews. In the June quarter its construction cost per square foot fell 5% from a year earlier, and the median time from starting a house to closing it improved by about three weeks.[12] Faster builds mean less money sits in unfinished houses. The company's debt-to-capital ratio, the share of its funding that comes from borrowing, was 23.0% at June 30, 2026, against a stated long-term target of about 20%.[1][12]

Its second advantage is a flexible land position. With 78% of lots controlled by contract, DHI can walk away from a lot deal if demand weakens, and its own lot count was down 13% from a year earlier.[2][12] Its third is the price point: a $362,000 average closing price puts it in the part of the market where the supply of new homes is thinnest, and 65% of its mortgage customers were first-time buyers.[12] Its incentives include rate buydowns, which are payments that lower the buyer's loan rate for a period.[2]

Where DHI lags its peers

Scale has not produced the highest returns. DHI's return on equity is 12.63% and its return on invested capital is 10.38%, against 13.86% and 13.58% at PulteGroup, 28.30% and 31.41% at NVR, and 14.39% and 11.68% at Toll Brothers. Only Lennar, at 5.96% and 5.18%, is lower.[13][29][30][31][28] The gross margin gap is larger still: PulteGroup's home sale margin was 24.8% in its latest quarter, against 20.7% at DHI, although PulteGroup sells houses at an average of $561,000, which is far above DHI's $362,000.[25][12]

Growth has also stalled. Revenue was $34,250.4 million in fiscal 2025, below the $36,801.4 million of fiscal 2024 and the $35,460.4 million of fiscal 2023, and management said on the July call that it had not seen growth on its top line for a few years.[3][12] It also said that opening about 30 markets in five years has cost it efficiency in overhead, which is visible in selling, general and administrative costs rising to 8.3% of revenue from 7.8%.[12]

What the housing market is doing now

The market is weak. The average 30-year mortgage rate was 7.32% in the week to October 22, 2026, the first weekly decline since August and only just below the 7.4% of the week before, and the Federal Reserve raised rates in September.[20] Builder confidence, measured by the National Association of Home Builders index, fell to 29 in October, the lowest reading since June 2012, and 43% of builders cut prices that month.[23] The Mortgage Bankers Association's purchase index was down 18% from a year earlier.[20]

DHI's peers had a hard week. PulteGroup's orders fell 6% and its shares dropped 8.24% on the day it reported, and it cut its full-year delivery target by as many as 2,000 homes.[25][26] NVR missed estimates for the third straight quarter, with revenue down 13%.[26] DHI itself held its orders flat in the June quarter, with a cancellation rate of 20%, and it cut its fiscal 2026 revenue guidance in July after saying that demand softened as the quarter went on.[1][12] Against that, its backlog is 16% larger by value than a year ago, and rates fell on October 22, which is the one piece of good news for the sector this week.[1][20] The October 22 rally reached beyond DHI. Lennar rose 7.67% that day, helped by reports that Berkshire Hathaway had built a stake of about 11%.[22] A note on DHI's own move put it down to lower Treasury yields and mortgage rates.[21] The 10-year yield fell to 5.04% from 5.096%, and one lender survey put the 30-year conforming rate at 7.52% on Tuesday.[32][33] M/I Homes said that rising rates had raised the cost of the buydowns it uses, which hurt its margins.[24]

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in Chapter 5 rests on one assumption that is most likely to be wrong. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong, and gives the date the evidence arrives. It then argues the bear case as strongly as the facts allow.

Fatal flaw 1: mortgage rates stay near 7.3% or rise, and orders break

The central case assumes orders stay flat. The 30-year rate was 7.32% on October 22 after a three-year high of 7.4%, the Federal Reserve raised rates in September, and the market puts a nearly 75% chance on another increase in December.[20][23] If rates rise again, DHI's incentives would have to grow to hold orders, and each point of incentive comes straight out of margin; Pulte's orders already fell 6% and the Mortgage Bankers Association purchase index was down 18% on the year.[25][20] In that case the low estimate of $9.18 of fiscal 2027 earnings would look like the middle, not the floor, and the stock would trade through the stop.

Fatal flaw 2: the margin keeps sliding because land costs rise faster than prices

In the June quarter DHI's revenue per square foot fell 3% while lot costs rose 5%, and only a 5% fall in construction costs kept the gross margin at 20.7%.[12] Management said that further cost savings are getting harder to find as the company nears an optimal state. If construction costs stop falling, the margin falls to about 20% again, as it did in the September 2025 quarter, and fiscal 2027 earnings land near the low estimate.[12][6][12]

Fatal flaw 3: the buyback outruns the cash

DHI plans at least $3.25 billion of repurchases and about $0.5 billion of dividends in fiscal 2026, which is $3.75 billion against a cash flow guide of at least $3.0 billion, and debt has risen by $1,147.1 million in nine months.[10][1] If the September quarter's cash flow falls short of the $2.1 billion it needs, or if lenders tighten, the company would have to slow repurchases, and the share count shrinkage that supports earnings per share would stop. 7. What we would do, and when

The decision for the shares is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, and the rule behind it is a ladder that grades how far a price sits above support, the level where buyers have stepped in before. The ladder allows full size within 0.6 average true ranges of support, standard size within 1.2, a small limit-order starter within 1.6, and no purchase at the market beyond that. DHI is 4.0 ranges above its $130.40 floor, so it sits in the no-purchase band, and the reward-to-risk at the close is 0.17 to 1 against a floor of 2.5 to 1 for this bucket. The ENTERING_MOMENTUM label in the key figures does not change that: it records that the turnaround test passed, not that the price is a buy. One more point on size, because the key figures show 3.5%: that is the formula ceiling, the first purchase is 14 shares, about 2.3% of net worth, and 7 shares, about 1.1%, if the order fills before the October 29 report. The reasons follow below.

A limit order is an order to buy only at a price you set or lower, and the entry zone is the range of prices where such an order is placed. We derived the zone from the numbers. The top, $134.60, is the highest price at which the reward to the $150.75 central target is at least 2.5 times the risk to the $128.27 stop: at $134.60 the reward is $16.15 and the risk is $6.33, a ratio of 2.55 to 1, with a stop 4.70% below the entry. The bottom, $130.40, is the base floor itself, where the ratio is 9.55 to 1 and the stop is 1.63% below the entry. The top of the zone is 0.98 average true ranges above support, which is inside the standard band.

Two different measures should not be mixed up. Reward-to-risk compares two distances, from the entry to the central target and from the entry to the stop, so it says nothing about how likely each outcome is. The probability-weighted price return weighs all three scenarios in Chapter 5, which gives $143.24. That is 2.9% below the $147.48 close and 6.4% above the $134.60 top of the zone, before dividends and trading costs. So at the close the expected price return is slightly negative, and at the top of the zone it is modestly positive. The 2.55 to 1 ratio is a test of how the trade is built, and it does not promise a 6.4% gain.

The stop is placed at a structural invalidation level, which means a price that, if broken, shows the idea has failed instead of just wobbling. For this bucket the rule is the lower of the 20-day swing low and the 20-day average, minus half an average true range. That is $130.40 less $2.135, or $128.27. A second plausible stop, the 63-day base low minus one full range, is $126.13, but it would cut the ratio at the top of the zone to 1.91 to 1, below the floor, so we use $128.27. An average is either simple (each day counts equally) or exponential (recent days count more); a 21-day exponential average and a 50-day simple average are the lines a rising stock tends to hold, and they are the lines we would trail a stop under after a gain.

Size comes from risk, not conviction alone. Portfolio heat is the total the portfolio would lose if every stop were hit at once, and the report caps it at 12% of net worth. Risk-unit sizing sets a weight so that a stop-out costs a fixed number of basis points (hundredths of a percent) of net worth. A tier ranks conviction: TIER_3 allows 2% to 3.5% of net worth and a 50 basis point risk budget. The formula gives 50 divided by 4.70% stop distance, or 10.6%, which is capped by the tier at 3.5%, or $2,883 of the $82,376.82 net worth, which is 21 shares at $134.60 (3.4% of net worth after rounding down to whole shares). This is the formula weight shown in the key figures at the top of the report; it is a ceiling, not the plan.

We hold the first purchase well below that ceiling for a second reason, the gap. A stop does not protect against a price that opens below it. If the stock gapped from $134.60 to the low-case $106.76, a 20.7% fall, a 3.5% weight would lose 71 basis points of net worth, above the 50 basis point budget. A weight of up to 2.4% would keep that gap loss at the budget, and rounding down to whole shares gives 14 shares, $1,884, which is 2.3% of net worth. That position would lose 47 basis points in the gap and 11 basis points at the stop. Because results come on October 29, any position taken before that date is held to 7 shares, $942, which is 1.1% of net worth, until the first full session after the report. The portfolio holds no DHI shares today, so no existing holding breaches the TIER_3 ceiling.

If the shares are bought and rise, the let-winners-run rule applies: do not sell merely because the relative strength index passes 70 or the weight passes 15%, let the weight grow to the 22% ceiling, and move the stop up to the rising 21-day exponential average or 50-day simple average. A position of 2.3% would need to multiply nearly ten times before that matters, so in practice the rule means holding while the trend and the earnings estimates stay intact.
