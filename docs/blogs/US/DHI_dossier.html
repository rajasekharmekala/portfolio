1. What the company does

D.R. Horton builds and sells single-family homes. It has been the largest US homebuilder by number of homes sold since 2002 and operates in 126 markets in 36 states.[1] In the twelve months to June 30, 2026 it closed 84,655 homes, plus 3,129 single-family rental homes and 2,370 multi-family rental units.[1] Prices run from about $200,000 to over $1,000,000, but the average home closed in the June quarter sold for $362,000. That is roughly $155,000, or 30%, below the average new home in the country.[14] The low price is deliberate: first-time buyers made up 65% of its mortgage arm's closings in the June quarter.[14]

A buyer signs a contract, which the company calls a sales order. The home is built, and the revenue is booked only when the sale closes and the buyer takes the keys.[3] So the numbers move in a chain: orders first, then the backlog of signed but unclosed homes, then closings, then revenue. At June 30, 2026 the backlog held 15,983 homes worth $6,179.4 million, up from 14,075 homes and $5,338.1 million a year earlier.[1] Not every contract closes. The cancellation rate, which is the share of gross orders that are cancelled before closing, was 20% in the June quarter, up from 17% a year earlier and 16% in March.[1][14] The chief financial officer said that qualification, meaning a buyer's ability to qualify for a mortgage, is still largely the biggest reason for cancellations.[14]

In the first nine months of fiscal 2026 (October 2025 to June 2026) consolidated revenue was $23,672.2 million. Home sales brought in $22,240.1 million of it, or 94.0%.[1] The rest came from three side businesses: a mortgage, title and insurance arm with $598.2 million of revenue, rental properties built for sale to investors with $587.4 million, and land and lot sales with $246.5 million.[1] The side businesses are small, yet they matter for two reasons. The mortgage arm gives the company a tool to cut a buyer's interest rate, and Forestar, a lot developer in which D.R. Horton owns 62%, gives it a steady supply of finished building lots.[3][14]

How the land model works

A homebuilder needs lots to build on. Owning land is expensive and risky, because land bought at a high price loses value when the market turns. D.R. Horton mostly avoids that risk by controlling lots through purchase contracts instead of owning them. At June 30, 2026 it had about 570,000 lots, of which 22% were owned and 78% were controlled through contracts.[14] Think of it as holding a reservation on a parking space with the right to buy it later, rather than buying every space up front. If demand weakens, the company can walk away from a contract and lose only the deposit.

Forestar makes the model work. It develops raw land into finished lots and sells most of them to D.R. Horton or other builders. Forestar owned or controlled about 92,000 lots, and 66% of its owned lots were under contract or subject to a first offer to D.R. Horton.[14] In the nine months to June 30, 2026, 67% of homes closed stood on lots developed by Forestar or other third parties, up from 65% a year earlier.[1] The cost side is less friendly: lot costs per square foot rose 5% in a year, even as the cost of wood, labor and other building materials fell 5%.[14]

Where the homes are built and sold

Demand is spread across six regions. In the nine months to June 30, 2026 the company took 66,376 net orders. South Central (mainly Texas) took 17,763 or 26.8%, the Southeast 15,427 or 23.2%, the East 13,877 or 20.9%, the North 8,925 or 13.4%, the Southwest 7,044 or 10.6% and the Northwest 3,340 or 5.0%.[1] No single metropolitan area dominates, which protects the company from a local downturn but also means it cannot escape a national one.

The regions did not move together. A summary of the July call notes strength in the Mid-Atlantic and Florida, and weakness in the Northwest, specifically Seattle, linked to layoffs in the technology sector.[14] The company is also seasonal. Orders peak in the spring selling season: the March quarter took 24,992 net orders and the December quarter only 18,300.[2][8][7] That seasonality is why we compare each quarter with the same quarter a year earlier, not with the quarter before it.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • RSI (relative strength index) is a 0-to-100 meter of how fast the price has been rising or falling; below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • ATR (average true range) is the average distance the price travels in a day; for DHI it is $4.02, so one normal day moves the stock about three percent.
  • 20-day moving average, 50-day moving average and 200-day moving average mean the average closing price over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading days; they are slow lines that the price tends to bounce off or fall through.
  • Stage 2 is a confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is a confirmed downtrend on the Weinstein scale, which sorts every chart into four phases from basing to markup, topping and markdown.
  • base is a sideways stretch of trading where sellers run out; the pivot is the top of that base; a breakout is a move above the pivot on strong volume; a retest is a pullback that tests the old level from above; and a measured move is the height of the base projected upward as a price target.
  • stop loss is the price at which the thesis is wrong and the position is closed; a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better and never chases; the entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.
  • asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop: (target minus price) divided by (price minus stop), and our floors are 3.5 to 1 for tactical and turnaround ideas.
  • EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares, and the price-to-earnings ratio, written P/E, is the price paid for each dollar of one year of that profit.
  • EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before financing costs, taxes and the accounting charge for wear on assets; for a homebuilder we also add back the interest charged to cost of sales.
  • ROIC (return on invested capital) is the after-tax operating profit divided by the money that debt holders and shareholders have put into the business.
  • cash flow from operations is the cash the business generates from its day-to-day activity before financing; capital expenditure is spending on buildings and equipment; and free cash flow (FCF) is the first minus the second, which is the cash left for owners.
  • net debt is interest-bearing debt minus the cash on hand; working capital is the cash tied up in running the business, which for a homebuilder is mostly homes and lots under construction.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation) is the cost of paying employees in shares, and dilution is the fall in each shareholder's slice when new shares are issued.
  • gross margin is the share of a sale left after the direct cost of the product; here it means home sales gross margin, which D.R. Horton reports after the cost of the land, the construction and the sales incentives.
  • guidance is the company's own published forecast for a coming period, and a consensus estimate is the average of the forecasts that analysts publish.
  • A buyback is when a company buys its own shares, which shrinks the share count and lifts profit per share.
  • Housing terms: net sales orders are new signed contracts less cancellations; the backlog is signed contracts not yet closed; an incentive is a discount or a paid-down mortgage rate (a rate buydown) that the builder gives to win a sale; and completed unsold homes are finished homes with no buyer, which cost the builder interest and risk price cuts.
  • Options terms: a call option is a contract that gains value if the stock rises above its strike price before the expiry date; the 190 call on DHI expiring January 15, 2027 pays off only if the stock finishes above $190, and each contract controls 100 shares.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

D.R. Horton's competitive position rests on scale, a land-light model and a price point most rivals do not serve. It has been the largest US builder by volume for 24 straight years, and it ranks in the top 20% of S&P 500 companies on return on assets over three, five and ten years.[6][14] Scale buys materials and trade labor at lower prices, which shows in the June quarter: the cost of building a home per square foot fell 5% in a year while the selling price per square foot fell 3%.[14] The median time from starting a home to closing it improved by about three weeks, so each home ties up cash for less time.[14]

The weakness is visible in the margins. Home sales gross margin fell from 28.7% in fiscal 2022 to 21.5% in fiscal 2025 and to 20.4% in the first nine months of fiscal 2026.[5][3][2] Pretax margin fell from 22.8% to 12.3% over the same stretch, and diluted EPS fell from $16.51 to $10.49 on a trailing basis.[4][1][3] The chief financial officer said on the July call that current returns are below where he expects them to be over the long run and that the company has not seen top-line growth for a few years.[14] Return on equity (the profit earned on shareholders' money) slid from 19.9% in fiscal 2024 to 14.6% in fiscal 2025 and 12.8% for the twelve months to June 30, 2026.[9][6][1]

Cash returns to owners remain large. In the nine months to June 30, 2026 the company repurchased 14.6 million shares for $2.2 billion and paid $388.3 million of dividends, and it cut the share count by 6% in a year.[1] The dividend of $0.45 a quarter is the twelfth straight yearly increase.[6] The cost of this policy shows on the balance sheet: notes payable rose from $5,965.5 million at September 30, 2025 to $7,112.6 million at June 30, 2026, and debt to total capital moved from 19.8% to 23.0%, against a stated long-run target of about 20%.[1][6][14]

Where the moat is

The moat is cost and capital efficiency, not brand. A buyer at this price point compares monthly payments, not names, so D.R. Horton wins by building a smaller, cheaper home at a lower cost than a local builder can. Its average closing price of $362,000 is about 30% below the national new-home average.[14] Only 22% of its lots are owned, and in the nine months to June 30, 2026, 67% of closings came off lots developed by Forestar or other third parties, so less capital is stuck in land.[14][1] The homebuilding return on inventory was 17.0% for the twelve months to June 30, 2026, below 20.1% for fiscal 2025 and 27.8% for fiscal 2024, but still a healthy return on the homes and lots it holds.[1][6][9] A moat is real only if it survives a downturn, and the 2023 to 2026 record gives some evidence: pretax margin fell by almost half from its peak, yet the company stayed solidly profitable and kept returning cash.

Where the business is thin

The business is a price taker on affordability. When mortgage rates rise, the company pays to bring buyers' monthly payments down, and that cost lands in gross margin. The 30-year mortgage rate averaged 7.40% on October 15, 2026, up from 6.27% a year earlier and 6.65% on August 20.[22][23] The NAHB builder confidence index fell three points to 32 in September; 38% of builders cut prices and 66% used incentives, the highest share since December.[24] Costs are turning too: the producer price index for final demand rose 1.3% in September and is up 6.3% on the year, and a manager on the July call said the company may start to see a slight lumber headwind again into 2027.[25][14] A company with a fixed price point and rising input costs has two choices, lose volume or lose margin, and both reduce profit.

The second weakness is that growth has stopped. Revenue fell 6.9% in fiscal 2025 and is down 2.6% on a trailing basis.[3][1] Net sales orders were flat in the June quarter at 23,084 homes, and management cut its full-year revenue forecast to $32.5 billion to $33.0 billion because demand softened during the quarter.[1][14] Selling, general and administrative cost (SG&A) rose to 8.3% of homebuilding revenue from 7.8% a year earlier because the company added communities and markets that have not yet reached scale.[2][14]

How the peers compare

The peers show that D.R. Horton is not the weakest operator in a weak group. Its home sales gross margin of 20.7% sits between PulteGroup at 25.0% and Toll Brothers at 23.9% on one side and KB Home at 16.5% and Lennar at 15.8% on the other.[1][19][20][21][18] Its order growth was flat (23,084 homes against 23,071), which beat Lennar (down 9%) and KB Home (down 12%), but trailed PulteGroup (up 6%) and Toll Brothers (up 5%).[1][18][21][19][20] Lennar, PulteGroup, Toll Brothers, NVR, KB Home and Meritage are all in a Stage 4 downtrend in our ledger, so the sector, not just this company, is the problem.[30] Chapter 3 sets the figures side by side.

Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Management has a clear habit with one-quarter margin forecasts: it gives itself room and usually beats the range. For the last three quarters it forecast home sales gross margin at 20.0% to 20.5%, then 19.0% to 19.5%, then 19.7% to 20.2%, and it delivered 20.4%, 20.1% and 20.7%.[16][17][15][11][12][2] Two of those results include 40 basis points from one-off items (a warranty recovery in December and a litigation benefit in March), so the clean figures were 20.0% and 19.7%.[17][15] The record before that was worse: for the September 2025 quarter the company landed about 40 basis points below the low end of its own range, mostly because the incentives needed to hit its volume target were higher than planned.[16]

Year-long forecasts have gone the other way. The first fiscal 2026 forecast, made on October 28, 2025, was revenue of $33.5 billion to $35.0 billion and 86,000 to 88,000 homes closed.[6] It was cut on April 21 to $33.5 billion to $34.5 billion and 86,000 to 87,500 homes, and cut again on July 21 to $32.5 billion to $33.0 billion and 83,800 to 84,300 homes.[8][1] The top of the latest range is below the bottom of the first one. Management's explanation on the July call was that orders came in below its internal plan because demand softened during the quarter.[14]

Capital allocation is the steadiest part of the record. The company planned to buy back about $2.5 billion of stock and pay about $500 million of dividends in fiscal 2026, and in nine months it has repurchased 14.6 million shares for $2.2 billion and paid $388.3 million.[6][1] The cost has been leverage: debt to total capital is 23.0%, above the 20% the chief financial officer calls the long-run target.[1][14]

For you as an owner of the options, the lesson is that management's near-term margin range is a fair guide and its full-year volume forecast is not. Pay is tied to the same returns this chapter tracks. Putting the two halves together, Layer 2 gives a mixed verdict: two measures pass, none fail and management's record is good on margin and capital returns but weak on volume. That is not a failing grade, but it is below our bar of four passes out of five, and the three watch items all point the same way. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in this report rests on an assumption that could fail. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, says what evidence would show it wrong, and gives the date we expect to learn it. It also contains the red-team challenge, which is the strongest case we can make against our own conclusion, and the one claim on which the whole report turns.

Fiscal 2027 profit per share of $10.03

The weakest assumption is that the pretax margin falls 0.8 points, from about 12.3% to 11.5%. We drew it from three facts. Incentives are rising across the industry. The cost savings the company has captured are fading. And the company has a habit of overshooting on volume forecasts. But it met or beat its margin range in each of the last three quarters. The June quarter was 0.5 points above the top of the range. So a flat margin is easy to defend.[2][15][17][16] The evidence that would show us wrong is a September-quarter gross margin of 21.0% or more and a fiscal 2027 revenue forecast of $33.0 billion or more. We learn it on October 29, 2026. The company reports that day and gives its first fiscal 2027 forecast, as it did for fiscal 2026 on October 28, 2025.[1][6] A second date is the December-quarter report. The company has not scheduled it yet, and last year it came on January 20.[7] If we are wrong in the favourable direction, central EPS moves toward $11 and the reward-to-risk improves. It would still not clear the 3.5 floor on its own. One stress shows how sensitive the price result is. If the pretax margin simply stays at 12.3% instead of falling 0.8 points, EPS is about $10.74 and the price at 13.5 times is about $144.99, a gain of about 4%. So a negative central price return does not hold up under this single assumption. The exit verdict survives that stress because the rule fails (Stage 4 and the breached book exit level), not because every earnings outcome is negative.

The 13.5 times earnings multiple

The weakest assumption is that the stock keeps trading near its own three-year median of 13.3 times while profit falls. History says homebuilders can do the opposite. At the end of September 2023 the stock traded at 7.78 times that year's profit. Investors feared a downturn that was not yet in the numbers. At the end of September 2025 it traded at 14.65 times, because they were looking through to a recovery.[26][3][4] A multiple is a mood, and it can swing by a third in either direction. We used 11.5 times for the bear case and 14.5 times for the bull case to reflect that. The evidence that would show us wrong in the bullish direction is a mortgage rate that falls back toward 6.5%. That would let the market look past a weak winter and pay more for recovering profit. The Freddie Mac survey is published every Thursday, so we learn this week by week. The first useful check is the survey of October 22, 2026.[22][23] In the bearish direction, a P/E below 11 on trailing profit would suggest the market expects a deeper cut than we do.

The cash flow behind the Reverse DCF

The weakest assumption is that trailing free cash flow of $3.2 billion is a fair picture of what the business produces. It includes a large cash release in the September 2025 quarter, when operating cash flow was $2,471.8 million. Only $880.8 million came in during the first nine months of fiscal 2026.[3][1] The company's forecast of at least $3.0 billion for the year needs $2,119.2 million in the September quarter. If it comes in light, trailing free cash flow drops toward $2.9 billion and implied growth rises to about 2.4%. A homebuilder's cash flow depends on how fast it builds homes and buys lots. A quarter of faster spending can hide a healthy business, and a quarter of slower spending can flatter a weak one. The evidence that would show us wrong is the September-quarter cash flow in the October 29 release. The full-year cash flow statement follows in the annual report. The company filed it on November 19 last year, so we expect it in mid-to-late November.[1][3]

Reading the chart as Stage 4 without a base

The weakest assumption is that the two-day bounce from $130.40 is a pause and not the start of a base. The facts for a base are real. The low was set on October 14 after tests at $132.18 and $132.11. The 63-day volume dry-up ratio is 0.45. The stock has led the S&P 500 by 1.0 point over 28 days.[27][30] The facts against it are also real. The October 14 low undercut the earlier lows. The 50-day average is still falling. The highest price since September 22 is only $142.90. We would be wrong if the stock closes above $144.86 on a weekly basis and the 50-day average stops falling. We expect that to take about a month, so the check date is mid-November. A close below $128.39 would settle the question the other way. A mistake here costs little on the downside, because we are not buying shares. It costs the chance of a bounce if we sell the calls, and we judge that chance small given the 36.3% gap to the strike.

Red-team challenge: the strongest case against our verdict

The strongest case against selling goes like this. DHI has the best chart among the large builders. It is 18.4% below its high, against 20.6% to 42.4% for the others. Its trailing P/E of 13.3 times and free cash flow yield of 8.2% are not demanding. It met or beat its margin forecast in each of the last three quarters and shrinks its share count by 6% a year. It reports on October 29 with expectations already cut, so a better-than-feared print could move the stock by 5% to 10% in a day. A call option gains value from that kind of jump even when the strike is far away, so selling before the event may give up some of that value. We cannot say how much, because we do not have a live quote.[30][1]

We keep the verdict for three reasons, and none of them is a claim that selling is certain to beat holding. First, the exit rule is met on its own terms.
