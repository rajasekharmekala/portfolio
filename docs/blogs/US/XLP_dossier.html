Chapter 1

The State Street Consumer Staples Select Sector SPDR ETF (NYSE Arca: XLP) was launched on December 16, 1998. It listed on the exchange on December 22, 1998 as one of the original nine Select Sector SPDR Funds carved out of the S&P 500. Before 1998, investors seeking defensive sector exposure faced a difficult trade-off. They could buy and rebalance thirty to forty individual food, beverage, household, and tobacco stocks on their own. Or they could pay active mutual fund fees of 0.75% to 1.20% per year. Worse still, mutual fund holders often received annual capital-gains tax bills triggered when other investors redeemed shares for cash.

XLP solved both problems at once. It paired the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS) with an in-kind creation and redemption mechanism inside an open-end ETF trust. That trust is The Select Sector SPDR Trust (SEC CIK 0001064641), managed by SSGA Funds Management, Inc. Under this design, Authorized Participants (APs) create or redeem XLP shares in large Creation Units. They swap baskets of the underlying S&P 500 staples stocks directly for ETF shares rather than forcing cash sales. As SEC Form 485BPOS and Form N-1A filings confirm, in-kind redemptions generally avoid taxable capital gains for ongoing holders. Over nearly 28 years, XLP has grown to $14,719.44 million in Assets Under Management across 163.77 million shares (as of November 19, 2026). It charges a 0.08% gross expense ratio and trades with a 0.01% 30-day median bid-ask spread.

Many investors assume XLP holds a static basket of packaged-food and soap makers. In reality, XLP's business mix has shifted sharply over the past twenty years. In 1999, XLP was mainly a branded consumer packaged goods (CPG) and tobacco fund. Procter & Gamble, Coca-Cola, Philip Morris (before the 2008 Altria/PMI split), PepsiCo, and Kraft led the portfolio. Two structural forces later reshaped the index. First, warehouse clubs and omnichannel discounters compounded scale far faster than food makers. Second, GICS taxonomy updates reclassified key retailers. In March 2023, GICS moved Target Corporation from Consumer Discretionary into Consumer Staples as its grocery and essentials mix grew.

By November 2026, Consumer Staples Distribution & Retail reached 34.75% of XLP. That single group now outweighs Beverages (19.76%) and Food Products (15.20%) combined. Meanwhile, the S&P 500 index filter pruned weaker incumbents over time. Legacy drugstore chains suffered margin erosion from pharmacy reimbursement cuts and online competition, ending with Walgreens Boots Alliance leaving major benchmarks. Over-leveraged food roll-ups also shrank. Kraft Heinz recorded a $15.4 billion goodwill and intangible write-down in 2019 that permanently reduced its index weight. XLP's cap-weighted structure shifted capital away from laggards and into Walmart (11.37%) and Costco (9.82%).

XLP owns companies selling daily necessities like groceries, paper goods, detergent, oral care, beverages, and nicotine. Its aggregate earnings behave differently across the four phases of the economic cycle:

Phase 1 — Recessions & Credit Stress (2001–2002, 2008–2009, 2020): When jobs or credit tighten, households cut dining out, travel, and big-ticket goods. They spend more on food-at-home, hygiene, and value retail. Walmart and Costco gain wallet share as shoppers trade down, while Procter & Gamble and Colgate-Palmolive hold steady replenishment volumes. Phase 2 — Inflation Shocks (2021–2023): When resin, pulp, aluminum, freight, and crop prices surge, top brands flex their pricing power. They pass through high-single-digit price hikes, lifting nominal sales and gross profit dollars.

Phase 3 — Post-Inflation Digestion (2024–2026): Once cumulative price hikes outrun wage growth, unit demand pushes back. In FY2025–FY2026, organic volumes stalled or fell at P&G (0%), Coca-Cola (0%), PepsiCo (-2%), and Mondelez (-3.7%). Shoppers shifted toward store brands, lifting U.S. private-label unit share to a record 23.5%. Phase 4 — Early-Cycle Booms: During industrial or tech-led expansions, XLP's steady 1%–5% organic growth trails cyclical sectors. It often lags risk-on rallies until the next defensive rotation.

To keep its tax-advantaged status under Internal Revenue Code Subchapter M, XLP cannot let mega-caps grow without limit. S&P Dow Jones Indices enforces a modified market-cap capping rule every quarter. Rebalances take effect after the close on the third Friday of March, June, September, and December. S&P also runs a secondary check on the second-to-last business day of each quarter. No single stock may breach a 24% trigger without being capped back to 23%. Also, the sum of all stocks above 4.8% weight cannot exceed 50% of the index. When that top tier breaches 50%, excess weight shifts down to smaller holdings capped at 4.5%. That rule explains why Colgate-Palmolive (4.50%), Altria (4.49%), Target (4.34%), Mondelez (4.30%), and PepsiCo (4.15%) cluster tightly together.

Structural Rule: RIC Diversification Capping Creates Mechanical Rebalance Flows

Because S&P DJI enforces a 23% single-stock cap and a 50% aggregate cap on positions above 4.8%, XLP's largest winners (Walmart at 11.37%, Costco at 9.82%, P&G at 7.90%, Coca-Cola at 7.53%, PMI at 5.88%—totaling 42.50%) sit just below the 50% threshold.

If Walmart and Costco rally further relative to mid-tier staples, the 4.8%/50% rule can force mechanical quarterly trims on the fifth- or sixth-largest constituent, creating predictable index-rebalance turnover.

Tracing XLP from December 1998 to November 2026 shows five clear operating regimes. During the 2000–2002 tech bust, household staples earnings grew steadily while tech capital spending collapsed. During the 2008–2009 Global Financial Crisis, tight credit pushed families into Walmart supercenters and Costco warehouses. In 2020, pandemic lockdowns sparked massive pantry loading for cleaning supplies, paper goods, and packaged food, followed by supply bottlenecks in 2021.

The 2021–2023 inflation wave pushed CPG brands to raise prices by 15%–25% cumulatively to cover input spikes. By 2024–2026, however, fundamentals split in two. Value retailers (Walmart and Costco) and smoke-free nicotine leaders (PMI) grew unit volume and share. Meanwhile, legacy food and beverage makers faced flat-to-negative volume elasticity, store-brand competition, and GLP-1 adoption. Across all five regimes, XLP's 0.08% fee and quarterly index rules kept the fund aligned with surviving leaders.

ETF Execution & Tracking Fidelity: Over 28 years, XLP's 0.08% fee has produced very tight tracking against its benchmark index. As of November 19, 2026, XLP held just $23.25 million in net cash (0.16% of AUM) to handle quarterly dividends and daily AP flows. XLP holds physical shares of all 33 index companies (34 holdings including cash sleeves) rather than swaps or leverage. That structure avoids derivative roll drag and supports tight secondary-market arbitrage.

Trust Governance & Systematic Management: XLP is advised by SSGA Funds Management, Inc. Day-to-day portfolio management sits with the Systematic Equity Beta Group (Emiliano Rabinovich, CFA; Keith Richardson; Olga Winner, CFA). Under the Investment Company Act of 1940, XLP is a non-diversified fund. It commits 100% of its equity exposure to GICS Consumer Staples, accepting sector focus in exchange for pure factor exposure.

Second, Omnichannel Store-Fulfilled & Club Warehouses (Walmart, Costco, Target) use automated regional centers, cross-docking, and store backrooms as local fulfillment hubs. In Q4 FY26, Walmart's eCommerce grew 24%, with expedited store-fulfilled delivery up over 50%.
