Chapter 1: From Indianapolis Apothecary to the Incretin Age

Colonel Eli Lilly founded Eli Lilly and Company in May 1876 in Indianapolis, Indiana. He was a chemist and Union veteran tired of weak, uneven patent tonics. Instead of selling cures straight to buyers, he sold tested drugs only through doctors. By the 1880s, his firm sold gelatin-coated pills, fruit syrups, and lab-tested batches. That early rule still drives the firm today. When Lilly pairs hard biology with top plant scale, it compounds for decades. When it leans on one patent without a deep pipeline, it hits steep sales cliffs.

The first big shift came in 1923 with Iletin, the first mass-made insulin. Lilly teamed up with Toronto scientists Frederick Banting and Charles Best to scale it. Moving insulin from a lab bench to national supply meant solving purity, cold storage, and batch strength. Those skills made Lilly the top U.S. diabetes firm for a century. In 1982, Lilly repeated that feat with Humulin, the first human insulin made with DNA tech alongside Genentech. Lilly willingly replaced its own animal insulin before rivals could.

From 1987 to 2010, Lilly shifted toward brain and primary-care drugs. The 1987 launch of Prozac (fluoxetine), the first SSRI pill, changed depression care and powered growth through the 1990s. When Prozac lost U.S. patent protection in 2001, Lilly bridged the gap with Zyprexa (1996) and Cymbalta (2004). Yet leaning on small-molecule brain drugs set up the worst drop in modern company history. In the 2011–2014 'YZ' cliff, Zyprexa (2011) and Cymbalta (2013) lost U.S. patents within 26 months. That shock wiped out over $8 billion in high-margin sales and forced deep cuts.

Lilly's 150-year history shows that enduring value creation has always come from solving complex biology plus industrial manufacturing—first with Iletin and Humulin, and now with tirzepatide, Foundayo, and retatrutide.

Lilly planted the seeds of its current rise during the 2011–2014 patent cliff. Under John Lechleiter and then CEO David A. Management focused R&D on four core areas: metabolic care, cancer, immune disease, and brain health. Lilly's chemists also refused to stop at single GLP-1 drugs, where Novo Nordisk led early. Instead, they built tirzepatide, a single peptide that targets both GIP and GLP-1 receptors.

Approved as Mounjaro for diabetes in May 2022 and Zepbound for obesity in November 2023, tirzepatide beat older GLP-1 drugs on both blood sugar and weight loss (over 20% in SURMOUNT-1). It sparked the fastest sales ramp in drug history. Combined Mounjaro and Zepbound sales hit $36.5 billion in FY2025, or 56% of Lilly's $65,179 million total, and topped $16 billion in Q3 2026 alone. Lilly also licensed oral small-molecule orforglipron from Chugai, winning FDA approval as Foundayo on April 1, 2026. Meanwhile, triple-agonist retatrutide cleared Phase 3 TRIUMPH trials ahead of a Q1 2027 filing.

Price-Blind Historical Takeaway

Lilly has survived three major patent cliffs over the past 25 years (Prozac in 2001, Zyprexa in 2011, Cymbalta in 2013) and emerged with a broader, more durable biologics and peptide/small-molecule platform each time. The central business question entering 2027 is whether Lilly can convert today's tirzepatide cash gusher into a multi-decade cardiometabolic, oncology, immunology, and neuroscience franchise before payer price compression and eventual 2036+ patent expirations arrive.

From 2017 to 2026, Lilly paired internal lab work with focused deal-making. Unlike peers that chased giant, debt-heavy mergers (such as Pfizer-Wyeth or BMS-Celgene), Lilly stuck to $1 billion to $8 billion bolt-on deals. Loxo Oncology ($8 billion in 2019) added cancer drugs Retevmo and Jaypirca, while Dermira (2020) brought Ebglyss. Avid and Prevail boosted brain imaging and gene therapy. Chugai's 2018 orforglipron license later became Foundayo in April 2026.

Just as key, Lilly cut legacy clutter. Spinning off Elanco in 2018–2019 and letting older primary-care drugs fade kept R&D output among the best in big pharma. By 2025, six drugs—Mounjaro, Zepbound, Verzenio, Trulicity, Taltz, and Jardiance—each topped $3 billion in yearly sales. Newer launches Kisunla, Ebglyss, Omvoh, and Jaypirca also began scaling toward multi-hundred-million-dollar run-rates.

To see how Lilly earns its profits, look at one real-world case in each of its four pillars:

1. Cardiometabolic Health (Diabetes & Obesity): Mounjaro, Zepbound & Foundayo

Patient & Problem: A 48-year-old patient has Class II obesity (BMI 36) plus sleep apnea or Type 2 diabetes. Diet, exercise, and older pills have failed, leaving high heart and metabolic risk.

Prescriber & Product: A doctor prescribes weekly Zepbound (tirzepatide) by auto-injector pen. Since April 2026, patients who prefer a pill can take once-daily Foundayo (orforglipron) with no food or water timing rules.

Manufacturing & Delivery: Tirzepatide needs complex peptide synthesis and sterile fill-finish into single-dose pens or KwikPens. Wholesalers like McKesson, Cencora, and Cardinal Health ship it cold-chain, or LillyDirect delivers straight to homes. Foundayo uses standard tablet plants, skipping cold storage.

Pricing & Reimbursement: Commercial plans cover ~55–65% of eligible lives after prior checks and 35–55% PBM rebates. Uninsured or denied patients often use LillyDirect cash tiers at $349–$499/month.

2. Oncology: Verzenio & Jaypirca

Patient & Problem: A 54-year-old woman diagnosed with node-positive, HR-positive, HER2-negative early breast cancer faces a 20–30% risk of distant recurrence after surgery and chemotherapy.

Prescriber & Product: A medical oncologist prescribes Verzenio (abemaciclib) 150 mg twice-daily oral tablets for two years alongside endocrine therapy (adjuvant setting), where Verzenio is the only CDK4/6 inhibitor with proven overall survival and invasive disease-free survival benefit in high-risk early disease. In relapsed mantle cell lymphoma or CLL, hematologists similarly prescribe Lilly's non-covalent BTK inhibitor Jaypirca (pirtobrutinib).

Manufacturing & Delivery: Oral small-molecule tablets manufactured at Lilly's global oral solid-dose facilities and dispensed through specialty pharmacies (e.g., CVS Specialty, Accredo) with monthly adherence monitoring.

Pricing & Reimbursement: High-value oncology biologics/orals carry strong commercial and Medicare Part D coverage with minimal formulary exclusion because clinical guidelines (NCCN Category 1) mandate access; net realization is governed by statutory 340B/Medicaid/Medicare Part D redesign discounts rather than aggressive PBM tiering.

3. Immunology: Taltz, Ebglyss & Omvoh

Patient & Problem: A 32-year-old adult with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (eczema) or ulcerative colitis experiences debilitating flares, sleep loss, and inadequate relief from topical steroids or legacy anti-TNF biologics.

Prescriber & Product: A dermatologist prescribes Ebglyss (lebrikizumab, an IL-13 monoclonal antibody) with monthly maintenance dosing after induction, or a gastroenterologist prescribes Omvoh (mirikizumab, an IL-23p19 antagonist) for ulcerative colitis/Crohn's disease; in plaque psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis, Taltz (ixekizumab) remains a $3.9B+ workhorse.

Manufacturing & Delivery: Mammalian cell-culture bioreactor fermentation (CHO cells) producing monoclonal antibodies, purified and filled into prefilled syringes/auto-injectors, delivered via specialty pharmacies with nurse-ambassador injection training.

Pricing & Reimbursement: Immunology is fiercely competitive (against Dupixent, Skyrizi, and Rinvoq), meaning PBMs demand substantial rebates and step-therapy ('fail-first' on older agents) for preferred formulary placement.

4. Neuroscience: Kisunla (Donanemab) & Emgality

Patient & Problem: A 71-year-old patient with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or mild dementia due to confirmed amyloid-beta pathology faces irreversible cognitive decline.

Prescriber & Product: A neurologist confirms amyloid plaque via PET scan or CSF/blood biomarker, orders baseline brain MRI to screen for ARIA risk, and prescribes Kisunla (donanemab-azbt), an anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody.

Manufacturing & Delivery: Biologic IV infusion administered every four weeks at hospital outpatient or freestanding infusion centers; uniquely, Kisunla's label allows finite-duration dosing—stopping therapy once amyloid plaques are cleared on follow-up PET.

Pricing & Reimbursement: Reimbursed primarily under Medicare Part B (medical benefit, ASP + 6% framework) with registry participation; commercial uptake has scaled steadily ($109M in Q4 2025, +54% QoQ) as infusion infrastructure and diagnostic PET capacity expand.

Regional and channel mix also shape Lilly's sales quality. In FY2025, Mounjaro earned $13,651 million in the U.S. and $9,315 million abroad (40.6% ex-U.S.). That shows strong uptake across Europe, Japan, and the Middle East. By contrast, Zepbound's $13,542 million in FY2025 sales was 99.6% U.S. ($13,484 million U.S. vs. $58 million ex-U.S.). Overseas obesity payers have moved slowly, though multi-dose KwikPens are now rolling out.

Across all four pillars, strong trial data backs Lilly's formulary edge. In metabolic care, SURMOUNT-1 and SURMOUNT-OSA showed 21%–22% mean weight loss and sleep-apnea relief for tirzepatide. Retatrutide's Phase 3 TRIUMPH trials also hit up to ~28% weight loss. In cancer, monarchE proved Verzenio cuts recurrence and boosts survival in high-risk early breast cancer. In brain health, TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 showed Kisunla slowed early Alzheimer's decline by 35% with finite dosing.

Lilly's older drugs also form a cash bridge while new launches scale. Trulicity ($4,276 million in FY2025), Jardiance ($3,260 million partner sales), Humalog, and Cyramza lead this group. Management guides doctors to move diabetes patients from Trulicity to Mounjaro. That keeps patients inside Lilly's ecosystem instead of losing them to rival GLP-1 brands.

Q3 2026 filings sharpen two portfolio risks. First, tirzepatide concentration climbed rather than eased: Mounjaro ($10,723 million) and Zepbound ($5,352 million) generated $16,075 million in Q3 2026—65.9% of total revenue ($24,397 million)—and $43,768 million (65.2%) across 9M 2026. Second, regulatory pricing pressure now reaches beyond diabetes: under the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), HHS selected Jardiance (66% discount effective 2026, with no further Boehringer Ingelheim sales-based milestones remaining after $910 million in 9M 2026) and in January 2026 selected both Trulicity ($4,276 million FY2025) and Verzenio ($5,723 million FY2025)—15.4% of FY2025 revenue—for Medicare price-setting effective 2028.

Chapter 3: Inside the $50 Billion Factory & the Gross-to-Net Machine

Eli Lilly sells through two very different paths: the U.S. wholesale-PBM system and a fast-growing direct consumer platform. Over 80% of U.S. volume still moves through three wholesalers: McKesson, Cencora, and Cardinal Health. Meanwhile, three big Pharmacy Benefit Managers (CVS Caremark, Express Scripts, and OptumRx) set net prices. List prices for top incretins and biologics often top $1,000 a month. Yet PBM rebates, 340B hospital cuts, and government rules trim net realized prices by 40% to 60%.

Lilly launched LillyDirect in 2024 and scaled it fast through 2026 to bypass payer friction and fight copycat compounders. The platform links uninsured or denied patients with telehealth doctors. It then ships Zepbound vials and oral Foundayo pills straight to their homes at clear cash prices ($349–$499/month). This direct path cuts out wholesaler and PBM rebates. Even at lower sticker prices, Lilly keeps strong unit margins and gains live demand data.

On the supply side, Lilly is running the largest plant buildout in drug history. From 2020 to early 2025, Lilly pledged over $50 billion for U.S. plants. In February 2025, it added $27 billion for four new sites: three for API synthesis and one for sterile fill-finish, including a $3.5 billion plant in Fogelsville, Pennsylvania. Annual PPE capex jumped from $1,873 million in 2021 to $3,448 million in 2023, $5,058 million in 2024, and $7,841 million in 2025. Oral Foundayo's April 2026 approval also eases long-term pen bottlenecks, since pills need far less capex and cold storage than injectables.

Broader commercial rebates, Medicare deals, and LillyDirect cash tiers drove that price decline. Because gross margins on peptides and oral pills top 80%, strong volume still lifts operating profit. Lilly's >$50 billion plant network spans Indiana (Indianapolis and Lebanon), North Carolina (Research Triangle Park and Concord), Wisconsin (Kenosha), Pennsylvania (Fogelsville at $3.5 billion), and Limerick, Ireland. New sterile plants take 36 to 60 months to clear FDA cGMP checks from groundbreaking to commercial launch. As a result, Lilly's 2020–2023 projects are hitting full output across 2025–2027. Adding LillyDirect also improves working capital over time. Wholesale orders carry 30-to-60-day payment terms and slow PBM rebate true-ups on the balance sheet. By contrast, LillyDirect cash orders settle right away with no PBM rebate lag.

On costs, peptide synthesis and cell-culture plants gain sharp scale benefits. As new lines in Concord and Limerick shift from test runs to full output, fixed costs spread over more units. That helped push gross margin to 83.04% in FY2025 despite $1,997 million in D&A and rapid global growth.
