1. What the company does

FNGU is an exchange-traded note, or ETN. An ETN is a bond-like promise from a bank. It is not a fund, and it owns no stocks. Bank of Montreal created these notes with a $25 principal amount, issued them on 24 February 2025, and set the maturity at 17 February 2045.[1][2] On 23 October 2026 the bank showed a closing indicative value of $29.10 per note, 80,000,000 notes outstanding and a market capitalization of $2,328,000,000.[1] The closing indicative value, or CIV, is the note's own end-of-day price tag. A formula in the pricing supplement sets it: the long index amount, minus the financing amount, minus the daily financing charge, minus the daily investor fee, and never below zero.[2] The note closed at $29.12 on the same day, a premium of about 0.07% to that value (our calculation).[1][24]

Each day the note aims to move three times as far as the index moved that day. The bank resets the exposure at every close, so the multiple is 3 on each new morning and drifts during a day: after a 20% index rise the effective multiple is 2.25, and after a 20% fall it is 6.00.[2] Two costs come out of the CIV every day. The investor fee is 0.95% a year.[2][10][15] The financing charge applies to two times the previous CIV, at the bank prime loan rate plus 2.25 points. Prime was 7.00% on 21 October 2026, so financing alone costs 2 x (7.00% + 2.25%) = 18.50% a year.[2][14] The note pays no interest and no dividends.

The index behind the note is the NYSE FANG+ Index. It holds ten stocks and gives each a 10% weight at every quarterly reconstitution. Six slots are fixed for Meta, Apple, Amazon, Netflix, Microsoft and Alphabet. Four slots are ranked each quarter on company size, trading volume, price-to-sales and sales growth.[12][13] On 23 October 2026 the weights had drifted from 10% to a range of 8.86% (Netflix) to 11.36% (Palantir). The other eight are Microsoft, Apple, Amazon, NVIDIA, Meta, Broadcom, Alphabet and Micron.[9] The next reconstitution takes effect after the close on 18 December 2026.[13] ICE reports an annualised total return of 28.07% for the index from 19 September 2014 to 30 September 2026, against 19.23% for the NASDAQ-100 and 13.70% for the S&P 500, and notes that the years before 26 September 2017 are back-tested.[11]

The FNGU ticker has a history. The original FNGU note (due 2038, launched January 2018) was redeemed by Bank of Montreal. The bank announced the redemption on 19 February 2025 and said the costs of supporting the leverage had risen, so the old fees no longer matched the market.[6] It sent the call notice on 1 May 2025.[7] A new note, first listed as FNGB, replaced it. It started trading on 20 February 2025 and, according to the bank's addendum, took over the FNGU ticker on 24 June 2025.[4][5][7] That is why the price history in this report starts on 20 February 2025.[24]

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Closing indicative value (CIV) is the end-of-day value of the note computed from the index and the daily charges. The note's market price can differ from it.
  • Daily reset means the bank re-sets the exposure to three times the index at every close. Gains and losses then compound from the new, higher or lower starting value.
  • Volatility drag is the loss that comes from daily resetting when the index zigzags. An index that ends where it began leaves the note lower.
  • Call right is the bank's option to redeem the notes early, in whole or in part, on at least 14 calendar days' notice.
  • Average true range (ATR) is the typical size of one day's price swing, measured over 14 days. It is $1.79 for FNGU, or about 6.1% of the price.
  • Relative strength index (RSI) is a 0 to 100 gauge of how fast the price has fallen or risen over 14 days. Below 30 is called oversold and above 70 overbought.
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash a company has left after paying for its operations and its capital spending.
  • Price-to-earnings (P/E) is the price of a stock divided by its yearly earnings per share. A forward P/E uses the earnings analysts expect over the next year.
  • Entry zone is the price range in which we would be willing to buy, and a stop loss is the price at which we would sell to end a losing trade.
  • Asymmetry, or reward-to-risk, is the possible gain to the target divided by the possible loss to the stop. We ask for at least 2.5 to 1 on tactical trades.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The note does what it says on the label. Over the last 252 trading days to 23 October 2026 the daily returns of the note and of the index moved almost in lockstep: the correlation is 0.9988 and the slope is 2.95, close to the target of 3 (our calculation).[24][25] We also rebuilt the note's price from the index, the prime rate and the published fee schedule. Starting at the first close on 20 February 2025, the rebuilt price ends at $28.77 against an actual $29.12, a gap of 1.2% over 20 months (our calculation).[2][14][24][25] That small gap is consistent with the published costs being the real costs, and with the financing spread still at 2.25 points. Scale helps too. BMO reports $2.328 billion of market value, and the ledger shows 1,508,800 shares traded on 23 October, about $43.9 million, which is 0.56 times the 20-day average.

The weakness is the cost of the daily reset. The note lost ground to the index in every window we measured. In the last 12 months the index gained +12.6% but the note gained only +1.5%, and a perfect daily-reset path with no fees would have gained +22.2% (our calculation).[24][25] The pricing supplement itself says it is extremely likely that the value of the notes will decline to near zero by maturity, and likely sooner, and it shows a hypothetical index that swings 5% a day and ends flat while the note loses 7.23%.[2] The 5% case is a good check for our formula, and Chapter 5 shows it matches to the cent.

The issuer is the second risk. The notes are senior unsecured debt of Bank of Montreal, so a default would leave holders as ordinary creditors.[1] The bank looks strong. Morningstar DBRS confirmed its long-term issuer rating at AA on 22 May 2026, and the common equity tier 1 ratio, a measure of capital strength, was 13.0% at 31 July 2026 against 13.5% a year earlier.[22][23] Reported net income for the quarter fell 25% to $1,750 million (Canadian dollars) on a goodwill charge tied to a business sale, while adjusted net income rose 19% to $2,859 million.[22]

The bigger structural risk is that the bank can end the note. It may call all or part of the notes at any time on 14 calendar days' notice, and the payout is the average CIV over a five-day window, or a single day if the notes called are worth $500,000,000 or less.[2] It used that right in 2025 on the original FNGU, and in January 2026 it announced a 1-for-10 reverse split of the inverse FNGD note, effective 9 February 2026.[6][7][8] It can also raise the financing spread from 2.25 points to as much as 4.00 points.[2][10] The latest pricing-supplement amendment we found, dated 21 May 2025, covers 80,000,000 notes, the same number the bank shows outstanding today, and we found no later amendment in the bank's filing list.[1][3] If 80,000,000 is the cap, the bank cannot create new notes without a fresh filing, and the price could drift above the indicative value when demand is high. On 23 October the premium was only 0.07%. FINRA, which oversees US brokers, points the same way. It says daily-reset products typically are unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them longer than one trading session.[26][27]

The number

We estimate the note's explicit yearly cost at 19.45% of its value, before any volatility drag.

How it was calculated

The financing charge is two times the note's value multiplied by the prime rate plus the 2.25-point spread: 2 x (7.00% + 2.25%) = 18.50%. The investor fee adds 0.95%.

The inputs

Prime of 7.00% on 21 October 2026 from the Federal Reserve data on FRED; the 2.25-point spread, the 2x financing factor and the 0.95% fee from the pricing supplement and the product sheet.[2][10][14]

The range

If the bank raised the spread to its 4.00-point cap the cost would be 22.95%. If prime fell by 0.25 points the cost would be 18.95%. Over the life of the note the average prime was 7.07%, close to today's level.[2][14]

If the number is off

Each 1 point of prime changes the yearly cost by 2 points. A higher cost lowers every scenario price in Chapter 5 and raises the index return needed to break even.

The number

We estimate that the index must rise about 12.5% over a year, or 3.0% over a quarter, for the note to end unchanged.

How it was calculated

A daily-reset 3x note ends at (index growth) cubed, times exp(-3 x sigma squared x years) for volatility drag, times exp(-cost x years). We set the ending value to 1 and solve for index growth, with sigma at 23% and cost at 19.45%.

The inputs

Index volatility of 22.8% over 252 days and 21.5% over 63 days from our calculation on the daily closes, rounded to 23%; cost from the estimate above.[25] The formula matches the supplement's printed decay example to the cent.[2]

The range

With volatility of 20% the break-even is 11.1%, and with 26% it is 14.2%. With the spread at 4.00 points and volatility at 23% it is 13.8%.

If the number is off

If the index runs hotter than this, the note gains. If it merely matches its 12.6% of the past year, the note is flat. The decision is therefore a bet on index returns above the break-even, with no margin if volatility stays high.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate has a weakest assumption. This chapter names it, says what evidence would show it wrong, and says when we will know. It then gives the bear case against our own verdict (the best argument that rejecting is a mistake) and three fatal-flaw scenarios that could end a holding regardless of the index.

The weakest assumption in each estimate

For the yearly cost of 19.45%, the weakest assumption is that prime stays at 7.00% and the spread stays at 2.25 points. A rate cut or a spread increase would change it. We will know within days, because the bank must publish a spread change at least five business days before it takes effect.[2] For the break-even return of 12.5%, the weakest assumption is volatility of 23%. If volatility drops to 15% the break-even is about 9%. The next 63 trading days, ending about 26 January 2027, will show the realised figure.

For the central price of $29.99, the weakest assumption is the 4% index gain. Nothing in the ledger supports a forecast better than a coin flip: the momentum gate failed all three tests. The index's next reconstitution on 18 December 2026 and the end of the three-month window are the dates that test it.[13] For the valuation stress, the weakest assumption is that earnings stay unchanged while the multiple falls. If earnings rose 10% during the fall, the index loss would be smaller. For the gap-loss estimate, the weakest assumption is that the worst day in 421 trading days is a fair guide. It is a short sample that ends before any tail event the index has not yet had.

The bear case against our verdict

The strongest argument that REJECTED is wrong runs like this. The note is 0.27 ATR above a recent swing low, its 200-day average is rising, its relative strength over 63 days is plus 12.64 points, and its RSI of 33.88 is near oversold. The index has recovered from every drawdown it has had, including a 22.9% fall from the 29 October 2025 peak. The sponsor counts 130 trading days below that high before the index reclaimed it on 8 May 2026.[13] Our count on the index closes agrees (our calculation).[25] The index reached a record on 7 October 2026.[13] A tactical buyer could say the pullback is the entry and that a four-week trade needs only a 0.9% index gain to break even. That argument is strong for a short hold.

Our reply is the numbers. At a tight stop of $27.74 the reward-to-risk ratio is 0.63 to 1 on the central target, well under the 2.5 to 1 floor. Only the bull target gives a ratio above the floor (6.0 to 1), and that ratio is an artefact of a stop that sits 0.77 ATR below the close. The momentum gate also fails, and the note has fallen 20.91% in two weeks without a base. The honest reading is that a buyer might make money, but the odds are not good enough for this book.

Fatal flaw 1: the bank calls the note at a bad moment

The bank may call the notes at any time on 14 calendar days' notice, and the payout is the average CIV over a five-day window.[2] It did this with the original FNGU, which was launched in January 2018 and was redeemed in May 2025 because its costs had risen.[6][7] If a call arrived in a sharp drawdown, an owner would be paid the average of five days' values, which locks in the loss and removes the chance to hold through the rebound. The likelihood is low while the note has $2.3 billion outstanding and the bank earns its spread, but the 2025 record shows the bank acts when its economics change.

Fatal flaw 2: an overnight or intraday gap takes the value toward zero

If the index fell 33.3% in one day, the note's indicative value would reach zero and would stay at zero for the rest of the note's life.[1][2] The index's worst day in our data was -6.6%, so the gap is far from that level, but the note's intraday exposure rises as the index falls: after a 20% fall the effective multiple is 6.00.[2] A 15% index fall in one session would be a 45% loss in one day. A stop order cannot protect against that, because the order would fill at whatever price the market gives after the gap.

Fatal flaw 3: financing costs rise while the trend stalls

If prime stays at 7.00% and the bank lifts the spread to 4.00 points, the explicit cost rises to 22.95% a year. The break-even index return for a year then rises to 13.8%.[2][14] In a year when the index goes nowhere, a long-dated holder loses about 30% from decay and cost, as the 12-month model shows. This flaw is slow rather than sudden. It shows up as steady loss against the index and is the main reason the note is a tool for days or weeks.
