1. What the company does

FNGU is the MicroSectors FANG+ 3× Leveraged ETNs due February 17, 2045, trading on NYSE Arca. An exchange-traded note (ETN) is not a fund. It is an unsecured debt note, so you lend money to the issuer and the issuer promises a payment linked to an index. The issuer is Bank of Montreal, which sets the fees and charges [1][9]. Each note had a $25 principal amount on the first trade date of February 19, 2025, and the note is part of the bank's Senior Medium-Term Notes, Series I [1].

The note aims to deliver three times the one-day return of the gross total return version of the NYSE FANG+ Index, before fees and charges [1]. If the index rises 1% in a day, the note aims to rise 3%. If the index falls 3.16%, as it did on October 16, 2026, the note aims to fall about 9.5%, and FNGU closed down 9.54% [41][43]. The note resets its exposure at the close of every trading day. A simple picture: a driver whose cruise control is set each evening to three times the speed reached that day. That reset makes the note a tool for one-day bets and a costly choice for a long hold.

The seed text for this name calls it a "MicroSectors FANG+ 3x Leveraged ETN" and the older web pages describe a note launched in January 2018. Those are two different securities. The 2018 note was due January 8, 2038, had CUSIP 063679534, and BMO called it in May 2025 because the costs of supporting its leverage had risen [6][7]. The note that trades as FNGU today is a new one, CUSIP 063679385, which began trading as FNGB on February 20, 2025 and took over the FNGU ticker on June 24, 2025 [2][4][5]. This explains why the ledger holds only 417 daily bars for FNGU against 501 for most names: the price history starts on February 20, 2025 [40][43].

The people who pay for the note are traders who want amplified exposure to large technology stocks for days, not years. BMO states that the notes are "not intended to be buy and hold investments" and are meant as daily trading tools [1]. The index behind the note holds ten companies at about 10% each: Palantir, Meta Platforms, Microsoft, NVIDIA, Amazon, Apple, Broadcom, Alphabet, Micron, Netflix. Their weights at the start of October 15, 2026 ran from 8.90% for Netflix to 11.11% for Palantir [10]. This book treats FNGU as the framework requires for leveraged products: it studies the index, the price trend, the costs and the decay risk, and it measures company quality on the ten stocks together. The positions replay of 2026-10-16 has no FNGU line, so the note is a pipeline challenger and the verdict applies to new money only [44].

The measures this book uses

Several measures come up again in later chapters, so here they are in plain words. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash a business has left after paying for equipment. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) is operating profit before borrowing costs, taxes and the wear on equipment. Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating profit divided by the money put into the business. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given to staff in shares. Net debt is debt minus cash.

The chart terms are these. The relative strength index (RSI) is a 0-to-100 gauge of how fast a price has been moving, and below 30 is called oversold. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price swing in dollars. A base is a flat floor that a price builds before a move, and a breakout is a move above the top of that floor. Stage 2 is the confirmed uptrend and Stage 4 is the downtrend. Gross margin is the share of revenue left after direct costs, and operating margin is the share left after all running costs. An entry zone is the band of prices where buying is planned.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Exchange-traded note (ETN) — a debt note from a bank that pays a return linked to an index; it is only as safe as the bank behind it.
  • Daily reset — the note rebuilds its 3× exposure at every close, so gains and losses compound day after day.
  • Closing Indicative Value (CIV) — the issuer's own daily calculation of what one note is worth; the market price can differ from it.
  • Intraday Indicative Value (IIV) — the same calculation updated every 15 seconds during the trading day.
  • Volatility decay — the slow loss a daily-reset note suffers when prices swing up and down, even if the index ends where it began.
  • Financing charge — the daily interest the note deducts for the money it notionally borrows to hold three times the index.
  • Call right — the issuer's right to end the note early and pay holders the average of five daily indicative values.
  • Premium or discount — the gap between the market price and the indicative value, as a percentage.
  • Equal weighting — each of the ten stocks is set to 10% of the index at each quarterly reset.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

This book scores every name in three layers: Layer 1 sets the hard floors for the business and the chart, Layer 2 judges the quality of the product itself, and Layer 3 sets the entry, the stop and the size. A note has no brand or factory, so its strength is how well it does the one job it promises, how safe its issuer is, and how it compares with rivals doing the same job. On the job, FNGU is sound. On cost, it is the most expensive way to hold this exposure that we could source. On the issuer, the credit is strong but the issuer keeps wide rights over the note. The Layer 2 scorecard in the next chapter puts numbers on each point.

Five products give access to this index or to close cousins of it. FNGU is the 3× note on the NYSE FANG+ Index. FNGO is the 2× note on the same index, with $791.07 million of assets and a 0.95% expense ratio. FNGS is the 1× note, with $618.64 million and 0.58%. BULZ is a 3× BMO note on a different, 15-stock index. TQQQ is a 3× fund on the Nasdaq-100 with $37.06 billion of assets and a 0.78% expense ratio [28][27][26][24]. On October 16, 2026 the index fell 3.16%, so a perfect 3× product would have fallen 9.48%. FNGU fell 9.54%, which is a gap of 0.06 points [41][40].

The cost difference is the main finding. We measured each product's return against the index over the last 250 sessions. FNGS gave up about 0.2% a year, FNGO about 5.3% a year and FNGU about 18.1% a year. These are our own measurements from daily closes: the gap between each product's compounded return and the compounded return of its multiple of the index over 250 sessions, annualised. They are close to the contract, and the 1.3-point difference between 19.45% and 18.1% comes from the lower prime rate earlier in the period and from compounding: the note charges interest on twice its value at prime plus 2.25%, which is 18.50% a year at a 7.00% prime rate, plus a 0.95% fee [40][41][42][1][20]. A 3× fund such as TQQQ borrows through index swaps with at least ten banks and states only a 0.78% expense ratio, though its financing costs are also paid inside the swaps [24]. FNGU's cost is visible, linked to a published rate, and large.

Strength: the note does what it says each day

The strongest evidence is the daily match. Over the last 250 sessions the average gap between FNGU's daily return and three times the index return was -0.074 percentage points. That average is almost all cost. Added up over 252 trading days it is 18.7% a year, and the compounded measure we use as the measured cost is 18.1% a year (the log gap between FNGU's actual return and compounded 3× the index return over the 250 sessions). The median size of the gap was 0.13 points, 95% of days were within 0.43 points, and the worst gap was -1.37 points on June 26, 2026 [40][41]. In the first three weeks after launch, from February 21 to March 7, 2025, the gaps were larger, up to 4.5 points, which looks like timing mismatches while the note was new. We treat the record since the June 2025 ticker change as the fair test.

The market price also stays close to value. The issuer's page gives a Closing Indicative Value of $31.15 for October 16, 2026 and the close was $31.18, a premium of about 0.10% [8][43]. Eleven dated pairs of price and indicative value, taken from the pricing supplement cover pages between February 25, 2025 and May 21, 2025 and from the issuer page, ranged from -0.65% to +0.55% [3][1][8]. That sample is small and was taken on days when BMO filed an amendment, so it is not a full audit. It does not show large premiums or discounts in that period.

Weakness: cost, decay and the issuer's rights

Three things weigh against the note. First, the cost: about 19.45% a year at today's prime rate, and up to 22.95% if BMO raises the spread to its 4.00% cap [1][20]. Second, volatility decay: because exposure resets daily, a choppy index loses value even when it goes nowhere. The pricing supplement says that in almost any scenario the long-term performance of the notes is likely to be negative, whatever the index does [1]. Third, the issuer's rights: BMO may call the notes on 14 days' notice and pay the average of five daily values, and it did exactly that to the old FNGU [1][7].

A fourth point is concentration. The index holds ten stocks and the top six are fixed names that stay in as long as they qualify; the other four are chosen by rank each quarter [11]. Between December 2024 and October 2026 two of those four places changed: CrowdStrike and ServiceNow left, Palantir and Micron joined [9][10]. Daily returns of FNGU and TQQQ moved together with a correlation of 0.92 over 416 sessions, so FNGU adds little diversification to an account that already holds TQQQ, which this one does [40][42][44].

Where the fall of October 16 came from

All ten stocks are tied to one theme: large technology companies that spend heavily on artificial intelligence. On October 16, 2026 the S&P 500 fell 1.1% and the Nasdaq composite fell 2.1%. Micron fell 5.6%, Broadcom 4.5%, Meta 3.7%, Alphabet 2.5% and Netflix 2.9% [21]. Palantir fell 7.22% and Apple rose 0.43% [43]. The causes were worries about how AI spending is financed with debt, bond yields near the highest levels since 2002, and inflation that came in hotter than expected: September consumer prices rose 3.7% from a year earlier [21][22]. The average fall of the ten stocks was about 3.1%, which fits the 3.16% fall in the index.

This matters for the note in one specific way. The same inflation news that hit the stocks raised the odds of another Federal Reserve rate increase in December to 74.3% [21]. The note's financing charge follows the prime rate, which moves with the Fed. A rate hike would hurt the stocks and raise the note's cost on the same day. The two risks arrive together.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every number in Chapter 5 rests on a few assumptions. This chapter names the weakest assumption behind each estimate, the evidence that would show it wrong and when we will know. It then gives three fatal-flaw scenarios, the strongest case against our own conclusion and the single claim the conclusion depends on.

The first three estimates in Chapter 5 rest on volatility and cost. We assumed index volatility of 22.7% a year and a cost of 19.45% a year. Neither is stable. Index volatility was 20.2% over 20 sessions, 20.7% over 60, 22.7% over 250 and 26.5% since the note began [41]. The cost follows the prime rate and the issuer's spread. At 40% volatility the three-month break-even rises from +2.96% to +5.8% and the base case falls from $31.96 to $29.47, which is -5.49% from the close, so a flat 40% volatility turns the base case into a loss. The evidence that would show us wrong is realized volatility below 15% or above 35% for a month, or a rate move. We will know within 20 trading days, and by the seven reports from October 20 to November 2.

The scenario estimates rest on chart levels and weights. The index prices 19,649, 19,289.5 and 16,493 are chart levels, not valuations, and the 30%, 50% and 20% weights are our rounding of statistical odds, not a forecast [41]. The ledger shows 63-day relative strength of +12.42% against the S&P 500, which is a counterweight to the bearish tilt [43]. The evidence that would show our weights wrong is a strong run of earnings from the seven reporters, or a drop in the December hike odds from 74.3% [21].

Fatal flaw 1: a gap through the stop

The $29.83 stop is $1.35 below the close, which is 0.78 of one normal day's swing. FNGU moved more than 5% in a day on 90 of the last 416 sessions, fell more than 9% on 10 of them, and its worst day was -19.72% on April 3, 2025, when the index fell 6.63% [40][41]. A stop can be jumped, either overnight or inside one session, and then the loss is larger than planned. The structure adds a harder edge: if the Intraday Indicative Value ever reaches zero the note is worth zero for the rest of its life, and that takes a one-day index fall of about 33% [1]. Treat the stop as a line where the idea is wrong, not as a guarantee of the exit price. This flaw would not change our verdict, because we are not buying, but it would change how any later entry is sized.

Fatal flaw 2: the issuer changes the terms or ends the note

BMO may call the notes on 14 days' notice, raise the financing spread from 2.25% to 4.00% on five business days' notice, or stop listing the notes [1].
