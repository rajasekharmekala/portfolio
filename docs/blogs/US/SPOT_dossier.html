Spotify (NYSE: SPOT) runs the largest paid audio streaming service, with 788 million monthly users and 306 million paying subscribers at the end of September 2026.[2] The business passes our quality tests: it turns 17.0% of revenue into free cash, it holds net cash, and pay in stock is only 1.5% of revenue.[2][1][5] The problem is the price: at $584.22 the stock is well above its recent support, and the reward is only 0.32 dollars per dollar of risk, against a floor of 2.0. 1. What the company does

Spotify sells two things to two kinds of customer. Listeners pay a monthly fee for Premium, which removes advertising and gives access to the full catalogue of music, podcasts and audiobooks. Advertisers pay to reach listeners who use the free service. Premium produced 89% of 2025 revenue, which was €15,350 million of €17,186 million.[5] In the third quarter of 2026 Premium revenue was €4,487 million and advertising revenue was €482 million.[1]

The free service is also a sales funnel. Spotify's annual report says the ad-supported service serves as a way to win new Premium subscribers.[5] At the end of September 2026 the platform had 788 million monthly active users (MAUs), meaning people who used it in the month. Of those, 499 million used the free tier and 306 million paid.[2] Each paying subscriber brought in €4.95 a month in the third quarter, a figure called average revenue per user (ARPU), which was up 9.3% on the year, or 7.5% once currency swings are removed. Price increases drove the gain.[2]

The main cost is paying the people who own the music. Royalty rates vary by country, some contracts have minimum payments, and some rights holders give discounts in return for promotion inside the app.[5] At September 30, 2026 Spotify owed rights holders €2,025 million in accrued fees.[1] Revenue left after direct costs is gross margin. It was 33.0% in the third quarter, with 34.5% in Premium and 18.7% in advertising.[1] Spotify also owns about 9% of Tencent Music Entertainment, carried at €1,066 million in long term investments.[1]

Spotify is a Luxembourg company that files an annual report on Form 20-F and reports in euros under IFRS accounting rules, while its shares trade in US dollars on the New York Stock Exchange.[1][5] We convert at 0.8828 euros per dollar, the rate Spotify itself used in its fourth-quarter outlook at the end of September 2026.[2] We found no later cited rate, so every converted figure carries that date. This matters for the answer, because the ledger's own reverse-DCF number mixes dollars and euros, a point we fix in chapter 5.

An owner is buying a toll on a habit. 2. Why the business is strong or weak

Spotify's strength is scale, and the numbers show it. Premium subscribers rose from 281 million to 306 million in a year, and monthly users from 713 million to 788 million.[2] Management's own investor-day account says Spotify gained 8 to 10 percentage points of US Premium market share over six years, according to Midia data, and that gross profit from its Marketplace programs in 2025 was four times the 2021 level.[16] These are company statements, so we treat them as claims rather than audited facts. The audited sign of strength is margin: gross margin rose from 24.95% in 2022 to 31.98% in 2025 and was 33.1% over the last twelve months (our calculation from filed figures).[6][5][1][14]

The margin gain has more than one source. The chief financial officer said about a third of the gross margin expansion since 2022 came from music, with the rest from audiobooks and podcasts, and that both music and non-music now earn more than a 30% gross margin.[16] In the third quarter, Premium margin rose 130 basis points to 34.5% because revenue grew faster than music royalty costs, audiobook costs and video podcast costs. Advertising margin rose 105 basis points to 18.7%.[2] A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point.

The business is also exposed. Four licensors, Universal Music Group, Sony Music Entertainment, Warner Music Group and Merlin, supplied content that made up about 72% of label-delivered streams in 2025.[5] The annual report warns that these rights holders may use their market power to seek harsher terms, and that licences may not be renewed.[5] Spotify also says that Apple, Alphabet and Amazon preload their own audio services on their devices, which is a built-in advantage.[5]

Where the moat is strong

The free tier feeds the paid tier, and the paid tier funds new products. The company paid more than $11 billion to the music industry in 2025, up more than 10% on the year, and says the all-time total exceeds $70 billion.[16] That scale gives Spotify the data to personalize each home screen, and it gives labels a reason to keep the service healthy. The Q3 results show the flywheel working: growth came from every region, and Spotify expects 316 million subscribers by year end, ten million more than today.[2] Strength here supports the quality score in chapter 3, but it does not set the entry price, which chapter 7 handles.

Where the moat is thin: rights holders, platforms, regulation and AI

Rights holders can change what the service costs to run. Publisher licences signed directly in the United States mostly run until late 2027, and the statutory US rate period for compositions covers 2023 to 2027, with rates from January 1, 2028 still to be set.[5] A lawsuit by the Mechanical Licensing Collective was filed in May 2024 and dismissed in January 2025, and the collective filed an amended complaint on October 1, 2025 that argues that Spotify under-reported royalties on its Premium bundle.[5] Spotify cannot price that risk, so we treat it as a cost risk in chapter 6.

Platform fees and regulation cut both ways. The European Commission found in March 2024 that Apple's conduct was illegal and fined it €1.84 billion, but Apple appealed, and Spotify says it cannot be sure Apple will change its behaviour in ways that help.[5] In May 2025, after a US court order, Apple let developers including Spotify link from their apps to web offers.[5] AI is both a tool and a threat. Spotify uses AI for its DJ and playlist features and flags that AI output can be wrong and that copyright protection for AI-made material is uncertain.[5] At the May investor day it announced licensing deals with Universal Music Group for a paid tool to create covers and remixes with artist consent.[16] The share of streams that is AI-generated is not published in the sources we checked (not found after three searches), so we cannot size that risk.

On the third-quarter call, an analyst asked whether AI assistants that take over the app could make Spotify an interchangeable back-end service.[26] In the results release, Co-CEO Gustav Söderström argued that entertainment is the opposite of a chore people want to hand off.[3] That is an argument, not evidence, and the churn figure that would test it is not published (not found after three searches). Walk-the-talk means comparing what management said it would do with what it did. The cleanest test is the quarterly guidance, because the same people give it every quarter. In 2026 the company met or beat guidance on subscribers and gross margin in every quarter. It missed on monthly users once, by one million, in the second quarter.[11][7][2][14]

The operating income beats need a closer look. Social charges are payroll taxes tied to the share price, and Spotify leaves share price moves out of its forecasts. 3. The numbers, in plain words

Five years of filed figures show a business that moved from near break-even to real profit. Revenue grew from €9,668 million in 2021 to €17,186 million in 2025. That is a compound rate of about 15.5% a year, or 13.6% over the last three years (our calculation).[6][5] Gross margin rose from 26.8% to 32.0%. Operating income was €94 million in 2021. It turned into losses of €659 million and €446 million in 2022 and 2023. Then it reached €1,365 million and €2,198 million in 2024 and 2025.[6][5] Net income of €2,212 million in 2025 was helped by income tax of only €12 million, so it overstates the profit that will recur.[5]

Cash flow tells the same story more cleanly. Spotify defines free cash flow (FCF) as cash from operations minus capital spending and the change in restricted cash.[5] FCF rose from €21 million in 2022 to €2,874 million in 2025. Over the twelve months to September 2026 it was €3,190 million, which is 17.0% of revenue (our calculation).[2] Two cautions follow. Q3 FCF of €735 million was 9% lower than a year earlier. Cash taxes are also well below the tax charge: €196 million was paid in nine months against an expense of €578 million.[2][1] Part of the gap is timing, because the company holds €548 million of deferred tax assets, so cash taxes should rise.[1]

The balance sheet is a strength. At September 30, 2026 Spotify held €6,032 million in cash and €3,856 million in short-term investments, a total of €9,888 million, and the Exchangeable Notes were gone.[1] Its only borrowings are lease liabilities of €452 million.[1] Stock-based compensation (SBC), the stock paid to employees, was €286 million over the last twelve months or 1.5% of revenue (our calculation). It was 3.2% in 2022 (our calculation).[6][1][5] Earnings also include finance income, which was about 19% of last-twelve-month pre-tax profit (our calculation), so operating income is the cleaner profit measure.[1][5]

The number

We estimate Spotify's return on invested capital at about 22.5% over the last twelve months.

How it was calculated

Operating income of €2,743 million, taxed at the 24.5% nine-month effective rate, gives about €2,071 million after tax. We divided it by equity of €8,741 million plus lease liabilities of €452 million. We did not subtract cash, so the capital total is large and the answer is cautious. This is our calculation.

The inputs

Operating income is €2,042 million for nine months plus €701 million for Q4 2025.[1][14] The tax rate is 24.5% for nine months.[1] Equity of €8,741 million and leases of €452 million are at September 30, 2026.[1] The vendor's 120.64% ROIC uses a different capital total and we do not rely on it.[20]

The range

If tax rises to 30%, the figure is about 20.9%. If operating income falls 20%, it is about 18%. Subtracting cash and the Tencent Music stake would make invested capital negative, so ROIC would have no meaningful value, which is why we used the cautious method.

If the number is off

A figure below 12% would fail the ROIC test and move the verdict toward REJECTED. Even a drop to 15% would pass, so this input is not what decides the case.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Every estimate in chapter 5 rests on a weakest assumption. For the reverse DCF it is the cash flow starting point, because low cash taxes, interest income and working capital (cash tied up in day-to-day operations) timing all lift it.[1] For the FY2028 EPS and the $628 target it is the claim that cost growth moderates while gross margin keeps rising, which management says will happen but has not yet shown in the numbers.[26][2] For the probability-weighted value it is the probabilities themselves, which are judgment.

The bear case, in short: Spotify misses the Q4 guide as social charges rise with the share price, operating cost growth stays near 20%, gross margin stalls at 33%, label renewals due around late 2027 cost more, and the multiple falls to 24 times FY2028 EPS of €15.00, which is below the FY2027 consensus of €15.40. That path leads to roughly $408, a 30% fall from the close. (At 24 times the FY2027 consensus the price would be about $419.)

Fatal flaw 1: rights holders take back the margin

Four licensors supplied about 72% of label-delivered streams, publisher licences mostly run to late 2027, the statutory US composition rate period ends in 2027, and the Mechanical Licensing Collective has an amended complaint filed on October 1, 2025 that argues Premium is mispriced as a bundle.[5] If Premium gross margin of 34.5% gives back even three points, gross margin falls toward 30% and the margin path to 35% to 40% fails.[1][16] We would see this in gross margin falling below 32.5% for two quarters or in a court ruling in the MLC case. The date it is known is spread over 2027 to 2028.

Fatal flaw 2: spending runs ahead of revenue and margins stall

Operating costs grew about 26% in Q3, and operating margin of 13.5% was 10 basis points below a year earlier, while free cash flow has fallen three quarters in a row.[2] The 2030 target needs margins above 20%. If cost growth does not fall in Q4 and in 2027, operating income of €820 million is at risk, with social charges adding about €40 million of headwind at today's price (our calculation).[2] The evidence would be a Q4 operating margin below 15.8%, the margin implied by the guide. We will know at the Q4 2026 report, which we estimate for early February 2027 because the company reported Q4 2025 on February 10, 2026 and has not confirmed a date.

Fatal flaw 3: the multiple shrinks faster than profit grows

At 33.5 times FY2027 EPS the stock pays for 28% growth in FY2027.[19] If the multiple falls to 25 times with EPS unchanged, the price is about $436, which is 25% below the close and below our $448.97 stop (our calculation). 7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, which means we place a buy order at a lower price and do not buy at the current market price (CMP) of $584.22. The rule that sets it is Layer 3, our third test, which maps the distance from support onto a ladder. Support is a price level where buyers have stepped in before. The ladder runs from full size within 0.6 ATR of support, standard size within 1.2, a small starter within 1.6, and no purchase beyond 1.6. Spotify is 2.74 ATR above the 50-day average of $526.01, which is the ledger's support anchor, so it sits in the no-purchase band. The reward-to-risk at the CMP is 0.32 to 1 against a floor of 2.0 for the CORE_ALPHA bucket, so the answer would be the same even without the chart.

A limit order is an order to buy only at a price you set or lower, and the entry zone is the range where we would accept a fill. We derived the zone top from the numbers. Our central target is $628 and the stop is $448.97. For a ratio of 2.0 the highest entry is (628 plus 2 times 448.97) divided by 3, which is $508.65. We set the ceiling at $504.00 because that is about 1.59 ATR above the 20-day swing low of $470.25, inside the starter band of the ladder, and $504.30 would be 1.6 ATR. At $504.00 the ratio is 2.25 to 1 and the stop is 10.92% below the ceiling. The zone bottom of $481.00 is the swing low plus 0.5 ATR, where the ratio is 4.59 to 1 and the stop is 6.66% below the entry. Both stop distances use the zone price as the base. Measured from the CMP, the stop is 23.15% away.

The stop is a structural invalidation level, meaning a price that would show the thesis is wrong. We used the Stage 2 rule for CORE_ALPHA: the lower of the 20-day swing low ($470.25) and the 50-day average ($526.01), minus one ATR of $21.28, which gives $448.97. The swing low is the lower of the two, so the stop and the ladder use the same swing low as their support. A second plausible stop is the 63-day base low of $462.00 minus one ATR, or $440.72, which is the Stage 1 rule and not the one for a Stage 2 chart. With that stop the ratio at the ceiling would be 1.96, just under the floor, which is why we show both. A fill at the zone means the stock fell through the 20-day and 50-day averages and sits near the 200-day average of $496.61, a weaker chart than today's, which is the price of the discount.

The zone is a place to start checking, not an order to leave on the book. We do not rest a standing buy order. When SPOT trades at or below $504.00, we run a same-day pre-fill check and place a day order only if every item passes. First, the price is inside $481.00 to $504.00. Second, the trend is intact enough: the 50-day average is still rising, SPOT is not below a falling 200-day average, the pullback is on lighter volume, and RSI has cooled toward 50. Third, we recompute support, the ATR, the stop and the position size from that day's data. Fourth, the reward-to-risk on the central target is at least 2.0 at the intended fill price. Fifth, no earnings report or major legal ruling is due within five trading days. If any item fails, we do not buy and any working order is cancelled. After every earnings report we rerun the whole analysis with the new results before any order is placed, and any order already working is cancelled until that rerun passes.

The $628 target depends on the multiple holding, and a lower price does not cure that. At 30 times FY2028 EPS the target is $628. At 29 times, one turn lower, it is $607, and the ratio at $504.00 falls from 2.25 to 1.87, below 2.0. The highest entry that still clears 2.0 at 29 times is $501.67 (our calculation). So a price-only pullback does not restore the margin of safety. The pre-fill check must rebuild the target from current estimates, and the 2.0 floor must hold at the price we would pay.

Sizing works in two steps. Portfolio heat is the share of total net worth we would lose if the stop is hit. Risk-unit sizing sets the position weight as the lower of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. A basis point (bps) is one hundredth of a percent. The conviction tiers run from TIER_1 (7% to 10% of net worth, 100 bps of risk) down to TIER_4 (1% to 2%, 35 bps), and TIER_0 means zero size. The yardstick for Spotify is TIER_3, which allows 2% to 3.5% and a 50 bps budget.
