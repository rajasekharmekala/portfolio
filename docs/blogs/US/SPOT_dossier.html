Before you read

Spotify is the world’s largest audio streaming service, with 788 million monthly users and 306 million paying subscribers.[2] It reports in euros, while its shares trade in US dollars. The business passes our quality tests: it earns 22.5% a year on the money invested in it, turns 16.9% of sales into spare cash and holds €9.9 billion of cash with no financial debt.[1][2] But at $538.40 the possible gain is only 0.64 times the possible loss, against our floor of 2.0, so the verdict is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT with a buy range of $481.00 to $497.00, where the ratio reaches 2.06. The verdict would be wrong if advertising and margins improve faster than we model, which would lift our $595 target, and the idea ends if the stock closes below $449.37.

1. What the company does

Spotify sells two things to two kinds of customer. Listeners pay a monthly fee for Premium, which removes advertising and opens the full catalogue of music, podcasts and audiobooks. Advertisers pay to reach the listeners who use the free service. Premium brought in €15,350 million of the €17,186 million of revenue in 2025, which is 89%.[8] In the third quarter of 2026 Premium revenue was €4,487 million and advertising revenue was €482 million.[2]

The free service is also a sales funnel. At the end of September 2026 the platform had 788 million monthly active users (people who used it in the last 30 days), and 306 million of them were paying subscribers.[2] Management argues that every year of 25 million or more net new subscribers since the 2018 listing shows how steady this funnel is, and it says under 4% of the world’s population pays today, against nearly 50% in Sweden.[23] That is a claim by the company, not a forecast we can verify, but it frames why the business keeps adding customers.

The main cost is paying the people who own the music. Royalty rates vary by country, many contracts set a minimum payment per user, and some rights holders give discounts in return for promotion inside the app.[8] Three record companies and an independent-label agency (Universal, Sony, Warner and Merlin) supplied about 72% of the label music streamed in 2025.[8] The revenue that is left after direct costs is the gross margin, and it was 33.0% in the third quarter, with 34.5% in Premium and 18.7% in advertising.[2]

Spotify is a Luxembourg company that files an annual report on Form 20-F and quarterly reports on Form 6-K, which is the paperwork a foreign company files with the US securities regulator instead of the 10-K and 10-Q.[1] It reports in euros under IFRS, an international accounting standard, while the shares trade in US dollars on the New York Stock Exchange. We keep the two currencies apart throughout this report. Euro figures carry a euro sign, dollar figures carry a dollar sign, and the one conversion we use is $1.1199 per euro, the Federal Reserve’s rate for October 23, 2026, the latest it had published.[19]

How the money flows from a listener to Spotify

Take the third quarter of 2026. Each paying subscriber brought in €4.95 a month on average, which is 9.3% more than a year earlier, or 7.5% more once currency moves are removed.[2] Multiply that by 306 million subscribers and you see why Premium is the profit engine. The company says the rise came from price increases, partly offset by growth in cheaper markets.[2] For the fourth quarter it guides to a 4.5% to 5% rise in revenue per subscriber, helped by recent price changes and by add-on products such as Audiobooks+, and held back by the lapping of price rises in more than 150 markets last year.[23]

Our own arithmetic on that third-quarter figure: €4.95 a month at a 34.5% Premium gross margin leaves about €1.71 a month of gross profit per subscriber, before the costs of building the product, marketing and running the company. That is a thin slice of each payment, and it is the reason the gross margin line is the first thing we watch in chapter 2B.

What it costs to run

Below gross profit sit research and development, sales and marketing, and general and administrative costs, which together were €967 million in the third quarter, up 26% from a year earlier.[2] What is left after those costs is operating income, and operating income divided by revenue is the operating margin. It was 13.5% in the third quarter.[2] Management says most of that growth came from marketing and from cloud and artificial intelligence spending, and that it expects operating expense growth to cool from the fourth quarter into 2027.[2][23] One cost item moves with the share price: Social Charges, which are payroll taxes on stock-based pay in some countries. They were €22 million in the third quarter, €13 million above the company’s own forecast, because the shares rose during the quarter.[2]

Headcount is modest for a company of this reach: 7,284 full-time employees at the end of September 2026.[2] The company also has almost no capital spending, only €62 million over the last twelve months, because it rents cloud capacity and licenses its content.[2] This is why profit converts to cash so easily, a point chapter 3 develops.

What an owner is really buying

An owner is buying a toll on a daily habit. Subscribers pay every month, and the company has added subscribers every year while raising prices.[23][8] The weak point is that the biggest cost line is set by a few music owners that Spotify does not control, and the weak point on the other side is that the free tier still earns little from advertising. Chapter 2 tests both of those claims against the numbers, and chapter 7 turns the result into a price.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Monthly active users (MAUs): people who used the service at least once in the last 30 days, counting free and paying users.
  • Premium subscriber: a person or household member on a paid plan; the count includes family and student plans.
  • Average revenue per user (ARPU): Premium subscription revenue divided by average subscribers and by three months, so it is a monthly figure per subscriber.
  • Royalties: payments to record companies, music publishers, audiobook publishers and podcast partners for the right to stream their work.
  • Social Charges: payroll taxes tied to stock-based pay in some countries; they rise when the share price rises.

  • Constant currency: growth with exchange-rate moves removed, so a falling or rising euro does not blur the trend.
  • Form 20-F and Form 6-K: the annual and interim reports a foreign company files with the US Securities and Exchange Commission.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Spotify’s strength is scale that keeps compounding. It has 788 million monthly users and 306 million paying subscribers, and it has added at least 25 million subscribers every year since going public with 71 million.[23] In 2025 it ended with 290 million subscribers, up 10% from 263 million.[8] A competitor must win those listeners one at a time, and the company’s annual report names Apple Music, YouTube Music and Amazon Music among the rivals it faces.[8] Scale also lets Spotify spread product and cloud costs over a larger customer count, which is visible in a research and development bill that fell from 13% of revenue in 2023 to 8% in 2025.[8]

Pricing power is the second strength. Premium revenue per subscriber rose 9.3% in the third quarter, or 7.5% without currency effects, even though subscribers rose 9% at the same time.[2] Price rises usually cost a subscription business some customers. Here the customer count kept growing, and net additions came from every region.[2] The evidence is one quarter and one set of price actions, so we treat it as encouraging rather than settled.

The third strength is where management is taking the business next. Audiobooks are expanding from 22 markets to more than 180 by the end of 2026, creator memberships launched in 34 markets, and the Spotify Partner Program grew to more than 35 new markets.[2] At its May 2026 Investor Day the company set 2030 targets of mid-teens revenue growth a year, a gross margin of 35% to 40% and an operating margin above 20%.[24] Targets are not results, and the next section shows what the results say so far.

Where the business is weaker than it looks

Music owners hold the pricing lever. The music from Universal, Sony, Warner and Merlin made up about 72% of label streams in 2025, so a dispute with any one of them would hurt.[8] The annual report also says some licenses carry most-favoured-nation terms, which can raise Spotify’s payments if a rival gets a better deal.[8] In mid-2025 the company flagged a regulatory charge in Premium worth about 0.4 percentage points of gross margin.[11] That is why a 33% gross margin, though higher every year since 2022, still leaves little room for error.

Advertising is the soft spot. Ad-Supported revenue fell 1% in 2025 to €1,836 million, and management says ad growth was only 3% a quarter in the first half of 2026 before it reached 9% at constant currency in the third quarter.[8][23] A third-party summary of the call says automated bidding channels are now 40% of ad revenue.[26] The recovery is a single quarter old, and the free tier is also being changed in emerging markets to cut low-value sign-ups, which management says slows monthly user growth on purpose.[27][23]

Control and succession are a third issue. Daniel Ek and Martin Lorentzon together held 69.3% of the voting power at the end of 2025, so outside shareholders cannot force a change.[8] At the April 2026 meeting about 36.0 million votes went against Ek and 44.9 million against Lorentzon, compared with 436.7 million and 427.7 million in favour, so some minority holders objected but could not change the result.[14] Between November 2025 and February 2026 Daniel Ek moved from chief executive to executive chairman, and by April 2026 Alex Norström and Gustav Söderström were co-chief executives.[10][7][6] Shareholders elected both of them to the board effective January 1, 2026.[15] The chief accounting officer was due to retire in May 2026, and a director resigned in September 2026.[16][17] None of these is a warning sign alone. Together they mean the company is run by a new team under the same two controlling founders.

What the evidence adds up to

On balance the business is strong and the quality evidence is consistent: gross margin has risen from 25.6% in 2023 to 33.1% over the last twelve months, operating income has turned from a loss of €446 million to €2,743 million, and cash flow has grown even faster.[8][2] The limits are the cost of music, a young advertising recovery and founder control. Management walk-the-talk: past promises vs delivered reality

Walk-the-talk asks whether management delivered what it promised one to two years ago. Spotify gives quarterly guidance rather than a full-year forecast, which makes the test clean. Across the eight quarterly reports we fetched, from the third quarter of 2024 to the third quarter of 2026, subscribers came in at or above guidance every time, and gross margin came in at or above guidance every time.[13][12][11][10][7][6][4][2] We did not retrieve the first-quarter 2025 report, so that quarter is outside the count.

The exceptions are informative. Operating income missed guidance twice: €477 million against €481 million in the fourth quarter of 2024, and €406 million against €539 million in the second quarter of 2025.[12][11] In both quarters the company explains the gap by items its guidance deliberately leaves out. Social Charges ran €80 million over forecast in the first and €98 million over in the second, and in the second quarter of 2025 an unplanned currency headwind cut revenue by €104 million.[12][11] A reader should not excuse a miss just because management names a cause. But a share-price-driven tax is outside its control, and the same effect helped in later quarters, when Social Charges were €41 million, €67 million and €49 million below forecast.[10][7][6]

Two larger promises matter more than any one quarter. In 2022 the company set long-term ambitions of a 40% gross margin, a 20% operating margin and $100 billion of revenue, and at the 2026 Investor Day it reaffirmed them and added a 2030 framework.[25][24] Today’s margins are 33.0% and 13.5%, so the promise is a work in progress, not a delivered result. The second promise is the capital plan. The company said it would resume share repurchases, called a buyback, and it has. Each year staff are paid in new shares, which causes dilution, the shrinking of each existing share’s slice. The buyback is meant to offset that. Through September 30, 2026 it had bought 3,287,031 shares repurchased for €1,395 million ($1,620 million), with about $1,880 million of authorization left, and the weighted basic share count was flat from a year earlier at 205.72 million.[1] It also repaid its €1,304 million of Exchangeable Notes in the first half of 2026 and now reports no debt other than leases.[3][23]

4. What the price is doing

A chart cannot tell us whether Spotify is a good business. It tells us who is in control right now, buyers or sellers, and how far the price sits from a level where buyers have stepped in before. Every reading below comes from our frozen end-of-day data for October 28, 2026. The closing price matches the quote on a public forecast page.[20] A 50-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 50 trading days. In a rising stock it acts as a slow floor. 5. What could happen next

A stock price is a bet on the future, and chapter 5 asks what bet today’s price is making. We do that in two ways. A reverse discounted cash flow (reverse DCF) starts from the price and works backward to the yearly growth in free cash flow that the price needs to be fair. Then we compare it with what analysts expect and with what we think is likely. The method uses our standard settings: a 10% yearly discount rate, ten years of growth, and a final-year value of 18 times that year’s free cash flow. We also quote the price-to-earnings ratio, which is the share price divided by earnings per share and shows how many years of current profit the price pays for.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

This chapter attacks our own estimates. For each one we state the assumption most likely to be wrong, the evidence that would show it, and the date we would know. We then set out the strongest honest case against owning the stock, and name the single claim on which the whole thesis hinges.

The reverse DCF: is €3,186 million a clean starting point?

The weakest assumption is that last year’s cash flow is a fair base. Third-quarter free cash flow fell 9% to €735 million, and the company blames working capital and cash tax timing.[2] Cash taxes paid were €75 million in Q3 against €5 million a year earlier, and the effective tax rate for the quarter was 26.5%.[2][1] If those taxes are permanent, the base cash flow is lower than we assume. The evidence that settles it is the fourth-quarter cash flow, due in early February 2027. If twelve-month free cash flow falls below about €3.0 billion, the implied growth rate in chapter 5 rises by about one point.

The 12-month target: is 29 times right, and is the margin path realistic?

Two inputs carry the target, and both are judgement. The first is the multiple of 29 times 2028 earnings, which we could not tie to a verified three-year median. The second is the path of full-year operating margin. We estimate it at about 14.7% for 2026 (nine months of actuals plus the fourth-quarter guide: €2,862 million on €19,479 million of revenue), then 16.6% in 2027 and 18.3% in 2028. That is about 1.8 points a year. It matches the 1.9-point gain from 12.8% in 2025 to 14.7% in 2026, so the pace is not new.[1][2] The model also assumes operating expense growth drops from 26% in the third quarter to 8% a year, a cliff the company has not guided. The company itself says quarterly margin can be variable and has declined to guide 2027.[23] A 5% fall in the euro would also lower the dollar target by about $30, because earnings are in euros. We will know the margin path from the 2027 guide in February 2027 and from each later quarter.

The red-team bear case

The strongest honest case against buying is this. The price has already recovered from $405.00 to $538.40, and the market has paid for a large part of the 2030 plan. Consensus earnings are being cut, not raised: 2026 EPS is down 6.5% in 90 days and nine analysts cut in the last 30 days.[21] The last two quarters’ EPS came in below the aggregator’s estimate, by 6.8% and 12.7%.[21] Free cash flow fell in Q3. Advertising, the weakest leg, has one good quarter. A company that beats its guidance almost every quarter does so partly because it guides cautiously, so a beat is a smaller signal than it looks.

Then there are structural risks. Three record companies and a licensing agency supply about 72% of label streams, and some contracts carry most-favoured-nation clauses.[8] The founders hold 69.3% of the votes, and the top management team has changed within a year.[8][15] Rates are high: our market data file shows the ten-year US Treasury yield at 4.99% on October 27, which lowers what long-dated cash flows are worth. We found no company-specific news on October 28 to explain the 3.07% drop, which means we cannot rule out that someone knows something we do not. Our response is to buy only in the buy range with a hard stop, and to size small.

If operating expenses instead grow 15% a year, 2028 EPS falls to about €16.15 ($18.09), the target to about $524.5, which is roughly $70 below ours, and the zone falls with it (chapter 7). 7. What we would do, and when

The decision is WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, which means we place a buy order at a lower price and do not buy at the current market price (CMP) of $538.40. Two rules decide it. The support ladder maps how far the price sits from the nearest verified support. It puts Spotify in the FULL_SIZE band, at 0.45 average true ranges above the 50-day average of $528.90, and that alone would allow a full-size purchase. The second rule is the Core floor. It measures asymmetry, which means how much more we can gain than lose. It demands that the central target be at least twice as far above the entry as the stop is below it. At $538.40 that ratio is 0.64 to 1, so the second rule fails. The ladder measures distance to a support level. The floor asks whether that level is a good place to risk money. Here it is not, because the structural stop sits far below.

A limit order is an order to buy only at a price you set or lower, and the entry zone is the range where we would accept a fill. We set the zone from the arithmetic: the central target is $595 and the stop is $449.37, so a 2.0 to 1 ratio needs an entry of $497.91 or lower ((595 + 2 × 449.37) / 3). We round down and use $497.00 as the top. The bottom is $481.00, about half an average true range above the 20-day swing low of $470.25. The top of the zone sits at the 200-day average of $496.83, and most of the zone lies below it, which the checklist below addresses directly. At $497.00 the ratio is 2.06 to 1, and at $481.00 it is 3.60 to 1.

The stop loss is the exit price if the idea is wrong. For a Core Alpha stock in a Stage 2 advance our rule is the lower of the 20-day swing low ($470.25) and the 50-day average ($528.90), minus one average true range ($20.88), which is $449.37. That is 16.5% below the close and 9.6% below the top of the zone. We also tested a tighter stop one average true range below the 50-day average, at about $508 ($528.90 minus $20.88). It gives only 1.86 to 1 at the close, and about $510 would be needed for 2.0 to 1. That stop would be 1.45 average true ranges, or 5.6%, below the close and below no verified low, because the 20-day swing low is $470.25 and the 63-day base low is $462.00. Our rule rejects it for a practical reason: a pullback of about 8% (7.7%) to the 200-day average, a level we list as support, would pass through it at 5.6% on the way, so we would be stopped out at the place where we propose to buy. A stop that tight improves the ratio by accepting a high chance of a shake-out, not by improving the odds.

The central target of $595 is 29 times our 2028 EPS of $20.53 (€18.33 at $1.1199 per euro). It is tied to a judgement multiple, so we show how the answer moves. At 31.5 times, today’s 2027 multiple, the target is $647 and the ratio at the close is 1.22 to 1. At the street’s $634.57 average it is 1.08 to 1. Our cash-flow value rolled forward is $729, which gives 2.14 to 1, but that path needs the whole plan. At the probability-weighted $587 the ratio at the close is 0.54 to 1 and at $497.00 it is 1.88 to 1. The buy range clears 2.0 on the central target and on every anchor higher than ours, and it narrowly misses on the weighted value. The close clears 2.0 on only one anchor, the full-plan DCF.

The zone is a place to start checking, not an order to leave on the book. We do not rest a standing buy order. When Spotify trades at or below $497.00 we run a same-day check and place a day order only if all of these pass: the price is inside $481.00 to $497.00; the 50-day average has not turned down and the stock is not in Stage 4, which means the close is not below a falling 50-day average that is itself below a falling 200-day average; support, average true range, stop and size are recomputed from that day’s data; the central target still gives at least 2.0 to 1 at the intended fill; no earnings report or major legal ruling falls within five trading days; and no new filing has changed the facts. If any item fails, we cancel and rerun the analysis.

The zone and the 200-day average need an explicit rule. The 200-day average is $496.83 and has fallen from $524.73 over 63 sessions, so a fill from $481.00 to $496.82 is a fill below a falling 200-day average. Our red line is Stage 4, which needs a falling 50-day average as well, so such a fill is allowed. It is still a weaker chart position than a fill above the average, and we accept it only because the price is what makes the ratio 2.0 to 1. The condition is that the 50-day average is flat or rising on the day, and the plan is suspended if it turns down (see the invalidation trigger). The 3.60 to 1 at $481.00 is therefore an upper bound, not an expected fill. If instead the stock reclaims and holds the 200-day average while it flattens, the zone does not move, because the ratio, not the chart, caps it at $497.00; we would simply keep waiting for the price.

The zone is built from our central case, so we tested it on the downside. At 25 times our central EPS, the bear multiple, the target is $513 and the ratio at $497.00 is 0.34 to 1; a 2.0 to 1 entry would need about $471 ((513 + 2 × 449.37) / 3). If operating expenses grow 15% a year instead of 8%, 2028 EPS is about €16.15 ($18.09), the target at 29 times is about $524.5, the ratio at $497.00 is 0.58 to 1, and the 2.0 to 1 entry is about $474. Either case moves the entry to the 20-day swing low of $470.25 or just above it. Our decision rule is this. If the fourth-quarter report lowers our central target to $513 to $525, the zone moves down to about $471 to $474 and we keep waiting. If it lowers the target below about $512, the 2.0 to 1 entry falls below the swing low, which is a broken-structure price, and we drop the idea at that rerun instead of lowering the zone further. Weighted for a bear-tilted 15% / 50% / 35% view, the value is $546 and the ratio at $497.00 is about 1.0 to 1, which is why we size small.

The earnings-date rule is that we place no new order within five trading days before a company report. Spotify has not announced its fourth-quarter date. It reported on February 4, 2025 and on February 10, 2026, so we expect early February 2027 and treat the window from late January as closed.[12][7] The whole analysis is rerun after the report.

Sizing works in two steps. Portfolio heat is the share of total net worth we would lose if the stop is hit. Risk-unit sizing sets the weight as the lower of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. We assign TIER_3, which allows 2% to 3.5% of net worth and a risk budget of 50 basis points (a basis point is one hundredth of a percent), because two Layer 2 measures are on watch, estimates are falling, and the founders control the vote. At a 9.58% stop distance the formula gives 5.22%, so the 3.5% cap binds. On net worth of $88,229.73 that is about $3,088, or 6 shares at $497.00 (3.38% of net worth). If the stop were hit the loss would be about $286, or 32 basis points. A stop does not cap a gap loss: if the stock opened at $400 after bad news, the loss on 6 shares would be about $296 more than the stop loss. A gap down to our $376 bear price would lose 24.3% from $497.00, or about $726 on 6 shares, which is 82 basis points of net worth against the 50 basis point budget. The ratio of 2.06 to 1 assumes the stop fills at $449.37, and it does not allow for a gap.

Three incumbents, COF, BLK and VST, also pass 3 of 5 measures, as Spotify does (3 PASS, 2 WATCH, 0 FAIL, a weighted 4.0 of 5.0). The second test does: the most extended of those three is VST, at 1.26 average true ranges above its support, which is the reduced-size limit-order band, while Spotify is 0.45 average true ranges above its 50-day average, which is the full-size band. The book stays at 10 autowritten names. This is a narrow win and we state its limits. On the later tests Spotify is not ahead: its reward-to-risk is 0.64 to 1 at the close and 2.06 to 1 at the top of the buy range, the next dated catalyst, the fourth-quarter report, is about 99 to 105 calendar days away, and the central gain from the close is 10.5%.
