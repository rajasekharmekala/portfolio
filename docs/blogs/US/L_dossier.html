Loews (NYSE: L) is a holding company that owns 92 percent of the insurer CNA, the Boardwalk gas pipelines, Loews Hotels and part of a packaging company.[4] The verdict is REJECTED: at $111.69 the stock trades at 1.19 times book value, and the reward for the risk is 0.39 to 1 at the close and 0.93 to 1 at the top of the limit zone.[1][33] The parts are worth about $124 a share, but the return on equity is 8.9 percent against a 12 percent floor, and CNA's profit is falling.[1][3] We would reopen the work if the stock falls to $100, or if the November 2 report shows CNA's margins recovering and the Boardwalk lawsuit stays contained.[14] It is wrong if the court awards much more than $1.2 billion, or if CNA's reserves prove too low[6][7].

1. What the company does

Loews is a holding company, which means its main job is to own other companies. It has four main holdings. CNA Financial is a commercial property and casualty insurer, and Loews owns about 92 percent of it. Boardwalk Pipelines moves and stores natural gas and is wholly owned. Loews Hotels runs and part-owns hotels and is wholly owned. Altium Packaging, which makes rigid plastic packaging, is 53 percent owned and is not consolidated.[4][3] CNA alone was 81.2 percent of 2025 revenue, so Loews behaves much like an insurer with a pipeline and a hotel company attached.[4]

The parent company earns money in two ways. It receives cash from its subsidiaries, and it invests its own cash. In the first half of 2026 it received $885 million from them: $735 million from CNA, including a special payment of $497 million, and $150 million from Boardwalk.[3] At June 30 it held $4.4 billion of cash and investments against $1.8 billion of debt, so it has net cash of $2.6 billion (our arithmetic).[1] Loews says it is not responsible for the debts of its subsidiaries and gives no guarantees for them.[3]

CNA is where the profit comes from. A commercial insurer collects premiums from businesses, pays claims later, and invests the money in between. That pool of held money is called float, and it makes investment income a large part of the result. In the first half of 2026 CNA's property and casualty operations made an underwriting gain of only $33 million, while their investment income was $836 million.[3] Underwriting is judged by the combined ratio, which is claims plus expenses divided by premiums earned. Below 100 percent the insurer makes money on the policies themselves.[1]

Boardwalk owns a gas pipeline network, and most of its revenue is a fixed fee for reserved capacity under firm contracts, so it does not swing with the gas price.[3] Boardwalk's profit measure is EBITDA, which stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It was $1,174 million in 2025.[5] Loews Hotels earns fees and room revenue from its own hotels and owns half of eleven Universal Orlando hotels with 11,000 rooms.[6] Altium Packaging only appears as an equity-method loss at the parent, which was $21 million in the first half of 2026 because resin costs rose before price increases took effect.[3]

For an owner, the useful way to read Loews is per share. The company has no meaningful dividend, only $0.25 a share a year, a yield of 0.22 percent.[27] It spends its spare cash on buying back stock instead. The share count fell from about 248 million at the end of 2021 to 206.0 million at the end of 2025, a drop of 17 percent (our arithmetic).[12][5] The case for owning Loews is that book value per share and the businesses grow while the share count shrinks.

How the pieces connect

Loews calls its model a conglomerate, and its CEO has summed up the goal in six words: grow the numerator, shrink the denominator. The numerator is the value of the businesses. The denominator is the share count.[10] In 2018 Boardwalk earned about $750 million of EBITDA, and in 2025 it earned $1,174 million. Loews Hotels grew adjusted EBITDA from about $220 million in 2018 to $372 million in 2025.[10][5] Since the start of 2018 the company has spent about $6.3 billion retiring about 120 million shares.[10]

The structure has a cost. A reader cannot buy CNA's earnings directly at a clean price. The stock trades at 1.19 times book value, and CNA itself trades at 1.18 times its own book value, so the market is not paying extra for the pipeline or the hotels (our arithmetic: $111.69 over $93.52 and $48.87 over $41.34).[33][1][29][17] Chapter 3 tests whether that is a bargain or a fair price.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Combined ratio is claims plus expenses divided by premiums earned. The underlying combined ratio removes catastrophe losses and changes in old reserves, so it shows the current-year margin.
  • Core income is CNA's net income without investment gains and losses and pension settlements. Core return on equity divides it by CNA's equity.
  • Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding. The version without AOCI (accumulated other comprehensive income) removes unrealized gains and losses on bonds, which move with interest rates.
  • Float is premium money an insurer holds between collecting it and paying claims.
  • A reserve is money set aside for claims not yet paid. Prior-year development is the gain or loss when an old reserve is revised.
  • An equity-method holding is counted as one line of profit or loss, not consolidated. Altium Packaging is one.
  • Sum of the parts is the value of each business added together, less parent costs and debts. A holding company discount is the gap when the stock trades below that sum.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

Loews is a sound, well-financed group with a return problem. It is sound because the parent holds net cash of $2.6 billion, consolidated debt is 30.8 percent of debt plus equity (our arithmetic: $8,936 million over $8,936 million plus $20,032 million), and CNA has an A+ rating from AM Best after an upgrade in December 2025.[1][3][6] The problem is that all this safety earns a modest return. Net income was $1,687 million over the last twelve months on average equity of $18.9 billion, a return of 8.9 percent (our arithmetic).[1][5][3] Over the last five years the return never reached 12 percent (Chapter 3).

The stock's price reflects that. The market pays 1.19 times book value for a group that earns about 9 percent on it. That is neither cheap nor dear. It is the multiple you would expect for this return, and the peer table in Chapter 3 shows the same link between return and multiple across seven companies.[1][33]

Where the position is strong

The first strength is Boardwalk's contract book. Boardwalk ended 2025 with a contracted revenue backlog of nearly $19.6 billion, up 38 percent in a year, with a weighted average contract life of about nine years.[6] Its net debt is about 2.5 times EBITDA at the subsidiary level, and in the second quarter it had no borrowings under its $1 billion revolver, with $984 million available.[6][3] It plans growth projects that add 4.5 billion cubic feet per day of capacity for about $3.4 billion, with in-service dates running through 2030. It had spent $381 million of that by June 30.[3] Those projects carry $9.4 billion of estimated revenue under signed agreements, though all of them depend on regulatory approval and construction going well.[3]

The second strength is the hotel recovery. Adjusted EBITDA rose 26 percent to $137 million in the second quarter, and occupancy was 81 percent against 78 percent a year earlier, with room rates up 7 percent.[1][2] In the first half, adjusted EBITDA was $261 million against $190 million, a gain of 37 percent (our arithmetic).[1] Three new Orlando hotels with 2,000 rooms opened in 2025 alongside the opening of Universal's Epic Universe park, and the renovation at the Miami Beach hotel is substantially complete.[6][1]

The third strength is how the parent treats its owners. Its leaders own a large part of the stock: all directors and officers together hold 19.0 percent, and James Tisch alone holds 7.8 percent.[13] The CEO, Ben Tisch, set out the buyback rule in February 2025: use corporate cash to buy shares when the stock trades at a large discount to intrinsic value and no better project exists.[10] The 2025 record fits that rule: 8.9 million shares for $782 million, an average of $87.72, about 4 percent of the shares outstanding.[6]

Where the position is thin

CNA is the thin spot. Its core income, which is profit without investment gains and losses, fell to $324 million in the second quarter from $335 million and to $549 million in the first half from $616 million, a drop of 10.9 percent (our arithmetic).[1] Its property and casualty combined ratio was 96.5 percent in the second quarter against 94.1 percent, and 99.4 percent in the first half against 96.3 percent. The underlying ratio, which removes catastrophes and old reserve changes, rose to 94.2 percent from 91.7 percent.[1] The cause is loss cost inflation outrunning rate increases in casualty lines. Renewal premiums rose only 2 percent in the second quarter, and the CEO said insurance is the industry on the shakiest footing of all those Loews operates in.[2]

Management is also cautious about growth. In the first quarter it said it would not expect growth to reaccelerate meaningfully in the near term.[7] New business grew 11 percent in the second quarter and net written premiums grew 4 percent, so the company is still writing, but with higher loss assumptions.[2][17] Premium growth that slows while loss costs climb is the usual early pattern of a soft insurance market.

A second thin spot is how much of the profit is investment income, which is not a business edge. CNA's net investment income rose to $701 million in the second quarter from $662 million, and limited partnerships and common stocks returned 4.3 percent in the quarter.[3] Those returns depend on markets. A fall in stocks or private funds would hit CNA's earnings at the same moment as a weak underwriting year.

Reserves and the insurance cycle: how much is cushion, how much is risk?

Reserves are the amounts an insurer sets aside for claims it has not yet paid. If the estimates are too low, the insurer takes a charge later. CNA took one in the first quarter of 2026, a $106 million reserve charge tied to excess casualty and professional liability for recent accident years.[7] For the first half, unfavorable prior-year development in property and casualty was $94 million, against $57 million a year earlier.[3] In the second quarter a separate $77 million after-tax charge hit CNA's run-off book for legacy mass tort abuse claims, against $88 million a year earlier.[3]

Catastrophe losses were $148 million in the first half against $159 million, so weather did not cause the margin loss.[3] Management admitted in the first quarter that its loss assumptions were not conservative enough and that it had acted to strengthen them.[7] Our reading is that reserve risk is real but was addressed in 2026. It remains the first thing to check in the third-quarter report, because a second charge in a row would turn the story from adjustment to trend.

The Boardwalk lawsuit: a debt that is not on the balance sheet

In 2018 Loews bought out the public owners of Boardwalk at a price set by a formula in the partnership agreement, using an opinion from counsel. Former unit holders sued. In 2021 the Delaware Court of Chancery awarded them about $690 million plus pre-judgment interest of about $166 million. In December 2025 the Delaware Supreme Court, by a vote of 3 to 2, held that the general partner had breached the agreement. It sent the remaining claims against Loews, tortious interference and unjust enrichment, back to the lower court.[3][6] A law firm summary cites the court's January 20, 2026 decision in the same case, which confirmed the remand on the tortious interference claim.[32]

Briefing on the remanded claims was finished in June 2026 and a hearing was held in July 2026. The company has recorded no accrual and says it cannot estimate a loss.[3] The CEO has said the original award, if applied in the most penal way, would exceed $1.2 billion today, against the $1.5 billion Loews paid for the units in 2018.[6] That is his figure for the most severe reading, and he says it would be more than $1.2 billion, so it is a marker and not a cap. We found no ruling on the remand as of October 29, 2026 (research record boardwalk_chancery_remand_decision). Taking $1.2 billion as a marker for the severe case, it equals $5.9 per share (our arithmetic: $1,200 million over 204.4 million shares) and 46 percent of the parent's $2.6 billion of net cash. At $1.5 billion, the price Loews paid in 2018, the loss would be about $7.3 a share, 34 percent of the parent's $4.4 billion of gross cash and 58 percent of its net cash (our arithmetic). Either amount is large but payable, and the lawsuit is one reason management has slowed its buybacks.[7]

Capital allocation: buybacks first, but slower now

Buybacks are the main use of cash. Cash flow statements show $1,136 million in 2021, $729 million in 2022, $849 million in 2023, $608 million in 2024 and $806 million in 2025.[11][4] In the first half of 2026 the figure fell to $185 million from $651 million a year earlier.[3] The company bought 1.7 million shares for $177 million, an average of about $104 (our arithmetic).[3] The CEO gave the reasons in May: the stock has risen, the Boardwalk capital plan has grown to about $3.2 billion, cycles can turn, and the lawsuit is unresolved.[7] In February he had already said that activity slowed in the second half of 2025 as the stock reached new all-time highs.[6]

This is consistent with the stated rule, and a stock buyer should read it as a signal. When management says the stock is below intrinsic value yet buys less of it as the price rises, it is telling you that around $100 to $105 it saw value and at $112 it sees less. That fits our own sum of the parts, where the price is only 10 percent below the central value.

Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after spending on plant and equipment. For an insurer it is not owner cash, because premium float runs through it. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given in shares. Net debt is borrowings minus cash.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

Chapter 6 tests our own numbers. Each estimate has a weakest assumption. For the return on equity it is the averaging method, and the 12 percent floor is out of reach under every method we tried. For the sum of the parts it is Boardwalk's multiple, where each turn is $5.8 a share. For the 2027 estimate it is CNA's margin, because CNA is 63 percent of our earnings (our arithmetic: $1,100 million of $1,750 million) and one point of combined ratio is about $0.38 of earnings. For the expected value it is the probabilities, which are our judgment.

The red-team bear case is that the stock is not a bargain, and that the parts that look cheap carry the risks. The market pays $10.7 billion for everything except CNA, and Boardwalk is worth about that alone, but Boardwalk is also the unit with the lawsuit. If Loews loses and the parent has to keep more cash, the sum of the parts shrinks and buybacks stop. The three fatal-flaw scenarios follow.

Fatal flaw 1: the Boardwalk judgment is far larger than $1.2 billion

The Delaware Supreme Court has already found that the general partner breached the partnership agreement. What remains is whether Loews itself is liable for tortious interference and unjust enrichment, and for how much. The company has no accrual and calls the amount impossible to estimate.[3] The CEO's figure for the most severe reading is that the award would exceed $1.2 billion, against $1.5 billion paid in 2018, so $1.2 billion is a marker and not a limit.[6]

A loss of $1.2 billion is $5.9 a share and 27 percent of the parent's gross cash. A loss of $1.5 billion is about $7.3 a share, 34 percent of gross cash and 58 percent of net cash (our arithmetic). Either would not threaten solvency. It would stop buybacks, which are the main way the discount closes, and it could lead Loews to hold back Boardwalk's distributions. A ruling above $1.2 billion with added interest would also raise the question of further claims. We would know when the Chancery Court rules, and no date is published. We found no ruling by October 29, 2026.

Fatal flaw 2: CNA's casualty margin and reserves keep deteriorating

CNA writes excess casualty and workers' compensation, which are long-tail lines where claims are paid years later. Management says loss costs are outrunning rate. The first-quarter charge was $106 million, the second-quarter run-off charge $77 million, and the combined ratio rose 3.1 points in the half.[7][3][1] If the underlying ratio moved from 94.5 percent to 98 percent, 3.5 points would cost about $1.33 a share of annual earnings (our arithmetic: 3.5 times $0.38) and about $17 of price at 13 times.

CNA also runs a long-term care book in run-off. In the second quarter CNA swapped $1 billion of BBB bonds for longer A-rated bonds in that portfolio and extended its duration to 10 years.[2] That reduces credit risk, but it raises interest-rate sensitivity. The first test is the November 2 report. The next is the fourth-quarter reserve review in February 2027, which in 2025 produced an unfavorable charge on its asbestos and environmental reserves under a $4 billion loss portfolio transfer.[6]

Fatal flaw 3: rates, credit and concentration hit book value

CNA's fixed-maturity portfolio was $43.9 billion at June 30, with a net unrealized loss of $1.5 billion. About 36 percent of it is rated BBB ($15.9 billion), and $1.8 billion is below investment grade (our arithmetic: 15,918 over 43,882).[3] The accumulated other comprehensive loss of $1,175 million is $5.7 a share of book value (our arithmetic). Ten-year Treasury yields were 5.05 percent on October 28 in the ledger, so a further rise would widen the loss and cut book value, though only on paper unless CNA must sell.[33]

The hotel business is concentrated too. Eleven Universal Orlando hotels, in which Loews holds 50 percent, and two Arlington hotels drive most of the growth.[6] A weak Orlando season would cut a segment that is under 10 percent of first-half profit but is the part of the story that is improving fastest. We rate this flaw as the least likely to be fatal and the most likely to matter at the margin.

7. What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED, and the reason is a rule and not a feeling about the company. No archetype in scoring.json qualifies. The COMPOUNDER test needs a 12 percent return on equity, and Loews earns 8.9 percent. The asymmetry floor for a CORE_ALPHA idea is 2.0 to 1, and Loews gives 0.39 to 1 at the close and 0.93 to 1 at the top of the support zone, using a target of $115 and a stop of $103.18.[33]

The only coherent thesis is a multi-year per-share compounding case, which is a CORE_ALPHA idea with a 2.0 to 1 floor and a stop one ATR below the lower of the swing low and the 50-day average.

Layer 3 is the step that turns the chart into a buy zone, a stop and a position size. Risk-unit sizing sets the size from the loss at the stop: the portfolio is allowed to lose a fixed number of basis points (hundredths of a percent) if the stop is hit, and the weight is that budget divided by the stop distance, capped by the tier. The let-winners-run row applies only after a purchase, and we hold no position.[33]

The idea is not dead. Loews is a sound group with real assets, and the price is only 10 percent below our sum of the parts. The rules below say what would reopen it.
