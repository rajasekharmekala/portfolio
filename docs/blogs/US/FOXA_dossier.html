1. What the company does

Fox makes money three ways. Cable, satellite and streaming companies pay it a monthly fee for each home that carries its channels. Independent local stations pay it to carry FOX network shows. Advertisers pay it to run commercials on FOX News, the FOX network, 29 local stations and the free streaming service Tubi. A smaller stream comes from sublicensing sports rights and selling shows it produces. In fiscal 2026, the year ended June 30, 2026, revenue was $17.13 billion. About 47% was distribution revenue, which means fees paid by distributors and affiliate stations. About 43% was advertising, and about 10% was other income[1][2].

Fox's main profit measure is adjusted EBITDA, short for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. Two segments matter most. Cable Network Programming holds FOX News, FOX Business and the sports channels FS1, FS2, the Big Ten Network and FOX Deportes. It earned $3.10 billion on $7.35 billion of revenue in fiscal 2026[1]. Television holds the FOX network, Tubi and the owned stations. It earned $1.44 billion on $9.67 billion[1][2]. Corporate and Other holds the FOX One subscription service, the Credible consumer finance marketplace, the studio lot and head-office costs. It lost $631 million[1][2]. Cable produces about 79% of company-wide adjusted EBITDA (our calculation: $3,099 million divided by $3,906 million). One segment carries most of the profit.

Two features set Fox apart. First, it sells live events and live news. People still watch these as they happen, so advertisers keep paying while taped shows lose viewers. FOX holds NFL rights through the 2029 season, the MLB postseason with the World Series, NASCAR, Big Ten football and the 2026 FIFA Men's World Cup[2][6]. The World Cup ran across the last quarter of fiscal 2026 and the first quarter of fiscal 2027[7]. Second, results swing with a calendar of events. A Super Bowl, a World Cup or an election year can each move revenue by hundreds of millions of dollars. Fiscal 2026 had the World Cup but no Super Bowl and no presidential election. Revenue still grew 5%[1].

This report prices Class A shares, ticker FOXA, at $66.60 on October 23, 2026. Class B shares, ticker FOX, carry one vote per share on all matters. Class A holders vote only in limited cases set out in the charter[2]. The two classes trade at different prices. In the fourth quarter of fiscal 2026 Fox paid an average of $61.76 per Class A share and $55.48 per Class B share in its buyback, which means repurchases of its own shares[2]. Trusts for Lachlan, Grace and Chloe Murdoch hold about 38.8% of Class B through a vehicle called LGC Holdco. A September 2025 stockholders agreement caps the family's combined voting power at 44% of Class B votes[2][25].

The Roku purchase in one paragraph

On June 14, 2026 Fox signed an agreement to buy Roku, and the companies announced it on June 15. Roku runs a connected-television platform that reaches more than half of US broadband households[8][2]. The price is $160 per Roku share, or about $22 billion including Roku's net cash. Each Roku share receives $96.00 in cash and 0.9693 Fox Class A shares. Fox holders would own about 73% of the combined company[8]. Both sets of shareholders approved the deal on October 14, 2026. The Justice Department asked both companies for more documents in September. Both say they expect to close in the first half of 2027[13][12][14]. This one deal changes Fox's share count, debt and growth rate, so it shapes every chapter that follows.

Plain-English glossary for this report

  • Adjusted EBITDA is revenue minus operating costs and overhead, before depreciation, restructuring, interest, investment gains and taxes. Fox's own definition is in its earnings release[1].
  • Free cash flow (FCF) is cash from operating activities minus capital expenditures. Fox defines it the same way on its earnings calls[6].
  • AVOD means advertising-supported video on demand, a free streaming service paid for by commercials. Tubi is Fox's AVOD service.
  • MVPD means multichannel video programming distributor. It is a cable, satellite or live-streaming bundle that carries Fox channels for a per-home fee.
  • Cord-cutters and cord-nevers are viewers who dropped or never bought a pay-TV bundle. Fox says close to 70% of Tubi viewers are in this group[6].
  • Political advertising is spending by candidates and committees. It peaks in even-numbered years, and 2026 is a midterm election year.

2. Why the business is strong or weak

The strongest asset is FOX News. It was the top cable network in total-day and primetime viewing for the eleventh straight year in fiscal 2026. It has led weekday primetime cable news for more than 24 years[2]. Cable advertising revenue rose 10% in fiscal 2026 on higher news and sports pricing, even though ratings were lower[1]. The weak spot is the audience trend. Nielsen data show FOX News averaged 2.064 million primetime viewers in the third calendar quarter of 2026. That is down 17% from a year earlier, and September alone fell 32%[16][17]. It still ranked first in cable. FOX News reached 55 million homes through pay TV on June 30, 2026, against 61 million a year earlier[16][2].

The second asset is live sports. Fox said in August 2026 that it will not amend its NFL contract, which runs through the 2029 season. Talks about the years after that will come closer to 2030[6]. It also added two NFL game windows for the 2026 season[2]. Sports draw large live audiences, but they are costly. Cable adjusted EBITDA rose only 2% in fiscal 2026 on revenue growth of 6%. Higher sports rights and production costs, led by soccer and the World Cup, took most of the gain[1][2].

The third asset is pricing power with distributors. Distribution revenue grew 4% in fiscal 2026. Higher rates per subscriber and higher fees from independent FOX-affiliated stations added about $440 million. A smaller average subscriber count took away about $160 million[2]. Management said net subscriber declines at third-party distributors were under 6.5% in the fourth quarter, before counting FOX One. It also said fiscal 2027 brings a more normal year of contract renewals. Only about one quarter of distribution revenue came up for renewal in fiscal 2026[6][7].

The fourth asset is Tubi. Its total view time grew 20% in fiscal 2026. It averaged about 2.2% of all US television viewing and streamed more than 13 billion hours[2]. Fourth-quarter revenue grew 35%, helped by a 17% rise in view time. Fox says Tubi earned a positive EBITDA in each quarter of fiscal 2026[6]. Fox reported Tubi revenue of more than $1.1 billion for fiscal 2025[7]. It did not publish Tubi's fiscal 2026 revenue in dollars in the documents we read, so we do not state a run-rate.

Where the business is weak

Four weaknesses matter to an owner. The first is concentration. Cable provides about 79% of adjusted EBITDA, and the FOX News audience is falling. The second is event dependence. Operating cash flow dropped 41% to $1.97 billion in fiscal 2026, from $3.32 billion. Advertising receipts were lower without the Super Bowl and the 2024 election, and sports rights payments were higher. Fox expects to collect World Cup advertising receivables early in fiscal 2027[2][6]. The third is the cost of new businesses. The Corporate and Other loss widened from $351 million to $631 million as FOX One launched[1][2]. The fourth is governance and legal risk. Class A holders have almost no vote, and Smartmatic's $2.7 billion defamation suit is still open[2].

What this means for an owner: the franchise is durable and profitable. You are also buying a business whose profit depends on one news channel and a calendar of sporting events. That is why Chapter 3 tests cash conversion, not just profit. It is also why Chapter 7 declines to buy at any price until the Roku deal is built into the valuation.

3. The numbers, in plain words

Chapter 3 turns the business into numbers. Revenue has grown 4.7% a year over the last three fiscal years, from $14.91 billion to $17.13 billion. Net income attributable to stockholders grew 10.8% a year, from $1.24 billion to $1.69 billion (our calculations)[1][3]. The profit growth looks better partly because fiscal 2023 profit was cut by $1.18 billion of restructuring and impairment charges[3]. Over the same years adjusted EBITDA rose from $3.19 billion to $3.91 billion.

Fiscal 2026 net income fell 26% to $1.69 billion, or $3.84 per share, from $4.91. Investments lost value. Non-operating other, net was a $773 million loss[1]. The company's adjusted EPS (earnings per share) removes that and restructuring items. It rose 13% (our calculation) to $5.42 from $4.78[1]. The choice of EPS changes the price-to-earnings ratio, which is the share price divided by earnings per share. At $66.60 it is 17.3 times reported EPS and 12.3 times adjusted EPS (our calculations). We use adjusted EPS because the Flutter swings are marks on an investment, not operating results.

Fox does not publish a gross profit line. Subtracting operating costs from revenue leaves 36.6% of revenue in fiscal 2026 (our proxy), up from 34.8% in fiscal 2022. Cash flow from operations is the cash the business collects from its core operations. It was $1.97 billion in fiscal 2026 and $3.32 billion in fiscal 2025[1]. Free cash flow after capital expenditure, the money spent on equipment and technology, was $1.47 billion and $2.99 billion. The gap is working capital, the cash tied up in receivables and rights payments. Receivables rose $1.06 billion in fiscal 2026 as World Cup advertising was billed. Inventories net of programming payable absorbed $493 million[1].

Fox's own return numbers are good. Our ROIC (return on invested capital) is 17.9% for fiscal 2026. That is after-tax operating income of $2,542 million on $14,215 million of equity, borrowings and redeemable minority interest, less cash, at the company's 24% tax rate. It is 13.8% if cash is left in invested capital[1][2]. Stock-based compensation is $132 million, or 0.77% of revenue, so shareholders are not paying much in dilution[1]. The weak static number is free cash flow margin, which we cover in the estimate below.

The number

We treat Fox's normal free cash flow margin as about 11% to 13% of revenue. That is not the 8.6% reported for fiscal 2026, and not the 18.4% of fiscal 2025.

We averaged free cash flow over two fiscal years. The Super Bowl and election working-capital gain of fiscal 2025 then offsets the World Cup drag of fiscal 2026. The math is ($2,993M + $1,468M) / ($16,300M + $17,126M) = 13.3%. The five-year figure is 11.8%.
