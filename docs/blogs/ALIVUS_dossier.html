Terms used in this report

This dossier is a worked record of one underwriting decision, written so that a reader who has never met a moving average can follow each step and check it. The verdict reads in a few minutes; the full arithmetic and sources take longer. Every technical or financial term is defined here once and then used the same way throughout.

The underwriting method has three layers, applied in order. Layer 1 is a set of hard floors. A company that fails any floor is rejected and the later layers are not run. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five operating measures, each graded PASS, WATCH or FAIL. Layer 3 is an entry-timing check that measures how far the price sits above the nearest believable support, in units of ATR (average true range), the usual daily price swing over fourteen sessions.

The financial terms come first. ROCE (return on capital employed) measures the operating profit a business earns on the money tied up in it. A figure of 15 percent is the Indian floor and 18 percent is preferred for Core names. Debt-to-equity compares borrowed money with owners' money, and the floor is below 0.70. CFO to PAT, the cash conversion ratio, compares cash from operations with reported profit over three years; a ratio of 0.70 or more means the profit is backed by real cash. ROE (return on equity) is profit as a share of shareholders' funds, and OPM (operating profit margin) is the share of revenue left after the direct costs of making the product.

The technical terms describe price behaviour. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, like a trend line drawn through daily noise. The 50-day and 200-day averages are the two most watched. Weinstein staging divides a stock's life into four phases: Stage 1 is a flat base, Stage 2 is the markup, Stage 3 is the topping phase where the trend loses momentum, and Stage 4 is the decline. RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer for momentum on a 0 to 100 scale, and a reading above 70 means the stock has run ahead of its own trend.

The momentum gate is a code test that decides whether a stock can take new money at all. Test A asks whether the stock is already in an uptrend and leading its benchmark. Test B asks whether it is entering an uptrend from a tight base, with volume quiet before a breakout. A stock that fails both has no allocation. The asymmetry ratio is the potential gain to a defensible target divided by the potential loss to the stop. The target must be anchored, either to forward earnings times a historical median price-to-earnings ratio (the share price divided by earnings per share), or to a measured move from a base. An arbitrary target is not allowed.

The reader needs three words most of all: stop, target and anchor. The stop is the price at which the position is exited. The target is the price at which the gain is taken. An anchor is the evidence that makes the target more than a guess. Without an anchor the asymmetry ratio has no meaning, which is why the report treats an unanchored target as a failure rather than as a weak pass.

What the company does and how it earns

In the same way, an API maker's sales follow the volumes its drugmaker customers sell, and its margin depends on the price it can charge for an ingredient that is hard to replace. The company also offers contract development and manufacturing, which means it makes products for other companies to a specification.

The company's history explains the shape of its revenue. It was an integral part of Glenmark Pharmaceuticals until a business transfer in January 2019 separated the API operations, and it was then listed on the exchanges in FY22. The investor material describes two streams: business with the former parent, called GPL in company language, and business with other customers, called non-GPL. Layer 1 gates: the financial and technical floors

Layer 1 is the set of floors a candidate must clear before anything else is considered. The financial floors ask whether the company earns a good return on the capital it uses, whether it avoids heavy borrowing, and whether its profits arrive as cash. The technical floor asks whether the chart is in a state where a new buyer is joining an established trend rather than arriving after it has finished. A failure in any row ends the analysis, and the report names the failure as the gate status.

The first test is return on capital. ROCE divides operating profit by the capital the business uses, so a figure of 23.8 percent means the company earns about 24 rupees of operating profit for every 100 rupees of capital in the business. The Indian floor is 15 percent and the preferred level for a Core name is above 18 percent. Screener's trailing figure clears both. [1] The return on equity of 17.9 percent on the same page tells the same story from the shareholders' side, and the annual figure of 16.94 percent for FY26 matches it. [3]

The second test is borrowing. The floor is a debt-to-equity ratio below 0.70. The FY26 balance sheet shows borrowings of ₹57 crore against shareholders' funds of ₹3,332.1 crore. [1] [3] Dividing the first by the second gives 0.017, which rounds to 0.02. The rule excludes lenders because borrowing is their raw material, and Alivus is a manufacturer, so the rule applies in full. The company passes it by a wide margin.

The third test is cash conversion, and it is the one a reader should check most carefully. The floor is cumulative cash from operations over three years divided by cumulative net profit over the same years, at least 0.70. Cash from operations was ₹413.5 crore in FY24, ₹391.5 crore in FY25 and ₹565.4 crore in FY26, a total of ₹1,370.4 crore. Net profit was ₹470.9 crore, ₹485.6 crore and ₹564.5 crore, a total of ₹1,521.0 crore. The ratio is 1,370.4 divided by 1,521.0, which is 0.90. [3] Every year clears the floor on its own: 0.88 in FY24, 0.81 in FY25 and 1.00 in FY26. FY24 and FY25 together give 0.84, so the pass does not depend on FY26. FY25 is the thinnest year, 0.11 above the floor.

The technical floor fails, and this is the row that decides the verdict. A Core candidate must be in a Stage 1 base breakout or a Stage 2 markup. A Tactical candidate must be in a Stage 2 markup above rising 50-day and 200-day averages. The ledger puts Alivus in Stage 3. The close of ₹1,344.40 is below the 50-day average of ₹1,365.31, which is 0.44 ATR below it, and the 20-day average of ₹1,370.44 is also above the price. The 200-day average of ₹1,096.02 is far below the price and rising, so the long-term trend is up, but the medium-term structure that a Stage 2 reading needs is not in place.

The financial floors pass with room, and that is why Alivus is worth a re-run if the triggers below occur. A return on capital in the low twenties with almost no borrowing is a business that does not need outside money to grow. The cash-conversion test has the least headroom of the financial floors: the three-year figure of 0.90 is 0.20 above the floor, and FY25 alone was 0.81, which shows that cash can lag profit in a single year when working capital, the cash tied up in stock and customer bills, moves. Technical posture and the momentum gate

The momentum gate is the code-computed test that decides whether a candidate can receive any allocation. It is separate from the three layers because it asks a narrower question: is this stock behaving like one that can be bought now? It has two routes. Test A is for names already in an uptrend and requires the price above a rising 50-day average, a 200-day average that is not falling, relative strength over 63 sessions at or above the benchmark, and a price no more than 1.2 ATR from support. Test B is for names entering an uptrend and requires the 50-day average not to fall more than half a percent over 21 sessions, relative strength rising over 28 sessions, the price within one ATR of the base pivot, which in this report is the 63-session base low of ₹1,086.10 from the ledger, with volume quiet during the base and volume expanding later. A stock that passes either route qualifies. A stock that fails both does not.

Alivus fails both routes. In Test A the structure condition fails because the close of ₹1,344.40 is below the 50-day average of ₹1,365.31, even though the 50-day average has risen from ₹1,267.43 over 21 sessions. The 200-day average is rising, from ₹970.45 over 63 sessions, so that condition passes. Relative strength over 63 sessions is 22.05 percent above the benchmark, which passes comfortably. The support-distance condition fails on the same measure: the close is 5.46 ATR above the base pivot of ₹1,086.10, against a maximum of 1.2. The support-distance condition is the largest gap in the whole gate, and it is the reason the stock is not a buy even if the trend were confirmed.

Test B fails on two of five conditions. The 50-day average rose 7.72 percent over 21 sessions, so the no-decline condition passes. Relative strength over 28 sessions is 1.28 percent above the benchmark and rising, which passes. The base is quiet, with volume at 0.15 of its 63-session level, against a limit of 0.9, which also passes. The close is 5.46 ATR above the base pivot of ₹1,086.10, against a limit of one ATR, and volume over 20 sessions is 0.62 of its average, against a required 1.0. Those are the confirmation conditions, and they are the ones a breakout would have to meet.

Asymmetry and target anchoring

Asymmetry is the ratio that tells a buyer whether the risk is worth taking. It divides the potential gain to a defensible target by the potential loss to the stop. A ratio of 3.0 to 1 means the buyer can lose one unit if the stop is hit and expects to gain three units if the target is reached. The house rule is at least 3.0 to 1 for Core names and at least 3.5 to 1 for Tactical names. The formula is the target minus the price, divided by the price minus the stop.

The rule matters only if the target is honest. The house method requires the target to be anchored either to forward earnings times a historical median price-to-earnings ratio, or to a measured move from a base. An arbitrary target, however optimistic, is not allowed. If the target cannot be verified, the gate fails on asymmetry. Alivus has forward earnings in the sources: the consensus of four analysts for FY27 is ₹55.07 per share, up from ₹47.56 in FY26. [9] What is missing is the three-year median price-to-earnings ratio. The searches for it returned a current multiple, a forward multiple and an estimate, but no three-year median, and one historical page turned out to describe a different company. [29] [30] Without that multiple the house target cannot be built, and the gate fails.

The stop comes first, because it sets the denominator. For Core names the stop sits below the lowest of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day and 200-day averages, minus one ATR. The lowest of those levels for Alivus is the 200-day average of ₹1,096.02, so the stop is ₹1,096.02 minus ₹47.31, which is ₹1,048.71. The downside to the stop is ₹1,344.40 minus ₹1,048.71, or ₹295.69 a share, about 22 percent of the price. For Tactical names the stop sits below the lowest of the swing low and the 20-day and 50-day averages, minus half an ATR. The lowest is the swing low of ₹1,298.00, so the Tactical stop is ₹1,274.35, and the downside is ₹70.05 a share, about 5.2 percent of the price.
