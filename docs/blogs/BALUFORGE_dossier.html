How the framework works

This chapter sets out the method before the company is judged by it. A reader who knows the terms can skip it; a newcomer should read it first.

The method has three layers, applied in order. Layer 1 is a set of hard gates. A company must pass every gate to be a candidate, and a failed gate stops the test there. The gates are return on capital employed (ROCE: the profit a business earns on the money tied up in it, as a percentage), debt-to-equity (how much the company owes compared with what its owners have put in), three-year cash conversion (the cash the business actually collected from operations, divided by its reported profit, summed over three years), a technical posture gate (whether the price trend is healthy), and an asymmetry gate (what you can win compared with what you can lose). Layer 2 scores three to five operating measures that matter for this kind of business. Layer 3 checks where the price sits relative to support, which decides how close to a buy the stock is. Here Layer 1 fails, so the Layer 2 and Layer 3 readings are shown for information and do not authorise any purchase.

A few ideas recur. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of sessions, which works like a smoothed trend line. The 20-session average is short term, the 50-session average is medium term, and the 200-session average is the long-term line most institutions watch. A Weinstein stage is a way of naming where a stock sits in its cycle. Stage 1 is a base, where the stock moves sideways after a fall. Stage 2 is a rising markup. Stage 3 is a top, and Stage 4 is a decline below a falling long-term average. ATR, the average true range, is the typical daily swing in rupees, and it is the yardstick for distances: one ATR is roughly one normal day's movement. RSI, the relative strength index, is a speed gauge from 0 to 100. Readings below 30 mean the stock has fallen fast, and readings above 70 mean it has risen fast.

A few more terms come up later. A breakout is a close above the top of a base, the sideways range where the stock has been building. A pivot is the price level a base must clear to confirm a new uptrend. A retest is a return to a broken level to see whether it now holds as support. A reward-to-risk ratio is what you can gain divided by what you can lose. EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided by its number of shares, and a price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by EPS. A measured move is a target set by adding the height of a base to its breakout level. Dilution is the loss of each holder's share of the company when new shares or convertible bonds are issued. Capital expenditure is money spent on plant and machinery, and working capital is the cash tied up in receivables and stock, net of what the company owes its suppliers.

Stops and sizing are the protective rules. A stop is a price at which the holding is sold without debate, decided in advance. For a Tactical holding the stop sits below the lower of the recent swing low and the 20- or 50-session average, minus half an ATR. The verdict labels follow the holding rules. KEEP_COMPOUNDING means every gate passes and the trend holds. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP means a strong business is pulling back to support and the allocation has room. TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING means the stock is stretched or has reached a target. REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER means support has broken, the operating tests have failed, or the financial thesis is impaired. Each verdict names its rule and the level that would change it.

What the company does

This chapter explains the business before the gates, because a gate is only meaningful once you know what the company sells and to whom. A forging company heats metal and presses it into shape, then machines it to tight tolerances. Its customers are other manufacturers, so its sales follow their order books. Balu Forge describes itself as a crankshaft maker that has diversified into precision forgings and machined parts [3]. It was founded in 1989 in Belgaum, Karnataka [3], and says it supplies more than 25 original equipment manufacturers in over 80 countries [2].

The plants and the capacity

Capacity is the ceiling on how much the company can sell. The investor presentation of June 2026 gives forging capacity of 100,000 tonnes a year (MTPA), with plans to expand it in phases to 150,000 tonnes, and machining capacity of 45,000 tonnes, to be raised to 80,000 tonnes [6][11]. The company's website states the expanded figures, 150,000 and 80,000 tonnes, without the word planned [2]. We read the presentation as the current position and the website as the target, because the presentation is the document the company made for investors on a stated date. What follows for the reader is that capacity is still being built. Management expects the gross block, the value of plant and equipment before depreciation, to reach ₹750 to 800 crore by FY27 [11]. The central tension is timing. Capacity spending runs ahead of the cash it will eventually produce, and the company also collects from customers slowly while paying suppliers faster than before. Capital spending does not itself lower operating cash flow, because the cash gate measures cash from operations. The gap comes mainly from receivables and payables, which the next chapter sets out.

The defence shift and the order mix

Defence, aerospace and railways are the new growth area. The company says these sectors rose from 5 percent of revenue in FY24 to 13 percent in FY26, and that they now make up about half of the order book [6]. The June 2026 presentation reports an order for 30,000 units of 152 millimetre artillery shells, with supplies starting in June 2026, and a five-year memorandum of understanding for large-calibre ammunition shells [6]. A separate February 2026 release describes a five-year memorandum for supplying empty shells from the Belgaum campus [17]. Defence work tends to carry higher margins and longer contracts than automotive work, which is why the company's future mix matters. It also tends to pay slowly, because government and state-linked buyers run long approval and payment cycles. That second point is the reason the receivables figures in Layer 2 matter.

Ownership, people and governance

Ownership shows who controls the company and whether they are adding or reducing their stake. Promoters held 53.51 percent of the shares at June 2026, down from 57.47 percent in September 2023 [1]. Shareholders approved a final dividend of 1.5 percent of face value at the 37th annual general meeting [19], a small payout that, in our reading, leaves most of the cash inside the business for growth.

Governance has had recent changes that a holder should track. The chief financial officer, Amit Ashok Todkari, intimated his resignation from that role on 25 May 2026. The company is not in trouble because of that item alone, but it is the kind of transaction that a careful holder checks against the cash flow statement.

What would change this view:

Three-year cash conversion: the gate that fails

Cash conversion is the ratio that decides this dossier. It is the cash the business collected from operations over three years, divided by the profit it reported over the same three years. A ratio of 1.0 means every rupee of profit arrived as cash. A ratio of 0.27 means that for every rupee of profit reported, about 27 paise arrived as cash from operations. The floor of 0.70 exists because reported profit can be inflated by receivables that never turn into cash, by revenue recognised before it is paid, and by working-capital build-up. A business that fails this test needs a reason, and the reason has to be visible in the accounts.

The calculation on the consolidated basis uses the figures in the audited statements. Consolidated operating cash flow was a negative ₹31.7 crore in FY24 on the restated basis in the FY25 report, ₹148.0 crore in FY25 and ₹31.7 crore in FY26 [9] [8]. The FY24 figure was first reported as a negative ₹18.0 crore [10], and the company restated it later. Profit after tax was ₹93.7 crore in FY24, ₹203.9 crore in FY25 and ₹258.9 crore in FY26 [10] [8]. The three-year total of cash is ₹147.97 crore and the total of profit is ₹556.42 crore, which gives 0.27. Using the first-reported FY24 cash figure gives 0.29, which still fails. The standalone company, which excludes the subsidiaries, gives 0.39, which also fails. So the answer does not depend on which basis is chosen. The consolidated basis is the one the framework uses, because the subsidiaries are part of the business that earns the profit.

Our shortlist file shows a cash conversion of 0.82. We could not reproduce that figure on any basis the audited statements support, so it is not used. Our shortlist file is an internal working file and not a source for this dossier, but a reader comparing the two should know which number the audited accounts support.

Why is the cash so low when the profit is high? The answer is in the working capital lines, which means cash tied up in receivables and stock, net of what the company owes its suppliers. Trade receivables rose to ₹425.1 crore at March 2026 from ₹327.3 crore a year earlier, and inventories rose to ₹145.3 crore from ₹98.1 crore [6]. Trade payables fell to ₹63.1 crore from ₹118.0 crore [6]. The company is paying suppliers faster and collecting from customers more slowly. Capital spending adds a separate pressure. The FY26 consolidated statements show ₹207.0 crore of plant and equipment purchases, against ₹415.4 crore the year before, and they were paid for with ₹246.8 crore of share issue proceeds and a ₹96.1 crore net rise in short-term borrowing [8]. The consequence is that growth has been paid for partly with new equity, which is why the share count rose from 11.08 crore to 12.14 crore shares [8], and partly with borrowing. Operating cash did not pay for it.

The cash row is the one the verdict rests on. The business passes the return and debt tests, and it is the cash test that fails on both bases. A stock can have excellent returns and still be a poor holding if the profit is not converting to cash, because a business that does not collect its money cannot repay its debts, fund its growth or pay dividends from operations. The number to watch next is the three-year cash conversion in the FY27 annual report, which covers FY25 to FY27. For the gate to pass, that window must add up to at least 0.70 of its profit. The FY27 test window runs from FY25 to FY27. FY25 (₹148.0 crore of operating cash) and FY26 (₹31.7 crore) are already inside it, so FY27 must supply the rest: 0.70 times the window's profit, less the ₹179.7 crore already banked. As an illustration only, holding FY27 profit flat at FY26's ₹258.9 crore gives a window profit of about ₹721.6 crore, a need of about ₹505 crore of cash, and an FY27 requirement of about ₹325 crore of operating cash. Layer 3: support and entry posture

Layer 3 is the timing gate. Even a good business can be a poor purchase if the price has run far from its support, because the risk is then large compared with the nearest level where buyers have stepped in. The framework measures the distance from the nearest verified support in units of ATR, and maps that distance onto a fixed ladder of bands. Inside half an ATR is the full-size band. Inside one ATR is the standard band. Inside 1.2 ATR is a limit-order band with reduced size. Beyond 1.2 ATR is the no-buy band, where the rule says to wait for a pullback, a retest or a new base.

Measuring the distance from support

The ATR is ₹22.86 (market ledger), so a normal day's swing for this stock is about 4.8 percent of the price. The support measure depends on which level is chosen. The support that the framework's code uses is the low of the 63-session base, at ₹422.60. The close of ₹481.20 is 2.56 ATR above that level, which places the stock in the no-buy band (market ledger). The nearest recent swing low, at ₹470.00 over the last twenty sessions, is only 0.49 ATR away and would sit in the full-size band. We do not use that reading to justify a buy, for two reasons. The swing low is a short-term level that the stock has already tested once, and the stock is below both its 50- and 200-session averages, so the trend tests fail regardless. The posture is therefore EXTENDED in the framework's sense, which means far above the base, and it is the no-buy band.

The word "extended" needs a warning. In everyday speech it suggests a stock that has run up too far. Here the stock has fallen 11.2 percent over the last month and sits 29 percent below its 52-week high of ₹678.00, all from the market ledger. It is extended only in the narrow sense that it is far above the base low that the code uses as support. The practical meaning is the same: the framework does not permit a buy at this distance from support, and the holder should not read the label as a sign of strength.

The momentum gate, computed in code

The momentum gate is a separate test that the framework's code check applies to every name before any capital is allocated. It has two halves. Test A asks whether the stock is in a confirmed uptrend now. Test B asks whether it is entering one at a base pivot with volume confirmation. A name that fails both halves is not allocated, however good its business [1]. Balu Forge fails both. Fatal-flaw red team

A red team argues the other side on purpose. Its job is to find the one or two facts that would kill the case for the holding, and to test whether the verdict survives them. We list the flaws in order of how much they could move the decision. A flaw that is serious but already reflected in the verdict is still listed, because the reader should see it.

Flaw one: the cash conversion is structural, not a timing problem

The strongest argument for the company is that the cash gap is temporary, caused by a few years of heavy capital spending and a slow collection cycle. If that is right, the gap would close as the plants mature. The evidence does not yet show that. Operating cash flow was negative in FY24 on the restated basis, positive but small in FY26, and receivables rose faster than sales [9] [6]. Management has guided to a gross block of ₹750 to 800 crore by FY27 [11], so more capital is coming before the existing capital has paid back in cash. The cause visible in the accounts is working capital rather than spending, because operating cash flow is measured before investment: receivables rose faster than sales while payables fell. Spending matters for a second reason, because it has to be funded. If the gap were temporary, it should be visible as a falling debtor-days figure over the next two quarters. We would not call it structural yet. We would call it unproven.

Flaw two: rising borrowing and a possible equity-linked raise

The company has proposed a foreign-currency convertible bond of up to USD 60 million, and it has asked shareholders to raise its borrowing limit to ₹1,000 crore [18] [7]. The stated uses include long-term working capital, capital expenditure, and repaying rupee loans. A convertible bond can later turn into shares, which would dilute existing holders. The February 2026 rating letter rated the company's bank facilities at A-minus with a stable outlook, in the single-A band on ICRA's scale [21]. A larger debt load would raise the debt-to-equity ratio well above today's 0.08, although it would still be far below the 0.70 ceiling if the funds are drawn gradually. The risk is that the cash gap is funded by debt and dilution rather than by operations, which would leave the three-year ratio unchanged while the balance sheet grows.

Flaw three: the tax search and the related-party property purchase

The auditors' review notes disclose that the income-tax department searched the company's premises during the January to March 2026 quarter, which the auditors call the fourth quarter of the preceding financial year, and has started block assessment proceedings, and that the outcome cannot yet be determined [7]. A block assessment can reopen several years of tax returns, so a liability could appear later. The same review draws attention to a ₹22.5 crore property purchase from the managing director, of which ₹9.58 crore had been advanced by June 2026 [7]. A related-party deal is not wrongdoing by itself, but it puts pressure on governance and on the cash, because the advance is money leaving the business before the property is transferred. We do not know the outcome of either matter, and the reader should check both in the next annual report.

Flaw four: the finance and governance changes

A chief financial officer stepped down in May 2026, and the company secretary resigned in September 2026 [16] [1]. The company said the finance chief had moved to a strategic role. Such changes are not proof of a problem, but they happen at the moment when the company is asking for more debt and capital, and when the cash figures need the most careful presentation. A reader should expect the next results to explain the cash gap in full.

Flaw five: the defence orders may be smaller or slower than the headlines

The defence and shell orders are real, and they are the basis of the company's growth story [6] [17]. The order book is about half defence, aerospace and railways. But the company does not publish the order value, so it is not possible to tell how long the orders will take to deliver, how much of the book is multi-year, or how much margin the defence work earns. Defence contracts can be large and slow, and the payment cycle can be long. If the orders are slow to deliver, the receivables problem could get worse before it gets better.

Flaw six: the valuation compresses if the cash gap persists

The price-to-earnings ratio of about 20 times looks low against the peers, but the discount is the market's judgement of the cash problem. If the cash gap persists, investors are likely to keep the discount or widen it. A company that grows profit at 88 percent a year while converting only 27 percent to cash will eventually be valued on its cash, not its profit. The price already reflects some of that risk, which is why the discount to peers is wide.

What would change this view:

  • If the company published an order book value and debtor days fell below 100 days in two consecutive quarters, the red-team case that the cash gap is structural would weaken.
  • If the borrowing and bond proposals were approved and drawn in size, with the cash gap widening, the case for replacement would strengthen.
  • If the block assessment was concluded with a material demand, the governance and balance-sheet case would need to be reopened.

Glossary

Asymmetry: the ratio of reward to risk, the distance to the target divided by the distance to the stop. ATR: the average true range, the typical daily price swing. Base: a sideways range after a fall, where a stock builds before it moves. Breakout: a close above the top of a base. Capital expenditure: money spent on plant and machinery. Cash conversion: operating cash flow divided by reported profit over a period. Debt-to-equity: borrowings divided by owners' equity. Debtor days: the average number of days a company waits to be paid. Dilution: the loss of each holder's share when new shares or convertible bonds are issued. EPS: earnings per share, the profit for each share. Gross block: the cost of plant and equipment before depreciation. Measured move: a target set by adding a base's height to its breakout level. Net debt: borrowings less cash. Operating margin: the share of sales left after running costs. Pivot: the price a base must clear to confirm a new uptrend. Price-to-earnings: the share price divided by EPS. Retest: a return to a broken level to see whether it now holds. ROCE: earnings before interest and tax divided by capital employed. Reward-to-risk: what you can gain divided by what you can lose. Stage 1 to Stage 4: the four phases of a stock: base, markup, top and decline. Stop-loss: the price at which a holding is sold under a rule decided in advance. Working capital: cash tied up in receivables and stock, net of what the company owes its suppliers.
