1. What the company does

Sanghvi Movers rents out heavy cranes. A crane rental company owns a large fleet of machines, some able to lift up to 1,000 tonnes,[1] and hires them out with an operator to builders, power projects, steel plants and wind farms. The business is closer to a car-rental firm for heavy machinery than to a manufacturer. Its profit depends on how many cranes are working at any time (utilisation) and how much each working crane earns (yield). Both move with the investment cycle, which is why the stock tends to move with infrastructure and energy spending.

The company describes itself as Asia's largest crane rental operator and the world's fifth largest, with more than 485 cranes as of FY26 and depots across India, Saudi Arabia, Botswana and Qatar.[1] On the Q1 FY27 call, management said International Cranes magazine ranks it third globally by cumulative lifting capacity, behind two European competitors.[12] The two rankings measure different things, so this dossier does not rely on either as a precise position. Across both, the company is one of a handful of operators with a fleet large enough to take the biggest lifts.

The rental business and the wind business

Most revenue comes from crane hire. The company also runs an engineering and construction arm for wind turbine installation, now separated into a subsidiary called Sangreen Future Renewables. This arm is capital-light: it uses the company's cranes but does not own a large fleet of its own. Management said the wind arm was about 37 to 40 percent of quarterly revenue, with a full-year mix of about one third expected.[22][12] Its order book was about ₹680 crore at the close of the quarter.[22] The wind arm earns lower margins than the crane business, so as it grows the group margin moves toward the lower figure. That is one reason the margin question in chapter 6 matters.

The international push

The group is building a presence in Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Botswana. Management said the Saudi business reached cumulative positive EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) in its first year, and the Q1 call described the Middle East as the next growth leg.[13] For an owner, the practical point is that much of the next few years' growth depends on fleet still being bought and commissioned, and on customers paying on time.

Three rules shape the verdict, and they are explained here before the business is tested. Prices and technical figures come from the internal price ledger for 12 October 2026, which is the authoritative price source under the run rules. The holdings record is treated as accurate under the standing assumption, and the holdings freshness warning is accepted on that basis.

A business is strong when it earns more on its capital than that capital costs, and when the surplus turns into cash. It is weak when growth needs ever more capital, when margins slip, or when customers pay slowly. This chapter tests each of those using reported numbers.

The strengths: returns, growth and utilisation

Return on capital employed (ROCE) is 15.6 percent and return on equity is 13.9 percent on the latest annual data.[1] The company's own figure for group ROCE at March 2026 is about 16 percent, guided to 16.25 to 16.5 percent for FY27.[12] Neither figure is spectacular, but both sit above the 15 percent floor. Revenue rose from ₹224 crore in FY21 to ₹1,070 crore in FY26.[23] The three-year revenue compound growth rate is 33 percent. Profit compounded at 20 percent over three years and 57 percent over five.[1] The five-year figure is higher because earlier years were weak, so the 20 percent is the more honest current rate.

In FY26 the company reported profit after tax of ₹184 crore on revenue of ₹1,070 crore.[8] In Q1 FY27, revenue was ₹379 crore, up 38.89 percent year on year, and profit after tax was ₹65.25 crore against ₹50.26 crore a year earlier.[3][2] Fleet utilisation was reported at 86 percent in the quarter, with a yield of 2.29 percent for the India and Botswana business.[12] Full-year utilisation for FY26 was 79 percent, and the fourth quarter ran at 87 percent.[8] A fleet that is 86 percent booked is close to full, which points to demand rather than slack.

Management said the group had about ₹1,250 crore of orders executable within FY27, and an inquiry pipeline of about ₹5,600 crore.[12] That executable amount covers about 83 to 89 percent of FY27 guided revenue of ₹1,400 to ₹1,500 crore, so most of next year's work is already contracted. Contracts do not become cash on their own, which the next section explains.

The weak spots: margin compression and slower collections

The most important weakness is the margin. The core crane rental EBITDA margin, the profit left after running costs but before interest, depreciation and tax, fell to 47 percent in Q1 FY27 from 53 percent in FY26.[13] Management attributed it to a ₹6.2 crore provision for doubtful receivables, a forex loss, one-time staff incentives and a shift toward lower-margin rental of ancillary equipment.[22][13] Management said the margin should recover toward 51 percent as the provision normalises. The market has not accepted that explanation yet, and chapter 6 explains why it may be right to be sceptical.

The second weakness is collections. On the Q1 call the group's debtor days (the average number of days a bill stays unpaid) were 116. Crane rental was 124 days, the wind business 98 days and the Gulf business 201 days, which management blamed on the regional conflict. Management said collections improved in July.[12] Profit recorded before cash is collected is only as good as the collection.

Cash flow and capital intensity

Reported profit is backed by operating cash, with cash conversion of about 1.4 times over three years. Operating cash flow was ₹278 crore in FY26 and ₹162 crore in FY25, against profit of ₹184 crore and ₹157 crore.[7][5] Over FY24 to FY26, cumulative operating cash flow was ₹7,442 million and cumulative profit was ₹5,287 million, a ratio of about 1.41. One aggregator reports FY25 operating cash flow as ₹205 crore, which conflicts with the ₹162 crore in the other sources; this dossier does not use it.[18]

Once fleet spending is counted, the picture is weaker. Capital expenditure is the money spent on buying equipment, and in FY26 it was ₹4,075 million, which is larger than the operating cash the business generated. That leaves free cash flow negative: free cash flow is the cash left after buying equipment, and in FY26 it was negative ₹1,295 million. The gap is funded with debt.[5] Gross debt to equity was 0.54 times at Q1, against a ceiling of 0.72 times that management guided to for FY27.[12] The FY27 capex plan is ₹652 crore, of which ₹92 crore was capitalised in Q1.[22] Debt is moderate for the size of the equity, but it is rising while the margin is falling.

A catalyst is an event that could move the price. In a downtrend, the important question is which events could start a new trend. This chapter lists the catalysts that could be verified and those that could not.

Management's guidance is the company's own forecast for the year, and it sets FY27 consolidated revenue at ₹1,400 to ₹1,500 crore and EBITDA at ₹525 to ₹575 crore, against EBITDA of ₹429 crore in FY26.[12] The Q1 call kept EBITDA guidance unchanged rather than raising it, despite revenue growth of about 39 percent. 6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A good analysis says where it might be wrong. The numbers here come from aggregators, from management's own commentary and from a price ledger. Each can be wrong in a different way.

Margin compression may be structural, not temporary

Management called the margin fall temporary: a one-off provision, a forex loss, one-time incentives and a mix shift. Adding those back, the underlying crane margin is about 49 to 50 percent, still roughly three points below FY26.[13] The mix shift is the part to watch. Rental of ancillary equipment earns revenue with less capital, which can be sensible. But if the mix keeps moving that way, the margin may not return to 53 percent even when the one-offs fade. The margin question is therefore not only whether it recovers, but whether it recovers to a level that justifies the price. The evidence does not yet settle that.

Collections in the Gulf and the receivables build-up

Debtor days of 201 in the Gulf business are a warning. A receivable that takes more than six months to collect is a risk that a provision only partly covers. The ₹6.2 crore set aside in Q1 is small against a group that earned ₹184 crore of profit in FY26.[8] If collections do not normalise, provisions will grow and reported profit will fall. That is the main way the earnings in this dossier could prove too high.

Debt rising while cash flow is negative

Total debt rose from ₹438 crore in FY25 to ₹674 crore in FY26, while free cash flow was negative.[7] Gross debt to equity of 0.54 times at Q1 leaves room under management's 0.72 ceiling, but the room is not large if capex runs ahead of revenue or interest rates rise. The ceiling is management's guidance, not a covenant this dossier has read.

Data risk: the sources do not all agree

For FY25 operating cash flow, one aggregator gives ₹205 crore and others give ₹162 crore. One aggregator's annual figures are really a quarter's, and a valuation page carries financial tables at the wrong scale by a factor of ten, so its financials are not used.[4] The company's filed statements and the exchange PDFs could not be read. Where sources agree, the figure is used. Where they conflict, the figure is given with its conflict or not used. The other gate results would not change on the alternative values, but the ROCE pass is at risk on any weaker reading: a fall of more than 0.6 points would take ROCE under the 15 percent floor.

What this means for an owner

The most likely way this dossier is wrong is that the margin recovers faster than the sources suggest, collections normalise and the stock rebounds through its 50-day average. That would favour keeping the position. The other likely path is that the margin does not recover, Gulf receivables grow and the stock falls further, which would confirm the exit. The rules in chapter 7 do not require the owner to predict which one happens: the stop says what to do now, and the level that would change the verdict says what must happen first.

How it was calculated

The asymmetry ratio compares how far the price could rise with how far it could fall. The formula is (target base minus current price) divided by (current price minus stop loss). A ratio of 3.0 means the potential gain is three times the potential loss. Each line below shows an input and what it means for this name.

Line 1, the current price: ₹392.05, the internal ledger close on 12 October 2026. This is the cmp term.[15]

Line 2, the stop loss: ₹415.00, the trailing stop recorded against the holding. It sits above the current price.

Line 3, the risk term: current price minus stop loss is 392.05 minus 415.00, which equals −22.95. The denominator is negative, meaning the price is already below the stop, so the formula is not meaningful for this holding in its current state.

Line 4, the target base: N/A. The skill requires a target anchored to a verifiable forward EPS times a three-year median P/E, or a base measured move. The cached sources show no analyst target and no forward EPS. The P/E history is not agreed across sources, and no three-year median could be verified. The base high needed for a measured move is not in the ledger extract.[11][15][16][20]

Line 5, the reward term: target base minus current price, which is N/A because line 4 is N/A.

Line 6, the ratio: N/A. An unverified target means the gate status is FAIL_ASYMMETRY on this dimension, which is what this dossier records.
