Before you read

Adani Ports and Special Economic Zone (APSEZ) is India's largest private port operator. It also runs trains, warehouses and ships that move cargo to and from its ports. We reject it for the tactical-momentum shortlist on the 9 October 2026 session, because its ROCE (return on capital employed: yearly operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business) is 14.1% on the Screener measure, below our 15% floor, and its chart sits in Stage 3 rather than a rising Stage 2.[2] The company itself reports a 16% ROCE for FY26 using its own definition, so this failure depends on the measure and could reverse.[11] Our base-case reward-to-risk at today's price is 2.08 to 1, well below the 3.5 to 1 we need. The conclusion would be wrong if the results due on 28 October 2026 show ROCE at or above 15% and the price closes above ₹1,891.1 with its 50-day average turning up.[16]

What the company does

APSEZ earns money by charging shipping lines, cargo owners and rail operators to use its terminals and services. A ship arrives at a berth, cranes unload containers, coal, fertiliser or crude oil, and APSEZ bills for the use of the berth, the cargo handled and the storage. In the quarter to 30 June 2026 it handled 138.1 million metric tonnes (MMT, a million tonnes) of cargo, up 15% from 120.6 MMT a year earlier, and it holds 27.6% of all cargo moved by Indian ports.[5] The Mundra port in Gujarat alone handled 51.2 MMT in that quarter.[5]

The business has five parts. Domestic ports (14 ports and terminals on the west, east and south coasts) are the core: they produced revenue of ₹6,964 crore and EBITDA of ₹5,152 crore in the June 2026 quarter.[5] EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation) is the operating profit before financing costs and the wear on assets; here it is 74% of domestic port revenue, which means that for every ₹100 of port income about ₹74 is left after running costs. International ports (Haifa in Israel, Dar es Salaam in Tanzania, Colombo in Sri Lanka and, since December 2025, the North Queensland Export Terminal, or NQXT, in Australia) earned revenue of ₹1,747 crore and EBITDA of ₹730 crore.[5][7] Logistics (rail container trains, warehouses, trucking) earned ₹1,173 crore of revenue and ₹219 crore of EBITDA; marine services (tugs, dredgers and a 135-vessel fleet) earned ₹901 crore of revenue and ₹404 crore of EBITDA.[5]

The company argues that its cargo is sticky: 53% of volume comes from customers on long-term contracts or from cargo that is hard to move elsewhere.[5] It also gets paid on its own terms at most ports, because the agreements let it set tariffs (the prices charged per tonne or per container) without a regulator fixing them, according to the CARE rating agency.[17] That pricing freedom is why domestic port margins are so high. The weak side is that heavy capital spending (₹15,320 crore in FY26 on property, plant and equipment, including work in progress) must keep running to hold share.[7]

The company's stated ambition is to raise capacity from 653 MMT to 1 billion tonnes by 2030 and to grow revenue at 19% a year and EBITDA at 18% a year between FY26 and FY31.[10] It guided FY27 to revenue of ₹43,000 to ₹45,000 crore and EBITDA of ₹25,000 to ₹26,000 crore, with capex (capital expenditure: money spent on new assets) of ₹12,000 to ₹14,000 crore.[9] Management declined to raise the guidance in July and said it will revisit after the first half, which ends with the 28 October 2026 results.[24][16]

Glossary of terms used in this report

PAT (profit after tax) is the profit left once all costs and taxes are paid. EPS (earnings per share) is that profit divided by the number of shares. CFO (cash flow from operations) is the cash the business generates from running itself, before buying assets. Free cash flow is CFO minus capital spending. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money; lower means less reliance on lenders. Net debt is borrowings minus cash. Price-to-earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by a year's profit per share; it says how many years of profit you pay for. Dilution is the fall in each share's slice of profit when a company issues new shares. TEU is a twenty-foot equivalent unit, the standard way to count shipping containers. TTM (trailing twelve months) is the last four reported quarters added together. A moving average (20-, 50- and 200-day) is the average closing price over that many sessions; it smooths the chart. RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 gauge of how stretched recent gains or losses are; above 70 is stretched. Stage 2 is a rising price trend above rising 50- and 200-day averages; Stage 3 is a topping phase where the 50-day average stops rising. A base is a quiet sideways price area; a pivot is the price that ends it; a breakout is a close above the pivot. A stop loss is a pre-set price at which we leave a trade to cap the loss. Asymmetry or reward-to-risk is the possible gain divided by the possible loss. A measured move is a price target made by adding the height of a base to its top.

Why the business is strong or weak

The strength of APSEZ is that ports are scarce, hard-to-copy assets. A new deep-water port takes years to approve and build, so an operator with berths, land and rail links already in place keeps customers once they connect. This shows in the margins: domestic port EBITDA margin was 74% in the June 2026 quarter and the company says Indian ports earn about 72%.[10][24] Our own division of the Q4 FY26 slide figures (EBITDA ₹4,704 crore over revenue ₹6,566 crore) gives 71.6%, so the margin has risen about two points in a quarter.[9] Domestic ports also earn the best return: segment ROCE was 23% in FY26, against 10% for logistics, 13% for marine and 8% for international ports (which excludes NQXT), according to the company.[6][8]

The weak side is where the growth is coming from. International port revenue grew 80% in the June 2026 quarter, but a large part came from buying NQXT, a 50-million-tonne-a-year Australian coal export terminal, from the promoter group (the family-controlled owners). CARE reports the price was about 17 times EBITDA, an enterprise value of about A$3,975 million, paid by issuing new shares to the promoters.[17] That is a related-party deal, meaning the company bought from its own controlling shareholders. Issuing shares at a time of strong prices raised the share count from about 216.0 crore to about 230.4 crore (₹432.03 crore to ₹460.79 crore of capital at ₹2 face value), a 6.7% increase in shares.[7] This dilution shows up already: profit rose 10.2% in the June 2026 quarter but EPS rose only 2.4% (₹15.71 against ₹15.34).[4]

Organic domestic volume is also slower than the headline. Domestic cargo grew 2% in the June 2026 quarter (115.3 against 112.9 MMT), and the all-India share slipped from 27.8% to 27.6%.[5] Management blamed the Middle East crisis, which disrupted shipping and left containers parked at Mundra, and said cargo should resume when those containers move on.[24] The September 2026 update shows recovery: 46 MMT in September (+11%), 280 MMT for the first half of FY27 (+15%), led by containers (+15%).[14] It is an operator update and not audited, and it does not split domestic from international cargo, so organic growth is not shown.

Group-level legal risk is a standing weakness. A US prosecutor's indictment and a US securities regulator's civil complaint named a non-executive director of the company; the company itself is not named. In May 2026 the securities regulator asked a New York court to enter a consent judgment (civil penalties, with no admission) and the prosecutor moved to dismiss the criminal charges with prejudice, and both were still waiting for court approval when the company filed its June 2026 results on 29 July 2026.[4] CARE says the matters before the Indian securities regulator were closed with no penalty and treats the US outcome as a monitorable.[17] We have not verified any court ruling after 29 July 2026.

Credit quality is high. CARE reaffirmed AAA/Stable on 3 July 2026, CRISIL did the same on 20 November 2025, and the company's slides show S&P at BBB (Stable) after an upgrade from BBB-, with Moody's at Baa3 and Fitch at BBB-.[17][18][6] The gap between domestic AAA and the global BBB band is a reminder that overseas lenders price the group differently from Indian ones. CARE's downgrade triggers are a sustained net debt to EBITDA above 2.50 to 2.75 times, loans or guarantees to related parties, or adverse regulatory action.[17]

The financial gate fails on one number, ROCE, and the cause is a choice of definition. The company's 16% excludes NQXT because the terminal was consolidated only from the fourth quarter; our measure and Screener's include the full capital base but only one quarter of earnings. A ROCE of 12.2% to 14.3% on our own arithmetic, against the company's 16% and a floor of 15%, tells the reader that the floor sits inside the range of reasonable answers. The table does not prove that returns are low; it proves that they are not clearly above the floor on a uniform method. We apply the same Screener measure to every company in the run, so the verdict is FAIL_FINANCIAL.

Where the 16% comes from matters too. The company says it will lift consolidated ROCE by one percentage point every year, mostly from domestic ports, and the chief executive spoke of 20% by FY31 as one indicator among several.[8][24] If that happens, the gate would pass in FY27 or FY28; it does not pass today.

A ₹50 higher base target (₹2,042) lifts the ratio at ₹1,761 to 2.53, still short of 3.5. Only a price at or below ₹1,726, or a base target near ₹2,150, clears the floor.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate has one weakest assumption. For each we name the evidence that would show it wrong and when we will know.

  • FY27 EPS of ₹63 assumes the June quarter repeats. The quarter had ₹852.91 crore of other income (three times a year earlier) and a ₹267.86 crore exchange gain, partly offset by a ₹287.76 crore joint-venture loss.[4] If the second quarter shows EPS under ₹14, the base is too high. We will know on 28 October 2026.[16]
  • 15% FY28 growth assumes domestic volume re-accelerates. Domestic cargo grew only 2% in the June quarter and the all-India share slipped.[5] The September update shows recovery in total volume (+11%) but not the split.[14] The domestic figure in the 28 October release will settle this.
  • The 27.5 multiple assumes APSEZ keeps its three-year pricing. The current P/E is 30.1 to 30.5, so the base case already assumes the multiple falls by 8% to 10%.[3] If the multiple stays above 30, the base is too low; if the group's legal outlook worsens, it could fall to the bear's 25.35. The multiple is observable daily.
  • The ROCE gate assumes Screener's measure. On the company's definition the gate would pass at 16%. The gate result could flip when the annual report's definition and the Screener definition converge as NQXT earns a full year; the first full data point is the September 2026 half-year balance sheet, due with the 28 October 2026 results.[11]
  • The stop of ₹1,650.0 assumes orderly selling. A gap below it on adverse news (for instance a negative court ruling in the US matter, which was pending approval when last filed) would cost more than ₹111.[4] There is no scheduled date for the New York court's decision that we could verify.
  • Probabilities of 20%, 50% and 30% are judgement. If the Q2 results meet guidance and the chart turns, we would move 10 points from the bear to the base. The next check is 28 October 2026.

Data limits that cannot be fixed by waiting: we read the main statements of the audited results and only the first 80 pages of the annual report, so related-party notes, contingent liabilities and key audit matters were not read in full.[7][13] Price data comes from a vendor feed of daily bars, not an exchange file, and the NSE quote page loads its numbers by script.[1][23] Several Q1 FY27 and rating items are older than 60 days because they are the latest available filings; the freshest are the September cargo update (2 October), the board notice (5 October) and the Paradip filing (6 October).[14][16][15]
