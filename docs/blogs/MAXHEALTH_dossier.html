The company earns a return on capital of 14.7 percent, below our 15 percent floor, and the share price is in a falling trend that our momentum test does not accept. What the company does

Max Healthcare earns its money the way most hospital chains do. A patient pays for a bed for each day of a stay, plus the doctors, surgery, scans, medicines and nursing around that stay. Some patients pay in cash, some through insurance, and some through government health schemes. The company's own investor page describes it as "one of India's largest healthcare organizations", operating 21 healthcare facilities with more than 6,100 beds across the National Capital Region (Delhi and its neighbours), Punjab, Uttarakhand, Maharashtra and Odisha.[11] Almost 85 percent of that bed capacity sits in metro or Tier 1 cities, which are the large urban centres where patients can pay for specialist care.[11]

The company is also in two adjacent businesses. Max@Home offers health and wellness services at home, and Max Lab runs pathology (lab test) services outside its hospitals. In the first quarter of the financial year 2026-27 (April to June 2026), Max@Home reported revenue of Rs 78 crore, up 32 percent, and Max Lab reported Rs 58 crore, up 20 percent.[5] These two businesses are small next to the hospitals, but they bring patients into the network and are the fastest growing lines in the company's own reporting.

The company's main hospital operations are the reason the stock is covered. Its newer capacity is in Lucknow, Bhubaneswar, Mohali, Nagpur and Pune, among other cities. In September 2026 the company inaugurated a new 400-bed tower at Max Smart in Saket, Delhi, after a Union Minister opened it, according to its own exchange filing list.[12] In May 2026 the company also completed a majority acquisition of Kalinga Hospital in Odisha, which now contributes to the group's revenue.[18] In the first quarter after the acquisition, Kalinga contributed Rs 19 crore of revenue and Rs 2 crore of operating profit (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA)), at an occupancy rate of 50 percent.[4] In August 2026 the board also approved Rs 425 crore for a new tower at the Vaishali hospital, which would add about 202 beds and is due for commissioning by November 2029.[6][31][36]

The promoter group holds a minority stake, and institutions hold most of the shares. The promoter group holds 23.71 percent of the shares, and the Trendlyne data shows 0.0 percent of the promoter holding pledged as security for loans in the June 2026 quarter.[3][35] Foreign portfolio investors held 41.78 percent in that quarter, down from 60.39 percent in September 2023, while domestic institutions, mainly mutual funds and insurers, rose to 29.95 percent.[1][3] Foreign money has been selling while domestic money has been buying. The sources we read do not explain this shift, so we do not treat it as a cause of the price move.

The company's chairman and managing director is Abhay Soi, who also led the company before the amalgamation that formed the present entity.[11]

Why the business is strong or weak

Competitive position

Screener's company summary calls Max Healthcare India's largest hospital chain by market value and the second largest by revenue and EBITDA.[1] That position is the company's main strength. A large network can negotiate with insurers, recruit specialist doctors across many sites, and spread the cost of shared services such as procurement and laboratories. The company's own description of its network stresses the same point: its beds are concentrated in metro and Tier 1 cities, where insured and self-paying patients are more numerous than in smaller towns.[11]

The sector is growing into that position. Indian hospitals have about 1.5 to 1.6 beds per 1,000 people against a global benchmark of about 3, which is a structural reason to expect more beds to be built over time.[28] The credit rating agency ICRA expected occupancy for its sample of 11 listed hospital chains to stay between 62 and 64 percent in FY26, with average revenue per occupied bed rising by 6 to 8 percent.[27] A consulting survey of hospital chief executives in the first half of 2026 found them "cautiously optimistic" about FY27 growth, with a gradual rise in revenue per bed expected.[29] These are sector figures, not company figures, and they are the yardstick for the scorecard below.

The company's weakness is the cost of growth. It is adding beds faster than it is adding profit. Revenue for the first quarter of FY27 was Rs 2,982 crore, up 16 percent, and operating EBITDA was Rs 704 crore, up 15 percent. But the operating margin, which is the share of sales left after running costs, was 24.8 percent, against 24.9 percent a year earlier and 26.8 percent in the previous quarter, because new hospitals and the Kalinga acquisition carry costs before they fill.[4][5] Press coverage of the same results gave the same headline figures.[7][8][9][10]

The price trend is labelled in the ledger as Stage 4, which the framework uses for a stock that is in a sustained decline. The stage system, originally described by the American analyst Stan Weinstein, divides a stock's life into four phases. A base is a period of sideways trading. An advance, called Stage 2, is when the price rises above a rising long-term average. A top is Stage 3, and a decline is Stage 4. A stock in Stage 4 is one the framework does not want to buy, because the trend is working against the buyer. Our framework allows Core positions only after a breakout, which is a move above the top of a base, or within a Stage 2 advance, and Tactical positions only from a Stage 2 advance above rising 50-day and 200-day averages. The company is in neither.

The relative strength index (RSI) is a momentum gauge that runs from 0 to 100, and it compares the size of recent gains with recent losses. The reading for MAXHEALTH is 25.59, below the 30 level that is usually called oversold. An oversold reading means the stock has fallen unusually fast in the short term, which can produce a bounce. It does not mean the stock must bounce, and here the longer trend is still down. The average true range (ATR) is the typical daily price movement over 14 sessions, and it is the unit the framework uses for distances. The ATR is Rs 31.25. The stock's distance above its 63-day low of Rs 854.00 is therefore 0.38 ATR, which means the price sits close to that level.

The momentum test is a fixed calculation, run against the ledger, and it returns NO_MOMENTUM. Test A asks whether a stock is already in a trend, and Test B asks whether it is entering one. A stock that fails both tests is not allocated, however good its business.

The target anchor and the upside test

Asymmetry is the ratio of the upside to a verified target against the downside to the stop. The framework requires the target to be anchored to forward earnings multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, or to a technical measured move, which is a target found by adding the height of a base to its breakout level. Here neither anchor could be verified, so the asymmetry gate fails. The estimate below shows the working and why the number is not admissible.

The number

No admissible target. The gate result is FAIL_ASYMMETRY. For reference only, the Trendlyne sell-side average of Rs 1,251.67 (6 analysts, 11 reports) and the 27-analyst target of Rs 1,160 are shown, but neither is an anchor the framework accepts.[13][14] The reference arithmetic is: (1,251.67 minus 865.95) divided by 865.95 gives an upside of 44.5 percent. The stop distance is Rs 43.20 (865.95 minus 822.75), or 4.99 percent of the close, so the same average would give a ratio of about 8.9 to 1 (385.72 divided by 43.20). That ratio is not the framework's asymmetry, because its target is unverified. The technical failure in chapter 4 is what blocks acting on it, not the missing anchor alone.

How it was calculated

The framework's anchor is forward earnings per share multiplied by the three-year median P/E. We derived forward earnings per share from the consensus FY27 net income of Rs 1,852.6 crore, divided by the share count of about 97.3 crore implied by the shareholding data. That gives about Rs 19.03 per share, and the stock's price implies a FY27 P/E of about 45.5 times. We could not find a verified three-year median P/E for this company from the sources we could read, so the multiplication cannot be completed. The technical anchor also fails, because a measured move needs a base, and the stock is in Stage 4.

The inputs

Consensus FY27 net income of Rs 1,852.6 crore (MarketScreener, net income of 18,526 million rupees).[15] The MarketScreener analyst pages list 27 analysts, an average target of 1,160.23 rupees and a high target of 1,320 rupees.[32][33] FY26 net income of Rs 1,442 crore (Screener consolidated).[1] Share count of about 97.3 crore, from the promoter holding of 230,777,699 shares, which is 23.71 percent of the total.[3] Close of Rs 865.95 (ledger, 12 Oct 2026). Three-year median P/E: not found in any source we could read, so it is recorded as unverified.

The range

If a verified three-year median P/E were available, the forward earnings figure of about Rs 19.03 would produce a target that depends entirely on that multiple. A median of 45 times would put the target close to the current price, because the stock already trades at about 45.5 times FY27 earnings. A median of 61 times would put the target at the Rs 1,160 analyst figure, which is the multiple the analysts' target implies. Neither reading supports a buy on this framework, and neither can be used until the median is verified.

If the number is off

If the number is off, the rejection stands, because the gate fails on the anchor as well as on return on capital and the technical posture. If a verified anchor became available and the forward earnings were close to the consensus, the reader would then face a stock trading at a high multiple on a falling price. The reader should not treat a broker target as a reason to buy until it can be reproduced from a verified median multiple and forward earnings.

The bear case is the most likely outcome, and the bull case has no verified anchor. Chapter 6 asks which assumption in these numbers is weakest and when we would know.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Each estimate in this report rests on a few assumptions. The weakest is that the return on capital stays below 15 percent for at least the next reporting period, because that figure is the one most likely to move. If it reached 15 percent in the next set of results, the financial gate would pass, but the technical gate would still fail, so the stock would stay rejected until the trend also changed.

The second weak assumption is that sell-side forecasts describe the company's real earnings path. The consensus of Rs 1,852.6 crore for FY27 implies net income growth of about 28 percent, and revenue growth forecasts in the same source are higher still.[15] Forecasts of this kind usually assume that new beds fill on schedule. If the beds fill more slowly than the analysts assume, the forecast would be too high. The company's own data shows the first sign of this risk: receivable days have risen and net debt has risen, which is the wrong direction for a business that is expanding quickly.[4]

The third weak assumption concerns the Kalinga Hospital acquisition. BRS Capital Two Pte. Limited, which was another shareholder of Kalinga before Max Healthcare acquired a majority stake, has filed petitions at the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) connected with the acquisition. The hearing was adjourned to 29 October 2026.[17][18] The Q1 call summary describes the matter as litigation with minority shareholders of Kalinga after the acquisition.[4] The outcome could change the cost of the acquisition or the control of the hospital. We do not know the outcome, and our estimates assume no large payment.

What evidence would show these estimates are wrong

The return on capital would be wrong if the next reported figure, or the header on the same Screener page once new results are filed, rises above 15 percent while the company keeps adding beds. That would show that the new capacity is earning its way. The bear case would be wrong if the price closes above the 20-day average of Rs 981.28 for several sessions and the 50-day average turns upward, because a trend change of that kind is what the momentum test looks for. The receivable-days assumption would be wrong if the days fall back from 95 towards 87, which would show that the cash lag was temporary.

A dismissal of the petitions would remove one risk and would be a point in favour of the bull case. A significant payment would reduce free cash flow and widen net debt, which would support the bear case. The current estimates would be wrong if a dismissal were followed by a rise in the return on capital, because the rejection rests on the return and the trend, not on the petitions alone.

When we will know

The NCLT hearing is set for 29 October 2026, which is the nearest dated event.[17] The company's next quarterly results will show the return on capital, receivable days and net debt for the September 2026 quarter, but the date of that release is not confirmed in the sources we could read. The company's investor page lists a schedule of investor conferences, but those dates are not in the sources we used. The three-year median P/E and a verified forward earnings figure would need a source we did not find, and their absence is the reason the target anchor fails today.

The fatal-flaw red team

A red team tries to break the case against the stock, and here it is the case for the stock that should be tested hardest. Three scenarios could make the rejection wrong.

  1. The sell-side is right and the market re-rates the stock. If analysts' earnings forecasts are met and the market accepts a higher multiple, the stock could rise toward the Rs 1,160 average target. The framework would still require a verified anchor and a passing return on capital, so this scenario would show that the rejection was a timing decision. We record it as a real risk.
  2. The reference stop is hit and the stock keeps falling. If the Rs 854.00 low breaks and the price falls to the Core stop of Rs 822.75, the stock would sit well below the level the framework requires for any buyer, and the trend would confirm the rejection. This is the bear case, and it is the outcome the framework is designed to avoid.
  3. Growth in receivables and debt absorbs the earnings. If receivable days stay at 95 and net debt keeps rising as the company builds new hospitals, the return on capital could stay below the 15 percent floor for several years. This scenario is the one most consistent with the company's own figures for the June quarter.[4]

The weakest assumption is the return on capital, and the test that would show it wrong is the next reported figure. Chapter 7 turns the rejection into a plan for what to do, and when.

What we would do, and when

The decision is REJECTED. The stock fails three gates: return on capital (14.7 percent against 15.0 percent), the technical posture (Stage 4, with the momentum gate returning NO_MOMENTUM), and the asymmetry test (no verified target anchor). The rule that controls the decision is simple: any single failure in Layer 1 rejects the stock, and Layer 2 and Layer 3 are not used to override it. The rejection does not change even under the alternative reading discussed in chapter 3, because the technical failure in chapter 4 rejects the stock on its own. This report does not assess any existing holding, which is a portfolio question for a separate audit. The verdict means the stock does not enter the shortlist.

What would change this decision

The verdict would be reviewed if the price closes above Rs 981.28, the 20-day average, for three sessions in a row on volume above its 20-day average, and if the 50-day average turns upward from Rs 1,007.80.

A reported return on capital of 15.0 percent or more on the consolidated page would clear the financial gate and allow the technical and asymmetry gates to be tested again. Receivable days would be a separate supporting signal only, not a condition of this trigger.

The decision would also need a verified target anchor. That requires a three-year median P/E from a source we can read, and a forward earnings figure that matches it. Until both are available, the asymmetry gate fails regardless of the price. A close below Rs 854.00 would confirm the rejection, because it would mean the stock had broken its recent low and was moving towards the reference stop at Rs 822.75.

Buy price, stop and targets

The framework defines an entry zone as the price range in which a buyer would start a position, given the stock's support and the risk to the stop. No entry zone is set, because the stock is rejected. The reference stop is Rs 822.75 under the Core rule and Rs 838.38 under the Tactical rule. Both are shown so the reader can see where a position would be cut if the stock were ever admitted. The target is not set. The framework requires a verified target anchor before any target can be stated, and none has been verified.

Shortlist action and peer candidates

The shortlist action is REJECTED_BELOW_HURDLE. The company should not be seeded as a peer candidate for the watchlist, because it fails the screen this report applies. The peers in the table in chapter 2 may be worth separate review. Apollo Hospitals and Global Health both show a return on capital of 17.4 percent on their Screener pages, above the framework's floor. Their technical and asymmetry gates have not been run in this report, so they are names for our next research cycle to consider, not names this report recommends.[23][25]

Takeaway: the decision is to do nothing with the stock until its numbers change. The triggers above are the events that would reopen the question, and the reader should check them on the next results and on the first close above the 20-day average.

Run notes and gate exceptions

This report carries gate exceptions, and they are listed here in plain English. The first is the return on capital failure. The gate line reads "return on capital 14.7 percent is below the 15.0 percent floor", and it cannot be cured now, because the next quarterly results have not been filed and the framework does not allow rounding. It lowers confidence in the conclusion only at the margin, because the technical and asymmetry failures would reject the stock on their own. A cure would be a reported return on capital of 15.0 percent or more on the consolidated page after the next quarterly filing.

The second exception is the age of several figures. The financial tables run to March 2026, the latest audited year. The shareholding and the first-quarter results run to June 2026. The sector figures from ICRA come from an article dated 2 January 2026, and the Deloitte survey and the InsightRx report are dated in the first half and July 2026. Each of these is older than the 60-day window, so each is disclosed here rather than corrected. The as-of dates in the notes are the true dates of the figures. Ages are counted in calendar days from each as-of date to 12 October 2026. At the report date the financial tables are 225 days old, the June 2026 quarter figures 133 days old, the ICRA summary 283 days old, and the InsightRx and Deloitte figures 103 and 133 days old. The cure for each is the next filing: the September 2026 quarter results (release date not confirmed in our sources) or the FY27 annual figures.

The review record. Round 2 was the final independent review. Its verdict was REVISE, not APPROVE, and this report is published on a best-effort basis after that review limit. The round-2 findings were applied where the evidence supported them, and no finding remains open. The open limits are the stale as-of dates listed above, the missing three-year median P/E, and the absence of a hospital line in our scorecard list. The verdict REJECTED is not affected by those limits, because it rests on the return on capital and the technical failure. The unresolved list is recorded in the working file for this run.

The third exception is the missing target anchor. The three-year median price-to-earnings ratio was not found, so the asymmetry gate fails with the reason "target unverified". A cure would be a verified median from a source the reader can check, and a forward earnings figure that matches it.

Other limits should be known. The company's investor site and the National Stock Exchange filing PDFs could not be read directly, so the company's own statements are taken from the investor page text and the exchange announcement list, and the operating figures from a press summary of the earnings call. Our written sector list has no hospital line, so the five-measure scorecard was chosen for this business and is labelled as such.
