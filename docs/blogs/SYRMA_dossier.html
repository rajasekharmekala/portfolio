A red-team pass asks what could make the attractive narrative wrong. Three failure paths matter most. First, cash can lag profit if inventory and receivables rise faster than sales; that is why CFO/PAT remains a hard unresolved gate. Second, a high multiple can compress even when the business grows, because the price already discounts years of execution. Case 1: working-capital reversal

Working-capital reversal is the most important accounting risk because an EMS provider pays for components before receiving the customer’s final payment. A sudden build in inventory can support delivery reliability for a while, but it also consumes cash and may create obsolescence if a programme changes. Receivables can rise for a different reason: a customer may accept the product while payment waits for a milestone or quality sign-off. The red-team test is therefore not one quarter of CFO; it is the cumulative three-year relationship between CFO and PAT, reconciled to the audited statements. If conversion remains below 0.70x, the capital allocator should reject the position even if sales growth remains high.

Case 2: valuation de-rating

Valuation de-rating can hurt an investor even when the operating business improves. The third question is price location: SMA50 is a reference line, not a guarantee, and ATR is a volatility measure rather than a forecast. If price returns near the support boundary, the position still needs a risk limit and a clear invalidation point. The fourth question is target anchoring: a target must come from sourced analyst work, company guidance, or a verified measured move; a round number chosen because it feels plausible is not evidence. Only then can asymmetry be calculated. The fifth question is business quality: confirm whether operating margin, order visibility, customer concentration and capacity claims are current and sourced. Missing data should remain missing. The sixth question is portfolio fit: even a passing company can be unsuitable if the intended allocation is too large or duplicates an existing exposure. Because this is an isolated company report, the reader should apply the same rule to the portfolio context rather than assume that a peer’s valuation supplies SYRMA’s valuation. The seventh question is timing: a breakout needs volume and a retest needs a defined level; neither is established by a single green candle. The eighth question is review discipline: record the trigger, date, source and action in a journal so that a later decision can be audited. These steps preserve the rejected verdict while making the path to reconsideration explicit. They also prevent the common error of changing the conclusion first and searching for support later. The report’s evidence gap is therefore actionable: retrieve the missing fields, reconcile them, test the thresholds, and only then decide whether the gate has opened. Until that work is complete, the prudent action is to keep the company on watch rather than convert uncertainty into a purchase.

A disciplined review also checks the evidence chain itself. Save the document used, note whether it is an exchange filing, annual report, investor presentation or rating note, and state the retrieval date. If two sources disagree, do not average them silently: show both definitions and prefer the filing with the clearer period. If a later report changes a number, mark the change as a new as-of observation instead of rewriting the older history. The same care applies to technical levels. A close, average, volume multiple and RSI belong to a particular session, while a business ratio may belong to a quarter or year. Mixing those clocks can create a false sense of precision. The rejection is thus a safeguard against decision drift. It keeps the company eligible for future work without pretending that eligibility is ownership. When the next review arrives, start with the missing evidence list, test each stated threshold, and record why every trigger did or did not fire. This makes the next decision faster, clearer and easier for another reader to audit.
