How to read this report

The report tests the company in layers. Layer 1 is the first set of hard tests: the company must pass every financial floor and the technical floor, and one failure rejects it. Layer 2 is an operating scorecard, and it is used only for names that pass Layer 1, so it is not used here.

The momentum gate is a separate price test, computed from closing prices. It has two routes. Test A asks whether the stock is already in a rising trend and not stretched. Test B asks whether it is turning up from a base, which is a sideways period in which a stock stops falling and builds a floor. A stock that fails both routes receives no allocation. Relative strength compares the stock's return with the market's return over the same period, and SPY, an exchange-traded fund that tracks the S&P 500, stands in for the market.

Company figures come from the SEC, the US securities regulator. Its public database, EDGAR, holds the filings: the annual report on Form 10-K, the quarterly report on Form 10-Q, and the current report on Form 8-K. The structured figures behind them are published in XBRL, a standard machine-readable format. The notes list each source.

Shareholders' equity is the company's net worth on paper, meaning assets minus liabilities. Operating cash flow is the cash the business produces from its normal work, before spending on equipment. NO_MOMENTUM is the gate's label for a stock that fails both routes.

What the company does

Cleveland-Cliffs describes itself in its annual report as "a leading North America-based steel producer with focus on value-added sheet products, particularly for the automotive industry" [1]. Steel sheet is the flat product that becomes car doors, body panels and parts. Making it needs iron ore, coal or scrap, very hot furnaces, rolling mills and a large workforce. The annual report also refers to its legacy business of producing iron ore pellets [1]. Put simply, the business turns iron ore and scrap into sheet steel for car makers and other buyers.

That model has consequences for an investor. Steel is a cyclical product, so prices and volumes rise and fall with the economy and with imports. The company carries heavy fixed costs in its mills and mines, so a fall in prices or volumes can turn a profit into a loss very quickly. The 2025 results show that effect. The company's net loss was $1,428 million in the cash flow statement of its annual report, which begins from that loss before its cash adjustments [1]. The net loss attributable to Cliffs shareholders was $1,478 million [2].

The first half of 2026 shows a smaller loss. The six-month net loss was $363 million before noncontrolling interests, and $382 million attributable to Cliffs shareholders [3]. The same six months of 2025 showed a total net loss of $959 million [3]. That is an improvement, and it is the first sign in the record that the losses may be narrowing. It is not yet a return to profit, and the cash flow picture is still negative, as chapter 3 shows.

What this means for an owner: this is a large steel business whose earnings swing with its market. The question for the underwriting is whether the business is earning more than its cost of capital through a cycle, or whether 2025 and 2026 show that the cycle has turned against it for longer than the balance sheet can absorb. The next two chapters answer that question with the filed numbers.

Glossary for this report

Terms used in this report, in the order they first appear. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization; it is a rough measure of operating cash earnings before the cost of debt and tax. Capital expenditure is money spent on mills, mines, equipment and other long-lived assets. Free cash flow means cash from operating activities minus capital expenditure, the money left after the company pays for the equipment it needs to keep running. Net debt is total borrowing minus cash on hand. Stock-based compensation (SBC) is pay given to staff in shares. With these terms in hand, chapter 2 asks whether the franchise earns a return that justifies its capital.

Why the business is strong or weak

A competitive position is the reason a business keeps its customers and its margins. The company describes itself as a fully-integrated steel enterprise with an expansive footprint [1]. The annual report says some producers rely on imported pig iron, and that its supply has at times been disrupted [1]. Those are reasons the franchise could be strong.

The record does not show that the advantage is turning into profit. In 2025 operating income was a loss of $1,579 million, against a loss of $763 million in 2024 [4]. The loss is not mainly a write-down: the goodwill impairment record shows no charge in 2024 or 2025, with a charge of $125 million in 2023 [5]. The losses are therefore operating losses, which means the core business earned less than its costs in those years. That is a harder picture than a one-time charge would be, because it does not reverse by itself when a write-down is over.

The company's cash picture confirms the point. Operating cash flow was negative $462 million in 2025, after a positive $105 million in 2024 and $2,267 million in 2023 [6]. A steel producer can run through a bad year on cash in the bank for a while, and the cash balance at the end of 2025 was $57 million [7]. That is a thin buffer for a business with this size of debt.

What this means for an owner: the franchise has real assets, notably its integrated footprint and its automotive customers. Those assets have not yet produced profit or cash in the last two years. The owner should ask what would have to change in the steel market, in costs or in the company's own mix for the advantage to show up in the numbers, and chapter 3 sets out the size of that change.

Estimate 1: the free cash flow needed to pass the gate

This estimate asks how far the company's cash generation sits from the 12 percent floor. Cash is the test an owner can check most easily, because it is what is left after the business pays for its equipment. The gap is in the billions, which is larger than any year in the record.

The number

To pass the 12 percent free cash flow floor on the 2025 sales base, the company would need about $2.2 billion of free cash flow in a year, compared with a negative $1.0 billion in 2025. The gap is about $3.3 billion.

How it was calculated

Twelve percent of net sales of $18,610 million is $2,233.2 million. The 2025 free cash flow was negative $1,023 million, which is operating cash flow of negative $462 million minus capital expenditure of $561 million. The gap is $2,233.2 million plus $1,023 million, or $3,256.2 million.

The inputs

Why these estimates might be wrong

The verdict rests on the 2025 operating loss, negative free cash flow and negative EBITDA. The weakest assumption behind it is that the first-half improvement in the net loss, from $959 million to $363 million, is a one-off, so the cash test would be read too harshly. The evidence that would test the assumption is the first-half 2026 result. The six-month net loss narrowed from $959 million to $363 million in the quarterly report [3], which fits a recovery. But operating cash flow was still negative at $95 million, and capital expenditure was $309 million, so the business is still spending more than it generates [6][9].

The second weak point is the debt. Non-current debt rose from $3,137 million at the end of 2023 to $7,253 million at the end of 2025 [11]. A company with that debt needs a cash flow that covers interest and repayments. The record does not show the interest expense figure for 2025 in a usable form, so the report cannot measure how much of the cash flow goes to interest. Until the interest and maturity schedule are read in full, the debt conclusion should be treated as a reading of the trend, not a final count.

The third point is the goodwill and asset line. The company has recorded no goodwill impairment in 2024 or 2025 [5], so the losses are not write-downs of past purchases. That is the reason the loss is more persuasive as a signal about the operating business than about accounting. A future write-down would add to the loss, so the reader should look at the operating line first.
