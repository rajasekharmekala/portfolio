This report is a screening file for one company, Indo-MIM Ltd, listed on the NSE under the symbol INDOMIM. It is not investment advice and it does not predict the share price. It answers one question: does the business, its price trend and its price-to-reward arithmetic meet the rules we set before looking at the stock? The answer here is no, so the file explains which rules failed, by how much, and what would have to change for the answer to flip.

The report is built in layers. Layer 1 is a set of four to five gates on quality and price behaviour. Only a company that passes Layer 1 is scored under Layers 2 and 3. Here Layer 1 fails, so the later layers are described but not scored. Read the decision callout first, then the key figures, then the gate table. The gate table shows each rule, the company's value, and PASS or FAIL in plain text.

Return on capital employed (ROCE) is the profit a business earns on all the money tied up in it. Think of a shop that earns 25 rupees a year for every 100 rupees of stock, equipment and cash it runs. A higher figure means the business turns capital into profit well.

Cash from operations divided by profit after tax (CFO/PAT) checks whether reported profit arrives as cash. A ratio near 1.0 means profit is backed by cash. Think of a salary that is actually paid into the bank, not just written on a payslip.

Debt-to-equity compares borrowed money with the owners' capital. A ratio of 0.49 means the company owes about half of what its owners have put in and kept. It is like a home loan that is less than half the value of the house.

The four stages describe where a stock sits in its life cycle. Stage 1 is a quiet base after a fall. Stage 2 is the rising markup phase, when the price trend is up and the long averages slope upward. Stage 3 is a top, and Stage 4 is a breakdown. A stock in Stage 2 is like a train that is climbing steadily, with the tracks still rising.

A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of sessions. The 20-day average shows the short trend; the 50-day and 200-day averages show longer trends. It is a smoothed line that drops the daily noise, like a rolling average of a student's marks.

The relative strength index (RSI) is a 0 to 100 gauge of recent buying and selling pressure. Readings above 70 suggest the price has run hot, and readings below 30 suggest it has been sold hard. A reading of 57.8 is a middle reading, like a thermometer at a comfortable room temperature.

The average true range (ATR) measures a typical daily price swing. An ATR of 67.33 means the stock usually moves about 67 rupees in a day. ATR is a yardstick: a distance of 7.83 ATR is roughly eight typical days of movement away from a reference point.

Test A and Test B are momentum checks. Test A asks whether the stock is in a healthy uptrend that has not run too far. Test B asks whether the stock is near a base, with quiet volume and rising relative strength. Each test has sub-conditions. Failing a single condition is enough to fail a test, and this stock fails both.

Asymmetry is the ratio of upside to the planned target against the risk to the stop. An asymmetry of 3.0 means the planned gain is three times the planned loss. It is like a coin toss that pays three to one. A ratio of 2.21 pays less than the rule asks for.

A stop-loss is the price at which the position would be sold to cap the loss. The rule here places the stop below the lower of the recent swing low and the 50-day and 200-day averages, minus one ATR. It is a fire exit placed just outside the room, not in the middle of it.

An anchored target is a price goal that comes from something measurable, such as forward earnings multiplied by a historical multiple, or a chart measured move. A target without an anchor is a wish. If the target cannot be anchored, the asymmetry test fails automatically.

What the company does

Indo-MIM Ltd makes small, complicated metal parts for other manufacturers. The core process is metal injection molding (MIM). In MIM, metal powder is mixed with a binder, shaped in a mould much like a plastic part, then debinded and sintered in a furnace. The result is a dense metal part with a complex shape that would be expensive to machine. The company started its business in 1996 and describes itself as one of the world's largest MIM makers [4]. It also offers investment casting, ceramic injection molding and 3D metal printing [4]. The company manufactures over 9,000 types of products in fiscal 2026, and its automotive products group makes parts used in vehicle safety and fuel systems [28].

The customer base is spread across sectors. The company reports that it serves more than 1,100 customers across automotive, aerospace, defence, medical and consumer segments, with 91% repeat revenue and 77% of revenue earned offshore, as of 31 Mar 2026 [8]. It shipped about ₹4,321 crore of revenue in FY26, up 28%, with ₹534 crore of profit after tax, up 26%, as of 31 Mar 2026 [8]. The IPO offer summary reports revenue of ₹4,320.70 crore in FY26 against ₹3,373.97 crore in FY25, which is a total-income style figure and not the same line as Screener's sales [26]. The company runs plants across India, the US, the UK and Mexico, and it holds quality accreditations including NADCAP, IATF 16949 and AS9100 [4].

The firearms and defence line is a company-stated part of the business. The company says it has produced more than 3,000 varieties of firearm products over 26 years [23]. That page is the company's own marketing material, so it is a claim, not an independent check. A wholly owned subsidiary for firearm components was reported as formed in December 2025 [28], but the subsidiary page could not be opened, so its scale is not verified. The sector is small in India. The India MIM market reached USD 162.3 million in 2025 [11], and the global MIM market was valued at USD 5.51 billion in 2025 [12]. The company is listed on the NSE and BSE after an IPO that opened on 23 Jul 2026 and listed on 30 Jul 2026 at an issue price of ₹485 [10]. The issue size was about ₹3,811 crore [22]. The shares listed at ₹700.00, a listing gain of 44.33% over the issue price [19], and the issue was subscribed 72.37 times [20]. The issue size was also reported as ₹3,811.21 crore by a second adviser [21].

Layers 2 and 3: not scored

Layers 2 and 3 are the scoring stages of the framework. Layer 2 is a key performance indicator scorecard that rates the business on its operating measures. Layer 3 is the entry posture, which sets the timing and sizing of an entry for a stock that has passed the earlier gates. Neither layer is scored here, because Layer 1 failed. The framework stops at the first failed gate so that a weak case does not look stronger through a detailed scoring exercise. A high Layer 2 score would not rescue a stock that fails the price test, and an entry plan would be meaningless without a passing technical posture. Stopping here is deliberate. It keeps the report honest about what was and was not evaluated. The business quality results, including ROCE and cash conversion, are reported in the Layer 1 section because they are part of the gate. The operating scorecard items that would sit in Layer 2 are not built, and no score is invented for them. The entry posture is also not set, so no entry zone, sizing or staged buying plan is proposed. If the stock later passes Layer 1, the scoring would start from the beginning with a fresh price history and a fresh source check. The report does not carry any Layer 2 or Layer 3 score forward from elsewhere.

Asymmetry and the target

The bull target would be a more optimistic goal. The framework requires an anchored target for the asymmetry to count, and this report anchors the base target on a technical measured move. The anchor is not ideal, because the price history since listing is only about two and a half months long.

The framework is strict on this point. A target that cannot be anchored is treated as unverifiable, and an unverifiable target is an automatic asymmetry failure. This report uses the measured move because it is the only anchor the data allows, and it reports that choice openly rather than hiding it. Fatal flaws: the red-team case

The red-team case is the strongest honest argument against the company, as an independent reviewer would make it. It does not argue against the business quality, which is strong. It argues that the price and the timing are wrong and that the evidence cannot yet support the optimistic case. Each flaw below has evidence and a consequence. A flaw that cannot be sourced is not listed.

The flaws are ordered from the most decisive to the least. The first two concern price, because the price tests decide the verdict. The third and fourth concern supply and guidance, which could change the price path. The fifth concerns the evidence, which limits how confident anyone can be in any single figure.

Flaw one: the price has run far ahead of earnings

The first flaw is valuation. The trailing P/E is 93.9 [1], and the consensus FY27 earnings per share of 18.47 rupees puts the price at about 66 times forward earnings at the ledger close [16]. The average analyst target is 1,139.00 rupees, and one broker's target is 900 rupees [16]. The consequence is that the market is paying for growth well beyond the consensus numbers. If the company misses the FY27 target, the multiple has further to fall, and the stock would need a sharp rerating in the other direction to justify the price. A simple check makes the point. At a forward multiple of about 66 times, the same price would imply about 74 times if earnings came in 10% below consensus, which is a much harder valuation to defend.

Flaw two: the stock is extended with no clean base

The second flaw is the price position. The stock is 7.83 ATR above its base low of 700.0, and it rose 24.63% in the last month. Volume is 0.59 times its 20-day average, so buyers are not confirming the move with heavy trading [10]. The stock has no base in the sense the framework uses, because it has not spent a sustained period building a floor. The consequence is that any entry now would have a stop far below the price, which makes the risk large relative to the reward. The gate outcome, NO_MOMENTUM, reflects this directly. The gate is not measuring a weak company. It is measuring that the price has moved far from the level where a stop could sit close to an entry, and that is the core of the risk problem.

Flaw three: supply from lock-up and a large existing holder

The third flaw is supply. The promoter group holds 77.65% of the company [1], and the holding was fully locked in as of July 2026 [5]. The IPO was mostly an offer for sale of about ₹3,312 crore by existing holders [4]. A lock-up agreement was reported to end on 26 Aug 2026 [16]. The consequence is that a large block of shares can come to market over time. The price may face pressure from that supply even if the business continues to grow. The reported pledge of 0.0% as of 29 Jul 2026 is reassuring, but it is one data point [5]. Investors should watch the next shareholding filings to see whether the promoter stake or the pledge moves once the lock-up has ended.

Flaw four: the guidance is not consistent

The fourth flaw is the forward picture. The company's chief executive reports a 20% FY27 revenue growth target and a 5,000 crore revenue goal [9]. First-quarter revenue growth of 9.4% sits well below a 20% annual target. One quarter is not a trend, but the gap is the kind of thing the market watches closely at each result. Another report says the company guided 10 to 15% growth for FY27 [8]. First-quarter sales grew 9.4% [6], and profit grew faster because the margin widened. The chief executive also credits part of the margin gain to the weaker rupee. The consequence is that the profit story depends on two things that may not last: a currency tailwind and a margin level the company itself calls a long-term band of 28 to 30% [9].

Flaw five: the history is short and some data disagree

The fifth flaw is the quality of the evidence itself. The stock has only about two and a half months of trading history, so no three-year valuation range, no 200-day trend and no full stage read exist yet. Several sources disagree on basic figures. The FY24 profit is 352 crore on Screener and 284 crore in the offer summary [1][3]. The Q1 revenue figure of 10,031.10 million is the standalone number, not the consolidated figure of 12,187.42 million that most sources quote [18]. The consequence is that the company's history is still being established in public. A decision that depends on precise historical ratios should wait until the audited figures can be checked directly. Until then, every ratio in this file carries some uncertainty, and the report says so each time a conflict matters.

The verdict is REJECTED. The framework requires Layer 1 to pass before any allocation, and this stock fails two Layer 1 gates, the technical posture and the asymmetry. The rule that triggered the verdict is the Layer 1 rule that stops the process at the first failure. The action is no position, no entry plan and no staged buying. The report does not recommend a short position, which is outside the framework's scope.

The upgrade level has two parts, and both must hold. The first is a price at or below 1153.6, which would bring the asymmetry to 3.0 on the same measured-move target. The second is a technical posture that passes, which means a Stage 1 breakout or a Stage 2 markup, with volume expanding. Price alone is not enough. Glossary

ROCE, return on capital employed: operating profit divided by the capital used in the business, shown as a percentage. ROE, return on equity: profit divided by owners' capital. CFO, cash from operations: cash the core business generates, before investment and financing. PAT, profit after tax: the profit left after all costs and tax. CFO/PAT: cash divided by profit over the same period. OPM, operating profit margin: operating profit as a share of sales. EPS, earnings per share: profit divided by the number of shares. P/E, price-to-earnings ratio: the price divided by earnings per share. Debt-to-equity: borrowings divided by owners' capital. Stage 1 to 4: the four phases of a stock's trend, from base to markup to top to breakdown. Moving average: the average closing price over a set number of sessions. SMA: simple moving average. RSI, relative strength index: a 0 to 100 gauge of recent momentum. ATR, average true range: the typical daily price swing. Swing low: a recent local low in the price. Base: a period of sideways trading after a fall. Base pivot: the level a stock must clear to break out of its base. Relative strength: the stock's performance compared with a benchmark. Measured move: a technical target found by adding the height of a range to its breakout level. Asymmetry: planned upside divided by planned risk. Stop-loss: the price at which a position is exited to cap the loss. Anchored target: a target built from a measured input, not a wish. Lock-up: a period during which existing holders agree not to sell. Offer for sale: an IPO component in which existing holders sell shares and receive the proceeds. Pledge: shares given as security for a loan.
