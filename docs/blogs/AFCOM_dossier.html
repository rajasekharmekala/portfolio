The report uses a framework with three layers. Layer 1 is a set of hard gates. A company must pass every gate to be a candidate. The gates are return on capital employed (ROCE, the profit a business earns on the money invested in it, expressed as a percentage), debt-to-equity (how much the company borrows compared with what its owners have put in), three-year cash conversion (CFO divided by profit, summed over three years; CFO means cash from operations, the cash the business actually collects from its work), a technical posture gate (the price trend), and an asymmetry gate. Asymmetry is the ratio of what you can win to what you can lose. If a gate fails, the company is not tested on the later layers. Layer 2 scores three to five operating measures, and Layer 3 checks where the price sits relative to support. Here Layer 1 fails, so Layers 2 and 3 are not scored.

Several ideas recur through the report. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of sessions, like a smoothed trend line. The 20-day average is short-term; the 50-day and 200-day averages are medium and long term. Weinstein stages describe the four phases of a stock: Stage 1 is a base, Stage 2 is a rising markup, Stage 3 is a topping or distribution phase, and Stage 4 is a decline. A stock in Stage 2 that trades above its rising 50-day and 200-day averages passes the technical gate for a Tactical holding. ATR, the average true range, measures a normal daily swing. We use it as a yardstick: a distance of one ATR is one normal day's movement. RSI, the relative strength index, is a speed gauge from 0 to 100. Readings near 50 are neutral.

Stops and sizing are the protective rules. A stop is a price at which the position is exited without debate. For a Tactical holding, the stop sits below the lower of the recent swing low and the 20-day or 50-day average, minus half an ATR. The report also shows how much money would be lost if the stop were hit. Position size matters because no single holding should be able to hurt the whole portfolio badly. AFCOM is about 3.7% of the portfolio, so the money at risk to its stop is small in portfolio terms, but the position still carries a loss today.

What the company does

Afcom Holdings is a cargo airline. It flies freight, not passengers. The company was incorporated in February 2013 and describes its business as transporting cargo on an airport-to-airport basis [1]. It is Chennai based. The rating agency describes an own fleet of freighter aircraft, with two dry-leased aircraft driving much of its growth. It carries general cargo, high-value shipments, perishables, pharmaceuticals, express and specialised cargo, and it is expanding into domestic and ASEAN markets [5]. A simple way to see the business is a dedicated lane. The company rents or owns the aircraft, sells space on its flights to freight customers, and earns a fee per kilogram. Its costs are fuel, aircraft lease payments, crews, airport charges and handling.

The fleet and the numbers that drive it

Freighter economics depend on how many flights an aircraft completes and how full each one is. The company's own investor presentation, summarised by Arthneeti, describes a two-aircraft base and an average of 19 weekly trips per aircraft [3]. The same source reports cargo volume of 24,353 tonnes for FY26 and a cargo yield of ₹270.59 per kilogram in Q1 FY27, up 13.7% from a year earlier and above an IATA Asia-Pacific benchmark of ₹238 [3]. Yield is the price per kilogram. Higher yield with steady trips means more revenue per flight.

The fleet plan is the main reason the company has raised capital. The presentation mentions up to five Boeing 737-800 freighters for regional routes and a longer-term plan for a Boeing 777 freighter, with no current operations on that type [3]. Each new aircraft adds lease cost before it adds revenue. That timing matters for the cash gate discussed below. A freighter that is paid for in advance, or leased on terms that make early payments large, can make operating cash look weak even while the business grows.

Routes and partners

The most visible new route is Navi Mumbai to Dubai World Central, which began on 17 August 2026 according to a filing reported by Tulsian and Sahi [12] [14] [15]. One trade report gives the start as 16 August, so the date has a one-day conflict. The same report describes three weekly freighter flights, each carrying up to 20 tonnes [13]. Fuel is the second cost that decides margins. On 1 September 2026, the company's long-term aviation fuel supply agreement with Hindustan Petroleum Corporation became effective. It follows IATA model terms and covers HPCL's aviation fuelling network across India [16]. A fixed supply route reduces the risk of running short at an airport and gives some control over fuel access. It does not remove the price of jet fuel, which passes through to customers only to the extent that rates allow.

A ROCE of 28.0% means the company earned about 28 paise a year for every rupee of capital employed, measured the way the data provider defines it. Gate 2: debt-to-equity fails

Debt-to-equity compares what the company owes with what its owners have invested, including retained profit. A ratio of 0.88 means the company owes 88 paise of borrowing for every rupee of owners' capital. The ceiling for this framework is 0.70, so the company is over the limit by 0.18. In rupee terms, borrowings would need to fall by about ₹81 crore, from ₹401 crore to about ₹320 crore, to reach 0.70 on the same equity base of ₹457 crore [1].

Gate 3: cash conversion fails

Cash conversion asks whether reported profit turns into cash. A profitable company that books sales it has not yet collected, or that spends cash on aircraft deposits, can show high profit and low cash at the same time. The framework uses three years of data to smooth out one-off timing. The company's cash from operations was ₹31 crore in FY24, minus ₹45 crore in FY25 and ₹36 crore in FY26. Profit after tax was ₹25 crore, ₹37 crore and ₹122 crore in the same years [1]. Summed, that is ₹22 crore of operating cash against ₹184 crore of profit, a ratio of 0.12. The floor is 0.70.

The gap is about ₹107 crore. To reach 0.70 on the same three years, the company would have needed about ₹129 crore of operating cash, which is ₹107 crore more than it reported. Free cash flow, which deducts capital spending from operating cash, is worse. It was ₹31 crore in FY24, minus ₹290 crore in FY25 and minus ₹76 crore in FY26, a three-year total of minus ₹335 crore [1]. That spending went into the fleet. The fleet is the reason for the growth, so the cash gap is not hidden waste, but it is a real cost that the owners have funded through equity and borrowing.

Debtor days also matter. Screener reports 85 debtor days for March 2026, up from 56 in March 2024 and below the 118 days of March 2022 [1]. Debtor days are the number of days it takes customers to pay. Eighty-five days is long for an operator that sells capacity in advance. The reading does not prove a problem, but it points the same way as the cash gap.

Gate 4: technical posture fails

The technical gate for a Tactical holding asks two things. First, is the price in a Stage 2 markup, meaning above a rising 50-day average and a rising 200-day average? Second, has it avoided a Stage 4 breakdown or a death cross, where the 50-day falls below the 200-day? AFCOM passes the second part and fails the first. The close on 9 October 2026 was 1,460.60. The 50-day average was 1,505.55 and was still rising, but the price sat 44.95 rupees below it, or 0.63 ATR. The 200-day average was 1,075.31 and was rising, so the long-term trend is up, and the stock is 35.8% above that line (ledger). The ledger classifies the stage as Stage 3, which the ledger labels Topping or Pullback.

What does Stage 3 mean for the holding? It means the stock has already had its markup and is now in a phase where buyers and sellers are more evenly matched. Price tends to move sideways or fall back, and the 50-day average often turns from a support line into a resistance line. The ledger shows that the close is below the 50-day, which is the signature of that phase. The stock is not in Stage 4, because the 50-day remains above the 200-day and the close is above the 200-day. The Layer 1 technical test is therefore a fail on the posture requirement, but not a breakdown. The distinction matters for the verdict below.

Gate 5: asymmetry fails

The asymmetry floor asks whether the potential gain is large compared with the potential loss, and whether the target has an anchor that can be checked. For a Tactical holding the floor is 3.5. The formula in the framework is the base target minus the current price, divided by the price minus the stop. Using the stop from the tactical rule and the 52-week high as the target, the ratio is 1.21. A reward of 250.40 rupees is set against a risk of 206.44 rupees. The company would need a target of about 2,183 rupees to reach 3.5, which is 472 rupees above the 52-week high.

The target choice is generous to the stock. We set it at the 52-week high of 1,711.00 because that is a level the stock has already traded at and it is verifiable in the ledger. A measured-move target that extends the range from the 63-day low to the high gives 2,132.05. Even that bull figure gives only 3.25 against the 3.5 floor. The result is therefore a fail on either target. A measured move is a target found by adding the depth of a base to the level where the base ends. The ledger does not contain a 63-day base high, so we could not compute a textbook base measured move, and that limitation is stated in the run notes.

Momentum test

The momentum gate is a second test that every candidate must pass before it can receive an allocation. The asymmetry table shows the calculation, and the result is the same at every step. The base target gives 1.21, the bull target gives 3.25, and the floor is 3.5. The required target of about 2,183 is far above the 52-week high, so the gate would pass only if the stock made a new high well beyond anything it has reached. The stop is the other input to check. It is set by a rule, not by a view on the company, and it sits 206.44 rupees below the close. A wider stop would give a smaller ratio. A tighter stop would give a larger ratio, but it would be more likely to be hit on a normal day, since one ATR is 71.59 rupees.

The sensitivity to the price is also worth noting. At the 12 October close of 1,490.00, the base ratio falls to 0.94, because the risk grows. At the 13 October quote of 1,508, it falls to 0.80. The stop does not move with the price in this framework, so a higher price means more rupees at risk and a smaller ratio. That is why the asymmetry gate gets harder for a stock that rises into a fixed target. A reader might expect the opposite. It is a feature of the formula, and it is a reason to be cautious about buying into a stock near the top of its range.

Here is the same arithmetic in your own money. Suppose you sold the 120 shares at the stop of 1,254.16. You would receive 1,50,499. The cost was 1,94,400, so the loss against cost is 43,901. If the stock instead recovered to the 52-week high of 1,711.00, the position would be worth 2,05,320, a gain of 10,920 against cost. The stop therefore protects you from a larger loss, but it also locks in a loss of about 22.6% against your average purchase price. That is the cost of a stop that sits below a position bought at a much higher level.

A reader can run the same check on any price. Enter the close, the stop and the target, and the ratio follows. At a close of 1,400, the risk to the stop would be 145.84 rupees and the base reward 311.00, a ratio of 2.13. The ratio rises as the price falls, which is the reverse of the chase-the-trend intuition. That is why the framework treats a falling price as a potential opportunity only when the trend and cash tests also pass. A low price with a failed balance sheet is a cheap stock for a reason, not a bargain. The next chapter sets out the run notes and the gate exceptions that qualify all of this.

What would change this view:

  • If the close rose to 1,711.00, the base target would no longer offer any reward, so the target would have to be re-set on a new high before the ratio could be measured again.
  • If the stop were moved up to the 50-day average after a confirmed Stage 2 reclaim, the risk per share would shrink and the ratio would rise, though the framework sets the stop by rule, not by preference.

Glossary

ATR (average true range) is the average daily price swing over 14 sessions, in rupees. One ATR is a normal day's movement, and it is used to judge how far a price is from a level. Asymmetry is the ratio of reward to risk for a position, measured against a stop. CFO (cash from operations) is the cash a business generates from its day-to-day work, before spending on assets. CFO / PAT divides that cash by profit after tax; a ratio near 1 means profit is turning into cash. D/E (debt-to-equity) is borrowings divided by owners' equity. DMA means the day moving average, the average closing price over a number of sessions. Free cash flow is operating cash minus spending on fixed assets. PAT (profit after tax) is the bottom-line profit. QIP (qualified institutions placement) is a share sale to institutional investors. ROCE (return on capital employed) is operating profit divided by capital employed. ROE (return on equity) is profit divided by owners' equity. RSI (relative strength index) is a 0 to 100 measure of how fast a price has risen or fallen; readings near 50 are neutral. Stage 2 to Stage 4 are the four phases of a stock's trend described in the framework.

The method for this dossier followed a fixed order. First, the ledger was read for every technical figure. Second, the research records were built from pages that had been fetched into the execution cache, and each quoted passage was checked against the cached text before it was accepted. Third, every derived figure was calculated from recorded inputs and the arithmetic is shown in the tables.
