What the company does

Interarch makes pre-engineered buildings, which are steel frames and roof-and-wall panels cut in a factory and bolted together on site. Think of a flat-pack shed for a factory or warehouse: most of the work happens under a roof, so the site work is quicker and more predictable. The company describes itself as the second-largest player of this kind in India by installed capacity.[1] The company is listed on both the NSE and the BSE, and its brands include TRACDEK roofing and cladding systems.[4][5] The business is a manufacturing and project business, so its profit depends on how many plants run, how fast orders turn into sales, and how much cash the projects collect.

Why the business is strong or weak

The underwriting framework runs three layers, one after another. Layer 1 is a set of hard gates: a company must pass every one or it is rejected, and no score can make up for a failed gate. Layer 2 is a short scorecard of the operating numbers that drive this particular business. Layer 3 is the timing check, which asks whether the price is near a support level where a buyer can enter with a defined loss limit.[30]

Layer 1: the hard gates

The first gate is about returns. ROCE, or return on capital employed, is the operating profit a business earns on all the money tied up in it, both owners' money and borrowed money. The second gate is about borrowing. Debt-to-equity is the amount of borrowed money divided by the owners' money, so a low number means the company runs on its own funds. The third gate is about cash. Cash flow from operations (CFO) is the cash a business actually collects after paying its running costs, and CFO divided by reported profit (PAT, profit after tax) shows whether the profit has turned into cash.[30]

The fourth gate is about the price trend. A base is a sideways stretch where a stock stops falling and builds a floor. Stage 2 is the rising phase that follows a base, and Stage 4 is the falling phase in which the price sits below its long averages and those averages are falling. The fifth gate asks whether the stock has a target that can be checked. Asymmetry is the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss. A measured move is a target found by adding the height of a base to the level the base breaks above. The other accepted route is forward earnings times a three-year median price-to-earnings multiple, which is the share price divided by earnings per share. If no target can be checked, the gate fails.[29][30]

Read each row as one question with a hurdle. The hurdle is the minimum (or maximum) the framework allows. The reading is what the sources show. The result is a plain pass or a named fail, so a fail points to the exact reason. A single named fail is enough to reject the company as a new buy.

Cash conversion, the number that fails

Cash conversion is the test that separates a profitable business from a business that books profit it does not collect. Working capital is the money tied up in stock and unpaid bills while a project is underway, and it is one of the main reasons profit and cash diverge. Capital expenditure is the money spent on new plants and machines, and it also uses cash before any return arrives. Interarch's plant build-out shows both forces at work, with capital expenditure of about ₹1.19 billion in the latest twelve months.[10]

The number

Three-year CFO/PAT for FY23 to FY25, from the company's own audited statements, is 0.60.[3]

How it was calculated

Add the three years of cash from operations, then divide by the three years of profit after tax. In crore, CFO is 31.35 plus 81.52 plus 53.56, which is 166.43. PAT is 81.46 plus 86.26 plus 107.83, which is 275.55. Then 166.43 divided by 275.55 gives 0.60.[3]

What Stage 4 means for a holder

Stage 4 is the phase in which a stock trades below its long averages and those averages slope down. The rule is simple: buyers are not in control, and each rally tends to meet sellers. Interarch fits this definition on the 12 October close, with the price 10.9 percent below its 200-day average and the 200-day average itself lower than it was 63 sessions ago.[26] A stock in this phase may still bounce, and the 28-day relative strength is a small sign of that. The framework does not buy bounces, because a bounce inside a Stage 4 trend is the typical way a holder gets trapped.

Three fatal-flaw scenarios

A fatal-flaw scenario is a way the thesis could break completely, not just underperform. The first is that cash never catches up with profit. Profit grew about 19 percent a year, but operating cash flow turned negative in FY26.[1][13] If the next annual results show the same pattern, the company's profit becomes harder to trust, and the market is likely to keep paying a lower multiple. The confirming number is the cash row at the FY27 annual results.

The second is that the order book does not refill. The book of ₹1,850 crore covers less than one year of trailing sales.[17][1] If new orders slow while the new plants are running, utilisation falls, and the guidance of ₹2,700 crore for FY28 becomes a number that management has to cut.[17] A cut would reset the growth story that supports the current multiple. The confirming signal is a quarterly order book below ₹1,850 crore.

The third is that the capital structure and governance weaken together. The company received a Punjab GST demand of ₹8.30 crore in October 2026, and its president for corporate finance and strategy exits on 14 October 2026.[2] Unverified reports of a share sale to institutions and of a proposed face-value sub-division could not be confirmed from any page we could cache, so neither is used in any figure. If equity is added while cash conversion is weak, the return on capital falls because the same profit is spread over more shareholders' money. The management exit and the tax demand do not change the cash arithmetic and are not weighed in the verdict. They are a reason not to add to the position, and they are listed as governance risks.

Base case, bull case and bear case

The base case is the most likely path on the evidence we have: the order book stays near ₹1,850 crore, profit stays flat for a while, and the price trades between the 52-week low of ₹1,600 and the 200-day average near ₹1,878 until the next results. Share issues and corporate actions

Dilution is the fall in each existing shareholder's share of the company when new shares are issued. A share sale to institutions dilutes existing holders unless the cash raised earns more than the company's current return. A face-value sub-division changes the number of shares and the price per share together, so it does not change the value of a holding by itself. Neither event is quantified here. Before any sale, confirm with the broker that the holding is still 250 shares at the quoted price, because a change in share count would rescale the order size and every loss figure below.

The verdict and the rule behind it

The framework has four verdicts for a holding: keep and compound, accumulate on a dip, trim for profit, and replace an underperformer. The rule for replacement is clear: a holding that has broken its structural support, is in Stage 4, or has an impaired thesis becomes an immediate candidate for a capital swap.[30] Interarch meets the Stage 4 test on the ledger, and its cash conversion failure is the sort of thesis impairment the rule describes. The verdict is therefore REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER.[26][30] This is not a view that the company is a bad business. It earns a good return on capital. Demand is not the problem: the order book is growing and sales rose in the June quarter. The verdict rests on cash and trend, so the demand evidence is real but does not offset the cash gate. The verdict is that the capital is better held in cash until a gated challenger is found, because the benefit of a swap is not yet shown.[17][1]

The verdict moves back toward a hold only if the close rises above the 200-day average of ₹1,877.54 for two consecutive closes, the 50-day average turns up above ₹1,716.26, and the next annual cash row brings the three-year CFO/PAT to 0.70 or above.[26][29]

The ₹1,580.00 stop is the exit level for the remaining shares: the standing sell order fires on the first trade at or below ₹1,580.00, with no second confirmation.[28]

Action: exit to cash pending a challenger

The verdict is REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER, and the action is to exit to cash pending a challenger. This dossier does not name a challenger, because no challenger has been gated here, so the benefit of a swap is not demonstrated. The exit is staged so that the decision does not rest on a single price. Confirm the share count with the broker first, then work through three steps.

  1. Sell 125 shares at the next session's open, at market. This is tranche one.
  2. Sell the other 125 shares on the first close below ₹1,625.00, or after the results on 28 October 2026, whichever comes first.
  3. Keep a standing sell order at ₹1,580.00 for whatever shares remain. It fires on the first trade at or below that level.

The two levels have different jobs. ₹1,625.00 is 0.96 ATR below the close and is the breakdown test. ₹1,580.00 is 1.86 ATR below the close and is the exit level.[28][29] The arithmetic explains the staging. Holding back 125 shares until the break or the results exposes them to a fall to ₹1,580.00, which would cost ₹11,662.50 more than selling them today at ₹1,673.30 (125 times ₹93.30). Selling all 250 today removes that risk but gives up any chance that the ₹1,625.00 support holds. Set the expiry of any sell order to the number of sessions you are prepared to wait, and review it if the price gaps through the level.

Worked example. If you sell all 250 shares at ₹1,673.30, you receive ₹418,325.00. Against a cost of ₹439,813.59, that is a loss of ₹21,488.59. If the price slides to the stop of ₹1,580.00 before you sell, and the order fills at that level, you would receive ₹395,000.00, a loss of ₹44,813.59. A gap down can fill lower. The extra ₹23,325.00 is the extra loss from waiting if the price falls to the stop, which is why the first tranche goes at the next open.[27][28]

Interarch makes steel buildings for factories and warehouses, and it is a respectable operator with a good return on capital.[1] You hold it because the stored rows once recommended accumulating it at support, but today's ledger shows the price in a Stage 4 decline, with the 200-day average falling and the price 10.9 percent below it.[26] It fails the cash and price-trend gates, its target gate cannot be tested, and the verdict follows the rule for a replacement. Two closes above ₹1,877.54 with a rising 50-day average would trigger a re-underwrite, and a first trade at or below ₹1,580.00 triggers the standing exit.[28]
