Salesforce sells the software that large companies use to run sales, service and marketing, and it now sells AI "agents" on top of that data. The business is excellent by most measures: it turns 34.5 cents of every revenue dollar into free cash flow.[1][2] We still reject it. It pays its staff 8.34% of revenue in stock, and our written ceiling is 5.0%;[1][2] The $25B buyback and the $24.8B debt that funded it also push the return on capital below our 12.0% floor;[1][11] At $229.13 the reward-to-risk is about 0.5 to 1 against a floor of 3.0 to 1; Three of six hard gates fail, so no money is allocated and the name goes to the archive with a reconsider trigger;

1. What the company does

Salesforce, Inc. is a San Francisco software company listed on the New York Stock Exchange.[16] It rents software over the internet. Customers pay a subscription and the company keeps renewing the contract. The company reports as one segment.[1]

Where the revenue comes from

In the quarter to 2026-07-31 revenue was $11,345M. Subscription and support was $10,820M and professional services $525M.[4] The company now splits subscription revenue into two groups: Agentforce Apps ($7,193M, up 8% in constant currency) and Data 360, Headless Platform and Other ($3,618M, up 20%).[4] By region the quarter was Americas $7,404M, Europe $2,764M and Asia Pacific $1,168M.[4]

The AI bet

Management says Agentforce plus Data 360 annual recurring revenue (ARR (annual recurring revenue) (annual recurring revenue)) is about $3.9B, up 210%. Agentforce alone is above $1.5B, up 240%.[4] It also reports 7.0B "agentic work units" to date, of which 3.2B came in the latest quarter.[4] These are company figures, not audited ones. The hard number is the growth of contracted revenue, covered in chapter 3.

Acquisitions that change the picture

Informatica joined the group in fiscal 2027 and added $456M of revenue in the latest quarter. Management says it adds about 3 points to full-year growth.[4][3] Two more deals are pending and expected to close in the quarter ending 2026-10-31: Contentful for about $1.5B in cash and Intercom ("Fin") for about $3.6B in cash.[1]

What this means for an owner. You own a subscription business with very sticky customers and a growing AI add-on. Part of the reported growth is bought, not built. Chapter 2 asks how strong the franchise is once that is stripped out.

Plain-English glossary

  • ARR: annual recurring revenue, the yearly value of live subscriptions.
  • cRPO: current remaining performance obligation, the contracted revenue due in the next 12 months.
  • SBC: stock-based compensation, pay given to staff in shares.
  • FCF (free cash flow): free cash flow, operating cash flow minus capital spending.
  • ROIC: return on invested capital, after-tax operating profit divided by the money tied up in the business.
  • ASR: accelerated share repurchase, a bank delivers shares up front and the company pays the full price at once.
  • ATR: average true range, the typical daily price swing, used to measure distance from support.
  • EBITDA: operating profit plus depreciation and amortization.

2. Why the business is strong, and where it is weak

The strengths are real and measurable. The weaknesses are mostly about how the owners are paid and how the buyback was financed. Each point below names its evidence.

Strengths

  • Sticky customers. The attrition rate, which is the share of annualized contract value lost, was about 8% at 2024-01-31, 2025-01-31, 2026-01-31 and 2026-07-31 (the last one excludes Slack self-service, Informatica and current-year deals).[8][7][2][1] The company does not publish net revenue retention, so that figure is N/A.
  • Cash conversion. TTM operating cash flow of $15,750M less $596M of capital spending gives $15,154M of free cash flow, a 34.5% margin. Chapter 3 shows the working.[1][2][6]
  • Backlog is speeding up. cRPO was $29.4B at July 2025 (+11%), $33.6B at April 2026 (+14%) and $33.5B at July 2026 (+14% in constant currency).[19][6][4]
  • Margins are rising. Operating margin went from 2.1% in fiscal 2022 to 20.1% in fiscal 2026.[17]
  • Long-term targets. At its 2026-09-16 Investor Day the company repeated a fiscal 2030 revenue target of $63B or more, which implies an 11% or better annual growth rate including Informatica.[5]

Weaknesses

  • Stock pay is high. SBC was $3,509M in fiscal 2026, 8.45% of revenue, and management guides fiscal 2027 SBC to about 9.0% of revenue.[17][3] Shares were bought back to offset it, which costs cash that owners would otherwise keep.
  • Growth that is bought. Revenue grew about 11% in the latest quarter, but $456M of it is Informatica.[4] Strip that out and quarterly revenue is $10,889M against $10,236M a year earlier, about 6% growth by our arithmetic.[4][19] Management cites about 8% for Agentforce Apps in constant currency.[4]
  • Debt is now large. Long-term debt rose from $14.4B (2026-01-31) to $39.3B (2026-07-31). Quarterly interest expense was $473M against $67M a year earlier.[2][4]
  • Reported profit is flattered. Latest-quarter GAAP earnings per share of $4.29 include a $2.43 gain on strategic investments. Non-GAAP EPS (earnings per share) (earnings per share) is $5.90.[4]
  • Leadership change. The President and Chief Engineering and Customer Success Officer stepped down effective 2026-08-06 and becomes an adviser through 2027-08-06.[14]

What this means for an owner. The franchise is not the problem. The price of keeping it is: staff are paid heavily in shares, and a debt-funded buyback hides that cost while it lowers the return on capital. Chapter 3 puts numbers on each.

6. Why these estimates might be wrong

A bear case on our own rejection is useful. The three scenarios below each name a fact that would change the verdict.

Flaw 1: Stock pay may fall faster than guided

SBC was 10.5% of revenue in FY22 and 8.0% in FY24.[17] The company guides to about 9.0% for FY27.[3] If a large part of the headcount shrinks as AI agents take work, SBC could drop. Getting below 5.0% would need a cut of roughly 40% from today's rate. No filing supports that, so the gate stands.

Flaw 2: The buyback may be worth more than we credit

Management says the ASR, bought at an expected average of $182, returns over 40% on the money invested at the 2026-08-31 price.[5] If the share count truly falls at least 14%, per-share cash flow rises faster than our base assumes and the bull case becomes likelier. We used 800M shares, which already counts a large part of that.

Flaw 3: Our multiples are assumptions

We set 16.5, 20 and 12 times SBC-adjusted free cash flow without a verified three-year median. If the market pays 22 times for AI-driven growth, the base target would be near $329 and the ratio near 2.7 on the rule stop. We think that is not a base case, but it is the single claim our asymmetry result depends on. Even then the SBC and ROIC gates would still fail.

Other risks to the thesis

  • Integration. Informatica, Contentful and Fin add to goodwill, which is already $59.3B.[4][1]
  • Pace of organic growth. Ex-Informatica growth is about 6% on our math and about 8% for Apps in constant currency.[4][19]
  • Reporting quality. GAAP EPS includes $2,613M of strategic investment gains in the quarter.[4]
  • Governance. A new executive deferred compensation plan was approved on 2026-09-02, and its terms are in the filing.[15] We do not draw a conclusion from it. Proxy statements for 2025 and 2026 are on file, but we did not extract executive pay figures from them, so that detail is N/A.[9][10]

What this means for an owner. The rejection rests on two hard facts (SBC and ROIC) and one judgment (the target). The facts do not depend on our assumptions. Chapter 7 states the action.
