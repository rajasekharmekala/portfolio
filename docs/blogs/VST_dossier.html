What the company does

Vistra sells electricity and natural gas to retail customers and also generates power. It operates in 18 states and the District of Columbia. It serves about 5 million homes and businesses. Its power plants total about 44,000 megawatts (MW) of natural gas, nuclear, coal, solar and battery storage. The company reports five segments: Retail, Texas, East, West and Asset Closure.[1] Its nuclear fleet is six units at four sites. Comanche Peak Units 1 and 2 sit in ERCOT, the Texas grid. Beaver Valley Units 1 and 2, Perry and Davis-Besse sit in PJM, the mid-Atlantic and Ohio grid. Together the six units total 6,448 MW.[1]

In the second quarter of 2026, segment Adjusted EBITDA — earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating cash profit — was Retail $773 million, Texas $311 million, East $642 million, West $68 million, Corporate and Other -$27 million, and Asset Closure -$23 million. The first five lines add to the $1,767 million total for ongoing operations; the Asset Closure segment is excluded from that total.[2] Retail is the steadiest segment. Texas and East are the wholesale generation segments. They carry the most exposure to power prices, weather, and, increasingly, data-center demand.

Two deals dominate the near-term story. Vistra finished buying 2,600 MW of gas plants from Lotus Infrastructure Partners in October 2025. The plants sit in PJM, New England, New York and California.[7] In January 2026 Vistra agreed to buy Cogentrix Energy's 5,500 MW of gas plants across PJM, New England and ERCOT. The net price is about $4.0 billion: $2.3 billion cash, $0.9 billion in stock (5 million shares at $185 each), and $1.5 billion of assumed debt, less $0.7 billion of expected tax benefits. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, or FERC, approved the deal on 2026-08-06. It required Vistra to sell three conventional gas plants totaling 752 MW in New England and New York, including the 541 MW Casco Bay facility in Maine, to a buyer group of Northampton Capital Partners and Olympus Power. The trade-press article on this approval is paywalled and only its preview was read, so any market-monitor concerns it describes are not captured in this run.[5][6][41] Separately, Vistra signed 20-year power-purchase deals for nuclear power: 1,200 MW of Comanche Peak output for Amazon Web Services, and 2,176 MW of Perry and Davis-Besse output for Meta. First deliveries are phased from December 2026 through 2034.[8]

This report uses a short list of terms. Each is defined here, at its first use.

RSI is the relative strength index — a 0-to-100 gauge of how hard a price has risen or fallen over the last 14 days. Vistra's reading is 64.42.[34]

ATR is the average true range — the typical size of a stock's daily price swing. For Vistra it is $7.01.[34]

ROIC is return on invested capital. It is operating profit after tax, called NOPAT (net operating profit after tax), divided by the money that shareholders and lenders have put into the business, called invested capital (equity plus debt, minus cash).

FCF is free cash flow — the cash left from operations after capital spending. Vistra also reports its own "Adjusted FCFbG," or free cash flow before growth capital. That is a different, company-defined measure. It leaves out growth spending and nuclear fuel financing items. This report treats the two measures as separate and does not swap one for the other.

SBC is stock-based compensation — share awards paid to staff instead of cash.

EPS is earnings per share — profit divided by the share count.

The 20-day moving average, the 50-day moving average and the 200-day moving average are the mean closing price over the last 20, 50 and 200 trading sessions.

Stage 2 is the markup phase of a stock, when the price trades above a rising long-term trend line. Vistra instead sits in Stage 3 — a topping or pullback phase, when a stock has stalled after a Stage 2 run and its trend lines have not yet picked a new direction.[34] Stage 4 is the markdown phase, when the price trades below a falling long-term trend line.

A base is a quiet price range where a stock rests before its next big move. A pivot is the exact price at the edge of a base where a stock is expected to break out. A breakout is a move above a pivot on strong volume. A retest is a return to a prior breakout or average level to check that it still holds as support.

A stop loss is a preset price at which a position is sold to cap the loss. A limit order is an order to buy only at or below a stated price. The entry zone is the price range in which a purchase is allowed.

Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the ratio of the expected gain to a target over the expected loss to the stop.

Net debt is total debt minus cash. Guidance is the range of results management expects for a coming period. A buyback is a company's purchase of its own shares from the market.

A PPA, or power purchase agreement, is a long-term contract. A buyer agrees to pay for a generator's electricity, its capacity to generate, or both, usually for 10 to 20 years.

A capacity auction is a market process run by a grid operator, such as PJM. It pays generators in advance to be available in a future year, separate from what they earn selling the electricity itself.

A hedge, here, is a forward sale of power or fuel that locks in a price before delivery. It lowers exposure to swings in the spot market.

Why the business is strong or weak

The strength is real, and it is getting bigger. Vistra's nuclear and gas fleet sits inside two hot demand stories: Texas data-center load and PJM capacity scarcity. Management still expects yearly load growth of 4% to 6% in ERCOT and 2% to 3% in PJM through 2030. Data centers should become a bigger driver from 2028.[9] PJM's 2027/2028 capacity auction cleared at the FERC-permitted maximum of $333 per megawatt-day. That is the highest clearing price on record. It directly lifts the value of Vistra's East-segment fleet and its Beaver Valley, Perry and Davis-Besse nuclear units.[10] The AWS and Meta nuclear contracts, plus the new Helix Digital Infrastructure venture with KKR, the Kuwait Investment Authority and NVIDIA (Vistra's initial commitment up to $1.0 billion), stretch that demand story into the 2030s.[8][3]

The weakness is that the near-term Texas picture has softened, while the balance sheet absorbs two large, debt-funded deals at once. On the second-quarter call, the chief financial officer said current ERCOT forward prices sit below the levels Vistra used to build its $7.4 billion to $7.8 billion 2027 "opportunity" range. He said Vistra is trending toward the low end of that range, even after counting higher PJM prices, hedges and nuclear tax-credit protection. Cogentrix and the Meta contract are not yet counted in that range.[9] A Wells Fargo analyst also asked about a possible multi-month pause in Texas data-center permit reviews. Management does not expect its own late-2027 Comanche Peak delivery date to move, but the exchange shows the data-center pipeline carries real risk.[9] Total debt rose from $17.0 billion at 2025-12-31 to $19.6 billion at 2026-06-30. That is before Cogentrix adds its own $1.5 billion of assumed debt and $2.3 billion of cash consideration.[21]

Why these estimates might be wrong

Estimate 1 depends most on the choice to anchor the central case to today's own 10.2 times multiple, rather than to a historical average. Vistra's multiple has moved a lot over the past three years, as the AI-power story took hold and then partly unwound. The evidence that would prove this anchor wrong is the multiple merchant generators trade at after the Cogentrix deal closes, and after the third-quarter 2026 call updates 2026 and 2027 guidance, which management said would come "subject to Cogentrix's closing timetable."[9]

Estimate 2 depends most on the stop. A wider stop lowers position size but does not flip the ratio's direction. Using the 200-day average of $154.74 as a stop instead would shrink the loss to $6.74 and mechanically inflate every ratio, to 6.2 to 1 on the high case. That is why this report uses the scoring rule's own tactical formula — swing low or short-term averages, minus half an ATR — rather than a level picked to make the math look better. The weak spot in the fundamentals is the gap between GAAP free cash flow and the company's "Adjusted FCFbG." A reader who accepts the company's own guided $3.925-4.725 billion FCFbG as the cash yardstick, rather than the $1,318 million of 2025 GAAP free cash flow used here, would score the FCF-margin gate in chapter 3 very differently.[4]

Two other lenses could soften the payoff test, and neither changes the result. First, management's 2027 midpoint opportunity range for Adjusted EBITDA is $7.4 billion to $7.8 billion. At the same 10.196 times multiple and today's net debt, that range gives about $167.55 to $179.69 per share, so the payoff is 0.19 to 0.58 to 1 against the $31.55 risk. Second, the MarketBeat average analyst target of $217.61 would give 1.78 to 1. Both stay far below the 3.5 floor.[9][11]

The net debt gate also needs a caveat. The pending Cogentrix purchase adds about $2.3 billion of cash cost and $1.5 billion of assumed debt, or about $3.8 billion of net debt. Cogentrix's own EBITDA is not available in this run, so the combined ratio cannot be computed and is N/A. If Cogentrix added no EBITDA at all, net debt of about $22,960 million over the $7,200 million guided midpoint would be 3.19 times, above the 3.0 ceiling. That is a worst case, because the plants will add earnings. The Q3 2026 call is the place to re-check this gate.

Management already said current forward prices sit below the levels used to build the $7.4-7.8 billion 2027 opportunity range, and that Vistra is trending toward the low end. A further slide would pressure the Texas segment, the single biggest swing factor in the low case.[9]
  • Texas data-center permitting pauses longer than expected. A Wells Fargo analyst raised the chance of a multi-month review pause on early data-center interconnections. Management does not expect its own Comanche Peak delivery date to move, but a broader pause would slow the pipeline behind the AWS and Meta deals and the Helix venture.[9]
  • Cogentrix financing and integration disappoint. The deal adds $1.5 billion of assumed debt and $2.3 billion of cash cost, on top of a balance sheet that already grew $2.6 billion in the first half of 2026. A messy integration, or financing costs higher than assumed, would push net debt over guided EBITDA toward the 3.0-times ceiling faster than this report's math allows.[5][21]

The first hard check is the third-quarter 2026 earnings call and 10-Q, when management said it would update 2026 and 2027 guidance. The Cogentrix closing date, and its effect on net debt, is the second.
