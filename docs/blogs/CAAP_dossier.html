What the company does

How an airport concession earns money

CAAP is an airport operator rather than an airline. Under a concession, a public authority gives the company the right to operate an airport for a defined period. The operator invests in terminals and runways, collects regulated and commercial fees, and returns the asset or the operating right when the contract ends. The economic asset is therefore a stream of future cash flows, not a freehold airport. CAAP describes its portfolio and concession arrangements in its annual filing.[26]

Airlines and passengers create the first revenue stream. Passenger charges and landing fees rise with traffic, while retail, parking, food, duty-free, car rental and advertising rise with the number of people using the terminal and the time they spend there. The mix matters: commercial income can carry better margins, but it is exposed to passenger mix, local consumption, lease terms and currency. CAAP's filed segment disclosures distinguish airport activity from other operating lines.[27]

Governments and regulators remain part of the customer relationship. Tariff formulas, investment obligations, service standards, concession fees and permitted returns are set by contract or regulation. A tariff increase can support revenue, while a delayed adjustment or political intervention can reduce real cash flow. The company reports these contractual and regulatory dependencies as material risks.[28]

Where the exposure sits

The portfolio spans Argentina, Italy, Brazil, Ecuador, Armenia and Uruguay, with the exact set and ownership percentages subject to the filing period. This geographic spread reduces dependence on one airport, but it adds exchange-rate and sovereign risk. The 2025 filing says Argentina's AA2000 represented 54.0% of consolidated revenue, 52.8% of passengers and 52.9% of air traffic movements; that concentration is large enough to drive group results.[29]

Airport revenue is partly variable and partly contractual. A passenger decline reduces passenger-linked charges and commercial sales, while fixed concession payments, maintenance and debt service may remain. That operating leverage explains why a modest change in traffic can produce a larger change in free cash flow, cash from operations minus investment in the business. The filing's operating review and risk factors describe this sensitivity.[30]

Glossary

Concession means a time-limited right to operate public infrastructure. Adjusted EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, with management-defined adjustments; it is a cash-profit proxy, not cash itself. Free cash flow means cash from operations minus investment in the business. ROIC means return on invested capital, a measure of operating profit earned on the money tied up in the business. Relative strength index (RSI) is a 0-to-100 speedometer for recent price changes. ATR means average true range, a measure of typical daily price movement. Stage 2 is a sustained advance above rising moving averages; Stage 4 is a sustained decline below them.

Competitive position

Airport concessions can have local barriers: scarce land, permits, terminal capacity and a long operating history. Those barriers can protect the incumbent once a contract is awarded. They do not eliminate the risk that the concession authority changes the rules, requires capital spending or takes a different view of tariffs. CAAP's filing identifies regulatory, political, currency and concession risks. These risks are central to the business.[31]

The portfolio's diversification is useful but incomplete. A group can own several airports and still be economically concentrated in one country, one concession or one currency. The reported AA2000 share demonstrates that concentration. A peer comparison must therefore compare concession life, passenger recovery, leverage and cash conversion, not just headline EBITDA.[32]

Operating detail and evidence limits

Traffic is necessary but not sufficient

Passenger counts are the first operating signal because they determine how many people can pay an airport charge or enter a retail area. They are not the same as earnings. A passenger flying a short domestic route may produce a different fee and retail spend from an international passenger. A connecting passenger may use the terminal without producing the same parking or food revenue as a local origin-and-destination traveler. The company reports passenger and movement data by airport and geography, but the underwriting question is the conversion from those units into distributable cash. That conversion is not verified here.

Aircraft movements add a second lens. Movements can fall while passengers rise if aircraft are larger, or rise while passengers remain flat if load factors fall. The distinction matters because landing charges, passenger charges and terminal costs respond differently. A clean KPI set would show passengers, movements, load factor where available, commercial revenue per passenger and operating cost per passenger for the same period. Without those rows, the analyst can describe direction but cannot determine unit economics.

Regulation changes the payoff

A concession is a contract with a public counterparty. The operator may have a right to collect tariffs, but the right can be bounded by affordability, service quality, investment commitments and political decisions. When inflation is high, the timing of tariff resets matters as much as the nominal tariff. When a local currency falls against the dollar, a reported dollar result can decline even if local-currency traffic and revenue improve. CAAP's multi-country footprint makes this translation unavoidable.

Capital spending is another source of uncertainty. Airports need periodic terminal, runway, security and technology investment. Some projects support future passenger growth, while others are required to keep the concession in good standing. The same dollar of capex can therefore be growth capex or maintenance capex depending on the contract. The report cannot classify that spending until the notes and management discussion are captured in a current research record.

Debt must be read at the concession level

Airport groups often have debt at operating subsidiaries as well as at the holding company. A consolidated debt ratio can hide restrictions on moving cash upstream. The analyst should read maturity dates, interest-rate type, currency, secured status, covenants and permitted distributions. A debt instrument denominated in dollars can be safer when revenue is dollar-linked and riskier when revenue is local-currency-linked. The current report does not guess at those exposures.

Cash on the balance sheet is not automatically available to shareholders. It may belong to a subsidiary, be restricted by a concession agreement, be held for debt service or be needed for committed capital projects. The correct measure is cash available after operating needs, concession payments, capex and financing obligations. That is why the free cash flow margin test is a hard gate rather than a nice-to-have statistic.

Governance and ownership

CAAP is a foreign private issuer listed in the United States. Its reporting calendar and filing forms differ from those of a domestic US issuer. Investors must therefore read Form 20-F and Form 6-K together, and pay attention to non-IFRS measures, translations and segment definitions. The formal filing record is the appropriate source for ownership, related-party, risk-factor and governance claims. This dossier makes no ownership or insider claim that is not tied to a fetched record.

The research standard deliberately treats a missing number as a decision input. A missing ROIC is not silently converted into a pass; a missing debt ratio is not replaced with an old web estimate; a missing commercial-yield figure is not inferred from a peer. That discipline may produce more rejections, but it prevents a polished narrative from outrunning its evidence. For CAAP, the evidence gap is material because the company combines regulated assets, cross-border currencies and concession-level financing.

What a complete re-underwriting would add

A complete update would fetch the latest 20-F or 6-K, identify the exact statement periods, and record each scalar in the research contract. It would calculate ROIC from operating profit after tax divided by invested capital, with the definition documented. It would calculate free-cash-flow margin from operating cash flow less capital spending divided by revenue, reconcile adjusted EBITDA to cash, and calculate net debt divided by EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) using the same period and company perimeter. It would also compare passenger growth and commercial revenue per passenger with at least three airport peers using matching definitions.

The update would then rerun the momentum gate against the latest completed exchange session. A technical recovery is not enough by itself; the candidate needs a repaired structure and a passing financial gate. The order matters because a chart can improve on speculation, while the financial statement tells us whether the improvement can persist. Until both are present, the correct posture remains observation.

Why these estimates might be wrong

The operating assumption

The bull case assumes passenger volume becomes cash and that regulators allow economic recovery through tariffs or commercial income. The weak point is the missing current commercial yield and cash-conversion record. We will know more when the next 6-K or annual filing reports traffic, revenue, capex and cash together. A traffic rebound without cash would invalidate the favorable interpretation.

The balance-sheet assumption

The rejection assumes unresolved leverage could be material, but it does not assert a debt ratio that was not verified. The next filed balance sheet and debt note should show gross debt, cash, maturities, interest expense and covenant headroom. If net debt/EBITDA is below 3.0x and FCF margin is at least 12%, this particular Layer 1 objection can be removed; the technical gate would still remain.

The chart assumption

The momentum process can be wrong during a sharp reversal. The safeguard is not prediction; it is confirmation. A close above the base pivot with volume at or above the required expansion ratio, a rising 50-day average and positive relative strength would move the chart from rejected to review. Until then, a cheap-looking price is not evidence of a healthy setup.
