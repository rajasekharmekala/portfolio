A FAIL means it did not. The later sections explain why in plain words, and they show which numbers are firm and which are only estimates.

Here is one line for each idea used. ROCE, or return on capital employed, is the profit a business earns on all the money tied up in it, like the rent a landlord collects on every flat they own. CFO/PAT compares cash from operations with reported profit, like checking whether a salary actually reached the bank rather than only appearing on a payslip. Debt-to-equity compares borrowed money with the owners' money, like the loan on a house against the buyer's own down payment. Stage 1 to Stage 4 is the life cycle of a share price: basing, rising, topping and falling, like the seasons of a year. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, like a smoothed weather chart that hides daily noise. RSI, the relative strength index, is a 0 to 100 gauge of recent momentum, like a speedometer showing how hard the car is pushing. ATR, the average true range, measures a typical daily price swing in rupees, like the usual bumpiness of a road. Test A and Test B are two separate sets of momentum conditions, like two referees who must both confirm a player is fit before he starts. Asymmetry compares the possible gain with the possible loss, like a bet that pays three times the stake if it wins and costs one stake if it loses. A stop-loss is an exit price set before the trade, like a seatbelt fastened before the drive begins. An anchored target is a price derived from a number you can verify, such as expected earnings multiplied by a historical multiple, like valuing a flat from its rent roll rather than from a guess.

Two more habits help a beginner. First, check the as-of date on every figure. A number from March is not the same as a number from June, and the report keeps those dates visible. Second, treat a rejection as information rather than failure. A rejected name can return to the list when its price structure changes, and the upgrade and reject levels below say exactly what that change would need to look like.

What the company does

CCL is an instant coffee company. It makes coffee on contract for other brands and sells its own branded coffee in India. The company describes itself as the largest exporter of instant coffee and a leading private label manufacturer worldwide, and it says it sells more than 1,000 blends under brands including Xtra, Speciale, Freeze Dried, Malgudi and THIS [23]. Its core activity is manufacturing instant coffee for private label and business-to-business customers around the world. Screener describes the business the same way, as instant coffee manufacturing for private label and B2B customers, plus a branded consumer coffee business [1].

The company reports three working parts. The first is the export business-to-business contract business, which runs on a cost-plus model: the customer pays for the green coffee and processing cost, plus a margin per kilogram. Management said Q1 FY27 volume growth in this business was about 20 per cent on that model, and that EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) per kilogram was about 140 rupees and was expected to hold through FY27 [3]. The second is the domestic branded business. Management reported gross domestic turnover of 180 crore rupees in Q1 FY27, with the branded part at 125 to 130 crore rupees [3]. The third is international consumer business, including the Percol brand in the United Kingdom and new markets in the United States and the Middle East [3].

Production sits in India and Vietnam. Management gave the Vietnam rated capacity as 36,000 tons, with utilisation of about 65 to 70 per cent, similar to India [3]. Aggregate utilisation across both countries was in the same 65 to 70 per cent range in the June quarter. That spare capacity matters because it lets volume rise without new plants, and it explains why management said no major capacity spending is planned for two years. Capital spending for FY27 was guided at 25 to 50 crore rupees, for maintenance and upgrades only [3].

The business is a commodity processor with a brand on top. Its margins depend on the price of green coffee beans and on the contracts that pass that cost through. The branded part is smaller but carries higher pricing power. Understanding this split explains most of what follows in the report. The next section turns to the strengths and weaknesses that flow from this structure.

With the business model in place, the next step is to test how strong that model is in the numbers.

Why the business is strong or weak

The evidence falls into two lists. Strengths are the things the company has done that the numbers confirm. Weaknesses are the things the numbers show that could go wrong. The second strength is growth in the top line and the bottom line. Sales rose to 4,457 crore rupees in FY26 from 3,106 crore in FY25, and profit after tax rose to 388 crore from 310 crore [1]. The FY26 annual report write-up puts revenue growth at 43.5 per cent for the year and profit growth at 25.1 per cent [12]. The weaknesses are just as concrete. Operating margin has narrowed. Screener shows the operating profit margin at 16 per cent for FY26, against 25 per cent in FY2020 and 23 per cent in FY2022 [1]. Cash generation has also been lumpy. Over FY23 to FY25 the company converted well under its profit into cash, and it only caught up in FY26 [1]. That is above the 15 per cent floor but well below the 18 per cent that the framework prefers for the Core bucket.

Two more weaknesses are structural. The first is exposure to green coffee prices. Management described the bean price as volatile, with bearish supply from Brazil pulling against bullish risk from El Nino in Vietnam, and it said the cost-plus contracts protect margins but not the timing of customer orders [3]. The second is that growth in the domestic brand is still being built. The company talked about gaining share in South India and in quick-commerce and modern-trade chains, but the branded business is still a small slice of revenue [3].

The business has real cash strengths and real margin and cycle weaknesses. The next section explains those numbers in plain words, one table at a time.

The numbers in plain words

The company's reported figures tell a short story. Sales are the money the company brings in. Profit after tax, or PAT, is what remains after every cost, interest and tax. Cash from operations, or CFO, is the cash the business actually collected from its operations in the same year. A healthy business shows PAT and CFO moving in the same direction over several years. A gap between them is not automatically a warning, because a company can book a sale before it collects the cash. A persistent gap is a warning, because profit that never turns into cash can be an accounting result rather than a real return.

The company's results have grown fast over the last four years, but the cash line has been uneven. That unevenness is why the framework uses three years of cash divided by three years of profit, and not a single year. The three-year window ending FY26 passes comfortably. A window ending FY25 would not. The report shows both views so that the reader can see how much the answer depends on the years chosen.

ROCE gate: passes narrowly

Return on capital employed, or ROCE, measures how much operating profit the company earns on the total capital it uses, including borrowed money. The framework requires at least 15 per cent. Screener reports ROCE of 16 per cent for the year ending 31 March 2026, up from 13 per cent in FY25 and 12 per cent in FY24 [1]. The result is a clear pass on the floor. The headline card on the same page shows 15.8 per cent, which is a slightly different snapshot, and the StockFin write-up of the FY26 annual report gives 22.15 per cent on its own basis [12]. The three figures disagree because the definitions differ. The report uses the Screener FY26 value because it is the one published on the same page as the other ratios, and it sits within the framework's floor under every version. The framework's Core preference is 18 per cent, so the company misses that preference. The margin above the floor is one percentage point on the Screener basis, and a drop in margin would be enough to fail the floor. That thinness is worth remembering when reading the rest of the report. The return is adequate, not generous, and it depends on sustaining the FY26 margin of 16 per cent. Because operating margin fell from 19 per cent in FY23 to 16 per cent in FY26, that sustainability is not assured. The gate passes today, but it is a narrow pass.

Debt-to-equity gate: passes comfortably

Debt-to-equity compares the money the company has borrowed with the money its owners have put in and kept in the business. The framework requires a ratio below 0.70, and this test applies to non-financial companies such as this one. Screener reports borrowings of 1,324 crore rupees at 31 March 2026, equity capital of 27 crore and reserves of 2,318 crore [1]. Dividing borrowings by the owners' funds, which is the 27 plus the 2,318, gives a ratio of about 0.56. A second published figure gives the same 0.56 [9]. The StockFin write-up gives 0.92 on a different basis that is not defined in the source [13], and the report does not use it. On the framework's basis the company passes with room to spare, about 0.14 below the ceiling.

Debt matters because it adds a fixed cost. Interest is due whatever the sales do. Management has used the recent cash to reduce that burden. Gross debt was 1,268 crore rupees at 30 June 2026, and net debt was 963 crore after cash balances [2]. The company said it expects to pay down a further term loan of 140 crore rupees over the next three quarters of FY27 [2]. India Ratings projected net leverage falling below 1.5 times by FY27 [5]. A lower debt load raises the margin of safety for shareholders. It also reduces the risk that a weak quarter forces an expensive refinancing. This gate is the strongest part of the company's profile, and it should be read as a genuine pass.

Three-year cash conversion gate: passes on the framework window

The cash conversion gate asks whether reported profit has been backed by real cash. The framework takes the sum of cash from operations over the last three audited years and divides it by the sum of profit after tax over the same years. The floor is 0.70. For CCL the three years are FY24, FY25 and FY26. Cash from operations was 55, 290 and 858 crore rupees, which totals 1,203 crore. Profit after tax was 250, 310 and 388 crore, which totals 948 crore [1]. The ratio is 1,203 divided by 948, or 1.27. The management statement on the 858 crore FY26 cash figure matches the Screener figure [2].

The pass depends on which three years are used. The FY26 cash figure is far above its profit, and that single year lifts the window well above the floor. Using FY23 to FY25 instead, the total cash is 518 crore and the total profit is 844 crore, a ratio of 0.61, which would fail. The report does not change the framework's window, but a careful reader should see that the pass rests on the strongest year in the record. The explanation management gives, working capital efficiency, is plausible but can reverse. If inventories or customer receivables rise again, cash will fall back toward profit levels. The gate therefore passes as a rule, while the quality of that pass is weaker than the headline 1.27 suggests. This is a point to watch at the next results.

Technical posture gate: fails

The technical gate asks whether the share is in a posture that supports a trade. The Core bucket needs either a Stage 1 base breakout or a Stage 2 markup, which is a rising phase with the price above rising averages. The Tactical bucket needs a Stage 2 move above rising 50- and 200-day averages. The gate also rules out a Stage 4 breakdown and a death cross on heavy volume. CCL is labelled Stage 1 (Basing) in the ledger, so it is not yet in a Stage 2 markup. The base has not broken out, because the price is still 2.69 ATR above the base floor and volume is only 0.35 times normal. The momentum gate, which is binding, returns NO_MOMENTUM. The 50-day average sits above the 200-day average, so there is no death cross, and there is no Stage 4 breakdown on the ledger. Those two exclusions are passed. The positive requirement is not met, so the technical gate fails.

The reader should see that this failure is about timing, not about the business. A share can have excellent cash flow and still be waiting for the market to confirm a trend. The framework does not ask the investor to buy that waiting period. It asks for confirmation first. The clearest signal that would change this gate is a close above the falling 50-day average of 1,090.37, on volume at least equal to its 20-day norm, with the 50-day average turning upward. Until that happens, the technical gate stays closed.

Asymmetry gate: cannot be scored

Asymmetry compares the reward with the risk. The framework defines it as the target minus the current price, divided by the current price minus the stop. The floor is 3.0 for CORE_ALPHA. The target has to be anchored: either forward earnings per share multiplied by a three-year median price-to-earnings ratio, or a technical measured move. An unverifiable target is recorded as FAIL_ASYMMETRY. The company has a verifiable forward EPS: the consensus for FY27 is 37.88 rupees [7], with a median of 37.85 rupees in a second aggregator [8]. But the three-year median P/E is not published on any page the research could read. The trailing P/E of 33.0 is only a single current value [1], and aggregator pages give five-year averages rather than a three-year median. Without that second number the anchor cannot be built, so the target cannot be verified. The result is FAIL_ASYMMETRY.

The stop level is computed by the rule for CORE_ALPHA. The stop is placed below the lowest of the 20-day swing low, the 50-day average and the 200-day average, minus one ATR. The lowest of those three is the swing low of 991.4 rupees. Subtracting the ATR of 29.52 gives a stop of 961.88 rupees. The risk per share is therefore 108.82 rupees, which is 10.2 per cent of the close. The reader should understand that the asymmetry gate would need a target of at least 1,397.16 rupees to reach the 3.0 floor, and that target would need an anchor the framework can verify. The gate is not scored. The arithmetic appears in full in the asymmetry section.

All five Layer 1 gates have now been explained. Two passes are real, one pass is narrow, and two fail. The next section explains why Layers 2 and 3 are not scored at all.

Layers 2 and 3: not scored

The framework is built in layers, and each layer depends on the one before it. Layer 1 checks whether a company is worth considering at all. Layer 2 is the key performance indicator scorecard, which rates the operating quality and the outlook of the business in more detail. Layer 3 is the entry posture, which decides how and when to buy a name that has already passed the first two layers. Those later layers exist to refine a choice that Layer 1 has already allowed. When Layer 1 fails, the choice has not been allowed, so the later layers have no decision to refine.

For CCL, Layer 1 fails on the technical posture and on the asymmetry test. Scoring the company in Layer 2 would give a number that looks precise but would not change the verdict. Scoring Layer 3 would require an entry plan for a trade the framework has already closed. The report therefore states plainly that Layer 2 and Layer 3 are not scored. No score is invented, and no entry zone is proposed. A reader who wants to see the company's operating quality can use the Layer 1 numbers in this report, which already show the margin trend, the cash conversion and the return on capital, without a composite score.

This choice protects against a common error. A detailed scorecard can make a weak technical setup look attractive, because the strong business numbers pull the total upward. The framework avoids that by letting the technical and price gates control the outcome. The company's quality is still described in full in the sections above, so nothing about the business is hidden. What is withheld is a composite number that would blend quality and timing into one figure. If the technical gate later passes, the Layer 2 and Layer 3 scoring would be run on fresh data at that time, not on the figures in this report. Until then the verdict stands as REJECTED.

Asymmetry and the target

Asymmetry is the core of the risk discipline in the framework. It asks how much the investor could gain if the thesis works, and how much the investor could lose if the stop is hit, and it asks the answer to be large relative to the loss. A ratio of 3.0 means the potential gain is three times the potential loss. A ratio below the floor means the trade does not pay enough for the risk it carries. The arithmetic needs three numbers: the current price, the stop and the target. For CCL the current price is the 13 October close of 1,070.7 rupees. The stop is 961.88 rupees, derived from the rule for CORE_ALPHA. The base target is not available, because the framework requires an anchor that cannot be built from the verifiable numbers.

The stop calculation is straightforward. The lowest of the swing low (991.4), the 50-day average (1,090.37) and the 200-day average (1,076.3) is 991.4. The rule subtracts one ATR, which is 29.52 rupees, giving 961.88 rupees. The risk per share is the close minus the stop: 1,070.7 minus 961.88, which is 108.82 rupees. The target that would meet the 3.0 floor is the close plus three times the risk: 1,070.7 plus 326.46, or 1,397.16 rupees. Any anchored target below that figure would fail the gate, however attractive it looked.

No base or bull target is recorded in the payload, because neither can be anchored. The research gives three reference figures that a reader may see quoted elsewhere, and none of them qualifies. The 52-week high of 1,242.2 rupees is a past price, not a target, and against the current close it would give an asymmetry of about 1.58. The consensus average of 1,346.36 rupees from MarketScreener would give about 2.53 if used as a target [17], but it is an opinion average and not an anchored value, so the framework does not accept it. A third figure, the 1,261 and 1,511 rupee base and bull cases in a model-based article, rests on a twenty per cent growth assumption and a trailing P/E of 32.44, not on the three-year median, so it does not qualify either [9]. Each of these falls short of the 3.0 floor or lacks an anchor. The honest conclusion is that the asymmetry gate cannot be passed on the current record, and the report does not invent a number to fill the gap.

Fatal flaws: the red-team case

A red-team review asks the strongest honest case against the conclusion. The aim is not to be negative for its own sake. It is to test whether the verdict would survive a determined critic. For CCL the verdict is REJECTED, so the critic's task is to argue the opposite: that the company is a sound business at a reasonable price, and that the framework is being too strict. The flaws below are the points where that argument is weakest, and they are the reasons the verdict holds.

Flaw one: the price has not confirmed any trend

The most serious flaw is that the price has not yet shown the kind of move the framework is designed to catch. The 50-day average is falling, having dropped from 1,150.63 to 1,090.37 over 21 sessions, and the price sits below it. Test A fails on three of four conditions, and Test B fails on three of five. The evidence is that the share has spent recent weeks drifting. The consequence is that any investment made now would be an early bet on a base that may still break down. The framework does not reward early bets without confirmation. It records the status as NO_MOMENTUM and waits. A critic can say the business quality justifies buying before the breakout. The answer is that the framework's rule exists precisely to stop that kind of judgement from overriding the measured posture. The consequence of ignoring the rule would be a position that carries the full risk of a falling trend with no sign that the trend has turned.

Flaw two: volume shows no buyers yet

The second flaw is that volume is weak. The volume multiple over 20 days is 0.35, which means recent trading is about a third of the normal level. A base that is about to break out normally shows volume building as the price approaches the pivot. Here the reverse is happening. The base has dried up, which is useful for a base, but it has not been followed by the expansion needed to confirm a move. The volume dry-up ratio of 0.32 passes the quiet-base test, yet the expansion test fails at 0.35 against a minimum of 1.0. The consequence is that a breakout, if it comes, may fail to hold because it lacks the buying behind it. A critic could argue that quiet volume before a rally is normal. That is true in some cases, and it is exactly why the framework requires the expansion to be measured rather than assumed.

Flaw three: the cash strength may not be repeatable

The third flaw is that the best cash year may be an outlier. CFO was 858 crore in FY26 against 290 in FY25 and 55 in FY24. The company credits working capital efficiencies, which means money was freed from inventories and receivables. That release happens once. If the business has to rebuild working capital as it grows, cash conversion could fall back. The three-year gate passes at 1.27 only because FY26 is so strong. Using the earlier three years, the ratio is 0.61. The consequence is that the cash pass should be treated as provisional. A critic could say the working capital release is a sign of better management. That may be true, and it would be a good thing to confirm in the next annual report. For now the gate is a pass that depends on one year.

Flaw four: margins are under pressure

The fourth flaw is margin. Operating margin fell from 19 per cent in FY23 to 16 per cent in FY26, and ROCE, at 16 per cent, sits only one point above the floor. Management says the cost-plus contracts pass cost changes through, and that EBITDA per kilogram will hold near 140 rupees for FY27. But the same management said green coffee prices remain volatile and that freight and packaging costs rose during the quarter. An analyst on the call also raised a listed peer's EBITDA per kilogram of 160 to 170 rupees against CCL's 135 to 140 rupees [3]. The consequence is that a small squeeze on margin would push ROCE below the 15 per cent floor, which would fail the first gate. A critic could say that the cost-plus model protects margin. That is true for the contract part, but the consequence of a squeeze is still a ROCE that sits close to the line.

Flaw five: the valuation leaves little room for error

The fifth flaw is valuation. At a trailing P/E of 33.0, the market is paying for a continuation of high growth. The FY27 consensus of 37.88 rupees implies EPS growth of about 30 per cent, which is a high bar to clear for a commodity processor whose margins have narrowed over three years. The stock is cheaper than the peer group on trailing earnings, which supports the bull case, but the peer group itself includes firms with far higher returns. The consequence is that a disappointment on growth would likely pull the multiple down, and the framework's target would have to be re-anchored at that point. A critic could say that consensus growth is conservative after a 20 per cent volume quarter. The report cannot test that claim, because the three-year median P/E needed for an anchored target is not available. The valuation argument therefore remains open, and the gate cannot be closed in the company's favour on the current record.

The upgrade level is a daily close above the falling 50-day average of 1,090.37 rupees, with that average turning upward, on volume at least equal to its 20-day norm. The reject level and the stop are two distinct lines: the first says the base has failed, and the second says any position would have been stopped out.

Glossary

ATR (average true range). A measure of the typical daily price swing over 14 sessions, in rupees. A larger ATR means a more volatile share. Asymmetry. The ratio of the reward to the target to the risk to the stop. Anchored target. A price target built from a verifiable number, such as forward earnings times a historical multiple, rather than from an opinion. Base. A sideways period in which the price consolidates after a decline. Base low. The lowest price inside the current base, here 991.4 rupees over 63 sessions. CFO (cash from operations). The cash a business generates from its operating activities in a year. CFO/PAT. Cash from operations divided by profit after tax, showing how much profit has turned into cash. Cost-plus contract. A supply arrangement in which the customer pays the cost of inputs plus an agreed margin. Consolidated. Figures that include the parent company and its subsidiaries. Death cross. A falling 50-day average crossing below a 200-day average, usually a negative signal. Debt-to-equity. Borrowings divided by the owners' funds. EBITDA. Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a measure of operating profit. EPS (earnings per share). Profit after tax divided by the number of shares. Forward P/E. The share price divided by expected earnings for the next year. Momentum. The speed and direction of a price move. NO_MOMENTUM. The code's label for a share that fails both Test A and Test B. OPM (operating profit margin). Operating profit as a percentage of sales. PAT (profit after tax). Profit left after all costs, interest and tax. P/E (price-to-earnings). The share price divided by earnings per share. ROCE (return on capital employed). Operating profit divided by the capital the business uses, including borrowings. ROE (return on equity). Profit after tax divided by owners' funds. RSI (relative strength index). A 0 to 100 measure of recent momentum, with 50 as neutral. Relative strength. How far a share's return has moved above or below the benchmark. Stage 1 to Stage 4. The four phases of a share's cycle: basing, rising markup, topping and falling breakdown. Stop-loss. A pre-set exit price that limits the loss on a position. Swing low. The lowest low over a recent window, used as a support level. Test A and Test B. The two momentum condition sets in the code. Volume multiple. Recent volume divided by its average over a set window.

Method and limits

The method follows the framework's order. First the ledger was read for the 13 October 2026 session, and the momentum gate was taken from the code output as binding. Second, the company's financials were gathered from the Screener consolidated pages, which give a multi-year view of sales, profit, cash flow and returns. Third, the company's own filings and investor pages were checked for the latest quarter, guidance, debt and ratings, and the earnings-call summaries of the June 2026 quarter were used for management commentary. Fourth, consensus aggregators were used for forward earnings and analyst views, with the caveat that these are estimates. Fifth, the Coffee Board statistics page and a trade-press write-up was used for the sector context. Sixth, peer figures were taken from the Screener pages of three listed food and beverage companies on the same date.

The limits are clear. The research used web pages rather than the primary filings themselves in several places, because the annual report PDF could not be parsed. The report makes no forecast, and every figure is either a dated fact from a named source or a plain arithmetic step shown in the text.
