Before you read

QLD is a traded fund, not an operating company. It seeks two times the daily return, the one-session price move, of the Nasdaq-100 Index before fees.[1] It resets exposure each day, so a multi-week result is not a simple two-times result. This report says REJECTED: price is in Stage 2, a markup trend above rising averages, but is well above support. A future review can reconsider the decision only after a new evidence set passes every gate, including a reward-to-risk ratio, meaning possible gain divided by possible loss, of at least 3.5 to 1; a Stage 4 decline, meaning a lasting fall below weak averages, keeps the rejection.

What the company does

Terms used later are defined here. A base is a sideways price area. A pivot is the level where a price move may begin. A breakout is a close above that level. A retest is a return to test the broken level. An entry zone is the planned buy range. Asymmetry means possible reward compared with possible loss. Net debt means borrowings less cash. Cash flow from operations is cash generated by normal activity. These definitions make clear why company metrics are not substituted for ETF data.

There is no company revenue model to underwrite. QLD is a pooled investment vehicle that issues shares and uses a mixture of derivatives and other instruments to seek a 2x daily result on the Nasdaq-100.[1] An ETF is a fund whose shares trade during the day on an exchange. The investor pays the market price for a share, and that price can differ from the fund's net asset value, or NAV, which is the per-share value of its holdings. ProShares says that shares trade at market price and are not individually redeemed from the fund.[2]

The underlying index is a rules-based basket of large non-financial companies listed on Nasdaq. Nasdaq's methodology describes a modified market-capitalisation weighting approach, periodic reviews, eligibility rules and rebalancing mechanics.[5] QLD therefore gives a concentrated growth-equity exposure through a fund wrapper. It does not select businesses on fundamental valuation, and it does not promise two times the index over a month, a year or a complete market cycle.

The fund's operating mechanics are simple to state and difficult to live with. At each close, the portfolio is rebalanced so that the next session begins with approximately twice the index's exposure. If the index rises 1% in a day, the target before fees is about +2%; if it falls 1%, the target is about -2%. Fees, financing, swap terms, trading costs, tracking differences and the path of daily returns all affect the realised result.[3] A flat index can still produce a loss after alternating up and down days because losses are applied to a changing base.

There are no customers, renewal rates, backlog, product margins, plant utilisation or employee base to assess. The economically relevant “customer” is the investor who wants a liquid, short-horizon expression of a bullish Nasdaq-100 view. Liquidity is useful, but it does not remove leverage, financing, counterparty or gap risk. ProShares expressly describes leveraged funds as non-diversified and warns about swaps, futures, imperfect benchmark correlation and market-price variance.[4]

Glossary

Leverage means using exposure larger than the investor's cash so that both gains and losses are magnified. Volatility drag is the compounding loss that can arise from alternating returns even when the index finishes near its starting level. Tracking difference is the gap between a fund's result and its stated benchmark target after fees and implementation costs. Relative strength index (RSI) is a 0–100 momentum gauge; it does not predict value. Average true range (ATR) measures typical daily trading range and is used here to express distance from support. A moving average is the average closing price over a chosen number of sessions. Stage 2 is a technical description of a sustained markup trend above rising moving averages. Stage 4 is a sustained decline below weakening moving averages.

Why the business is strong or weak

For an ETF, the first question is not whether ProShares has a durable product moat. It is whether the index exposure is transparent, liquid and fit for the stated holding period. The Nasdaq-100 methodology is public and rules-based, which lowers discretionary manager risk.[5] The trade-off is that the investor accepts the index's concentration and valuation regime rather than receiving a fundamental margin of safety.

The index has meaningful exposure to large technology, communication and consumer-platform companies. That can be an advantage when earnings growth and liquidity favour long-duration growth assets. It can be a weakness when real rates rise, chip spending disappoints, regulation changes, or a few mega-cap constituents fall together. QLD doubles the one-day exposure and therefore doubles the sensitivity to these common shocks.

Layer 2 uses ETF-specific operating KPIs rather than company KPIs. The metrics are: benchmark fidelity, cost, liquidity and path-risk control. They are chosen because QLD's economic output is a delivered daily exposure, not revenue or profit. Public sources provide the objective and warnings, but a current, independently verified realised tracking-difference series was not published in the fetched material; that KPI is therefore WATCH rather than PASS.

Three fatal flaws

  1. Path dependence. The daily reset can turn a flat or choppy index into a loss for QLD. ProShares warns that results over periods other than a day may be materially higher or lower than the daily target.[3]
  2. Leverage and gap loss. A large overnight Nasdaq-100 fall is transmitted at roughly twice the daily rate before implementation effects. Stop orders cannot guarantee an execution price in a gap.
  3. Concentration disguised as breadth. QLD may have many line items, but its benchmark is weighted toward large growth companies. A broad security count is not the same as independent economic drivers; the methodology defines the selection universe, not diversification from a common factor.[5]

These company measures do not apply to this fund. The correct answer is N/A, not a guess. The annual and semiannual fund reports are the relevant records.[6]

Why these estimates might be wrong

The bull estimate's weakest assumption is that the Nasdaq-100 continues to rise without a volatility shock. Evidence against it would be a close below the 50-day average followed by a falling 50-day slope, or a negative relative-strength reading against SPY. We would know after each completed session and would confirm the regime at the next monthly review.

The base estimate's weakest assumption is that the path of returns is benign. It fails when alternating index moves create volatility drag. A comparison of QLD's realised return with twice QQQ's cumulative return over the same window would show the difference, but a current audited series was not found in the fetched source set. That gap is disclosed rather than filled with an estimate.

The bear estimate's weakest assumption is that the stop can be executed near the chosen level. Overnight gaps, swap implementation, market stress and widening spreads can defeat a stop. The evidence would be a close below the stop or a material divergence from the benchmark. The next official fund report can update holdings and derivatives; it cannot remove market-gap risk.

Index methodology is another uncertainty. Nasdaq has published methodology material and consultation updates, so inclusion and weighting rules can change over time.[9] A methodology change that raises concentration or changes eligibility would alter the exposure even if the QLD ticker remains unchanged.
