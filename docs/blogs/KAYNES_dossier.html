Kaynes Technology is an Indian electronics maker that builds circuit boards and assembles equipment for other companies, and it is listed on the NSE and BSE. Its sales have grown fast, but in the year to March 2026 its cash from operations was minus ₹600 crore, while its profit was ₹364 crore [1]. The company fails two of the three financial floors in our Layer 1 gate, and its share price fails the technical floor too. So the verdict is REJECTED. The rule is simple: one failed floor rejects the stock, so the later scoring layers were not run.

What the company does

Kaynes makes electronics for other companies. The industry calls this electronics manufacturing services, or EMS, and it works a bit like a contract kitchen: the customer supplies the recipe, which here is the circuit design, and the contractor buys the parts, assembles the product, tests it and ships it. The company describes its offer as end-to-end, covering conceptual design, process engineering, integrated manufacturing and life-cycle support for customers in automotive, industrial, aerospace and defence, medical and railway markets, and in internet-connected devices, which are called IoT [2]. It was incorporated in 2008 [1].

The business has four parts, and a reader should keep them apart because they behave differently. The first and largest is the core EMS work, which drove most of the 40 percent sales growth in the June 2026 quarter [3]. The second is smart metering, in which Kaynes makes and installs electricity meters through its GridCrest business, and it is where most of the cash strain sits [3] [4]. The third is OSAT, short for outsourced semiconductor assembly and test, which means packaging finished chips for other companies. The fourth is printed circuit boards (PCBs), the boards that carry and connect the chips. In August 2026 management said Kaynes Semicon Unit 2 and Kaynes Circuit Chennai should both be operational by the third quarter of FY27 [3].

The order book is the number to watch first. The company reported an order book of ₹89,038 million at 30 June 2026, up from ₹74,011 million a year earlier [5]. CNBC-TV18 reports the same order book as ₹8,903.8 crore [6], which is the same figure written in crores. An order book is the value of signed work not yet delivered, so it shows how much of the coming quarters' sales is already contracted. It does not show whether the work will be paid for on time, and that question turns out to matter more for this company than the size of the book.

The customer mix adds to the caution. Management said the receivable from its largest electric two-wheeler customer, meaning money owed for goods already delivered, has fallen below ₹100 crore after a long effort to collect it [3]. A small number of big customers can make sales look healthy while the cash arrives slowly. What this means for an owner is that the business is real and growing, and the open question is whether the cash follows the sales. The next chapter tests that against the company's own figures.

Why the business is strong or weak

Start with what is working. Sales rose from ₹1,126 crore in the year to March 2023, to ₹1,805 crore in March 2024, and to ₹3,626 crore in March 2026 [1] [7]. Taking FY23 as the starting year, that is three years of growth. The compound rate over those three years is 47.7 percent, which Screener reports as 48 percent: (3,626 divided by 1,126) raised to the power of one-third, minus one. Compound growth is the steady yearly rate that would take the starting year's sales to the final year's. The first quarter of FY27 kept the pace: sales were ₹9,460 million, up 40 percent, and EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, which is operating profit before borrowing costs, tax and the wear on assets) was ₹1,476 million, up 31 percent [5].

The balance sheet is the second strength. Borrowings were ₹913 crore at March 2026, against equity capital of ₹67 crore and reserves of ₹4,681 crore [1]. Reserves are the profits the company has kept in the business, so the owners' money is about ₹4,748 crore. The company also raised equity twice, ₹1,400 crore in December 2023 and ₹1,600 crore in June 2025 [4], and the rating agency ICRA expects the raises, with government subsidies, to limit reliance on outside debt [4]. Borrowing is therefore light compared with the owners' money, and that matters for the leverage test in chapter 3.

The weakness is the cash, and it is the core of this verdict. Profit fell in the first quarter: profit after tax was ₹564 million, down 24 percent, and the profit margin, which is profit as a share of sales, dropped to 6.0 percent from 11.1 percent a year earlier [5]. Return on equity, which is the profit earned on the owners' money, was 8.69 percent [1]. The bigger problem is cash. Cash from operations, the cash the business generated day to day, was minus ₹600 crore in FY26 [1] [8]. Free cash flow, which is the cash left after operations and spending on plants, was minus ₹1,841 crore that year [1].

Most of that gap comes from the cash tied up in the business. Working capital is the money tied up in what customers owe the company and in its stock of parts and finished goods, less what the company owes its own suppliers. Debtor days, the average number of days before customers pay, rose from 77 in March 2025 to 154 in March 2026 [1]. In FY26 trade receivables reached ₹1,527.6 crore, and the cash outflow was ₹1,023.9 crore from receivables and ₹288.8 crore from inventory [9] [10]. Whalesbook puts working capital days at 87 in FY25 and 163 by the first quarter of FY27 [11], and Tradebrains reports trade receivables up 166 percent, from ₹575 crore to ₹1,528 crore [12]. In plain words, the company sold more than it collected, and it built up stock to serve the orders.

Management has acknowledged the problem and chosen to slow the weakest part. It stopped metering production and supply for a time, because in the June 2026 quarter about ₹240 crore of metering sales produced only ₹88 crore of collections [3]. The EMS business is judged better: a summary of the FY26 results call reports core working capital days improving from 83 in FY24 to 53 in FY26 [13]. Taken together, the strength is real scale and a light balance sheet, and the weakness is cash that does not keep pace with a fast-growing book. What this means for an owner is that the next test is cash, not sales.

The numbers, in plain words

Our Layer 1 gate is a set of hard floors. A stock must clear every floor before the later scoring applies, and one failure ends the review. Three of the floors are financial. ROCE stands for return on capital employed, which is the operating profit a business earns on all the money put into it, both owners' money and borrowed money; the floor is 15.0 percent. Debt-to-equity is the borrowed money divided by the owners' money, and the floor is below 0.70. The third floor asks whether profit is backed by cash: the three-year sum of cash from operations divided by the three-year sum of profit must be at least 0.70. Layer 1 disposition and final verdict

The financial floor fails. ROCE is 12.7 percent against a floor of 15.0 percent, and the year to March 2026 shows 13 percent, so the result holds either way. The three-year cash ratio is -0.71 against 0.70. Debt-to-equity passes at 0.19. Because each floor is binary, two failures are enough on their own to reject the stock. The technical floor fails as well: the ledger places the stock in Stage 1 (basing), the momentum gate returns NO_MOMENTUM, and no upward break from the sideways phase has been confirmed (chapter 4 sets out the figures).

The upside-to-downside check is not evaluated. Asymmetry is the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss, measured from the current price to a target and to a stop level, and the floors are 3.0 for core and 3.5 for tactical holdings. Because the financial floors already failed, we have not scored it, and Layers 2 and 3 were not evaluated for the same reason.

What the price is doing

The price figures in this chapter come only from the ledger, which carries the 9 October 2026 session. Three terms come first. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of sessions, and a 50-day average smooths out the daily noise over about ten weeks. Stage 2 means the uptrend phase, where the price sits above a rising 50-day average and the 200-day average is also rising. Stage 1 (basing) means the phase before that, when the price moves sideways after a fall and no uptrend has started yet. A base is the sideways stretch after a fall, where sellers run out and the price moves in a range. The ledger places Kaynes in Stage 1 (basing), so the stock is not yet in the uptrend that the tactical route requires.

RSI (relative strength index) measures how fast the price has moved up or down over the last 14 sessions, on a scale of 0 to 100; below 30 is called oversold, which means the stock has fallen unusually fast, but it does not mean a bounce is due. ATR (average true range) measures the typical daily move in rupees, so it is a ruler for distance. A pivot means the price level at the top of a sideways range, and a breakout is a close above that level on heavier trading. The table below lists the ledger values.

What could happen next

The bull case rests on cash. Management said it expects to turn cash positive by the end of the financial year, and it said about ₹200 crore of metering money came in during the first week of July [3]. The company has also published a plan for FY27 spending on new plants, about ₹300 crore each for OSAT and PCB and about ₹250 crore for EMS [3]. Whalesbook reports a wider plan of ₹8,500 crore of spending through FY28 [11], so the FY27 figure is one year of a multi-year programme that Whalesbook says runs through FY28. A broker summary carries a BUY recommendation from Motilal Oswal [14]. The bull case also depends on guidance, which is the forecast a company gives investors for the coming year. Press reports of the company's FY27 guidance cite 30 percent revenue growth and an EBITDA margin of about 17 percent [10]. If sales grew 30 percent on FY26's ₹3,626 crore, FY27 would be about ₹4,714 crore. Two points temper that guidance. Q1 FY27 sales of ₹946 crore would leave about ₹1,256 crore a quarter for the remaining three quarters to reach ₹4,714 crore, which is about 33 percent above Q1. The Q1 EBITDA margin was 15.6 percent [5] against the roughly 17 percent guided, and the company missed its own FY26 revenue target.

The bear case is the same cash problem seen from the other side. The company paused metering supply, and the first-quarter profit fell 24 percent [5]. ICRA expects operating margins to moderate slightly in FY27 as the new OSAT and PCB plants add fixed costs, and it says meaningful revenue from those plants starts only in FY2027 [4]. The company also missed its own FY26 revenue target, ending near ₹3,600 crore against a revised target of ₹4,000 crore [13]. The company itself reported a supply squeeze: suppliers are asking for advances on PCB components, and it can take six to eight months to place a component order [3]. Management also reported a slippage in the OSAT and PCB ramp-up caused by logistics disruption [3].

The key question is what the three-year cash ratio does next. The window that matters moves forward each year, and the next window is FY25 to FY27. Keeping FY25 and FY26 in that window, and assuming an illustrative FY27 profit of ₹400 crore, the ratio would need FY27 cash from operations of about ₹1,422 crore to reach 0.70. This is an illustration, not a forecast, and the profit figure is our assumption. The cash test cannot change before the FY27 annual results, which complete the FY25 to FY27 window. Because FY25 and FY26 weigh heavily on that sum, even a strong FY27 would not reopen the file quickly, so what follows is a wait for those results, not a quick reversal.

Why these estimates might be wrong

The sources do not agree on every figure, and the disagreement itself is worth knowing. The working capital days for FY26 are 133 in the consolidated table [1], 125 in one analysis [9], and about 122 in a concall summary [13]. The headline ROCE of 12.7 percent is recorded as of June 2026, and the year-row figure of 13 percent is as of March 2026. The source does not label the headline period, so the two figures come from different periods, and we do not know the reason for the difference. The 52-week high is ₹7,705 in the ledger, but the Screener header shows ₹7,140 [1]. We used the ledger for every price and the consolidated tables for every financial, and we did not try to reconcile the differences beyond showing them, because the verdict does not change under any of the figures. One measure does not fit. The company's own FY25 investor presentation prints a ROCE row of 19.4 and 19.2 percent and footnotes that ROCE there is adjusted for unused IPO and QIP proceeds [15]. That adjusted measure is not the one our floor uses, and we have no adjusted figure for FY26, so we did not substitute it. A reader should know that the company's own measure is higher than the Screener figure. The company's adjustment excludes unused IPO and QIP proceeds. The footnote does not say which raises it covers, and the sources we read do not settle that. Even if ROCE were adjusted this way, the rejection would stand on the cash floor and the technical floor.

The biggest uncertainty is the cash line. The FY26 annual report is the primary document, but it was not readable in our tools, so the cash figure rests on consolidated tables and two cross-checks. Those cross-checks agree with each other, which lowers the risk of a data error, but they do not replace a filing read. A second uncertainty is timing. The company says about ₹200 crore came in during early July [3], which counts toward the FY25 to FY27 window rather than the closed FY24 to FY26 one. If the cash arrives as promised, the cash test could clear, but not before the FY27 annual results, and the arithmetic makes that a slow process.

Governance is the third uncertainty. In March 2025 SEBI issued a show-cause notice to the managing director over the company's structured digital database for the year to March 2023, and the report noted concern about how that database was kept [16]. We did not find the outcome in the sources we read, so we cannot say whether the matter was closed. The promoter group's stake fell from 57.75 percent in March 2025 to 53.46 percent by March 2026 [17] [18]. The Trendlyne record shows no promoter shares pledged as of June 2026 [19], and a promoter-group declaration says no encumbrance was made during FY26 [20]. Both support a clean reading, but the stake sale is a fact that deserves attention.

The fourth uncertainty is the rating picture. The three agencies reaffirmed or raised their bank-loan ratings in early 2026: CRISIL at A/Stable for ₹770 crore of bank facilities [21] [22], ICRA at [ICRA] A- (Positive) for ₹780 crore [23] [24], and India Ratings at IND A-/Stable/IND A1 [25]. ICRA's rationale still calls the company's working capital intensity a constraint [4]. CRISIL had placed the rating on Rating Watch with Developing Implications on 15 December 2025 [26], and the March 2026 letter removed that watch [27], so the March action is a removal rather than a plain reaffirmation. A rating reaffirmation is a useful signal about the lenders' view of repayment, but it is not a verdict on cash conversion, and it is dated before the June 2026 quarter.

What we would do, and when

The action is to hold no position and keep the record in the archive, because the Layer 1 result requires it. We would not chase the stock on a bounce, since a bounce on thin trading tells us nothing about the cash problem. Quarterly results update the trailing return, so the results for the quarter to September 2026 are the first useful check on ROCE. The cash test can only move on the FY27 annual results, so the file is reviewed on those two dates. We did not assess valuation, because Layer 1 failed and the rulebook does not score price for a rejected name; the price trigger that follows is a monitoring level, not a valuation. The verdict is not a forecast that the company will fail, and it says nothing about the quality of its products.

The file reopens for a fresh Layer 1 and momentum re-run only when ROCE and the cash ratio both clear their floors, together with a momentum pass.

Trailing ROCE at or above 15.0 percent, shown in a filed quarterly release, is progress, but on its own it does not reopen the file while the cash ratio still fails.

The cash ratio is tested on the rolling window FY25 to FY27. On the illustrative FY27 profit of ₹400 crore, the ratio reaches 0.70 only if FY27 cash from operations is about ₹1,422 crore, which is read from the FY27 annual results. Clearing it alone is progress, not a reopening.

On price, a daily close above the 50-day average of ₹3,640.71 on volume at least equal to the 20-day average would be a reason to rerun the momentum test, not to buy.

A close below ₹3,131.0, the 63-day base low, would confirm that the sideways range has failed and push the next review point out.
