Terms used in this report

This dossier is written so that a reader who has never looked at a balance sheet or a price chart can follow each step and check it. The verdict can be read in a few minutes, and the full arithmetic and sources take longer. The terms below appear throughout, and each one is explained in plain words the first time it matters.

The underwriting method has three layers, applied in order. Layer 1 is a set of hard floors. A company that fails any floor is rejected, and the later layers are not run. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five operating measures that drive the specific business, graded PASS, WATCH or FAIL. Layer 3 is an entry-timing check: how far the price sits above the nearest believable level of support, measured in units of ATR (average true range), the average daily price swing over 14 sessions. Only a company that clears all three layers can be bought, and even then the timing rule decides whether to buy now, buy on a dip, or wait.

The financial terms come first. ROCE, return on capital employed, measures how much operating profit the business earns on the money tied up in it. A figure of 15 percent or more is the Indian floor, and 18 percent or more is preferred for Core names. Debt-to-equity compares borrowed money with the owners' money; below 0.70 is the floor. CFO to PAT, the cash conversion ratio, compares cash from operations with reported profit over three years. A ratio of 0.70 or more means the reported profit is backed by real cash. ROE, return on equity, is the same idea measured against shareholders' funds. P/E, the price-to-earnings ratio, is the share price divided by earnings per share; a P/E of 60 means the price is 60 times one year of earnings.

The technical terms describe price behaviour. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, a bit like a trend line drawn through the daily noise. The 20-day, 50-day and 200-day averages are the three the house rules use. Weinstein staging divides a stock's life into four seasons. Stage 1 is a flat base where buyers and sellers are balanced. Stage 2 is the markup, a sustained uptrend. Stage 3 is the topping phase, where the trend loses momentum. Stage 4 is the decline. Core names need to be in a Stage 1 base breakout or a Stage 2 markup. RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer for momentum on a 0 to 100 scale; below 30 is called oversold, which means the stock has fallen unusually fast, but it does not by itself mean a bounce is due.

The momentum gate is a code test that runs before any allocation. Test A asks whether the stock is already in an established uptrend that is also outperforming the benchmark. Test B asks whether it is entering an uptrend from a tight base with volume drying up before a breakout. A stock that fails both gets no allocation however good the business. The stop-loss and asymmetry rules then size the bet. Asymmetry is the potential gain to a defensible target divided by the potential loss to the stop. Core names need at least 3.0 to 1, and Tactical names need at least 3.5 to 1. The target must be anchored to forward earnings multiplied by a historical median P/E, or to a measured move from a base. An arbitrary target is not allowed.

The verdict labels follow from these tests. IMMEDIATE_BUY means the price is at support with the asymmetry floor met. ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP means it is near support and a small pullback is still needed. WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT means it is extended and should not be chased. REJECTED means a hard floor failed, and the report names the floor. Each verdict names its rule, the number that triggered it, and the level that would change it.

What the company does and how it earns

Global Health runs large multi-specialty hospitals. A simple way to picture the business is a set of hotel-sized buildings that are fully fitted out before anyone checks in. The fixed cost of the building, the equipment and the specialist doctors is paid whether the beds are full or empty, so the profit depends heavily on how many beds are occupied and how much each occupied bed earns. The company reports both numbers. Average revenue per occupied bed, known as ARPOB, reached ₹70,244 in the June 2026 quarter, up 5.5 percent, and occupancy was 62.6 percent [3]. Operational beds rose 22.1 percent to 3,037 over the same year [3].

Volume is the main driver this quarter. Inpatient volume rose 27.7 percent year on year and outpatient volume rose 34.5 percent [3], and InvestyWise's summary puts the inpatient rise at 28 percent [18]. Management attributed most of the ARPOB gain to patient volumes, a favourable case mix and higher-acuity specialties rather than to a general price increase [6]. That matters for the outlook, because a rise driven by volume and case mix depends on the company's ability to keep filling beds with complex cases, which is harder to forecast than a tariff. The second cluster of hospitals, which includes Lucknow, Patna and the newer Noida unit, had an ARPOB of ₹61,742, up 9 percent [19].

The Noida hospital is the main source of uncertainty in the numbers. It is still ramping up. Its EBITDA, earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, loss narrowed to ₹4.9 crore in the June quarter from ₹23.6 crore in the March quarter [6]. Management described occupancy there as 30 to 40 percent and still moving as new beds are added [6]. Management also said the unit was ahead of its own internal return expectations and that breakeven could come earlier than planned [19]. Those statements are management's view and have not yet been confirmed by a full year of results.

The earnings picture for the quarter is strong on revenue and mixed on profit. Consolidated total income was ₹1,326.2 crore, up 26.2 percent, and EBITDA was ₹315.3 crore, a margin of 23.8 percent [4] [17]. Profit after tax was ₹157.3 crore, down 1.1 percent on a year earlier, but the earlier quarter included a one-time gain of ₹19.6 crore, so the comparison is distorted [17]. Revenue from operations was ₹1,304.05 crore [5]. Excluding Noida, EBITDA rose 23.9 percent to ₹320.1 crore at a 25.8 percent margin [17]. The quarter's filing is dated 30 July 2026 on the company's results page [14], and a stock-data site lists the quarterly report as dated 31 July 2026 [23].

The company is also in a large build-out. The Guwahati project was raised from 400 to 650 beds, with an estimated project cost of about ₹970 crore [7] [21]. Management's five-year capital plan is about ₹4,850 crore, which it said would be funded through a combination of cash flow and debt [6] [20]. Free cash flow is the cash left after capital spending, and in the year to March 2026 it was negative at ₹123 crore [1]. So the business is growing and borrowing capacity will matter over the next few years.

The annual figures show the same shape over a longer period. Consolidated sales rose from ₹3,275 crore in the year to March 2024 to ₹4,410 crore in the year to March 2026 [1]. Net profit rose from ₹478 crore to ₹554 crore over the same two years [1]. Over three years, sales compounded at 18 percent and profit at 19 percent [1]. Operating margin is the share of sales left after running costs, and it was 21 percent in the year to March 2026 [1].

What this means for an owner. A shareholder in Global Health owns a hospital operator whose profit moves with patient volume, the mix of complex procedures, and the speed at which new beds fill. The company has more capacity coming, which is good for the long term if the beds fill and bad for cash in the short term if they do not. The numbers so far point in the right direction, with volume strong and the Noida loss narrowing. The next test is whether the new capacity earns its cost of capital, and that will show in the results over the next several quarters.

What would change this view:

  • If occupancy at the Noida hospital stalls near 40 percent for two quarters, the ramp is slower than management describes and the capital plan looks more expensive.
  • If ARPOB growth falls below inflation while volume keeps rising, the mix story is weakening.

The business is easier to describe than to forecast. The next chapter turns to the Layer 1 numbers, which measure whether the financial base is strong enough to justify a long holding at any price.

Layer 1 gates: the hard financial and technical floors

Layer 1 is the set of floors a candidate must clear before anything else is considered. The financial floors ask whether the company earns a good return on the money it uses, whether it is free of heavy debt, and whether its profit arrives as cash. The technical floor asks whether the chart shows a new buyer joining an uptrend rather than arriving after it has finished. A failure on any one of these ends the analysis, and the report names that failure as the gate status.

The return on capital is the first test. ROCE divides operating profit by the capital the business uses. A figure of 17.4 percent means the company earns about ₹17 of operating profit for every ₹100 of capital tied up in it. That clears the 15 percent floor, though it sits below the 18 percent that the house rules prefer for Core names [1]. The annual ROCE series from Screener shows 20 percent in the year to March 2024 and 17 percent in the year to March 2026, so the return has eased from its peak but has not fallen below the floor [1]. The return on equity is 15.2 percent [1]. The standalone Screener page gives a different P/E, and this report uses the consolidated figure throughout [1].

Debt is the second test. The floor is a debt-to-equity ratio below 0.70, and it does not apply to lenders. Global Health is an operator, not a lender, so the rule applies to it directly. At March 2026 the company had borrowings of ₹1,190 crore, equity capital of ₹54 crore and reserves of ₹3,908 crore [1]. Dividing borrowings by total equity of ₹3,962 crore gives 0.30. That passes, with room, but borrowings rose from ₹718 crore a year earlier, and the capital plan described in the business chapter could push the ratio higher.

Cash conversion is the third test, and it is where a reader should look carefully. The floor is cumulative operating cash flow divided by cumulative net profit over three years, at least 0.70. Cash from operations was ₹612 crore, ₹624 crore and ₹714 crore for the three years to March 2024, 2025 and 2026. Net profit was ₹478 crore, ₹481 crore and ₹554 crore over the same years [1]. The totals are ₹1,950 crore and ₹1,513 crore, so the ratio is 1.29. That passes. The cash line has been steady rather than volatile, which is a good sign for this test.

The technical floor is the fourth test, and it fails. A Core candidate must be in a Stage 1 base breakout or a Stage 2 markup, and a Tactical candidate must be in a Stage 2 markup above rising 50-day and 200-day averages. The ledger classifies the stock as Stage 3. The price of ₹1,280.20 is 3.2 percent above the 200-day average of ₹1,239.93, but it is 10.1 percent below the 50-day average of ₹1,423.95 and 8.7 percent below the 20-day average of ₹1,402.73 (ledger). The 50-day average rose 1.5 percent over 21 sessions, but it fell from ₹1,431.62 over the last ten sessions, which the ledger labels FALLING/FLAT. A Stage 2 uptrend needs the price above both averages and both averages rising. Global Health has neither. The stock is 17.1 percent below its 52-week high of ₹1,544 (ledger).

The stage test also checks for a Stage 4 breakdown, which is a death cross where the 50-day average falls below the 200-day average on heavy volume. The 50-day average is still ₹184 above the 200-day average, so that check passes. The company therefore sits in the Stage 3 topping phase. The advance that preceded it has stalled, and the quiet volume near the pivot is only an early sign that a new base might form, not a confirmed one.

Technical posture and the momentum gate

The momentum gate is the code test that decides whether a candidate can receive any allocation at all. It is separate from the three layers because it asks a narrower question: is this stock behaving like a stock that can be bought now? It has two routes. Test A, for names already in an uptrend, requires that the price sit above the 50-day average, that the 50-day average be rising over 21 sessions, that the 200-day average not be falling over 63 sessions, that the stock outperform its benchmark over 63 sessions, and that the price be within 1.2 ATR of support. Test B, for names entering an uptrend, requires that the 50-day average not fall by more than half a percent over 21 sessions, that relative strength be rising over 28 sessions, that the price sit within one ATR of the base pivot, that volume dry up during the base, and that volume expand later. A candidate that passes either route qualifies.

For Global Health, Test A fails on its structure condition, because the price of ₹1,280.20 is below the 50-day average of ₹1,423.95 (ledger). The other three Test A conditions pass: the 200-day average is not falling, the stock's 63-session relative strength is positive at 2.54 percent, and the price is 0.45 ATR above the 63-day base low, which is well within the 1.2 ATR limit. The price sitting below the 50-day average is the only Test A condition that fails. The 50-day average is up 1.54 percent over 21 sessions, from ₹1,402.31 to ₹1,423.95, but over ten sessions it fell from ₹1,431.62 (ledger). Its short-term slope is therefore flat to down, and with the price below it the stack that defines a Stage 2 structure is not in place.

Test B fails on two of its five conditions. The first is relative strength over 28 sessions, which is negative at minus 7.84 percent against the Nifty 50 benchmark, so the stock has been losing ground to the market over the past month. The second is volume expansion: the 20-day volume is 0.3 times the reference level, against the required 1.0 (ledger). The conditions that pass are the 50-day average holding within its limit over 21 sessions, the base pivot being close at 0.45 ATR, and volume drying up in the base at 0.13 times the 63-day level, which is below the 0.9 limit (ledger). Those passes are early signs of a base. The failures are the confirmation signs, and a base is not confirmed without them.

Asymmetry and target anchoring

Asymmetry is the ratio that tells a buyer whether the risk is worth taking. It divides the potential gain to a defensible target by the potential loss to the stop. A ratio of 3.0 to 1 means you can lose one unit if the stop is hit and expect to gain three units if the target is reached. The house floor is 3.0 to 1 for Core names and 3.5 to 1 for Tactical names. The formula is the target minus the current price, divided by the current price minus the stop.

The rule matters only if the target is honest. The house method requires the target to be anchored either to forward earnings multiplied by a historical median price-to-earnings ratio, or to a measured move from the base. Analysts' price targets are not an accepted anchor. If a target cannot be verified, the gate fails on asymmetry, however attractive the business looks. That failure is unverifiable rather than measured: the stock has not been shown to fall short of 3.0 to 1. The REJECTED verdict does not depend on it, because the technical and momentum failures are each sufficient on their own.

The stop-loss estimate

A stop-loss is the price at which the position is exited because the thesis has failed. It is a fixed rule, not a feeling. The house rules set the Core stop below the lowest of three levels, the 20-day swing low, the 50-day average and the 200-day average, and then subtract one ATR. The Tactical stop uses a shorter set of levels and half an ATR. ATR, the average true range, measures how far the price usually moves in a day, so subtracting it gives the stop room to survive normal noise.

For Global Health the three Core reference levels are the 20-day swing low of ₹1,258.80, the 50-day average of ₹1,423.95 and the 200-day average of ₹1,239.93. The lowest is the 200-day average at ₹1,239.93. The 14-day ATR is ₹47.92 (ledger). The Core stop is therefore ₹1,239.93 minus ₹47.92, which is ₹1,192.01. That is 6.9 percent below the close of ₹1,280.20.

The Tactical stop uses the 20-day swing low, the 20-day average and the 50-day average. The lowest of those is the swing low at ₹1,258.80. Subtracting half an ATR of ₹23.96 gives ₹1,234.84, which is 3.5 percent below the close. The Tactical stop is tighter because it uses shorter averages, which means a Tactical position would be exited sooner if the trend turned.

The number: Core and Tactical stops

Layers 2 and 3: why they are not evaluated

Layers 2 and 3 are the operating scorecard and the support-aware entry posture. The scorecard picks three to five measures that drive a specific business, such as occupancy and volume for a hospital operator, and grades each one PASS, WATCH or FAIL. The entry posture then measures how far the price sits above the nearest believable support, in ATR units, and assigns a buy action. Both layers are designed to answer a question that only arises after Layer 1 is passed: is this a good business at a price where a new buyer should act?

Global Health fails Layer 1 on the technical and momentum floors, and it fails the reward-to-risk floor because no target can be anchored. A company that fails Layer 1 is rejected under the house rules, and the scorecard and entry posture are not run for it. The report therefore does not grade occupancy, ARPOB or the Noida ramp on a PASS, WATCH or FAIL scale, and it does not assign an entry action. The facts above are still reported, because they explain the business, but they are not converted into a scorecard grade.

The reader should not take that as a judgment that the operating scorecard would fail. The volume and ARPOB figures would probably grade well, and the Noida ramp would probably be graded WATCH. But those grades would be a guess at this stage, and the rules say that an unrun layer is reported as not evaluated rather than estimated. If the technical floor passes on a later run, the scorecard would be built then, from the same sources cited here.

What would change this view:

  • If Layer 1 passes on a later run, the operating scorecard would be built from the June quarter disclosures and the next filing, and the entry posture would follow from the ledger's distance from support.

Fatal-flaw red team

A red team is a deliberate attempt to break the verdict. The question is not whether the business is good, which the financial floors already show, but whether the decision to reject it could be wrong, and how. Three fatal flaws are considered here, each stated as strongly as the evidence allows.

The first flaw is that the chart is breaking down rather than basing. The stock has fallen 14.4 percent in two weeks and 13.5 percent in a month (ledger). Its RSI is 33.6, which is near the oversold zone, and its relative strength over 28 sessions is minus 7.84 percent. A bear reading says this is a trend that keeps falling toward the 52-week low of ₹956, which would be a further 25 percent decline (ledger). A reader should note that the house rule does not predict that path. It only says the technical floor fails today, and a falling price is itself evidence that the floor is not yet met.

The second flaw is that growth is being funded with debt and cash that the company does not yet generate. The five-year capital plan of about ₹4,850 crore is larger than a year of operating cash flow, and free cash flow was negative at ₹123 crore in the latest full year [1] [6]. Borrowings rose from ₹718 crore to ₹1,190 crore in a single year [1]. If the ratio rises toward 0.70, the financial floor fails. Today the 0.30 figure leaves room, but the direction is the wrong way for a balance sheet.

The third flaw is that the earnings story depends on a ramp that is not yet proven, and on a valuation that already expects it. Noida occupancy is 30 to 40 percent and still moving, and Q1 profit fell 1.1 percent year on year, even though the base-quarter gain distorts the comparison [6] [17]. The trailing P/E is 60.2 and the forward P/E on the analyst median is about 48 [1] [8]. If the Noida ramp slips or ARPOB growth slows, the multiple could compress faster than the earnings grow. The reward-to-risk floor is already failed for that reason.

These flaws do not overturn the verdict; they reinforce it. The first is the technical floor itself. The second is a warning that would become a financial failure if the debt ratio moved toward 0.70. The third is the reason the asymmetry floor cannot be passed on today's evidence. The bull case, which is that volume growth and the Noida breakeven continue, would be tested by the same numbers, and the report would reopen the verdict if that case started to show in the price.

The final part gathers the terms and the notes on what was checked. Every number in the text traces to a research record or to the ledger. Each record cites a page that the report links to. The references are numbered in the order they first appear in the text.

Glossary

ROCE is operating profit divided by the capital the business uses. ROE is net profit divided by shareholders' funds. Debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by total equity. CFO is the cash the business generates from its activities. PAT is profit after tax. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. ARPOB is average revenue per occupied bed per day. Occupancy is the share of operational beds in use. P/E is the share price divided by earnings per share. ATR is the average true range over 14 sessions. Stage 1 to Stage 4 are the four phases of a stock's price cycle. The momentum gate is the code test for whether a stock can take new money. Asymmetry is potential gain divided by potential loss.

Methodology and data notes

The ledger figures are as of the session close on 13 October 2026. Every price, average and indicator is copied from the ledger without adjustment. The fundamental figures are the latest full-year and quarterly figures that could be read. Each one is dated by its period. The verdict does not depend on any figure that could not be verified. The technical and momentum failures rest on the ledger. The asymmetry failure rests on the absence of a verified multiple. The financial floors pass on figures from the Screener consolidated tables, and the report discloses that those tables are not the primary statement.
