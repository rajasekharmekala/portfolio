A moving average is the average closing price over a fixed number of sessions. Think of it as a smoothed trend line. The 20-day average is a short-term line, the 50-day is the medium-term line and the 200-day is the long-term line. When the price is above a line, recent buyers are in control of that horizon. When it is below, sellers are.

Weinstein stages describe the four seasons of a stock. Stage 1 is a quiet base after a decline. Stage 2 is the markup, the season of rising prices and rising averages, and it is the only season in which this report looks for new buying. Stage 3 is the top, where the price flattens and starts to wobble. Stage 4 is the markdown, the season of falling prices and falling averages, and it is the season in which this report looks for risk. A stock that is in Stage 4 is not automatically a short sale. For a long-only investor it is a reason to avoid, trim or hedge.

The relative strength index, or RSI, is a speedometer from 0 to 100 that measures how fast a price has moved recently. Readings from 55 to 72 are the momentum band this report uses for buy candidates: strong enough to show buyers are active, but not so hot that the move is exhausted. Readings below 30 mean a stock has fallen unusually fast. That can precede a bounce, but it does not guarantee one, and a falling trend can keep a reading low for weeks.

The average true range, or ATR, is the normal daily wobble of a price. A stock with an ATR of 50 rupees moves about 50 rupees on an ordinary day. The report uses ATR to size stops, because a stop placed inside the normal wobble will be hit by noise rather than by a real change in the trend.

Volume is how many shares changed hands, which is a way of counting how many people voted with their money. A breakout on heavy volume, shown here as at least 1.5 times the 20-day average volume, is more believable than one on thin trading. The report's volume multiple is today's volume divided by that average.

Option open interest and the put-call ratio are the market's fear and positioning gauges. Open interest is the number of option contracts still open at each strike price. The strike with the most call contracts is called a call wall, and option sellers who wrote those calls often hedge in a way that leans against a rally above it. The strike with the most put contracts is a put wall. The put-call ratio compares put open interest with call open interest. These levels are crowded positions that many participants watch. They are not guaranteed barriers, and the report says so each time it uses one.

A stop-loss is a price you decide before you buy, at which you sell to cap the loss. It is a pre-committed exit so that the decision is made when you are calm rather than when the price is falling fast. Reward-to-risk is the amount you could gain divided by the amount you could lose if the stop is hit. The report requires at least 3.5 times the risk as the potential reward before a setup qualifies as a standout. Position size is the last piece: the rule that no single holding should be able to do serious damage to the whole portfolio if its stop is hit.

1.2 Options Call and Put Walls: the Market's Ceilings and Floors

Options are the market's ceilings and floors in one sense: they are contracts whose open interest is spread across strike prices, and the strikes that hold the most contracts become crowded. The mechanism is simple. Someone who sold a call option at 23,000 has promised to deliver shares if the index finishes above that level, so as the index approaches 23,000 the seller tends to buy futures to hedge, which can slow the rally. The same logic applies below spot to put sellers at 22,500. The report uses the strikes with the most open interest as a map of where many participants are watching, and it says clearly that the map is not a law.

5. Smart Money Radar: Institutional Flows, Bulk Deals and Events

Smart money is a loose name for the large investors whose trades are visible to the public: foreign and domestic institutions, company insiders and the largest block buyers. Their trades are not always right, and a large buyer can be wrong for months. But their activity is one of the few independent checks on the price signals in the previous chapters. A breakout that is backed by institutions has a better chance of lasting than one that is driven only by short-term traders. This chapter asks whether the money that moves the market agrees with the Bull and Bear Lists or disagrees with them.

There are three sources in this chapter, and each has a different limit. The first is the institutional cash flow, which shows how much foreign and domestic money bought and sold on the exchange in total. The second is the derivatives position of institutions, which shows how they are betting on the index in futures. The third is the list of bulk and block deals, which shows large single trades that the exchange requires to be reported. Each source is a snapshot of one session or a short run of sessions. None of them is a complete picture of what an institution intends.

5.2 Bulk and Block Deals

A bulk deal is a trade in which a single client buys or sells more than 0.5 percent of a company's shares in one day. The exchange publishes these trades the same evening, and they show the name of the client. A block deal is a large negotiated trade in a separate window. The published record is useful because it shows who is trading large amounts, but it has a serious limitation on this session: many of the large trades show the same counterparty on both the buy and the sell side. When that happens, the trade is not evidence that a new investor has taken a position. It is a transfer between accounts, a broker's internal match, or a hedging trade, and the public file cannot tell which.

6. Tactical Playbook and Capital Allocation

The playbook is where the earlier chapters become a posture. It asks three questions in order. What exposure does the regime call for? Which names, if any, come closest to a standout setup? And how much of the book should be committed to any of them? The answers are short today, and the reasons for them matter as much as the answers themselves, so this chapter explains each step in full.

The standard's regime table is explicit about the Downtrend posture. Hold the maximum cash the policy allows, add no new momentum longs, and trim leveraged and Stage 4 holdings. The table exists because a Downtrend has historically been a period in which most new buying loses money, even when individual names look attractive. The cost of waiting in a Downtrend is usually small. The cost of buying into one is often large. That imbalance is the reason the posture is defensive.

6.3 Sizing, Stops and an Illustrative Example

Sizing is the step that protects the rest of the book from any single name. The principle is that a holding should be small enough that its stop, if hit, costs an amount the investor has already accepted. For a book of about 4.69 million rupees, a loss of 0.5 percent is about 23,400 rupees, and that is a reasonable upper limit for any single new position in a Downtrend. The example below uses that limit. It is illustrative only, because none of the three names is a buy today.

Suppose PNB had passed the volume test and you were willing to buy 100 shares at about its 9 October close of 116.98. The cost would be 11,698 rupees. The stop at 114.03 means the most you could lose is 100 times 2.95, which is 295 rupees. That is 0.006 percent of the 4.69 million rupee book, far inside the 0.5 percent limit, so a position of that size would be acceptable on risk grounds. The target at the 52-week high of 135.15 would produce a gain of 100 times 18.17, which is 1,817 rupees, a reward of 6.2 times the risk. The example also shows what the limit would permit. Because the risk per share is so small, a reader might be tempted to buy far more shares. The limit is a ceiling, not a target, and the reader should size the position by the amount of capital that the Downtrend posture allows, not by the stop distance alone. If the stop were instead set two average true ranges below the close, at 111.34, the stop-out loss would be 564 rupees, and the breakeven hit rate, which is the share of trades that must succeed for the approach to break even at that reward-to-risk, would rise from 14.0 to 23.7 percent, because the reward-to-risk would fall to 3.2 to 1. Brokerage, taxes and slippage would reduce the net gain, and the ledger does not provide lot-level or tax-lot data for these figures, so the example uses gross prices only.

7. Watchlist Synchronization and Idea Ingestion

The watchlist is the research funnel. A name on the watchlist is not a recommendation to buy. It is a candidate that will be examined in depth by the three-layer underwriting process, which tests the business, the price and the position size before anything is bought. Putting a name on the watchlist is therefore a way of saying that it deserves that examination, and the rules for admission are strict because the underwriting is expensive. This chapter explains how the market report feeds that funnel, and why the funnel receives no new names from this session.

The rule for admission is that a market report may name only the setups from section 6, and only when those setups clear the gate. On 9 October none of them does. Glossary

The entries below are in the order a reader meets the terms in the report. Each one is used with the meaning given here, and a term that appears in a table heading is also defined in the paragraph before that table.

Average true range (ATR). The typical daily movement of a price, averaged over 14 sessions. A stock with an ATR of 50 rupees usually moves about 50 rupees on an ordinary day. It is used to place stops outside normal noise.

Bear List. The report's list of 30 names that pass the distribution tests: price below its averages, averages in falling order, RSI below 45. It is a risk map, not a short-selling list.

Breadth. The share of stocks in the screen pool that sit above their own 50-day or 200-day average. Breadth shows whether a market move is broad or confined to a few names.

Bulk deal. A trade in which one client buys or sells more than 0.5 percent of a company's shares in a day. It is published by the exchange with the client's name.

Call wall and put wall. The strikes with the most open call and put contracts. They are crowded positions that often act as resistance or support. They are not guaranteed barriers.

Confirmed. The qualifier for a name that passes the trend, momentum and volume tests together. It does not by itself meet the reward-to-risk test for a standout setup.

Delivery percentage. The share of traded volume that was taken into the buyers' accounts rather than squared off on the same day. Higher figures suggest longer-term holders.

Distribution day. A session on which an index falls on heavier volume than the session before. A cluster of them signals that large holders are selling into weakness.

DII and FII. Domestic institutional investors, such as mutual funds and insurers, and foreign institutional investors. Their net flows show which side of the market is buying and selling.

Downtrend. The ledger regime label when the Nifty 50 sits below its key averages, breadth is weak and distribution days are heavy. The posture is maximum cash and no new momentum longs.

Max pain. The option strike at which the total value of open contracts would be smallest at expiry. It is reported because traders watch it, but the evidence that prices move toward it is weak.

Put-call ratio (PCR). Put open interest divided by call open interest. A reading above 1 means more put contracts are open, which is a defensive positioning.

Reward-to-risk. The distance from the close to the target divided by the distance from the close to the stop. A standout setup requires at least 3.5 to 1 in this report.

RSI (relative strength index). A 14-session measure of momentum from 0 to 100. The report's buy band is 55 to 72. Below 30 is conventionally called oversold.

Stage 1 to Stage 4. The four phases of a stock's trend: basing, markup, topping and markdown. Only Stage 2 names are candidates for new buying in this report.

Stop. A predetermined price at which a position is sold to cap the loss. The formula used here is the higher of the last 20-day swing low and the 50-day average less half the ATR.

Relative strength. How a stock or sector has performed compared with the index over a period. A positive reading means it has beaten the index; a negative reading means it has lagged.

Short sale. Selling a share you do not own, in the hope of buying it back more cheaply later. This report does not recommend short selling.

Sensex. The benchmark index of the Bombay Stock Exchange, made of thirty large companies.

Spot. The current level of an index or price, as opposed to the level of a future or option contract.

Swing low. The lowest price over a recent stretch of sessions, used as a reference for placing stops.

The book. Your whole portfolio of holdings, taken together.

Watch-only. A name with good arithmetic that fails at least one required test. It is tracked and not bought.
