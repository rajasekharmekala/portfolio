A moving average smooths a price into a trend line. The 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days, roughly one month. The 50-day moving average covers about ten weeks and the 200-day moving average covers about forty weeks. Think of them as lines drawn through a bumpy road to show which way it runs. A price above a rising average means buyers have been paying more over time. A price below a falling one means the opposite.

A share moves through four seasons called stages. Stage 2 is the growing season, when the price sits above a rising 50-day and 200-day average. Stage 4 is winter, when the price sits below falling averages. Stage 1 is the quiet spell after a fall and Stage 3 is the tired top before one. A base is a flat stretch of trading after a decline, like a landing on a staircase where the price rests before the next climb.

RSI (the relative strength index) is a speedometer for a price on a scale of 0 to 100. Above 70 means the price has run fast and may be stretched, and below 30 means it has fallen fast. ATR (the average true range) is the normal daily wobble of a share in rupees. If a share has an ATR of 50, a one-day move of 50 rupees is ordinary, so a stop placed only 20 rupees below the price would be hit by noise and not by news.

Volume is the number of shares that changed hands, which you can picture as people voting with their money. A rise on heavy volume carries more weight than a rise on thin volume. The put-call ratio divides the open put contracts by the open call contracts: puts are bets or insurance against a fall, calls are bets on a rise. An option wall is a strike price with an unusually large number of open contracts, which often acts as a ceiling (calls) or a floor (puts), because the sellers of those options defend them.

A stop loss is an exit price you choose before you buy, so that a wrong idea costs a known amount. A limit order is an order that fills only at your chosen price or better. An entry zone is the band of prices where you would be willing to buy. A breakout is a move above a price the share failed to pass before. The pivot is that trigger price, and a retest is a pullback that touches the broken level again to check that it now holds as support.

Reward-to-risk divides what you could gain by what you could lose. A ratio of 3.5 to 1 means each rupee at risk has a chance to earn 3.5 rupees. Asymmetry is the same idea seen as a shape: a bet whose upside is much larger than its downside. This report asks for at least 3.5 to 1 before it calls anything a setup. Position size is the other half of safety. Even a good idea should be small enough that being wrong does not hurt, which is why no single share should decide your year.

The regime is one of four words: Uptrend (broad), Uptrend (narrow breadth), Correction, or Downtrend, set by where the Nifty 50 sits against its 50-day and 200-day averages and by how many shares sit above their own 50-day average. Watch only means reward-to-risk is below 3.5 to 1, so there is no setup yet, even for a confirmed name, because the ratio is judged separately from the chart tests.

1.2 Options map: ceilings and floors that sellers defend

Options give a second reading of the market, because they show where traders have put real money. Large open interest in call options at a strike creates a ceiling, since the sellers of those calls profit if the index stays below it and tend to sell the index as it rises toward it. Large open interest in put options creates a floor for the opposite reason. The max pain strike is the level at which option buyers as a group lose the most, and prices often drift toward it into expiry. These are not laws. They describe where positions are heavy, and they can be overrun by news.

One feature of this session's chain deserves a plain warning. The contract in the sources expires on 19 October itself, the session date, so its strikes cluster around the closing price and say more about settlement than about the next month. 1.3 Global macro matrix: what reaches Indian stocks from outside

India buys most of its oil abroad and sells shares to foreign funds, so prices set in London, New York and the currency market reach Indian shares within hours. Oil is the clearest channel: a higher price raises costs for paint, tyre, aviation and chemical companies and lifts producers. The US 10-year yield matters because foreign funds compare Indian equity returns with what a US government bond pays. The dollar index and the rupee show how expensive it is for those funds to hold rupees.

4. The Bear List: Top 30 Stage 4 Breakdown Watchlist

Stage 4 is the season of decline, the phase in which a price sits below a falling 50-day average and a falling 200-day average, so every rally has been sold. The Bear List collects the shares in the deepest version of that state. Its hurdles mirror the Bull List's: price under the 50-day average under the 200-day average, RSI under 45, and distribution volume, meaning heavy trading on down days at more than 1.5 times the 20-day norm. A share that fails only the volume test is still in Stage 4, and the qualifier says so.

Why list shares nobody owns? Because a regime label is only as good as its evidence, and the Bear List is the evidence. 191 of 499 names are in Stage 4 this session, 38.3% of the universe, against 47 in Stage 2. The screen finds 6 names that pass every test, 120 more that miss on volume and 47 more in a relaxed form, so the 30 rows below are a selection from a very large pool and every tier has a full ten. 6 of the 30 are confirmed and 24 are labelled no distribution-volume confirmation, which means the trend and RSI tests pass and heavy-volume selling has not yet shown up, usually because the decline is slow and steady and not panicked.

The list is used three ways. A holder checks whether any of their names appears, and the answer this session is none of the 19 holdings, although two holdings are in Stage 4 for other reasons: Interarch Building Products sits under both averages with RSI 28.9, and Oriana Power shows Stage 4 on a price series that the 16 October audit identified as a stock-split artefact. A buyer uses the list to avoid new purchases in these names. A hedger uses it to choose which parts of the market to protect. Nothing here is a short-selling instruction, because a share at the bottom of a Stage 4 decline can bounce sharply and a short position can lose more than it started with. The sector tie matches Section 2: Financial Services supplies 7 rows, FMCG 4, and cement, chemicals, power and auto parts three each, which are the lagging groups on the industry map.

5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

Large investors matter because they trade in sizes that move prices, and because they have teams that read company filings before most people do. Foreign portfolio investors (FIIs) move money in and out of India as a group, and a week of heavy selling usually means the whole market is under pressure. Domestic institutions (DIIs), mostly mutual funds and insurers, are steadier buyers because retail savers feed them every month. Their flows are published each evening in provisional form, and the exchange also publishes who holds what in index futures and which very large trades happened on the day.

These signals have limits. A provisional flow figure can be revised, a long or short position in futures can be a hedge, and a large block trade may be a market maker's inventory and not a conviction. Glossary

  • Asymmetry: a bet whose possible gain is much larger than its possible loss, measured as reward-to-risk.
  • ATR: the average daily range of a share over 14 days, used to judge whether a stop is too close.
  • Bulk deal and block deal: large single-stock trades that the exchange publishes with buyer and seller names.
  • Breadth: the share of stocks above a moving average; low breadth means few stocks support the index.
  • Delivery percentage: the part of a day's traded quantity that was settled by delivery and not squared off within the day.
  • Distribution day: a down day on rising volume, a sign that large holders are selling.
  • Entry zone: the band of prices where a limit order would buy.
  • FII and DII: foreign portfolio investors and domestic institutions, whose daily net cash flows the exchange reports.
  • India VIX: the market's gauge of expected movement over the next 30 days, taken from option prices.
  • Index futures positions: contracts on the index held by each participant group; a net short means more short than long contracts.
  • Ledger: the file of verified prices and indicators that every number in this report comes from.
  • Limit order: an order that fills only at your price or better.
  • Max pain: the strike at which option buyers as a group lose the most if the index settles there at expiry.
  • Moving average: the average closing price over a set number of days; the report uses 20, 50 and 200.
  • Open interest: the number of option or futures contracts that are still open.
  • Option wall: a strike with an unusually large open interest, often a ceiling for calls or a floor for puts.
  • Promoter pledge: the part of the founders' shares pledged to lenders as security for loans.
  • Put-call ratio: put open interest divided by call open interest; above 1 means more puts than calls.
  • Regime: the market's overall weather: Uptrend (broad), Uptrend (narrow breadth), Correction or Downtrend.
  • Reward-to-risk: the possible gain to the target divided by the possible loss to the stop.
  • RSI: a 0 to 100 speed gauge of recent price moves; below 30 is oversold and above 70 is overbought.
  • Stage 1 to Stage 4: the four seasons of a share: base, markup, top and markdown.
  • Stop loss: the exit price chosen before buying.
  • Trade-for-trade (BE) series: an exchange series in which every trade is settled by delivery; the price file shows no delivery figure for it.
  • Volume multiple: today's volume divided by its 20-day average.
