The ratio is a fear gauge, and the strikes with the most open contracts act like a ceiling and a floor, because the traders who sold those options defend them. A stop-loss is an exit price you decide in advance, before emotion arrives. Risk and reward compares what you could lose with what you could gain; we ask for at least 2.5 rupees of possible gain for each rupee at risk. Position size is how much money goes into one idea, and it is the reason no single stock can hurt you badly.

1.2 Options Gamma Wall & Institutional Positioning Map

Options are contracts that let traders bet on where an index will be on a set date. The traders who sell those contracts have to defend their positions, so the strikes with the most open contracts behave like a ceiling and a floor. The ceiling is the call wall, the floor is the put wall, and max pain is the level at which sellers collectively pay out least. The same section also reads the foreign institutional investors' (FII) index-futures and options positions, which show whether big overseas funds are betting on a rise or a fall. 2. Sector & Thematic Rotation Matrix

Money in a stock market behaves like water in a house with many rooms: it rarely leaves the building at once, and more often drains from one room into another. Rotation is the name for that drain. In a healthy market, money flows from sectors that have already run into sectors that are just waking up, and the index keeps rising. In a market like today's, there are few rooms worth moving into, so most of the water simply runs out. Reading rotation tells a beginner two things the index cannot: where the exits are crowded (the sectors to avoid or trim), and where the little strength that exists sits (the only sectors from which long ideas are worth considering).

The ledger tracks nine sector indices from NSE, and places each in one of four quadrants by combining its trend with its strength relative to the benchmark. A sector in the Leading quadrant is both strong and gaining on the market. Improving means weak but turning up, Weakening means strong but fading, and Lagging means weak and getting weaker. 3. The Bull List: Stage 2 Momentum Leaders

The Bull List is the short list of stocks that have already completed the hardest part of a rise: a long, flat base followed by a breakout, which is a close above the top of that base, into a sustained uptrend. Analysts call that phase Stage 2. A stock qualifies when three things hold at once. Its price is above its 50-day average and that average is above the 200-day average, which says buyers have won for ten weeks and ten months. Its trading volume is at least 1.5 times the 20-day average on a day the price rises, which says the crowd is voting with money. Its RSI sits between 55 and 72, which means it is moving fast enough to matter but not so fast that it has outrun itself. A stock that passes these three tests is tagged confirmed. The fourth test, a reward of at least 2.5 times the risk to the stop, is applied afterwards in the last column of each table, because it depends on where you enter.

4. The Bear List: Stage 4 Breakdown Watchlist

If Stage 2 is spring and summer, Stage 4 is a long winter: the price has fallen below its 50-day average, that average has fallen below the 200-day average, and both are sloping down. The Bear List collects stocks in that condition that also show heavy selling: an RSI under 45 and volume at least 1.5 times the 20-day average on a day the price fell. Heavy selling on a falling price is called distribution, because large holders are handing shares to smaller ones. Anyone who has watched a stock slide 5% a day will recognise the pattern, but the point of the list is not that every name will keep falling. The point is to see where the pressure is before it reaches a portfolio.

A beginner might ask why a report that rarely recommends shorting spends a whole chapter on falling stocks. There are three reasons. The list is an avoid list: a stock in Stage 4 with distribution volume is a poor place to add money. It is a trim list: if you already own one, the rules in the audit tell you when to sell. And it is a hedge guide: sectors that appear repeatedly show where a hedge would do the most good. It is not a short signal on its own. Twenty of the thirty names have an RSI below 30, which means they have fallen unusually fast and are statistically prone to sharp bounces, and a short seller who ignores that is exposed to sudden 10% rallies.

6. Tactical Playbook & Capital Allocation

A playbook turns everything above into a small number of decisions, and it starts from the weather. The risk posture for each regime is fixed in advance so that no one improvises under stress. In an uptrend with broad breadth, the posture is to deploy toward the top of the equity band and add on pullbacks. In an uptrend with narrow breadth, it is normal deployment with leaders only. In a Correction, cash goes to the top of its band and only Stage 2 names within half an ATR of support are added. In a Downtrend, which is today's regime, the posture is maximum cash, trimming leveraged and Stage 4 holdings, and no new momentum longs. Those rules are the reason the three setups below are sized at zero.

Three setups appear anyway because a playbook that only says "do nothing" gives a reader no way to act when conditions change. 6.3 Sizing the First Setup in Rupees

A setup without a size is only an opinion. This subsection sizes Setup 1 as if the regime allowed a new entry, to show the arithmetic you would use when it does. The policy sizes a new tactical position by the amount of net worth you are willing to lose if the stop is hit (the risk budget) and caps the result by a tier limit. This report uses the smallest tier, TIER_4, for a new name in a weak market: a risk budget of 35 basis points (0.35% of net worth) and a cap of 2% of net worth. That choice is a judgment, made because the setup is conditional and the regime is a Downtrend.

Worked example: Anand Rathi Wealth at the top of the band. Entry is ₹2,197.60 and the stop is ₹2,147.60, so the stop distance is ₹50.00, or 2.28% of the entry. If the stop is hit, the loss is 38 x ₹50.00 = ₹1,900, which is 0.045% of net worth. If the measured-move target of ₹2,534.70 is reached, the gain is ₹12,810, so the reward is 6.74 times the risk. The example shows why the tier cap, not the stop, limits the size: the stop is tight, so the formula would allow a much bigger position than the cap permits. 7. Watchlist Synchronization & Idea Ingestion

The watchlist is the pipeline between a screen and a portfolio. A name enters it because a daily report spotted a chart worth examining, and from there it goes through a three-layer underwriting before it can be shortlisted or bought. Layer one asks whether the business is financially sound: debt, interest cover, promoter pledges and whether profits turn into cash. Layer two asks whether the operating story is improving, using a handful of company-specific measures. Layer three asks whether the chart and the reward-to-risk arithmetic justify buying now. A stock can have a beautiful chart and fail layer one, and a stock with a clean balance sheet can sit in Stage 4, so a name needs all three to earn money.

Glossary

50-DMA and 200-DMA
The average closing price over the last 50 or 200 trading days. A price above both averages, with both rising, is the basic sign of an uptrend.
ATR (average true range)
A stock's normal daily price range in rupees over 14 days. Stops are placed a set number of ATRs away so that ordinary wobble does not trigger them.
Bulk deal and block deal
A bulk deal is a trade above 0.5% of a company's shares that the exchange publishes; a block deal is a negotiated trade executed in a separate window. Neither shows intent by itself.
Cr (crore)
Ten million rupees. ₹1,000 Cr is ₹10 billion.
Delivery percentage
The share of traded shares that were actually taken into a buyer's account instead of being bought and sold within the same day. Low delivery on a high-volume day suggests churn.
DII and FII
Domestic institutional investors (funds, insurers, banks) and foreign institutional investors (overseas funds). Their daily cash-market net flows are published by the exchange.
Distribution day
A session in which the index falls at least 0.2% on higher volume than the session before. A cluster of them is a sign of institutional selling.
Max pain
The option strike at which option sellers pay out the least at expiry.
Open interest and put-call ratio (PCR)
Open interest counts option contracts not yet closed. PCR divides open put contracts by open call contracts; below 0.8 is call-heavy, above 1.2 is put-heavy.
Regime
The ledger's one-word reading of the market: Uptrend, Correction or Downtrend, based on the Nifty 50 against its averages and on breadth.
Reward-to-risk
Possible gain to the target divided by possible loss to the stop. This report requires at least 2.5:1 for a setup.
RSI (relative strength index)
A 0 to 100 measure of how fast a price has moved. Below 30 is oversold, above 70 is overbought.
Stage 1 to 4
Stage 1 is a base, Stage 2 the climb, Stage 3 a top and Stage 4 the decline, judged from price and the two averages.
Stop-loss
A pre-decided exit price. Here the stop is the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-DMA less half an ATR.
VIX (India VIX)
The market's expected 30-day volatility, implied by index options. Higher means more fear.
