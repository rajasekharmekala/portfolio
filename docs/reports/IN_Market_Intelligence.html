1. The big picture: regime, benchmarks and derivatives

The regime is the first question any market report has to answer, because every other decision depends on it. A regime is the condition of the market as a whole, measured from the index and from how many stocks are participating in its movement. The ledger uses the Nifty 50 as the benchmark. It asks three questions: is the index above or below its 50-day average, is the 50-day average itself rising or falling, and how many stocks in the broad pool are above their own averages. A regime matters because it changes the cost of being wrong. In a broad uptrend a stock that fails a screen is usually a stock with a specific problem. In a Downtrend, even a good company can fall as the tide goes out, so a sound stock is still a poor buy if the market's direction is against it. That is why the report asks the regime question before it looks at any single name. It also explains why the breadth number is as important as the index level. An index can rise on a handful of heavyweights while most stocks fall. Breadth shows how many stocks are actually taking part, and a narrow rally is less dependable than a wide one.

1.2 Options levels: the ceilings and floors

Options are contracts that give the right to buy or sell an index or stock at a fixed price, the strike, before a set date, the expiry. Open interest is the number of contracts that are still open. The strikes with the most open interest show where option sellers have placed their bets, and those sellers tend to defend their positions by buying or selling the index as it approaches those strikes. This is why large put and call open interest can act like a floor and a ceiling. It is not a law of nature, because the sellers can also be overwhelmed. But it is a real pattern, and it gives the reader a set of levels to watch.

Three measures summarise the derivatives picture. The first is the maximum-pain strike, the price at which the largest number of option contracts would expire worthless. The second is the call wall, the strike with the highest call open interest, which is usually read as resistance. The third is the put wall, the strike with the highest put open interest, which is usually read as support. The put-call ratio adds a sentiment reading. A ratio above one means more put open interest than call open interest, which many traders read as defensive positioning. The ratio computed from the exchange's own derivatives bhavcopy for the 13 October expiry is 1.40: total put open interest divided by total call open interest across all strikes. Vendor pages captured during the session show 1.33 to 1.40, and they differ because each counts a different set of strikes or was captured at a different time. The report uses the exchange-based figure.

Today is an expiry day, the last trading session before a weekly contract expires, and that matters for how much weight to give these levels. Open interest on an expiry day is concentrated in the expiring strikes, and the sellers of those contracts have a strong reason to hold the index near them until the close. The levels below are therefore most useful as a guide to where the market was defended today, and less useful as a map for the coming weeks. The next expiry will produce a new set of walls.

Indian stocks do not trade in isolation. They respond to the price of oil, which India imports in large quantity, to the dollar and the rupee, and to the yield on United States government bonds, which sets the return that global investors can earn without taking risk. The most important link is oil. When Brent crude rises, India's import bill rises, the rupee tends to weaken, and the inflation and interest-rate outlook shifts. Each of those effects reaches listed companies differently. Oil refiners and paint and tyre makers feel input costs first, while the oil and gas producers gain from the higher price. The rupee matters to exporters such as software firms, whose revenue is in dollars, and to importers, whose costs rise when it weakens. The United States Treasury yield matters because foreign investors compare the return on Indian shares with the return on American bonds, and a higher yield can pull money away from emerging markets.

2. Sector rotation: where money is moving

Sector rotation describes the way money moves between groups of stocks as the economy and the market change their minds. Think of it as water moving between rooms of a house: when one room fills, the water flows elsewhere. A sector can be leading, meaning its relative strength against the market is rising, or lagging, meaning its relative strength is falling. The ledger combines two tests. The first is whether the sector index is above or below its averages, which is an absolute test. The second is how far the sector has outperformed or underperformed the Nifty 50 over the last month, which is a relative test. A sector in the Leading quadrant has positive relative strength, and one in the Weakening quadrant has the same strength fading. The four quadrants are Leading, Improving, Weakening and Lagging.

3. The bull list: Stage 2 momentum leaders

The bull list is the set of stocks that the screen judges to be in Stage 2, the markup phase, where the trend is up and the price is strong. The screen does not select the stocks that the writer prefers. It applies a fixed set of hurdles that were written before the day's prices were known. A stock must trade above its 50-day average, which must itself be above its 200-day average. Its momentum, measured by the 14-day RSI, must sit between 55 and 72, which is strong without being overheated. Its volume on the day must be at least 1.5 times its 20-day average, which shows that the move has participation. And its reward-to-risk ratio, measured to the 52-week high, must be at least 3.5 to 1. A name that passes all of these is confirmed. A name that passes the trend and the momentum but not the volume is labelled awaiting volume confirmation. A name whose RSI sits outside the band is labelled with that fact.

4. The bear list: Stage 4 breakdown watchlist

The bear list is the set of stocks that the screen judges to be in Stage 4, the markdown phase, where the price is below its averages and the trend has turned down. The hurdles are the mirror image of the bull hurdles. A name must trade below its 50-day average, which must be below its 200-day average, or show a death cross where the 50-day crosses below the 200-day. Its RSI must be below 45, showing weak momentum. And its volume on down days must be heavy, at least 1.5 times the 20-day average, which shows that sellers are in control. A name that passes all of these is confirmed. A name that passes the trend and momentum but not the distribution-volume test carries the qualifier no distribution-volume confirmation, which is the most common qualifier in this list.

The screen produced thirty names across three cap tiers, which is a full list: ten large, ten mid and ten small. Six names are confirmed: three large caps (SCHAEFFLER, ICICIPRULI and MARUTI), two mid caps (TIINDIA and TIMKEN) and one small cap (MGL). The other twenty-four carry the qualifier that the distribution-volume test was not met. That qualifier does not mean the stock is safe. It means the stock is in a downtrend whose volume on the day does not show the heavy selling the rule asks for. A downtrend with light volume can still be a downtrend, but it is one where the selling is quieter and perhaps more persistent.

The bear list matters in a Downtrend for three reasons. The first is that it tells a long-only investor which holdings are most exposed. A holding on this list is a holding in a falling trend, and the regime posture says to trim such holdings. The second is that the list shows the sectors under the most pressure. Autos, capital goods, consumer staples and financial services each appear several times, which means the decline is spread across the economy rather than concentrated in one place. The third is that the list does not tell you to short. The report does not recommend short selling, because a stock in Stage 4 can stay in Stage 4 for a long time or can turn quickly. The purpose of the bear list is to say where to avoid, trim or hedge, and where a genuine bottom would have to appear before the risk eased.

5. Smart money and the event calendar

Smart money is a loose phrase for the large investors whose trades can move a stock: foreign and domestic institutions, mutual funds, insurance companies, promoters and the traders who handle big blocks of shares. Their trades are reported publicly in two ways. Bulk deals are trades where a single investor buys or sells more than half a per cent of a company's shares in one day. Block deals are negotiated trades in large blocks, executed in a window before the market opens. Both are published by the exchanges, and both can show who is buying and who is selling. Their limit is that a reported trade can be part of a larger plan, a hedge, or an internal transfer between related accounts, so a single deal rarely tells the whole story.

5.2 Delivery: who held the shares on the day

Delivery is the share of a stock's traded volume that was held by the buyer past the end of the trading day, rather than bought and sold within the same session. A high delivery share means the buyers intended to keep the shares, which is a sign of investors rather than traders. A low delivery share means most of the volume was intraday trading, which can be a sign of speculation. Delivery matters most when price and volume disagree. A stock that rises on heavy volume with low delivery is a stock whose move may be driven by traders who will sell the next day. A stock that falls on heavy volume with high delivery shows that most of the volume came from investors giving up their shares rather than day traders churning, which tends to mean that longer-term holders are reducing their stake.

5.3 Results, promoter pledges and the event rule

Two kinds of company events matter most for the holdings and the top setups: results dates and promoter pledges. A results date is the day a company reports its quarterly earnings. A high pledge can be a sign of financial stress, because a fall in the share price can trigger a forced sale of the pledged shares to repay the lender. The pledge figure is reported in the quarterly shareholding pattern, so it is not current on any given day.

The event rule the report applies is simple. Before a results date, the risk of an unexpected move rises, so a position that would be hard to hold through a large gap should be reduced in advance, not after the announcement. A worked example shows the arithmetic.

Worked example: the pre-results rule with rupee arithmetic. Suppose you hold 200 shares of a company that closed at 1,000 rupees, with a 50-day average of 950 and a stop at 900. Your position is worth 200,000 rupees, and your loss to the stop would be 20,000 rupees, which is 10 percent of the position. The company announces that its results will be released in two days. A results surprise could move the price 8 percent in either direction on the day. If it falls 8 percent, the price goes to 920, which is still above the stop, but if it falls 10 percent the stop at 900 is breached and the sale would be at a worse price than planned. A pre-results rule would say that a holder who cannot accept a 10 percent move should sell one third of the position before the announcement, which costs the expected gain on those shares but limits the loss on the rest. The arithmetic is simple: selling 66 shares at 1,000 rupees removes about 66,000 rupees of exposure to the gap. Whether to do so depends on your own tolerance and on the trend, which is why the report presents the rule as an option and not an order.

Glossary

Breadth. The share of stocks in the screening pool that trade above their own 50-day average. Breadth shows how many stocks are taking part in a move. A market with high breadth is more dependable than one where a handful of stocks carry the index. Here breadth is 21.5 percent, which is low.

Bulk deal and block deal. A bulk deal is a trade in which one investor buys or sells more than half a per cent of a listed company's shares in one day. A block deal is a negotiated trade in a large block of shares, executed in a window before the regular session. Both are published by the exchanges.

Call wall and put wall. The strike prices with the most open call and put contracts. The call wall is usually read as resistance, and the put wall as support, because option sellers tend to defend those levels.

Confirmed, awaiting volume confirmation, and RSI outside the band. The three qualifier labels used by the bull screen. Confirmed means every hurdle was met. Awaiting volume confirmation means the trend and momentum passed but the day's volume did not reach 1.5 times its 20-day average. RSI outside the band means the trend passed but the 14-day RSI sat outside the 55 to 72 range.

Delivery share. The percentage of a stock's traded volume that was held by the buyer past the end of the session. A high share means investors are holding the shares. A low share means most of the volume was intraday trading.

Distribution day. A day on which the index falls on heavier volume than normal. A cluster of distribution days is a sign that sellers are pushing into the market, and the ledger counts them over the last twenty-five sessions.

Downtrend. The regime label used when the index is below both its 50-day and 200-day averages. It is the most defensive of the four labels in the report.

Expiry day. The last trading session before a derivatives contract expires. Open interest on an expiry day is concentrated in the expiring strikes, which makes option levels less reliable as a guide beyond the day.

The 50-day and 200-day averages. The 50-day average is the medium trend line and the 200-day average is the long trend line. A 50-day average above the 200-day average describes a healthy trend, and the reverse describes a weak one.

FII and DII. Foreign institutional investors and domestic institutional investors. The daily net flow of each group is published by the exchanges as the difference between what they bought and what they sold in the cash market.

Maximum pain. The strike price at which the largest number of open option contracts would expire worthless. It is one of the levels option traders watch, especially on expiry day.

Open interest and put-call ratio. Open interest is the number of option contracts that are still open. The put-call ratio divides the open interest in puts by the open interest in calls. A ratio above one means more puts are open than calls.

Reward-to-risk ratio. The distance from the entry price to the target divided by the distance from the entry price to the stop. The mandate in this report is 3.5 to 1.

Stage 1 to Stage 4. The four stages of a stock's trend. Stage 1 is a base, Stage 2 is a sustained rise in price, Stage 3 is a top where momentum fades, and Stage 4 is a markdown in which the trend has turned down.

Stop-loss. A price, decided in advance, at which a position is exited to limit the loss. In this report the stop is the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average less half the ATR.

Swing low. The lowest price over the last twenty sessions. It marks the most recent level at which buyers stepped in, and it is used as the anchor for the stop.

Average true range (ATR). The average daily price range over fourteen sessions, a measure of normal daily movement. It is used to size stops so that they sit outside ordinary noise.

RSI (relative strength index). The relative strength index is a momentum measure from zero to one hundred over fourteen sessions. Above 70 a stock is overbought and below 30 it is oversold. The report uses the 55 to 72 band as the healthy range for a breakout.

Regime label. The ledger's classification of the market as Uptrend (broad), Uptrend (narrow breadth), Correction or Downtrend. Each label carries a posture from the standard.

Volume multiple. The day's traded volume divided by the average daily volume over the previous twenty sessions. A reading of 1.5 or more is the screen's volume hurdle.

Each exception is listed below in plain English, with the checker's own line where one exists, the reason it cannot be cured now, how it lowers confidence, and what would cure it.

Exception 1: incomplete sector classification. The ledger's sector indices for Auto, Energy, Financial Services, FMCG, Infrastructure, Media, Metal, PSU Bank and Realty returned no usable price history (the ledger reports "only 1 bars" for each). Without history the ledger cannot place those sectors in a quadrant, so the sector rotation analysis covers only Information Technology and Pharma, and the breadth counts stand in for the rest. This lowers confidence in the sector narrative, because the rotation across most of the economy cannot be seen. It would be cured by a rebuild of the ledger once the sector-index data source returns history for those symbols, after which the quadrant table would need to be redrawn in full.
