A moving average smooths a price into a trend line. The 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days, roughly one month. The 50-day moving average covers about ten weeks and the 200-day moving average covers about forty weeks. Think of them as lines drawn through a bumpy road to show which way it runs. A price above a rising average means buyers have been paying more over time. A price below a falling one means the opposite, and that is the situation of the Nifty 50 today: it closed at 22,687.65 against a 50-day average of 23,441.16 and a 200-day average of 24,142.06.

Stage analysis, which this report uses, describes four seasons of a share. Stage 1 is a quiet base, like winter's end. Stage 2 is the markup, the growing season, when the price sits above a rising 50-day average and above its 200-day average. Stage 3 is the top, a summer that has stopped growing. Stage 4 is the markdown, a falling price under a falling average, the winter we most want to avoid. The Nifty 50 is in Stage 4 now. A breakout is a move above the highest recent price, usually on heavy volume, and it is how a base ends. A stock that reaches Stage 2 while its market is in Stage 4 is swimming upstream, which is possible but needs more proof.

The relative strength index, RSI, is a speedometer from 0 to 100 that measures how fast a price has moved. Below 30 means oversold, which says a fall has been unusually steep and that bounces are likely. It does not say the fall is over. Above 70 means overbought, a very fast rise. The average true range, ATR, is the normal daily wobble of a share in rupees. A share with an ATR of 3 rupees moves about 3 rupees on an ordinary day, so a stop-loss placed 1 rupee away from the price will probably be hit by noise. Volume counts how many shares changed hands. A price move on twice the usual volume is a vote with real money behind it, and a move on thin volume is easier to reverse.

The put-call ratio compares open put options with open call options. A low reading means more call contracts are open than puts. The option walls are the strikes where the most contracts are open. Because those who sell options defend their strikes, a heavy call strike acts like a ceiling and a heavy put strike like a floor. A stop-loss is an exit price decided in advance, so that a loss stays small without a judgement made in a panic. Reward-to-risk divides what you could win by what you could lose: at 3.5 to 1, one winning trade pays for three and a half losing ones. Position size is how much of your money goes into one idea. It matters because a share that falls 30% costs you 3% of your portfolio if it is a tenth of the portfolio, but 15% if it is half.

1.2 Options map: the ceilings and floors traders are defending

Options are contracts that let their buyers bet on, or protect against, a move in the index by a set date. For every buyer there is a seller, and the sellers (called writers) are usually large professional desks that earn the premium and then defend their strike. Where a lot of contracts are open at one strike, the writers there have a reason to push the index away from it. A heavy call strike above the market therefore behaves like a ceiling, a heavy put strike below it like a floor, and the strike where option buyers collectively lose the most money, called max pain, tends to pull the index toward it as expiry nears. None of this is a prediction. It is a map of where the money is positioned for the 27 October weekly expiry.

On 22 October the map had a clear shape. Put open interest was 15.61 crore contracts against 18.99 crore of calls, giving a put-call ratio of 0.82, which means more calls than puts are open and the crowd is leaning bullish and not defensive ( AOC Calculator, 22 Oct 2026, 15:40 IST ; Finostat, NSE timestamp 22-Oct-2026 15:30 ). The highest call open interest sat at 24,000 and the highest put open interest at 22,000, a wide corridor that is 5.8% above and 3.0% below the close. Nearer the money, put writers were adding at 22,500 to 22,700, with the biggest build at 22,600, and call writers were active above 22,700, with 23,000 the first ceiling ( Bajaj Broking trade setup, 22 Oct 2026 ).

3. The Bull List: Top 30 Stage 2 Momentum Leaders

The Bull List is the short list of shares that have reached Stage 2, the phase in which a share moves from a base into a sustained uptrend. The ledger screens all 501 names of its Nifty 500 universe against hurdles set in advance (499 of them have the price history that the breadth figures need, and 497 had a one-day change in the exchange count): the price must be above its 50-day average, and the 50-day average above the 200-day average; volume must reach at least 1.5 times its 20-day normal; RSI must sit between 55 and 72; and the standard asks for a reward-to-risk of at least 3.5 to 1 before a name becomes a setup, a test applied in Section 6. A name that meets the first two tests and the volume and RSI tests is called confirmed. 4. The Bear List: Top 30 Stage 4 Breakdown Watchlist

Stage 4 is the season of decline. A share in Stage 4 trades under a falling 50-day average and under its 200-day average, and when it falls on heavy volume that is called distribution: large holders are selling to whoever will buy. The Bear List collects the thirty clearest examples, ten in each size tier. Its hurdles mirror the Bull List's: price under the 50-day average, the 50-day under the 200-day, RSI below 45, and volume above 1.5 times normal on a down day. 12 of the thirty meet every hurdle and are labelled confirmed. The other 18 have the trend and the RSI but not the heavy down-day volume, and are labelled no distribution-volume confirmation. The label is easy to misread: SRF, for instance, traded 8.74 times its normal volume but on a day when the price rose, so it carries the weaker label.

A beginner may reasonably ask why a list of falling shares matters if you never sell short. The answer has three parts. First, avoidance: a share in Stage 4 is a poor place to put new money, because most rebounds in the first months of a markdown fail. Second, trimming: a holding that joins the list is a candidate for a smaller position or an exit, as the playbook sets out. Third, hedging: for investors who do use options or index shorts, these are the names where the downside is already confirmed by the crowd. The list is therefore a risk radar and not a trade signal. Its composition is revealing. 29 of the 30 come from industries in which fewer than 20% of names sit above their 50-day average, with Financial Services supplying 8, Capital Goods 6 and Consumer Services 3. 5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

Charts show what prices have done. Flows show who has been doing it. Large investors matter because they trade in sizes that move prices, and they leave records: the exchange publishes how much foreign and domestic institutions bought and sold each day, what positions they hold in index futures, and every large single trade. That evidence has limits. Institutions are wrong often, they trade for reasons unrelated to a company's quality, and a figure for one day is noisy. So this chapter asks only one question: do the flows agree with the charts in Sections 3 and 4, or do they argue with them?

They agree, with one qualification. Foreign investors are selling and are positioned for more falls, which matches the Bear List. Domestic institutions, mainly mutual funds and insurers, are buying nearly as much as the foreigners sell, which explains why the Nifty 50 is down only 3.10% in a month while breadth has collapsed: the index is held up by a few large names that domestic funds keep buying. The large single trades add nothing directional, because the biggest of them are proprietary trading firms buying and selling the same shares. Glossary

20-day, 50-day and 200-day moving average
The average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 trading days; a line that smooths the price into a trend.
Stage 1, 2, 3 and 4
The four seasons of a share: a base, a markup, a top and a markdown. Stage 2 means price above a rising 50-day average and above the 200-day average; Stage 4 means price under a falling 50-day and under the 200-day.
Regime
The one-word label for the whole market: Uptrend (broad), Uptrend (narrow breadth), Correction or Downtrend, set by the Nifty 50 against its averages and the breadth of the market.
Breadth
The share of stocks above a given average. Thin breadth means a rally or a hold-up is carried by a few shares.
RSI
Relative strength index: a 0 to 100 speedometer. Under 30 is oversold; over 70 is overbought.
ATR
Average true range: the normal daily wobble of a share in rupees, used to place stops.
Volume multiple
Today's traded volume divided by its 20-day average. 1.5 or more means heavy trading.
Delivery percentage
The share of traded shares actually moved into investors' accounts and not squared off within the day; a sign of conviction.
Distribution day
A session where the index falls on heavier volume than the day before, a sign of institutional selling.
India VIX
The market's fear gauge, derived from Nifty option prices; higher means traders pay more for protection.
Put-call ratio
Open put contracts divided by open call contracts. Under 1 means more calls than puts are open.
Call wall and put wall
The strikes with the most open call or put contracts; they act like a ceiling and a floor because option writers defend them.
Max pain
The strike at which option buyers collectively lose the most money at expiry.
FII and DII
Foreign and domestic institutional investors; their net cash flows are published each evening.
Bulk and block deal
Large trades disclosed by the exchange with the names of buyer and seller.
Promoter pledge
Shares that a company's founding group has put up as collateral for a loan; a pledge can force selling in a falling market.
QIP
Qualified institutional placement: a share sale to institutions at a discount, which dilutes existing owners.
Stop-loss
An exit price decided in advance, so a loss stays small without a judgement made in a panic.
Reward-to-risk
The gain from entry to target divided by the loss from entry to stop; 3.5 to 1 is the floor for a tactical setup.
Risk unit
The amount you would lose if the stop is hit, expressed as a share of net worth, used to size a position.
Qualifier
The ledger's label for how strongly a name passes the screen: confirmed, awaiting volume confirmation, or outside the RSI band for the Bull List; confirmed or no distribution-volume confirmation for the Bear List.
Conditional setup
A pre-approved idea with a stated entry band, stop and target that receives size only when a market gate opens.
