With ten minutes, read the key figures, the lede and Section 6. With an hour, read everything in order. Each section ends with a short note on what it means for you and a pointer to the next one. Ten ideas carry the whole report, and each has an everyday comparison.

A moving average smooths a price into a trend line. The 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days, roughly one month. The 50-day moving average covers about ten weeks and the 200-day moving average covers about forty weeks. Think of them as lines drawn through a bumpy road to show which way it runs. A price above all three means buyers have been in control for a long time. The report also writes them as 20-DMA, 50-DMA and 200-DMA.

A share moves through four seasons called stages. Stage 2 is the growing season, when the price is above a rising 50-day and 200-day average. Stage 4 is winter, when the price is below falling averages. Stage 1 is the quiet spell after a fall and Stage 3 is the tired top before one. A base is a flat stretch of trading after a decline, like a landing on a staircase where the price rests before the next climb.

RSI (the relative strength index) is a speedometer for a price on a scale of 0 to 100. Above 70 means the price has risen very fast, below 30 means it has fallen very fast, and 55 to 72 on a rising share is a healthy pace. ATR (the average true range) is the normal daily wobble of a share in rupees. If a share has an ATR of 20, a daily move of 20 is ordinary, so a stop placed closer than that will be hit by noise.

Volume is the number of shares that changed hands, which you can picture as people voting with their money. A rise on heavy volume carries more weight than a rise on thin volume. Delivery percentage is the share of the day's quantity that buyers took home instead of trading back within the day. The put-call ratio compares bets on a fall (puts) with bets on a rise (calls). Option walls are the strikes with the most open contracts, and they act like a ceiling and a floor because the sellers of those options defend them.

A stop loss is an exit price you choose before you buy, so that a wrong idea costs a known amount. A limit order is an order that fills only at your chosen price or better. An entry zone is the band of prices where you would be willing to buy. A breakout is a move above a price the share failed to pass before. The pivot is that trigger price, and a retest is a pullback that touches the broken level again to check that it now holds as support.

Reward-to-risk divides what you could gain by what you could lose. A ratio of 3.5 to 1 means each rupee at risk has a chance to earn 3.5 rupees. Asymmetry is the same idea seen as a shape: a bet whose upside is much larger than its downside. This report asks for at least 3.5 to 1 before it calls anything a setup. Position size is how much of your money goes into one idea, and even a good idea should be small enough that a wrong call does not hurt badly.

1. The Big Picture: Market Regime, Benchmarks & Derivatives

A market regime is the market's overall weather. It is the most useful single fact in this report, because the same chart deserves a different reaction in a rising market than in a falling one. A breakout that works in spring fails far more often in winter. The report therefore fixes the regime first and lets it guide every later section. The rule is mechanical and uses only the benchmark, the Nifty 50. If the index closes above its 50-day and 200-day averages and breadth is wide, the regime is an uptrend. If it falls below the 50-day average but holds the 200-day, the regime is a correction. If it closes below the 200-day average, the regime is a Downtrend.

1.2 Options map: the ceiling and the floor

Options give a second reading of the market, because they show where traders have put real money. Buyers and sellers of options leave open contracts at each strike price. The strike with the most open call contracts tends to act as a ceiling, since sellers of those calls defend it. The strike with the most open put contracts tends to act as a floor. The max pain strike is the level at which option buyers as a group would lose the most, and prices often drift toward it as expiry nears. The figures below are for the nearest expiry, 19 October 2026, and come from the exchange's own derivatives file for Friday. The put-call ratio over every strike is 1.32, meaning put open interest (235,729,000) is larger than call open interest (178,055,995), which usually signals that many traders hold protection or expect support.

How to read the options map

Each row is a strike price with a role. Open interest counts live contracts, so a larger number means a stronger wall. Read the call wall row aloud: the biggest pile of open calls sits at 24,000, so rallies toward that level meet sellers. The put wall at 22,000 is the biggest pile of open puts, so declines toward it meet buyers. The spot row shows where the index closed relative to both, and the max pain row shows the level that favours option sellers on expiry day. A wide gap between the walls means a wide trading range is being defended. A wall is a crowded strike, not a law: when price runs through it, the sellers unwind and the wall moves.

A tier-3 idea has a risk budget of 50 basis points, which is 0.50% of net worth, and a ceiling of 3.5% of net worth. A narrow stop therefore does not let you bet more than the ceiling, and a wide stop shrinks the position automatically. 7.1 What the three-layer underwriting will test

Underwriting runs every company through three layers. Layer 1 is a set of pass-or-fail floors. Layer 2 scores three to five company-specific measures of how the business is changing, and four of five must pass. Layer 3 asks whether the price is close enough to support to enter now, which is the timing test. A name has to clear all three. The technical setups in Section 6 speak mainly to Layer 3, so Layers 1 and 2 are where the new names are most likely to fail.

Glossary

  • Asymmetry: a bet whose possible gain is much larger than its possible loss, measured as reward-to-risk.
  • ATR: the average daily range of a share over 14 days, used to judge whether a stop is too close.
  • Beta: how strongly a holding has moved with the index over one year; 1.0 moves one-for-one.
  • Breadth: the share of stocks above a moving average; low breadth means few stocks support the index.
  • Bulk deal and block deal: large single-stock trades that the exchange publishes with buyer and seller names.
  • Delivery percentage: the part of a day's traded quantity that was settled by delivery and not squared off within the day.
  • Distribution day: a down day on rising volume, a sign that large holders are selling.
  • Entry zone: the band of prices where a limit order would buy.
  • FII and DII: foreign portfolio investors and domestic institutions, whose daily net cash flows the exchange reports.
  • India VIX: the market's gauge of expected movement over the next 30 days, taken from option prices.
  • Index futures positions: contracts on the index held by each participant group; a net short means more short than long contracts.
  • Ledger: the file of verified prices and indicators that every number in this report comes from.
  • Limit order: an order that fills only at your price or better.
  • Max pain: the strike at which option buyers as a group lose the most if the index settles there at expiry.
  • Moving average: the average closing price over a set number of days; the report uses 20, 50 and 200.
  • Open interest: the number of option or futures contracts that are still open.
  • Option wall: a strike with an unusually large open interest, often a ceiling for calls or a floor for puts.
  • Promoter pledge: the part of the founders' shares pledged to lenders as security for loans.
  • Put-call ratio: put open interest divided by call open interest; above 1 means more puts than calls.
  • Qualifier tag: the ledger's label on each list row: confirmed, awaiting volume confirmation, or a note on which test failed.
  • Regime: the market's overall condition, set by the Nifty 50 against its averages: uptrend, correction or Downtrend.
  • Relative strength: how a sector or share has performed against the broad market.
  • Reward-to-risk: the possible gain to the target divided by the possible loss to the stop.
  • Risk budget: the percentage of net worth you accept losing if a stop is hit.
  • RSI: a 0 to 100 speedometer for recent price change; above 70 is fast, below 30 is slow.
  • Stage 1 to 4: the four phases of a share: base, advance, top and decline.
  • Stop loss: a pre-chosen exit price that limits the loss if the idea is wrong.
  • Underwriting: the three-layer test (hard floors, key measures, entry timing) that a watchlist name must pass.
