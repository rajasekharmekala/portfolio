Ten ideas carry the whole report. Each one has an everyday comparison.

A moving average is the average closing price over the last few weeks. Think of it as a line drawn through a bumpy road to show which way the road runs. A 20-day moving average is the average close of the last 20 trading days, about one month. The 50-day moving average is the average of the last 50 days, about ten weeks. The 200-day moving average is the average of the last 200 days, about 40 weeks, and it marks the long-term trend. When a price sits above all three, buyers have been in control for a long time. In this report the 50-day and 200-day averages are also written 50-DMA and 200-DMA.

A stock moves through four seasons, called Stages. Stage 2 is the rising phase, like spring and summer: the price is above a rising 50-day and 200-day average. Stage 4 is the falling phase, like winter: the price is below falling averages. Stage 1 is the quiet period after a fall, and Stage 3 is the tired top that comes before a fall. A base is a flat stretch of trading after a decline, like a landing on a staircase where the price rests before the next climb.

RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer for a price, from 0 to 100. Above 70 means the price has risen very fast. Below 30 means it has fallen very fast. A reading between 55 and 72 on a rising stock is a healthy pace. ATR (average true range) is the normal daily wobble of a stock in rupees. If a share has an ATR of 20, a move of 20 in one day is ordinary, and a stop placed closer than that will be hit by noise.

Volume is the number of shares that changed hands. Think of it as the count of people voting with their money. A rise on heavy volume carries more weight than a rise on thin volume. Delivery percentage is the share of traded quantity that buyers actually took home rather than traded back within the day. The put-call ratio compares bets on a fall (puts) with bets on a rise (calls). Option walls are the strikes with the most open contracts. They act like a ceiling and a floor, because the sellers of those options defend them.

A stop loss is an exit price you choose before you buy, so that a wrong idea costs a known amount. A limit order is an order that will only fill at your chosen price or better. An entry zone is the price band where you would be willing to buy. A breakout is a move above a price the stock failed to pass before. The pivot is that trigger price. A retest is a pullback that touches the broken level again to check that it now holds as support.

Reward-to-risk divides what you could gain by what you could lose. A ratio of 3.5 to 1 means each rupee at risk has a chance to earn 3.5 rupees. Asymmetry is the same idea seen as a shape: a bet where the upside is much larger than the downside. This report asks for at least 3.5 to 1 before it calls anything a setup. Position size is how much of your money goes into one idea. Even a good idea should be small enough that a wrong call does not hurt you badly.

The report also uses its own labels. The regime is one of four words: Uptrend (broad), Uptrend (narrow breadth), Correction, or Downtrend. The regime sets how aggressive a careful investor should be. 1. The Big Picture: Market Regime, Benchmarks & Derivatives

A market regime is the market's overall weather. It is the single most useful fact in this report, because the same stock chart deserves a different reaction in a rising market than in a falling one. A breakout that works in spring fails in winter far more often. For that reason the report fixes the regime first and lets it guide everything that follows. The rule is mechanical and uses only the benchmark, the Nifty 50. If it closes above its 50-day and 200-day averages and breadth is wide, the regime is an uptrend. If it falls below the 50-day but holds the 200-day, the regime is a correction. If it closes below the 200-day, the regime is a Downtrend.

1.2 Options map: the ceiling and the floor

Options give a second reading of the market, because they show where traders have put real money. Buyers and sellers of options leave open contracts at each strike price. The strike with the most open call contracts tends to act as a ceiling, since sellers of those calls defend it. The strike with the most open put contracts tends to act as a floor. The max pain strike is the price at which the most option buyers would lose, and prices often drift toward it near expiry. 1.3 Global macro matrix: what reaches Indian stocks from outside

Indian stocks do not trade alone. Oil, American interest rates, the dollar and the rupee all reach the market through earnings, funding costs and foreign flows. Oil matters most to a country that imports most of what it burns: dearer crude widens the import bill and squeezes the margins of paint, tyre, aviation and chemical companies. A high US ten-year yield pulls money toward American bonds and away from emerging-market shares. A strong dollar usually weakens the rupee, which hurts importers and helps exporters.

Money does not leave the market all at once. It moves between sectors, the way water moves between rooms of a house: one room fills while another drains. Tracking that movement matters because a rebound led by one sector behaves differently from a rebound led by many. If the leading sector is also a heavy part of the index, the index can rise while most shares fall, and that is what the breadth figure of 22.0% already hinted at.

4. The Bear List: Stage 4 Breakdown Watchlist

The Bear List is the mirror of the Bull List. It names shares in Stage 4, the falling phase: the price is below a falling 50-day average, and that average is below the 200-day average. A share earns the strict grade, confirmed, when four things are true: Stage 4, an RSI below 45, trading volume at least 1.5 times the 20-day average, and a fall on the day. The last two tests matter, because heavy volume on a falling day shows that holders are selling into weakness. The second grade, no distribution-volume confirmation, keeps the downtrend and the weak RSI but drops the volume or down-day test. A name can drop to this grade only because it bounced for one day.

Why care about a list of falling shares if you never sell short? Because it answers three practical questions. Which holdings should you cut? Which cheap-looking names are falling knives that deserve a wide berth? Where is the market still being sold? 5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

Large investors move prices because they trade in sizes that small investors cannot match. Foreign portfolio investors (FIIs), domestic mutual funds and insurers (DIIs), and the proprietary desks of brokers each leave a footprint in public data: daily cash flows, open positions in index futures and bulk deals. Reading that footprint is useful for one reason. When big money and the price chart agree, a trend is more likely to last. When they disagree, the chart is running on borrowed time.

Smart money has limits. The data are published after the close, so they describe what already happened. A net buyer on one day can be a net seller the next. Participants also trade for many reasons, and a hedge looks like a bet if you do not know the portfolio behind it. For that reason each figure here is read as a clue, then compared with sections 3 and 4 to see whether it supports them or argues against them.

The short answer for 16 October is mixed. Foreign investors bought cash shares on Friday after weeks of selling, and domestic funds turned sellers for the first time in the month. At the same time, foreign investors remain heavily short in index futures. The bulk-deal file carries no signal at all. Glossary

Distribution day
A day when an index falls on higher volume than the day before, a sign that large holders are selling. Nine occurred in the last 25 sessions.
FII and DII
Foreign institutional investors (also called FPIs) and domestic institutional investors such as mutual funds and insurers.
Breadth
The share of stocks above a chosen moving average. Wide breadth means many shares support the market, and narrow breadth means a few do.
Open interest
The number of option or futures contracts that are still open at the close. It shows how much money is committed at each strike.
Put-call ratio
Open put contracts divided by open call contracts. Above 1 means more puts than calls, which usually reflects hedging.
Max pain
The strike at which option buyers as a group lose the most, and sellers gain the most, if the index settles there at expiry.
Delivery percentage
The share of the day's traded quantity that was taken for delivery rather than squared off within the day.
Bulk deal and block deal
Large trades disclosed by the exchange. A bulk deal is traded in the normal market and a block deal in a special window.
Gap
A jump from one day's close to the next day's open that skips the prices in between, so a stop cannot protect against it.
Promoter holding
The share of a company held by its founders and their group, reported each quarter.
Confirmed
The strictest grade on the Bull and Bear lists: trend, RSI, volume of 1.5 times normal and the direction of the day all agree.
Watch only
A name whose reward-to-risk at the close is below 3.5 to 1, so there is no setup yet.
Regime
The market's overall condition, set by where the Nifty 50 closes against its 50-day and 200-day averages: Uptrend (broad), Uptrend (narrow breadth), Correction or Downtrend.
