Ten ideas carry the report, and each has an everyday comparison. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line drawn through a wobbly signal. The 50-day average (50-DMA) holds roughly the last ten weeks of mood; the 200-day average (200-DMA) holds roughly the last ten months. A Stage is one of four seasons in a share's life: Stage 1 is a flat winter base (a flat consolidation floor), Stage 2 is the spring and summer climb, Stage 3 is a topping autumn, and Stage 4 is a falling winter. We prefer to own Stage 2 and to avoid Stage 4. The RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 means the price has run very fast, below 30 means it has fallen very fast, and neither is a prediction by itself. The ATR (average true range) is a share's normal daily wobble in rupees, and we use it to place stops so that ordinary noise does not shake us out. Volume counts how many shares changed hands, which is how many people voted with their money; a move on twice the usual volume is a loud vote.

The put-call ratio and the option walls are a fear gauge and a set of ceilings and floors: when many traders have sold options at one price, that price tends to act as a ceiling or floor until expiry. A stop-loss is an exit price decided in advance, written down before the emotion arrives. Risk and reward compares what you can win with what you can lose: a setup that risks 50 rupees to make 130 has a reward-to-risk of 2.6, and our floor is 2.5. Position size is how much of your total money one idea may hold, which is why no single share is allowed to hurt you badly. Finally, the regime is the market's season as a whole. Guidance is management's own forecast for the year ahead, and book-to-bill is new orders won divided by revenue billed, so a figure above 1 means the order book is growing. A breakout is a move of the price above a level where it has repeatedly stalled, and a measured move is a target found by adding the height of the preceding base to the breakout point.

Two more words matter. A limit order is an instruction to buy only at a price you choose or lower, so you wait for the market to come to you. 1. The Big Picture: Market Regime, Benchmarks & Derivatives

A regime is the market's season as a whole, and it comes first because it decides how every other number should be read. In the right season a breakout above resistance is a gift; in the wrong one the same breakout is often a trap that reverses within days. The ledger reads the season from the benchmark's position against its 50-day and 200-day averages, from how many shares are above their own averages, from the count of heavy-volume selling days and from how many sectors are rising. What makes today instructive is the gap between the headline and the interior. The Nifty 50 finished a hair lower, yet small and mid-sized companies had one of their better days in weeks. That pattern, a flat index with a rising crowd, usually appears at one of two moments: the first day of a recovery, or a short bounce inside a fall. The ledger cannot tell which one this is, but it can say what would settle the question, and that is a set of levels this chapter marks. For a beginner the practical meaning is simple: a regime label is an instruction about how much risk to carry, and until the label changes the instruction stays defensive.

1.2 Options Gamma Wall & Institutional Positioning Map

Options are contracts that pay off if the index crosses a chosen price, and the traders who sell them must hedge by buying or selling the index as it moves. That hedging creates ceilings and floors. Where many calls have been sold, rallies meet selling; where many puts have been sold, falls meet buying. Add the foreign funds' futures positions and you get a map of who is betting on what. The map matters to a beginner because it shows the likely range for the next week and flags when a move is being fought by big traders.

How to read this table. A wall is the strike price with the largest open interest, meaning the most contracts still outstanding. A call wall above the market is a ceiling; a put wall below it is a floor. Max pain is the strike where option sellers collect the most and pay the least, and indices often drift toward it on expiry day. The put-call ratio divides outstanding puts by calls; around 1.0 is balanced. 2. Sector & Thematic Rotation Matrix

Rotation is money moving from one sector to another, like water moving between rooms of a house as the doors open and close. Any Stage 2 name stops being a leader when one of four things happens. The close falls below the 50-day average, which means the buyers who drove the move have lost control. Volume expands on a down day above 1.5 times the average, which shows large holders are selling into strength. The RSI rises above 75 on a falling volume, a blow-off pattern that often precedes a drop. Or the company reports results that miss and the price gaps below its stop. Each rule is a trigger to reduce, not a reason to panic.

Glossary

50-DMA and 200-DMA
The average closing price over the last 50 or 200 trading days. A price above both averages is in a healthier position than one below them.
ATR (average true range)
A share's typical daily movement in rupees over 14 days. Stops are placed a fraction of an ATR beyond support so that normal noise does not trigger them.
Bulk deal and block deal
A bulk deal is a trade above 0.5% of a company's shares. A block deal is a large negotiated trade in a separate window. Both are disclosed after the close.
Conditional setup
A prepared limit order with a size and a stop that stays switched off until a market-wide test is passed.
Crore and lakh
One lakh is 100,000 and one crore is 10,000,000. Amounts in this report marked Cr are crore rupees.
Delivery percentage
The share of the day's traded volume that was taken for delivery, meaning held by buyers rather than bought and sold within the day.
Distribution day
A day when an index falls on higher volume than the day before. Nine of the last 25 sessions were distribution days.
FII and DII
Foreign institutional investors and domestic institutional investors, the two large groups whose daily net buying and selling the exchange publishes.
Limit order
An instruction to buy at a stated price or lower, or to sell at a stated price or higher.
Max pain
The option strike at which option sellers as a group pay out the least at expiry.
Open interest (OI)
The number of option or futures contracts still outstanding. Large OI at a strike makes that strike a wall.
Put-call ratio (PCR)
Outstanding put contracts divided by outstanding call contracts. Around 1.0 is balanced.
Regime
The market's overall season as the ledger labels it: Uptrend (broad), Uptrend (narrow breadth), Correction or Downtrend.
Reward-to-risk
The gain from the entry to the target divided by the loss from the entry to the stop. The floor for a tactical setup is 2.5.
RSI (relative strength index)
A speedometer from 0 to 100 of how fast a price has moved. Above 70 is fast, below 30 is oversold.
Stage 1 to Stage 4
The four seasons of a share: Stage 1 base, Stage 2 climb, Stage 3 top, Stage 4 decline.
Stop-loss
A sell price decided in advance, below which the trade is treated as wrong.
VIX
India VIX measures how much protection option buyers are paying for over the next month. A higher number means more fear.

Methodology & data gaps

Every price, average, RSI, ATR, volume multiple, Stage, sector quadrant, regime label and breadth figure comes from the facts ledger built for the session of 27 October 2026. The ledger downloads two years of daily prices for the Nifty 500 universe, the benchmarks, the sector indices and every holding, shortlist and watchlist name, and it computes each number in code. The Bull List and Bear List are exactly the ledger's picks, ten per size tier where available, with the confirmation label copied from each row. Stops are the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average minus half an ATR; targets are the 52-week high, or a measured move when the price is within 1% of that high. Market capitalisation and the sector group come from the ledger's pick rows, never from memory.

Everything the price history cannot supply came from the research pass and is recorded with its address and date. 1. The holdings record is not current.
