Nine ideas carry the whole report, and each has an everyday comparison. A moving average is the average price over the last N days; think of it as a trend line that smooths out the daily wobble. The 50-day average (50-DMA) shows the last ten weeks of mood, and the 200-day average (200-DMA) shows the last ten months. A Stage is one of four seasons in a stock’s life: Stage 1 is a flat winter base, Stage 2 is the spring and summer climb, Stage 3 is a topping autumn, and Stage 4 is a falling winter. We prefer to own Stage 2 and avoid Stage 4. RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 means the stock has run very fast, below 30 means it has fallen very fast, and neither is a prediction by itself. ATR (average true range) is a stock’s normal daily wobble in rupees; we use it to place a stop-loss far enough away that ordinary noise does not knock you out. Volume is how many shares changed hands; a rise on 2 times normal volume is like a crowd voting with money. The put-call ratio and option walls come from options traders: the ratio is a fear gauge, and the strikes with the most open contracts act like a ceiling and a floor because the people who sold those options defend them. A stop-loss is an exit price you decide in advance, before emotion arrives. Risk and reward compares what you could lose to what you could gain; we want at least 2.5 rupees of possible gain for each rupee at risk. Position size is how much money goes into one idea; it is the reason no single stock should be able to hurt you badly.

1. The Big Picture: Market Regime, Benchmarks & Derivatives

A market regime is the overall weather of the stock market, and it matters more than any single stock because most stocks move with the weather. In a clear uptrend, even average companies drift upward and mistakes are forgiven. In a downtrend, even good companies fall, and a correct idea about a business can still lose money for months. 1.2 Options Gamma Wall & Institutional Positioning Map

Option contracts give traders the right to buy (a call) or sell (a put) the index at a fixed price, called the strike, before an expiry date. The people who sell those contracts take on risk, and when the index drifts toward a strike where they have sold heavily, they trade the index to defend their position. Because of that, the strikes with the largest open interest (the number of contracts still alive) behave like a ceiling above the market and a floor below it. 1.3 Global Macro Matrix & Transmission Channels

India imports most of the crude oil it burns, buys dollar-priced goods, and relies on foreign investors for part of its stock-market demand, so four outside prices matter every day: oil, US interest rates, the dollar and the rupee. They reach Indian stocks by different paths. Higher oil raises costs for airlines, paints, tyres, chemicals and fertiliser makers and pushes up inflation. Higher US interest rates make dollars more attractive and draw money out of emerging markets. A stronger dollar makes the rupee weaker and imported inputs dearer, but it helps exporters such as IT services and pharma, whose revenue is earned in dollars.

2. Sector & Thematic Rotation Matrix

Money in the stock market behaves like water moving between rooms of a house: when one room fills up, another drains. Professional investors call this sector rotation, and it matters to a beginner because the sector a stock sits in explains a large share of its price movement. A good company in a draining sector usually falls with it, and an average company in a filling sector often rises. The ledger measures rotation with two numbers per sector index: how much it gained or lost over one month compared with the Nifty 50, and whether its one-week change beat the Nifty 50 as well. A sector beating the Nifty over both periods is called Leading, one beating it over the week but not the month is Improving, one that beat it over the month but not the week is Weakening, and one trailing on both is Lagging.

6.3 Sizing the First Setup in Rupees

Position sizing answers a simple question: how much money may one idea hold? Assuming a new tactical idea starts in Tier 3, the scoring file caps it at 3.5% of net worth and gives it a risk budget of 50 basis points, which is half of one percent of the portfolio lost if the stop is hit. The number to look at is not the target but the loss at the stop, because that is the amount you must be able to accept before you buy. The worked example below applies that cap to the first setup.

Worked example: ANANDRATHI at the tier cap. Net worth is ₹41,95,770. At a 3.5% cap the position is worth at most ₹1,46,852. At the planned entry of 2,235.00 that buys 65 whole shares, costing ₹145,275, which is 3.46% of net worth. The stop at 2,147.60 is 87.40 below the entry, or 3.91%, so if the stop is hit the loss is 65 x 87.40 = ₹5,681, which is 0.14% of net worth. The target at 2,529.80 would earn 65 x 294.80 = ₹19,162, so the reward is 3.37 times the risk. Brokerage, taxes and slippage reduce both sides, and they are not modelled because lot-level tax data and the broker fee schedule are not in the ledger. A gap down through the stop on bad news would cost more than the figure shown.

The arithmetic shows why position size beats prediction. Even if ANANDRATHI hit its stop, the loss of ₹5,681 is smaller than one percent of the portfolio, because the position is capped at 3.5%. Compare that with your largest holding: a 10% fall in RNITAI, which is 23.52% of net worth, costs about ₹98,700, more than seventeen times the loss at this stop. The tier cap, not the target, decides how much a mistake costs, and that is the lesson to carry from this chapter into the next. If you decide to act on any setup, you should enter in two parts, half on the first touch of the band and half after a close above the band that holds for a day, which spreads the risk of buying a falling knife.

Glossary

50-DMA / 200-DMA
The average closing price over the last 50 or 200 trading days; a trend line that smooths out daily noise.
ATR (average true range)
A stock’s normal daily price range; used to place stops so ordinary noise does not trigger them.
Basis point
One hundredth of a percentage point; a 9 basis point fall in a yield means 0.09 percentage points.
Beta
How much a stock tends to move for each 1% move in the market; a beta of 1.2 means about 1.2%.
Block deal
A large negotiated trade done in a special exchange window; NSE listed none for 23 October 2026.
Breadth
The share of stocks above a trend line, such as the 50-DMA; a measure of how many stocks share in a move.
Bulk deal
A trade, or a client’s combined trades in one stock in one day, above 0.5% of the company’s shares.
Delivery percentage
The share of traded shares that were actually taken home rather than bought and sold within the day.
Distribution day
A session in which an index falls on heavier volume than the day before, a sign of large sellers.
FII / DII
Foreign institutional investors and domestic institutional investors (mutual funds, insurers, banks).
India VIX
The market’s expected volatility over the next 30 days, derived from Nifty option prices; a fear gauge.
Max pain
The option strike at which option buyers as a group lose the most at expiry; the index often drifts toward it.
Measured move
A target found by adding the height of a base to its breakout level.
Open interest
The number of option or futures contracts still outstanding at a strike or in total.
Put-call ratio (PCR)
Total put open interest divided by total call open interest; below 1 means calls outnumber puts.
Regime
The overall market weather set by the Nifty 50 against its averages, breadth and distribution days.
Reward-to-risk (R:R)
Possible gain divided by possible loss from the entry to the stop; this report requires at least 2.5.
RSI
A 0 to 100 speed gauge of recent gains against losses; above 70 is stretched, below 30 is oversold.
Sector quadrant
Leading, Improving, Weakening or Lagging, set by a sector’s one-month and one-week returns against the Nifty 50.
Stage 1 to 4
Weinstein’s four seasons: base, uptrend, top and downtrend.
Stop-loss
A price decided in advance at which you sell to limit a loss.
Volume multiple
Today’s traded shares divided by the 20-day average; 2.0 means twice the usual crowd.
