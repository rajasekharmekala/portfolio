2. Sector rotation: where money is moving

Sector rotation is the movement of investment between groups of stocks. When the economy turns, money tends to leave the groups that were led by the last cycle and move toward the groups that will lead the next one, a bit like water moving between rooms as the doors open. For a beginner, the useful question is simple: which sectors are holding up while the index falls, and which are giving way faster than it. The answer changes the choice of stocks, because a good company in a weak sector often keeps falling with its group.

The report measures sectors the same way it measures the index. Each sector index has a stage from its own price history, a one-month lead or lag against the Nifty 50, and a quadrant. The quadrant is the combination of trend and relative strength that the ledger uses to call a sector Leading, Improving, Weakening or Lagging. A sector is Leading when it is outperforming the benchmark over the month, which is a relative measure, and the stage tells you whether its own price trend is healthy. A sector can be Leading while still in Stage 4, which means it has fallen less than the market, not that it is rising.

5. Smart money and the event calendar

Smart money is a shorthand for the large, well-resourced investors whose trades are disclosed and whose decisions often lead the market by weeks. In India the most visible disclosures are bulk deals, where a single investor trades more than 0.5 percent of a company's listed shares in a day, and block deals, which are negotiated trades of large parcels at a fixed price. Both are reported to the exchanges, and the files are public. The reader should keep the limits of this evidence in mind. A block deal shows that a large trade happened. It does not show why, and a buyer and a seller in the same deal may be a single fund moving money between two of its own vehicles. The sensible use of the data is to check whether the big trades corroborate or contradict the picture from sections 3 and 4, not to copy the trades.

The event calendar is the second half of the picture. Results, placements and trading-window closures are dates when a stock's price can move far more than its normal daily range, and an investor who has not planned for them can be surprised. The calendar also shows which companies have restricted their insiders from trading, because insiders often know more than the public and the window closure is the company's way of managing that. A closed window is not an accusation of anything. It is a signal that the company expects a period of information to be released, and a careful investor treats that period with respect.

5.3 The promoter pledge gap

Promoter pledge is the share of a company's stake that its founders or controlling shareholders have pledged as security for loans. A pledge is not automatically a warning, because many companies have modest pledges that carry little risk. A high pledge becomes a warning when the share price falls, because a lender can sell the pledged shares to recover the loan, which adds supply to a market that is already weak. For a holder, the pledge figure is one of the few facts that can turn a normal decline into a forced sale.

The second exception is the promoter pledge. The standard calls for a pledge figure for each holding. The research pass searched for it for all 19 holdings, with three distinct queries each, and found no verifiable pledge figure in the reachable sources. The shareholding pages showed promoter holding but not the pledge line, and the company page on the main data site did not include its shareholding table in the cached version. The checker's line is that no pledge figure was recorded for any holding. The effect is that the report cannot tell the reader whether any holding carries a pledge risk, which is one of the facts that can turn an ordinary decline into a forced sale. The cure is to read the pledge line in each holding's latest quarterly shareholding filing on the exchange site. The item is listed in the research record as not found, with the queries tried, so the gap is visible rather than hidden.

The third exception is the results calendar. The standard asks for the results dates of the holdings and the setups over the next fourteen days. The research pass found dates for two of the nineteen holdings. For NETWEB and LAURUSLABS the table shows N/A with the reason. For the remaining names the research record shows not found after three queries each. The effect is that the calendar is incomplete, and an investor could be surprised by a results release that the report did not flag. The fourth exception is the catalyst for NETWEB, the one setup. The standard asks for the company-specific catalyst behind each setup. The fifth exception is global macro. The standard's market blueprint includes a table of global inputs, such as crude oil, US Treasury yields, the dollar index and the rupee against the dollar. The sixth exception is the advance and decline count. The standard's infobox includes the number of advancing and declining stocks, which the ledger does not compute. The row is omitted rather than estimated, and breadth is shown through the share of the pool above its averages, which the ledger does compute.

The seventh is a set of symbols that failed to return a price. The ledger's failure list names C2C, WSTLIFESTY and DUMMYHEG. C2C and WSTLIFESTY are names in the watchlist's archive and pending files, and they have no price bars in the feed. The eighth is the holdings freshness note. The ledger flags three names, BIRET, EMBASSY and ORIANA-SM, as stale against the as-of date. Under the standard the holdings transaction log is treated as the authoritative record, and the ledger was run with the stale-holdings allowance. The market price of ORIANA-SM used in this report is the 12 October close. The flag is a freshness note on the holding's record, not a reason to doubt the position, and it is disclosed here as the standard requires.

The ninth is the same-session reconciliation. The standard asks that every shared ticker carry the same price and stage in the sibling reports for the same session. The only portfolio audit on file is dated 9 October 2026, so it is not a same-session sibling and it is not used as a data source. The watchlist-to-shortlist report for 12 October is a same-session sibling, and it reports the same Downtrend regime. This report's prices and stages come from the 12 October ledger.

The tenth is the conflict between this report's stop for NETWEB and the stop on its watchlist record. The two stops differ by 540 rupees and are derived from different rules. The conflict is disclosed in section 7 and is not resolved here, because the underwriting owns the method for the watchlist record.

The eleventh is the reviewer gate. The standard requires one reviewer pass, with a second round only if the first asks for changes, and a strict cap of two rounds. The review record in the run manifest states the outcome of each round, the model used and whether any fallback applied. The status line at the top of this report is the status after that review, and no description in this report is stated as approved unless every reviewer pass approved it.

The twelfth concerns the posture finding. It is not an exception, but it is material: the posture for the Downtrend regime asks for maximum cash, and the book's cash is 0.08 percent of net worth. The report states the gap in section 6 and leaves the decision to the portfolio audit, which applies the rules to each holding and its cost.

Taken together, the exceptions do not change the regime, the setup or the posture. They do narrow what the report can claim about sectors, about the holdings' pledges and calendars, about the setup's business driver and about global inputs. A reader who acts on the report should first close the open items above, starting with the pledge and the calendar for the holdings, and the catalyst for NETWEB, because those are the facts most likely to change a decision inside the next week.

Glossary

50-day and 200-day average. The average closing price over the last 50 or 200 sessions. They are the medium and long trend lines used by the regime and the stage tests.

Breadth. The share of stocks in the screening pool that are above their own averages. It measures how many stocks are taking part in a move.

Block deal and bulk deal. Large trades reported to the exchange. A bulk deal is a single-day trade of more than 0.5 percent of a company's listed shares. A block deal is a negotiated trade of a large parcel at a fixed price.

Distribution day. A session in which the index falls by at least 0.2 percent on higher volume than the session before. A cluster of them shows persistent selling.

Delivery percentage. The share of a day's traded shares that were taken into accounts for delivery rather than squared off within the day.

Downtrend and the regime label. The ledger's label for a market whose close is below both its 50-day and 200-day averages. It sets the posture in section 6.

FII and DII. Foreign institutional investors and domestic institutional investors, the large funds whose flows move the index.

Max pain and option walls. Max pain is the expiry strike where option sellers as a group lose least. Option walls are strikes with the largest open interest, which act as resistance above the market and support below it.

Put-call ratio (PCR). Open put contracts divided by open call contracts. A low reading means calls outnumber puts.

Qualifier. The label on each screen row that says how strongly it passed: confirmed, awaiting volume confirmation, Stage 2 but RSI outside 55 to 72, or no distribution-volume confirmation.

Quadrant. The ledger's classification of a sector by its one-month lead over the benchmark and its trend, labelled Leading, Improving, Weakening or Lagging.

Reward to risk. The gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop. The mandate requires at least 3.5 to 1.

RSI. The relative strength index, a momentum measure on a 0 to 100 scale. Below 30 is oversold and above 70 is overbought in the usual convention; the screens use 55 to 72 as the healthy band for a Stage 2 name.

Stage 1 to Stage 4. The four phases of a stock's price cycle: basing, advancing, topping and declining, as assigned by the ledger from the price and the two averages.

Stop and target. The stop is the price at which a trade is wrong; the target is the price at which it is right. Here the stop is the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average minus half an ATR, and the target is the 52-week high.

ATR. The average true range, the normal daily price range of a stock over 14 sessions. It sets the scale for stops and for how far a price may move without meaning anything.

Volume multiple. Today's or the 20-day average volume divided by the 20-day average. A value of 1.5 or more is the screen's volume test.

QIP. A qualified institutional placement, a share issue to institutional investors that adds new shares to the market.

Methodology and data gaps

The session date is Monday 12 October 2026. The run took place after the close on the same day, so the run date and the session date are the same, and the report does not rely on a prior session's data for any figure. The ledger was built for that exact date, its benchmark bar is dated 12 October, and its freshness metadata shows the holdings flags described in the run notes. The regime, the stages, the averages, the RSI, the volume multiples, the closes and the trade arithmetic come from the ledger, and no number was re-derived by hand except the reward-to-risk ratios, which are the ledger's stop and target arithmetic restated.

The research sources used for the flows, the option levels, the bulk and block deals, the delivery figures and the event calendar are the NSE public data endpoints and archive files for the session, and two independent option-analytics and flow-data sites for the cross-checks. The trade arithmetic is defined in one place and applied everywhere. The stop is the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average minus half the 14-day ATR. The target is the 52-week high, which is the only technical anchor the standard accepts without a sourced analyst level, and no analyst target was used. The percentages stated in tables and prose are the ledger's own figures or simple arithmetic on them, rounded to one or two decimals.

Tax, brokerage and slippage are not included in the trade arithmetic, because lot-level tax data and brokerage schedules are not in this run's inputs. Those costs would reduce every gain slightly and widen every loss slightly, and the worked example says so. The report does not estimate them.

Each limitation is stated where it applies, and none of them was filled with an estimate. The conclusions the report draws are those the evidence supports, and each carries the level that would change it. A future run that closes the open items would be expected to test the same conclusions against new data, and it would be right to change them if the data changed.
