A moving average smooths a price into a trend line. The 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days, roughly one month. The 50-day moving average covers about ten weeks and the 200-day moving average covers about forty weeks. Think of them as lines drawn through a bumpy road to show which way it runs. A price above a rising average means buyers have been paying more over time. A price below a falling one means the opposite, and that is the situation of the Nifty 50 today: it closed at 22,600.00 against a 50-day average of 23,403.72 and a 200-day average of 24,125.36.

Stage analysis, which this report uses, describes four seasons of a share. Stage 1 is a quiet base, like the end of winter. Stage 2 is the growing season, when the price sits above a rising 50-day average and above its 200-day average. Stage 3 is the top, a summer that has stopped growing. Stage 4 is the markdown, a falling price under a falling average, the winter we most want to avoid. The Nifty 50 is in Stage 4 now. A breakout is a move above the highest recent price, usually on heavy volume, and it is how a base ends. A share that reaches Stage 2 while its market is in Stage 4 is swimming against the current, which is possible but needs more proof.

The relative strength index, RSI, is a speedometer from 0 to 100 that measures how fast a price has moved. Below 30 means oversold, which says a fall has been unusually steep and that bounces are likely. It does not say the fall is over. Above 70 means overbought, a very fast rise. The average true range, ATR, is the normal daily wobble of a share in rupees. A share with an ATR of 3 rupees moves about 3 rupees on an ordinary day, so a stop-loss placed 1 rupee away from the price will probably be hit by noise. Volume counts how many shares changed hands. A price move on twice the usual volume is a vote with real money behind it, and a move on thin volume is easier to reverse.

The put-call ratio compares open put options with open call options. A low reading means more call contracts are open than puts. The option walls are the strikes where the most contracts are open. Because those who sell options defend their strikes, a heavy call strike acts like a ceiling and a heavy put strike like a floor. A stop-loss is an exit price decided in advance, so that a loss stays small without a judgement made in a panic. Reward-to-risk divides what you could win by what you could lose: at 3.5 to 1, one winning trade pays for three and a half losing ones. Position size is how much of your money goes into one idea. It matters because a share that falls 30% costs you 3% of your portfolio if it is a tenth of the portfolio, but 15% if it is half.

1.2 Options map: the ceilings and floors traders are defending

An option is a contract that gives its buyer the right to buy (a call) or sell (a put) the index at a fixed price, called the strike, before a set date. People who sell those contracts collect a fee and then have reasons to defend certain strikes, so the strikes with the most open contracts behave like invisible walls. The biggest call strike works like a ceiling, because sellers push the price back down to keep their contracts worthless. The biggest put strike works like a floor. 2. Sector & Thematic Rotation Matrix

Rotation is what money does when it leaves one part of the market and enters another, like water moving between the rooms of a house. Investors rarely leave the market all at once. They sell what has run out of steam and buy what looks cheaper or safer, and that movement shows up first in sector prices. For a beginner the practical lesson is that the Bull List and the Bear List in Sections 3 and 4 should follow the sectors, because a strong share in a weak sector is swimming against a current, and a weak share in a strong sector is only a temporary laggard.

Leading means it beat the Nifty 50 over the month and over the week. Weakening means it beat the Nifty 50 over the month but lost ground this week. 5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

Large investors matter because they have research teams, long time horizons and the money to move prices. 6. Tactical Playbook & Capital Allocation

A playbook turns evidence into instructions, and in a falling market the first instruction is about size, not about which shares to buy. The ledger label is Downtrend, and the posture the editorial standard attaches to that label is plain: hold the most cash the policy allows, trim leveraged and Stage 4 holdings, and add no new momentum longs. 7.1 What the three-layer underwriting will test next

Underwriting is the checklist a company must pass before it can earn a place in your portfolio, and it matters to a beginner because a chart can look strong while the business behind it is weak. Layer 1 tests financial health with hard limits from the scoring file. Debt-to-equity is how much a company has borrowed for each rupee its owners have put in, and a ratio of 1.20 or more for a non-financial company is a red line. Interest cover is how many times profit pays the interest bill, and under 2.0 is a red line. A promoter pledge of 20% or more, or a three-year ratio of cash from operations to profit under 0.40 without a verified turnaround, are red lines too, and any one of them routes a name to rejection. Layer 2 picks the three to five operating numbers that run the business and checks whether management delivered what it promised a year or two ago. Layer 3 tests valuation and price behaviour: what growth today's price already assumes, how analyst estimates are moving, and whether the momentum test passes. A name that fails any layer is documented and rejected, and the reason is recorded so it is not re-screened without new evidence.

For these three names the tests are specific. Mphasis reports on 5 November, so Layer 2 waits for that result and for management's view on client spending. Glossary

Moving average (20, 50, 200 days)
The average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 trading days; a line that smooths the price into a trend.
Stage 1, 2, 3 and 4
The four seasons of a share: a base, a markup, a top and a markdown. Stage 2 means price above a rising 50-day average and above the 200-day average; Stage 4 means price under a falling 50-day and under the 200-day.
Regime
The one-word label for the whole market: Uptrend (broad), Uptrend (narrow breadth), Correction or Downtrend, set by the Nifty 50 against its averages and the breadth of the market.
Breadth
The share of stocks above a given average. Thin breadth means a rally or a hold-up is carried by a few shares.
RSI
Relative strength index: a 0 to 100 speedometer. Under 30 is oversold; over 70 is overbought.
ATR
Average true range: the normal daily wobble of a share in rupees, used to place stops.
Volume multiple
Today's traded volume divided by its 20-day average. 1.5 or more means heavy trading.
Delivery percentage
The share of traded shares actually moved into investors' accounts and not squared off within the day; a sign of conviction when high and of churn when low.
Distribution day
A session where the index falls on heavier volume than the day before, a sign of institutional selling.
India VIX
The market's fear gauge, derived from Nifty option prices; higher means traders pay more for protection.
Put-call ratio
Open put contracts divided by open call contracts. Under 1 means more calls than puts are open.
Call wall and put wall
The strikes with the most open call or put contracts; they act like a ceiling and a floor because option writers defend them.
Max pain
The strike at which option buyers collectively lose the most money at expiry.
FII and DII
Foreign and domestic institutional investors; their net cash flows are published each evening.
Bulk and block deal
Large trades disclosed by the exchange with the names of buyer and seller.
Promoter pledge
Shares that a company's founding group has put up as collateral for a loan; a pledge can force selling in a falling market.
Stop-loss
An exit price decided in advance, so a loss stays small without a judgement made in a panic.
Reward-to-risk
The gain from entry to target divided by the loss from entry to stop; 3.5 to 1 is the floor for a tactical setup.
Risk unit
The amount you would lose if the stop is hit, expressed as a share of net worth, used to size a position.
Qualifier
The ledger's label for how strongly a name passes the screen: confirmed, awaiting volume confirmation, or outside the RSI band for the Bull List; confirmed or no distribution-volume confirmation for the Bear List.
Conditional setup
A pre-approved idea with a stated entry band, stop and target that receives size only when a market gate opens.
Gate
A market test, here three Nifty 50 closes above its 50-day average, that must pass before any setup receives money.
