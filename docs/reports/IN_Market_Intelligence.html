Ten ideas carry the report, and each has an everyday comparison. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line drawn through a wobbly signal; the 50-day average (50-DMA) holds roughly ten weeks of mood and the 200-day average (200-DMA) roughly ten months. A Stage is one of four seasons in a share's life: Stage 1 is a flat winter base, Stage 2 is the spring and summer climb, Stage 3 is a topping autumn, and Stage 4 is a falling winter. We prefer to own Stage 2 and to avoid Stage 4. The RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 means the price has run very fast, below 30 means it has fallen very fast, and neither is a prediction on its own. The ATR (average true range) is a share's normal daily wobble in rupees, and it places stops so that ordinary noise does not shake you out. Volume counts how many shares changed hands, which is how many people voted with their money; a move on twice the usual volume is a loud vote.

The put-call ratio and the option walls are a fear gauge and a set of ceilings and floors: where many traders have sold options at one price, that price tends to act as a ceiling or a floor until the contracts expire. A stop-loss is an exit price decided in advance, written down before emotion arrives. Reward-to-risk compares what you can win with what you can lose: a plan that risks 50 rupees to make 130 has a ratio of 2.6, and the portfolio's floor is 2.5. Position size is how much of your total money one idea may hold, which is why no single share is allowed to hurt you badly. A limit order is an instruction to buy only at a price you choose or lower, so you wait for the market to come to you.

5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

Institutions move more money than any individual, and their footprints show up in three places: daily buying and selling in the cash market, their open positions in index futures and options, and large single trades that the exchange must publish. These footprints are worth reading because they are the only part of the market that reports who did what. They also have limits. A fund that sells shares may be hedging a bet somewhere else, a market maker that buys and sells the same shares has no opinion, and a positive flow on one day says little about a trend. The practical use is to ask whether the money agrees with the price. Today the answer is partly yes and partly no: both foreign and domestic institutions bought in the cash market, which agrees with the bounce in mid and small caps, but foreign funds keep a very large short position in index futures, which disagrees with any claim that the fall is over.

This chapter does four jobs. It lays out the last eight sessions of cash flows, reads the positions of the four participant groups in index derivatives, checks the day's large trades for a directional message, and then turns to your own book: which of your 19 holdings report results soon, what their promoters hold, and what the pre-event rule says to do. Pre-earnings risk is the one risk that a stop-loss cannot control, because a result is announced outside market hours and a share can open far below the price at which the stop was set. The rule used throughout is that a holding with results in the next seven calendar days gets no new money and is sized as though its stop could be skipped.

Glossary

50-DMA and 200-DMA
The average closing price over the last 50 or 200 trading days. A price above both averages is in a healthier position than one below them.
ATR (average true range)
A share's typical daily movement in rupees over 14 days. Stops are placed a fraction of an ATR beyond support so that normal noise does not trigger them.
Bulk deal and block deal
A bulk deal is a trade above 0.5% of a company's shares in a day. A block deal is a large negotiated trade in a separate window. Both are disclosed after the close.
Breadth
The share of stocks that trade above a given average. Low breadth means the index is being held up by a few names.
Conditional setup
A prepared limit order with a size and a stop that stays switched off until a stated condition, here the availability of cash, is met.
Crore and lakh
One lakh is 100,000 and one crore is 10,000,000. Amounts marked Cr are crore rupees.
Delivery percentage
The share of the day's traded volume that was taken for delivery, meaning held by buyers rather than bought and sold within the day.
Distribution day
A day when an index falls on higher volume than the day before. The ledger counts them over 25 sessions.
FII and DII
Foreign institutional investors and domestic institutional investors, the two large groups whose daily net buying and selling the exchange publishes.
Limit order
An instruction to buy at a stated price or lower, or to sell at a stated price or higher.
Max pain
The strike price at which option buyers as a group would lose the most money at expiry. Many traders watch it; evidence that the index is drawn to it is weak.
Pilot size
Half of the standard size for the idea's tier, used for the newest and least certain positions. For a Tier 4 idea that is 1% of net worth.
Pledge
Shares that a company's founders have put up as collateral for a loan. A high pledge is a risk, because a fall in the share price can force the lender to sell.
Put-call ratio (PCR)
The number of open put contracts divided by the number of open call contracts. A ratio near 1 means balance; higher means more protection is being bought.
Reward-to-risk
The gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop, both measured from the entry price. The portfolio's floor is 2.5.
RSI (relative strength index)
A speedometer from 0 to 100 for how fast a price has risen or fallen. Above 70 is very fast up; below 30 is very fast down.
Stage 1 to Stage 4
The four seasons of a share: a flat base, a climb, a top, and a decline. The ledger assigns each share and index a stage from its price against its averages.
Stop-loss
An exit price decided in advance. In this report it is the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average minus half an ATR.
