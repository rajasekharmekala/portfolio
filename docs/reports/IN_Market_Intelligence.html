A moving average smooths a price into a trend line. The 20-day moving average is the average closing price over the last 20 trading days, roughly one month. The 50-day moving average covers about ten weeks and the 200-day moving average covers about forty weeks. Think of them as lines drawn through a bumpy road to show which way it runs. A price above a rising average means buyers have been paying more over time. A price below a falling one means the opposite, and that is the situation the Nifty 50 is in today, closing 22,811.35 against a 50-day average of 23,479.08 and a 200-day average of 24,158.33.

A share moves through four seasons called stages. Stage 2 is the growing season, when the price sits above a rising 50-day and 200-day average. Stage 4 is winter, when the price sits below falling averages. Stage 1 is the quiet spell after a fall and Stage 3 is the tired top before one. A base is a flat stretch of trading after a decline, like a landing on a staircase where the price rests before the next climb. A breakout is a move above a price that the share failed to pass before. Of the 499 names in the Nifty 500 universe, 36 are in Stage 2 and 203 are in Stage 4, which is the market's season in one sentence.

RSI (the relative strength index) is a speedometer for a price on a scale of 0 to 100. Above 70 means the price has run fast and may be stretched, and below 30 means it has fallen fast. ATR (the average true range) is the normal daily wobble of a share in rupees. If a share has an ATR of 50, a one-day move of 50 rupees is ordinary, so a stop placed only 20 rupees below the price would be hit by noise. Volume is the number of shares that changed hands, and a volume multiple of 2.0 means twice the 20-day average: it shows how many people voted with their money for the day's move.

Company results use shorthand. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is operating profit before those four items, and a margin is a profit divided by revenue. Banks add their own terms: GNPA (gross non-performing assets) are loans where borrowers have stopped paying, NNPA (net non-performing assets) are the same loans after the bank has set money aside, and NIM (net interest margin) is the gap between what a bank earns on loans and pays on deposits. A price-to-earnings ratio divides the share price by yearly profit per share, so a high figure means investors pay more for each rupee of profit.

The put-call ratio and option walls come from the options market. A put gives its owner the right to sell at a set price and a call the right to buy. When put holders outnumber call holders the ratio is above 1 and the crowd is hedging against a fall, and when it is below 1 the crowd leans the other way. A wall is a price level where many contracts are open. Sellers of those contracts defend the level, so the biggest call level acts like a ceiling and the biggest put level like a floor. They are tendencies, not laws, and they change every day.

Three ideas turn analysis into action. A stop loss is an exit price chosen before you buy, so that a mistake costs a known amount. Reward-to-risk is the possible gain divided by the possible loss: a ratio of 4 means you stand to make four rupees for every rupee you risk, and this report asks for at least 3.5. A limit order is an order that buys only at your chosen price or better, which stops you from chasing. Position size is how many shares you buy, and it is set so that a stopped-out trade costs a small, fixed slice of your wealth, so no single share can hurt you badly.

1.2 Options map: the ceilings and floors traders are defending

Options give a second view of the market because they show where people have put money on a future price. Each open contract has a seller on the other side, and sellers defend their levels by trading the index. When a very large number of calls is open at one strike, sellers have a reason to push the index back below it, so the level acts like a ceiling. When a very large number of puts is open at one strike, sellers have a reason to buy the index above it, so the level acts like a floor. Each row shows a stop and a target derived by rule and not by opinion. The stop is the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average less half an ATR. Worked example: why the pre-event rule exists

You hold 40 shares of NETWEB at the close of 4,594.10, worth ₹183,764, which is 4.19% of net worth. Results arrive on Saturday, so the share cannot trade until Monday. Suppose it opens 8% lower at 4,226.57. The loss is 40 x (4,594.10 - 4,226.57) = ₹14,701, or 0.34% of net worth. Suppose it opens 15% lower at 3,904.99: the loss is ₹27,565, or 0.63% of net worth. A stop order cannot help, because the price never trades between Friday's close and Monday's opening. If you cut the holding to 20 shares before the result, the same 15% gap costs ₹13,782. The cost of cutting is giving up half of a possible gain, which is the price of a result you cannot predict.

Glossary

ATR (average true range)
The normal daily wobble of a share in rupees. Stops are placed in multiples of it so that ordinary noise does not trigger them.
Breadth
The share of stocks above a moving average. Low breadth means few stocks are taking part in a move.
Distribution day
A day on which an index falls on higher volume than the day before, the footprint of large holders selling.
FII and DII
Foreign institutional investors and domestic institutional investors, whose daily cash-market net flows the exchange publishes in rupees crore.
Max pain
The option strike at which sellers as a group pay out the least at expiry.
Moving average
The average closing price over a set number of days, drawn as a trend line (20, 50 and 200 days in this report).
Put-call ratio
Open put contracts divided by open call contracts. Below 1 means more calls than puts.
Regime
The market's season, set from the Nifty 50 against its averages and from breadth: Uptrend, Correction or Downtrend.
Reward-to-risk
Target minus entry, divided by entry minus stop. The report asks for at least 3.5 to 1.
RSI (relative strength index)
A 0 to 100 speedometer for price momentum. The Bull List asks for 55 to 72, the Bear List for under 45.
Stage 1 to 4
The four seasons of a share: basing, growing (Stage 2), topping, and declining (Stage 4).
Stop loss
A price chosen in advance at which you exit, so that a mistake costs a known amount.
Volume multiple
The day's volume divided by the 20-day average. Above 1.5 counts as heavy.

Every term above is explained again in plain words where it first appears in the report, so this list is a place to look something up and not a lesson to learn first. The most useful habit is to read a verdict together with its rule, because a verdict without its rule is only an opinion.
