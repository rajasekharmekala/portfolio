Execution, customer and cash risks

The FY26 model is stronger than the old ground-handling model. Afcom ran more flights itself and logged 1,129 charter trips. This can lift revenue. It also leaves earnings exposed to aircraft access, lease costs, route permits, customer schedules, and disruption. The annual report links strong charter demand in part to Gulf-hub disruption. It warns that demand may not last [1]. Investors should test repeat scheduled work separately from disruption-linked charters before using FY26 margins for later years.

Customer concentration adds to the risk. The two largest unnamed customers made ₹158.58 crore and ₹98.91 crore of sales. Each was above 10%. The filings do not name them or show renewal dates, contract prices, or receivables by customer. We cannot tell whether FY26 flying came from long-term deals or a few flexible charters. The next useful disclosure is route use and cash collection by customer, not only a larger fleet [1][3].

Cash conversion is the clearest quality gap. FY26 CFO was ₹35.99 crore, about 15% of EBITDA by the annual report’s measure. FY27 Q1 cash flow was positive, but it covers one unaudited quarter. The older FY25 report and the FY26 restated comparison show different FY25 CFO figures. The FY26 report does not show FY24 CFO on the same basis [1][3][4]. We need a restated comparison and another current cash-flow print before using a three-year conversion ratio. For now, value the company from audited EPS, not a cash-flow multiple.
