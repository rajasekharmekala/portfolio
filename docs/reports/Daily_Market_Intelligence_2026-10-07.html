To understand the mechanics of this report, you only need to master eight fundamental concepts, each grounded in an everyday analogy:

  • Moving Averages (50-DMA and 200-DMA): Think of a moving average as the average price paid by all market participants over a specific period, much like looking at a city's seasonal average temperature rather than daily fluctuations. The 50-day moving average (50-DMA, roughly 10 weeks of trading) reflects medium-term institutional trend health, while the 200-day moving average (200-DMA, roughly 40 weeks) represents long-term structural equilibrium. When price trades comfortably above a rising 50-DMA, buyers are firmly in control; when it breaks below, supply is overwhelming demand.
  • Stan Weinstein's 4 Market Stages: A stock moves through four distinct seasons across its market cycle. Stage 1 is the base-building accumulation phase (quiet sideways trading after a prior decline). Stage 2 is the advancing markup phase (the only environment where aggressive long capital should be deployed). Stage 3 is the distribution topping phase (churning volume without forward price progress). Stage 4 is the declining markdown phase (persistent distribution where capital must be protected, hedged, or exited).
  • Relative Strength Index (RSI 14): Think of RSI as a momentum speedometer scaled from 0 to 100. A reading between 55 and 72 indicates powerful, sustainable Stage 2 upward momentum. Readings above 75 warn of an overextended vehicle vulnerable to a sharp pullback, while readings below 30 denote deeply oversold conditions where panic liquidation has occurred.
  • Average True Range (ATR 14): ATR measures the normal daily volatility wobble of a stock in rupees. We use ATR to place non-arbitrary, volatility-aware stop losses. Placing a stop loss 0.5 ATR below key moving average support ensures that ordinary daily market noise will not prematurely trigger an exit, while a true structural failure will immediately protect capital.
  • Volume Expansion Multiple: Volume is voter turnout in the market. When a stock breaks out of a base on volume greater than 1.5 times (1.5x) its 20-day average volume, it confirms that large institutional funds (pension funds, mutual funds, foreign portfolio investors) are actively committing substantial capital.
  • Put-Call Ratio (PCR) & Options Gamma Walls: Options are insurance contracts written primarily by well-capitalized institutional market makers. The Put-Call Ratio (PCR) measures open interest in put options divided by call options. A low PCR (below 0.75) signals heavy call writing and defensive hedging. The Call Wall represents the strike price with the largest call open interest, acting as an overhead structural ceiling. The Put Wall represents the strike price with the highest put open interest, serving as a structural support floor.
  • Stop-Loss & Risk-to-Reward Asymmetry (R:R ≥ 3.5:1): A stop-loss is a pre-decided emergency exit order placed at your broker. We strictly mandate a risk-to-reward asymmetry of at least 3.5:1 on every tactical setup. This means we demand at least ₹3.50 of verified upside profit potential for every ₹1.00 of capital exposed to risk.
  • Position Sizing & Risk Budgeting: The most important rule in professional investing is that no single position should ever threaten portfolio solvency. By calibrating position size so that the distance between your entry price and stop loss represents no more than 0.5% to 1.0% of your total net worth, you ensure long-term mathematical survival regardless of market turbulence.

In modern financial markets, institutional asset managers rarely move 100% of their assets into cash during a correction. Instead, portfolio managers execute structural sector rotation: they trim expensive, over-owned cyclicals that are vulnerable to economic slowdowns, and reallocate those proceeds into defensive, counter-cyclical sectors or resilient niche industries demonstrating earnings visibility. For a beginner, identifying which sectors are leading and which are lagging is the single most effective way to avoid buying stocks that face persistent institutional supply.

To assist beginners and private-wealth investors in mastering institutional financial terminology, this glossary defines the critical analytical metrics utilized throughout this briefing:

  • 50-Day Moving Average (50-DMA): The mathematical average of closing prices over the preceding 50 trading sessions (approximately 10 weeks). It represents the medium-term cost basis of institutional investors. A rising 50-DMA serves as a primary support benchmark in Stage 2 markups.
  • 200-Day Moving Average (200-DMA): The mathematical average of closing prices over the preceding 200 trading sessions (approximately 40 weeks or 10 months). It delineates long-term structural bull and bear markets. Securities trading below a declining 200-DMA are in long-term structural downtrends.
  • Average True Range (ATR 14): A technical volatility indicator formulated by J. Welles Wilder that measures the average daily trading range in rupees across the trailing 14 sessions, accounting for overnight gaps. ATR is utilized to establish volatility-aware stop losses that prevent premature shakeouts.
  • Distribution Day: An institutional market condition defined by William O'Neil where a benchmark index closes down by 0.20% or more on volume higher than the preceding session. The accumulation of 5 or more distribution days within a 25-session window signals severe institutional liquidation.
  • Gamma Wall (Call Wall / Put Wall): In options market microstructure, the strike price containing the largest concentration of open interest contracts. Because options dealers must dynamically delta-hedge their books, a major Call Wall acts as an automated supply ceiling, while a major Put Wall functions as an automated support floor.
  • Market Breadth (% > 50-DMA): The percentage of universe constituents (Nifty 500) currently trading above their respective 50-day moving averages. Readings below 30% denote pervasive market weakness, while readings below 20% signify severe structural liquidation.
  • Max Pain Strike: The strike price at which the aggregate financial payout to options buyers is minimized upon expiry, representing the price point where institutional options writers realize maximum cumulative premium decay.
  • Put-Call Ratio (PCR): The total open interest in put options divided by the total open interest in call options. A low PCR (below 0.75) reflects defensive call-writing and bearish positioning, whereas a high PCR (above 1.30) indicates extreme bullishness or oversold contrarian potential.
  • Relative Strength Index (RSI 14): A bounded momentum oscillator ranging from 0 to 100 that measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes. Readings between 55 and 72 characterize healthy Stage 2 trend momentum; readings above 75 warn of overextension, and readings below 30 denote oversold conditions.
  • Stan Weinstein Market Stages: A foundational lifecycle model categorizing price action into four distinct phases: Stage 1 (Basing/Accumulation), Stage 2 (Advancing Markup), Stage 3 (Topping/Distribution), and Stage 4 (Declining Markdown). Institutional capital is deployed exclusively in Stage 2.
  • Volume Expansion Multiple: The ratio of the current session's trading volume relative to its 20-day simple moving average volume. A multiple ≥ 1.5x on a breakout day confirms genuine institutional sponsorship.
