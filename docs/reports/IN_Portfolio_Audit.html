Several key words appear throughout, each with an everyday comparison. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N sessions, like a trend line drawn through a noisy week of temperatures. The 20-day and 50-day averages describe the short and medium trend, and the 200-day average describes the long one. The 21-day EMA is an exponential moving average, which weights recent days more heavily, like a coach who watches the last few laps more closely than the first. The stages are the four seasons of a stock. Stage 1 is basing (winter, the stock rests), Stage 2 is markup (spring and summer, the stock rises above a rising average), Stage 3 is topping (autumn, the advance stalls and the average flattens) and Stage 4 is markdown (late autumn into winter, the stock falls below a falling average).

RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer from 0 to 100. A reading below 30 means the stock has fallen unusually fast, and a reading above 70 means it has risen unusually fast. ATR, the average true range, is the normal daily wobble of a stock in rupees. This audit places stops in ATR units so that a calm stock and a jumpy stock both get fair room. A stop is an exit price you decide in advance, while the market is calm, so that you do not have to decide in a panic. A limit order fills only at your price or better. A GTT (good till triggered) order is a stop or limit order that your broker keeps open until its price is reached.

Relative strength compares a stock's return with the market's return over the same period. A value above zero means the stock has beaten the index. Asymmetry is the ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss; this audit asks for at least 3.5 times for tactical ideas and at least 3.0 times for core ideas. Concentration is how much of your net worth sits in one name. The rule caps any single stock at 20% of net worth, and it contains one exception, described in Chapter 2, for a winning stock that is still strong. Money at risk is what you would lose if every stop were hit, which is a worst case of the plan and not a forecast.

Two more terms appear in the market chapter. Distribution days are sessions when the index falls on higher volume than the day before, a sign that large investors are selling. Breadth is the share of stocks trading above their own 50-day average; when breadth is low, a rising index is being carried by a few names. Put-call ratio (PCR) divides the open put options by the open call options. A low figure means more calls than puts are held, so traders are relatively optimistic, and option walls are the strikes with the most open contracts, which often act as ceilings and floors.

2.2 Concentration and the super-performer exception

Concentration is the amount you have placed in any one company. The audit rules cap a single stock at 20% of net worth, because a company can fall sharply on news that has nothing to do with the rest of the market, and a single position above one-fifth of the portfolio can do lasting damage. The cap is a discipline, not a reflex. It forces you to ask whether the exposure you have is the exposure you intend.

RNIT AI is 23.0% of net worth. At ₹108.00 for each of its 10,000 shares, the position is worth ₹10.80 lakh against a net worth of ₹46.96 lakh. The technical and asymmetry tests can be recomputed from the ledger, and the ledger's stage is the figure this audit relies on. That is why a company that passed the financial gate last month can still fail the technical gate this week, and why the report treats the financial record as slow-moving and the stage as live.

4.2 Displacement: when a candidate would replace a holding

A displacement is the decision to sell a holding in order to buy a candidate that the rules rate more highly. The audit rules set a three-condition test. Condition 1 asks whether the holding has triggered an exit or a trim. Condition 2 asks whether the candidate has passed every gate and is at support. Condition 3 asks whether the candidate's asymmetry exceeds the holding's by at least 1.5 times, after allowing for the cost of trading. All three must hold for a displacement to be eligible. The test does not require the displacement to happen, and in a Downtrend it cannot be executed, because the regime blocks the purchase that would follow the sale.

INTERARCH passes Condition 1, because it has triggered an exit under the Stage 4 rule. Condition 2 is not met by any candidate that is also a new name, because on the ledger only WELENT passes all the tests, and WELENT is already held. Condition 3 cannot be verified for INTERARCH, because the shortlist record gives no base target for it, so its asymmetry cannot be computed. The displacement is therefore not eligible, and the sale stands on its own merit. SANGHVIMOV follows the same pattern: Condition 1 is met by the trim, but the record gives no base target, so Condition 3 cannot be met.

AFCOM is the one holding where all three conditions can be computed against a candidate. Its trim triggers Condition 1. Its record asymmetry is 1.92, and WELENT's is 4.03, a ratio of about 2.1 times, which clears the 1.5 times hurdle. Condition 2 is met, but only by WELENT, which you already own, so a sale of AFCOM would add to WELENT rather than introduce a new name. Adding is blocked in a Downtrend. On that basis the displacement is not eligible in practice, and the report records no displacement event and sends no displacement flag.

TDPOWERSYS carries a REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER verdict in the record. Condition 1 is not met, because the ledger shows the stock in Stage 2, above a rising 50-day average, and that does not trigger a trim or an exit. The record's verdict is therefore overridden for this audit, and the holding is kept with a trailing stop. TARIL is in a similar position to AFCOM for Condition 1, but the record gives it no base target, so Condition 3 cannot be met. BLISSGVS has a record replacement verdict and a ledger close above its rising 50-day average, so Condition 1 is not met, and the one-share holding is a NO TRADE. The displacement tests show that the record's verdicts are a starting point and that the ledger's trend has the final say.

What would make a displacement eligible and executable

  • A displacement becomes eligible for a holding if the holding triggers an exit or trim, a challenger passes every gate, is in Stage 2 on the ledger and sits inside its zone, and the challenger's computable asymmetry is at least 1.5 times the holding's.
  • It becomes executable only when the regime is at least Correction, because a sale that frees cash for a purchase the regime blocks would add risk without a corresponding gain.

5.2 Scenarios: what could happen after the plan

A plan is only as good as its response to the outcomes it did not expect. Four scenarios are worth working through in money terms, because each one shows a different part of the risk. The first is the worst case the plan contemplates: every stop in Chapter 6 is hit before anything else happens. The money at risk after the plan is ₹508,618, which is 10.8% of net worth, and the portfolio would fall to about ₹41.87 lakh, with cash rising by the same amount that the stops release. The second scenario is a broad market fall of 10%, where every holding falls by the same proportion. The equity book after the plan is ₹38.64 lakh, so a uniform 10% fall would cost ₹3.86 lakh, or 8.2% of net worth. The third scenario is the one that matters most to a beginner: nothing fills. Suppose the limit orders in Chapter 6 do not execute because the market never trades at those prices. 7.1 The biggest risks and unknowns

Every plan has risks that the rules do not remove, and it is more useful to name them than to pretend they are handled. The first is a gap. A stop is a price, and a stock can open well below it after an overnight announcement, so the sale executes at the opening price rather than at the stop. For the core names with wide stops that is a limited concern, but for the tactical names close to their stops it is a real one. The second is concentration in a single company. RNIT AI is 23.0% of net worth, and the exception keeps it that way. If the company reported a bad result, the loss could be large before the trailing stop could act. The third is the data. The ORIANA-SM correction shows that the ledger's price history can be wrong, and the shortlist records disagree with the ledger on stage for most of the names that pass. Both problems are repairable, but until they are, every stage label in the report should be read with that caveat.

The fourth risk is the regime itself. A Downtrend can last several months, and during that time the cash of ₹8.32 lakh earns little. If the market rebounds quickly, the plan will have bought protection at the cost of participation, and the report accepts that cost. The fifth is the record-keeping. The plan assumes the holdings record is accurate, which the ledger flags as stale, and the report discloses that assumption. If a trade fills at a different price or quantity from the one assumed here, the bridge must be updated from the broker's contract notes, not from this document.

The sixth unknown is the cost of the trades. Brokerage, the securities transaction tax, stamp duty and the tax on realised gains are not in the ledger, and no lot-level data was available to compute the tax on each sale. Those costs are shown as N/A rather than estimated. The tax on the INTERARCH and SANGHVIMOV losses would be a capital loss that can offset other gains, and the tax on RNIT AI's gain would arise only if it were sold. Those are questions for a tax adviser, and the report does not give tax advice.

7.2 How often to run this audit

Run the audit again after any of three events: the Nifty closes through a regime level in the watch list, a stop triggers, or a company reports results or a corporate action that changes its figures. Otherwise re-run it at least once a month, and re-read the stops every week, because a trailing stop should move only when its anchor moves. Between runs, the rules in this chapter are enough to check any single decision, and a decision that the rules do not cover should wait for the next full audit rather than be improvised.

When to re-run the audit

  • After any stop triggers, to decide whether the exit should be followed by a re-entry test.
  • After the Nifty closes through a regime level in the watch list.
  • After a company reports a result or a corporate action, and after the ORIANA-SM price history is corrected.
  • At least once a month, even if none of the above has happened.

Glossary

ATR (average true range)
The average daily price range of a stock over 14 sessions, in rupees. It is the normal wobble, used to place stops in units that fit each stock.
Asymmetry
The ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss. The audit asks for at least 3.5 times for tactical ideas and 3.0 times for core ideas.
Breadth
The share of stocks trading above their own 50-day average. Low breadth means the market is weak in depth even when the index is steady.
Concentration
How much of your net worth sits in one company. The audit caps a single stock at 20%, with one exception for a strong winner.
Distribution day
A session in which the index falls on higher volume than the day before, a sign that large holders are selling.
EMA (exponential moving average)
An average that gives recent days more weight than older ones, so it turns sooner than a simple average.
GTT (good till triggered)
A stop or limit order held by the broker until its price is reached, then sent to the exchange.
Money at risk
The loss if every stop in the book were hit. It is a worst case used to size the plan, not a forecast.
Relative strength
A stock's return compared with the market's over the same period. Above zero means it has beaten the index.
RSI (relative strength index)
A 0 to 100 gauge of recent price momentum. Below 30 is oversold, above 70 is overbought.
Stage 1 to Stage 4
The four trend phases used by the ledger: basing, markup, topping and markdown.
Stop
A price, set in advance, at which a position is sold to limit the loss.
Return on capital employed (ROCE)
The operating profit a company earns on the money invested in it, as a percentage. Higher is better, and the audit's core test is 18% or more.
Debt-to-equity ratio
How much a company borrows compared with what its owners have put in. A low figure means the business is not dependent on lenders.
Cash-to-profit ratio
Operating cash flow divided by reported profit. A figure near or above 1 says the reported profit is turning into cash.
Price-to-earnings ratio
The share price divided by the profit per share. It says how many years of current profit the price pays for.
Operating margin
The share of sales left as operating profit before interest and tax.
Base
A sideways stretch in which the price stops falling and steadies before its next move.
Limit order
An order that fills only at your chosen price or better.
Entry zone
The price range in which the shortlist judges a candidate can be bought at support.
