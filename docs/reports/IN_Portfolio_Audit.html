Start here: what this audit is and how to read it

This is an audit of the stocks you own in your Zerodha account. It asks three questions. What is the market doing? Is each holding still earning its place? And if you started today with only cash, what would you buy? It is a research briefing, not an order, and it does not know your tax lots, your goals or your need for cash. If you have ten minutes, read the key figures, the lede below and Chapter 6, which lists every order. If you have an hour, read the chapters in order, because each one builds on the one before.

The audit is written by three independent analysts and one editor. The first reads the market and says how much risk the rules allow. The second audits the holdings one by one and has no loyalty to anything you already own. The third is a greenfield picker who is told nothing about your holdings, your cost or your profit, and who must say what it would buy with ₹4,573,985 in cash. The editor puts the three readings side by side. Keeping the picker blind to your book is deliberate: an owner tends to defend what he owns, and a blind picker shows what the shortlist is worth on its own merits.

The labels have exact triggers. EXIT means sell everything. It fires when a hard red line is broken and no written exception applies, such as a promoter pledge above 20% of the promoter holding, or debt above the line in substance. A broken cash-conversion line (three-year operating cash flow below 40% of profit) has a written exception for a turnaround proven in the latest two quarters, so when that exception is only partly met or still unverified the label is TRIM or a time-boxed HOLD, and the case note says which. TRIM means sell part, and it fires when a position is above its size ceiling (15.0% of net worth, or 22.0% for a proven winner) or when a weak name still has a chart that lets you sell at a fair price. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep the shares but sell all of them if a named closing price breaks. HOLD / TRAIL means keep the shares and lift the stop as the price rises. NO TRADE / WATCH means the position is too small to act on. Stops are placed at the lower of the recent swing low and the 50-day average, less one average daily range for core holdings (half a range for tactical ones). A challenger replaces a holding only if the holding has triggered a sale, the challenger passed every gate and sits within half an average daily range of support, and its reward-to-risk beats the holding's by 1.5 times. Reward-to-risk must be 3.0 or better for a core holding and 3.5 or better for a tactical one.

A few ideas carry the whole report. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line drawn through a wobbly road. The Stage system sorts every stock into four seasons: Stage 1 is a base after a decline, Stage 2 is a rising markup, Stage 3 is a topping or pullback phase, and Stage 4 is a markdown. RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer for price: below 30 the stock has fallen unusually fast, above 70 it has risen unusually fast. ATR (average true range) is the stock's normal daily wobble, and we use it to place stops so that ordinary noise does not knock you out. Volume is how many shares changed hands, which is how many people voted with money. A stop loss is an exit price decided in advance, so fear does not decide it later. Reward-to-risk divides what you can win by what you can lose. Risk-unit sizing sets a position's size from the loss you accept if the stop is hit, so a wide stop earns a smaller position. Effective market exposure says how much of a Nifty move your book really feels once each stock's beta (its tendency to swing more or less than the index) is counted. Hidden overlap asks whether two holdings are really one bet, because they rise and fall together.

Two rules sit above the rest. The let-winners-run rule forbids selling a Stage 2 winner mechanically just because its RSI passes 70 or its weight passes 15.0%, but only while its fundamentals are intact and its estimates are stable or rising, and only up to a weight of 22.0%. And a red line is a binary fault, such as too much debt, a heavy pledge or cash that never arrives, which overrides the chart. Every number in this audit comes from the 16 October 2026 ledger, the shortlist record or the dated research file, and every verdict quotes the rule that produced it and the price that would change it.

A few more terms recur in the tables. A limit order is an order to buy or sell only at your price or better. The entry zone is the price range where a purchase would be acceptable. A breakout is a move above a price the stock failed to pass before. Asymmetry is another word for reward-to-risk: the possible gain divided by the possible loss. Operating margin is the share of sales left after the costs of running the business. Debt-to-equity compares a company's borrowings with its shareholders' funds. ROCE (return on capital employed) measures the operating profit earned on the money invested in the business, and EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a measure of operating profit before those charges.

1.2 What your portfolio really behaves like: effective market exposure

Net worth tells you what the account is worth. Effective market exposure tells you how much of the market's swing the account actually feels. Each stock has a beta, a number describing how much it tends to move when the Nifty 50 moves 1%. A stock with a beta of 1.5 moves about 1.5% for a 1% index move, and a stock with a beta of 0.5 moves about half as much. Weighting each holding's beta by its share of the portfolio gives one figure for the whole book. The position file computes it from one-year betas, with no leverage or options in the account, and reports: "Your total net worth is ₹4,573,985.12, but because of stock volatility (beta), leveraged positions, and options, your portfolio behaves like ₹4,012,081.74 (87.7% effective exposure to Nifty 50). In plain words: a 1% drop in the Nifty 50 is expected to move your portfolio by about 0.88% (roughly ₹40,120.82)." That sentence is the whole reading in plain English.

The number is lower than the 100% invested figure suggests, 87.7% against 99.9%, because the two largest holdings are low-beta stocks: RNITAI carries a beta of 0.55 and SEDEMAC 0.28. That is a warning as much as a comfort. Both are small, thinly traded shares, and a one-year beta measured on thin trading understates how far they can fall in a panic, because the next sale may have no buyer near the last price. Treat 87.7% as the floor of your real sensitivity, not its ceiling. After the plan in Chapter 6 the beta falls from 0.88 to 0.55, and effective exposure falls to 55.2%, which is ₹2,525,805 of the Nifty's movement.

1.3 Which holdings move in lockstep: hidden overlap

Owning 19 stocks sounds diversified, but diversification is about independence, not count. Two stocks that rise and fall together on the same days are one bet held twice, and a book full of such pairs falls harder than its names suggest. The test is the one-year correlation of daily returns: a value of 1.0 means two stocks move in perfect step, zero means no link. The position file flags any pair at or above the threshold and joins pairs that chain together into clusters. The file's own sentence reads: "Across your 19 equity holdings, no pair has a 1-year daily return correlation of 0.60 or higher, indicating low hidden overlap between positions." The file prints 0.60 in that sentence while the audit rules name 0.65 as the lockstep line, but no pair clears even the lower figure, so the conclusion is the same under both.

That is good news with a limit. A low pairwise correlation over the past year does not remove the shared story behind eight of your names. INTERARCH, EPACKPEB, SANGHVIMOV, ASTRAMICRO, TDPOWERSYS, TARIL, APARINDS and BALUFORGE all sell into India's capital goods cycle, which depends on government capex, grid investment and defence orders. In calm months their share prices follow company news and look independent. In a sell-off, funds sell the whole group at once and the correlations jump toward 1.0. So the lockstep check is clean today, and the next subsection shows why the capital goods group still deserves a ceiling: it holds 37.40% of net worth, and the market report counts only 27.5% of its 69 names above their 50-day average.

Chapter 2: Active Portfolio Health and Concentration Risk Audit

Concentration is the quiet way a good portfolio becomes a fragile one. When one stock is a fifth of your money, that stock decides your year, whatever the other eighteen do. The rules set two ceilings. The standard cap is 15.0% of net worth for any single stock, and a proven Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals may run to 22.0% before it is cut, because selling a leader mechanically on a number is how investors lose the few stocks that matter. Your largest holding, RNITAI, is 23.37% of net worth, which is above both ceilings, and the second largest, SEDEMAC, is 14.20%. Together they are 37.57% of the portfolio, and the top three holdings are 46.32%.

Concentration is not the only fault the audit looks for. Each holding is tested against four hard red lines, which are binary faults that override any chart: too much debt (debt-to-equity at or above 1.20, or interest cover under 2.0), a promoter pledge at or above 20% of the promoter's shares, three-year cumulative operating cash flow under 40% of profit with no verified turnaround, and a Stage 4 breakdown with no base. A holding that breaks one is routed to a sale even if its chart looks fine. Holdings that pass every red line are then tested against the softer floors for a compounder or a tactical breakout, and against their stop. 2.2 The trim arithmetic: how the size of each sale is set

A trim has two numbers, how much to sell and where to stop. The size comes from the ceilings in section 2 and from risk-unit sizing, which asks how much of net worth you would lose if the stop were hit. For a holding that is too large, the first test is the weight cap. For a holding that is within the cap but fragile, the second test is the loss to the stop against the risk budget for its tier: 100, 75, 50 or 35 basis points of net worth, which is 1.00%, 0.75%, 0.50% or 0.35%. A wider stop means a larger loss per share, so the same budget allows fewer shares.

Worked example: trimming RNITAI

RNITAI closed at 106.90. You hold 10,000 shares worth ₹1,069,000, which is 23.37% of ₹4,573,985. The cap test: 15.0% of net worth is ₹686,098, which buys 6,418 shares at the close, so the minimum sale to reach the cap is 3,582 shares. The plan sells 4,000 shares and keeps 6,000, worth ₹641,400 or 14.02%, which leaves a one-point buffer so a 7% rise does not put the position back over the cap. The risk test: the stop is 85.14, which is 20.4% under the close, so the 6,000 kept shares would lose ₹130,560, or 2.85% of net worth, if the stop were hit; the full 10,000 shares would lose ₹217,600, or 4.76%. The sale itself is large for this stock: the ledger shows 27,350 shares traded on 16 October, so a sale of 4,000 shares is 15% of one day's volume, and it should be worked in limit orders between 105 and 112 over several sessions. One more number matters. The risk-unit size for RNITAI at today's close is 2.46% of net worth, so the 14.02% that remains is about 5.7 times that size. The residual is therefore an exception and not a target. It is kept because a faster sale in a stock this thin would cost an unknown amount in price impact, and it is dated: the case note sets a further cut at the close of 16 November 2026 unless the half-year cash test is met.

The same logic sizes the other nine sales. SEDEMAC is inside the 15.0% cap at 14.20%, but its stop of 2,834.18 is 12.7% below the close, so the full position would lose ₹82,544, or 1.80% of net worth, against a tier budget of 0.75%. Selling 100 of 200 shares halves that loss to 0.90%. That is a judgement call, not a rule firing, and the case note says so. For INTERARCH, ASTRAMICRO, AFCOM and NETWEB the weak-name rule sets the size at about half or down to the tier ceiling, and for TARIL and ORIANA-SM the red lines set it at all of the shares.

Nothing in this arithmetic needs the market to cooperate. If every limit order fills at the close, the book's weights, the cash balance and the money at risk all land on the figures in the money bridge. If some orders do not fill, the book stays closer to today's state, and Chapter 6 says what to do about each unfilled order after a fixed number of sessions. 2.3 Applying the Let-Winners-Run rule to compounding winners

A good portfolio is carried by a few large winners, and the rule exists to keep an investor from cutting them early. It says never to trim a Stage 2 winner solely because its RSI is above 70 or its weight has crossed 15.0%, provided the fundamentals are intact and the estimates are stable or rising, and it allows the weight to grow to 22.0% while the stop trails the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average. Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine that all of your money, ₹4,573,985, were in cash this morning, and that you knew nothing about what you paid for anything. What would you buy from the shortlist, and how much of each? That question is the greenfield exercise, and its value lies in what it removes. A real owner is anchored to his cost, to the gain he hates to give back and to the loss he hopes to recover. None of those facts is a reason to own a stock tomorrow. The third analyst, the greenfield picker, is told only prices, gates, stops and targets, and is asked to choose six to twelve high-conviction ideas at support or to buy nothing.

Two rules shape its answer. A candidate must pass all three gates (the financial gate, the technical gate and the reward-to-risk gate), and it must be close to a support level, within half an average daily range for a full entry. In a Downtrend the regime table also says no new momentum buys. Chapter 1 put the market in a Downtrend, so the bar is high from the start. The shortlist that the picker reads is the vetted list in the shortlist record, which holds 18 names, all of which you happen to own, plus the pipeline of underwritten watchlist names that are not on it. The picker reads both and starts from zero.

3.1 The zero-bias target portfolio

Zero-holding-bias framework: The target below comes from an analyst that sees no holdings, no cost basis and no profit. If it held ₹4,573,985 in pure cash today, the table shows the conviction-weighted capital it would deploy across the vetted shortlist. Every row is 0.0% because no candidate passed all three gates at the 16 October close.

The table lists all 18 shortlist names with the numbers the picker used: the planned entry zone, the stop from the stop formulas, the base-case target and the reward-to-risk ratio that results. They are ordered by reward-to-risk, highest first, because that is the gate most names fail. The target weight is zero for every name, so the greenfield portfolio is 100.00% cash. 3.2 How risk-unit sizing would size a purchase

Risk-unit sizing answers a simple question: how many shares can I buy so that, if the stop is hit, I lose no more than a fixed slice of net worth? Each conviction tier has a risk budget: Tier 1 is 100 basis points (1.00% of net worth), Tier 2 is 75, Tier 3 is 50 and Tier 4 is 35, while Tier 0 gets nothing. The weight is the budget divided by the percentage distance from entry to stop, capped by the tier's own maximum weight: Tier 1 up to 10%, Tier 2 up to 6.5%, Tier 3 up to 3.5% and Tier 4 up to 2.0%. A wide stop cuts the size automatically. The total heat across all stops is capped at 12.0% of net worth. The sizing applies to new money; for holdings it serves as a yardstick of how big the position should have been.

Chapter 4: Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

A funnel is a series of filters, and a name that reaches the bottom has passed all of them. The audit uses three gates, in a fixed order. The financial gate asks whether the business is sound: no hard red line, cash that arrives, sensible debt. The technical gate asks whether the market agrees: a Stage 2 chart, momentum and a price near support. The reward-to-risk gate asks whether the price pays enough for the risk: at least 3.0 to 1 for a core compounder and 3.5 to 1 for a tactical or turnaround name. A fourth layer, the operating KPIs, scores five company-specific measures such as order intake, margins and customer concentration, and a name needs four of five (or three of four) to qualify as a compounder.

The funnel exists because each gate catches a different failure. A good business can sit in a bad chart, and a good chart can belong to a bad business. A name with both can still be too expensive for its stop. Running every name through all three gates, and recording where each one stops, makes the verdicts comparable and shows which fix would change them. The chapter first narrates the gates with real names, then tests every sale in Chapter 2 against the best replacement, then counts the costs of acting, and finally says why the market report's fresh ideas are not yet challengers.

4.2 The three-condition swap test for every sale

A swap replaces one holding with another. The rules allow it only when three conditions hold together. First, the holding has triggered an exit or a trim. Second, the challenger has passed 100% of the gates and sits strictly at support, within 0.5 ATR of its support level. Third, the challenger's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's, after tax and friction. If any condition fails, the sale does not become a swap and the proceeds stay in cash. The test guards against a common habit, selling a weak holding only to buy something equally weak that happens to be exciting.

The only underwritten challenger outside your book is SAGILITY. It fails the second condition for two separate reasons. Its reward-to-risk at the close of 45.30 is 2.98, below the 3.5 floor, so it has not passed every gate, and its close is 0.76 ATR above its 50-day average of 44.34, which is outside the 0.5 ATR window that defines strictly at support. 4.3 Friction: tax, brokerage and slippage

Selling costs money, and a good plan counts the cost. There are three kinds. Tax is charged on the gain from a sale, at a rate that depends on how long the shares were held and on your other gains and losses in the year. Brokerage and statutory charges are small and fixed by the broker's schedule. Slippage is the difference between the price you expect and the price you get, and it is largest in thinly traded shares. The audit can compute the gross gain on each sale from your cost per share, but it cannot compute the tax, because the tax lots, holding periods and your year-to-date gains are not in the record this audit reads. Tax is therefore N/A in rupees, and the reason is specific: the holding period of each lot is not modelled.

The gross figures are still informative. If all ten sales fill at the 16 October close, the plan realises a net gain of ₹301,602. That is ₹342,880 of gains on RNITAI, SEDEMAC, TARIL, EPACKPEB and ORIANA-SM, less ₹41,278 of losses on INTERARCH, ASTRAMICRO, AFCOM, NETWEB and TDPOWERSYS. A gain given up to tax is not the same as a gain lost. The rule that the plan follows is that a position that breaks a written rule is cut, and the tax is the price of cutting it. Waiting to avoid tax has its own cost, which is the risk that the stop is hit first. Everything so far is analysis. This chapter turns it into orders you can place at the broker, in the sequence that protects you most. The orders come in three phases. Where the record's stop is judged on the close, the practical choice is a price alert at the level plus a decision at the close, and where you want protection from an intraday collapse the choice is a GTT, which can fire on a brief wick below the level. Chapter 7: Rulebook and Monitoring

A portfolio audit is only as useful as the rules it can be held to later. This chapter restates every rule that produced a verdict, with the number it produced today, so that you can check the next audit against this one. It then lists the dates and prices that would change the plan, the risks that no rule removes, and how often the audit should be run. The aim is to make you independent of the report: with the rules and the levels in hand, you can look at a price on any day and know which row of the table it belongs to.

Rules are written in advance for a reason. The decision to cut a position is hardest when the stock is falling, because loss aversion argues for waiting, and hardest when it is rising, because greed argues for holding. A rule written on a calm Sunday avoids both. The rules below are not perfect. Several of them are blunt, and some produce verdicts that a patient reader may dislike, such as the sale of a stock that has just risen 13%. Their value comes from being applied the same way every time and from being open to inspection.

Glossary

  • ATR (average true range): the stock's normal daily swing over 14 sessions, used to place stops and to measure distance from support.
  • Asymmetry or reward-to-risk: the possible gain to the target divided by the possible loss to the stop; 3.0 is the Core floor and 3.5 the Tactical floor.
  • Beta: how much a stock tends to move when the Nifty 50 moves 1%.
  • Cash conversion (CFO/PAT): operating cash flow divided by profit; the red line is 0.40 over three years.
  • Core Alpha: the long-term compounding bucket, with a target of 30% to 40% of net worth.
  • Distribution day: a day when the index falls on heavier volume, a sign that large holders are selling.
  • Effective market exposure: the book's weight in each stock times that stock's beta, summed, expressed as a share of net worth.
  • GTT (good-till-triggered): a broker order that waits until a trigger price is traded, then places a sell or buy order.
  • Heat: the total loss across all stops if every stop were hit, as a share of net worth; the ceiling is 12.0%.
  • Hidden overlap: two holdings whose daily returns move together closely enough to act as one bet.
  • Let-Winners-Run rule: a Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals may grow to 22.0% of net worth before it is trimmed.
  • Moving average: the average closing price over a number of days; the 50-day and 200-day averages mark the medium and long trend.
  • Pledge: shares that a promoter has put up as security for a loan; above 20% of the promoter holding is a red line.
  • QIP (qualified institutional placement): a share sale by a listed company to institutions, which adds new shares.
  • Red line: a binary fault, such as debt, pledge, cash conversion or a markdown with no base, that overrides the chart.
  • Risk-unit sizing: setting a position's weight so that the loss at the stop equals a fixed slice of net worth, capped by the tier maximum.
  • ROCE: return on capital employed, operating profit divided by the money invested in the business.
  • RSI (relative strength index): a 0 to 100 speedometer of recent price change; under 30 is oversold and over 70 is overbought.
  • Stage 1 to 4: four seasons of a stock: base, markup, topping, markdown.
  • Stop-loss: an exit price chosen in advance; a closing-basis stop is judged on the day's close, not on a brief dip.
  • Swap test: three conditions that must all hold before a sale is replaced by a purchase.
  • Tactical Momentum: the shorter-term bucket of rising stocks, with a target of 50% to 65% of net worth.
  • VIX (India VIX): the market's expected volatility, read as a fear gauge.
