Start here: what this audit is and how to read it

This is an audit of the shares you own in your Zerodha account. It asks three questions. What is the market doing today? Is each holding still earning its place? And if you started with only cash, what would you buy? It is a research briefing and not an order. It does not know your tax lots, your goals or your need for cash. With ten minutes, read the key figures, the lede below and Chapter 6, which lists every order. With an hour, read the chapters in order, because each one leans on the one before.

The audit combines three independent readings and one editor. The first reads the market and says how much risk the rules allow. The second audits your holdings one by one and has no loyalty to anything you already own. The third is a greenfield reading that is told nothing about your holdings, your cost or your profit, and that must say what it would buy with ₹4,510,119 in cash. The editor lays the three readings side by side. The greenfield reading is kept blind on purpose: an owner defends what he owns, and a blind picker shows what the shortlist is worth on its own merits.

The labels carry exact triggers. EXIT means sell everything, and it fires when a hard red line is broken and no written exception applies, such as a promoter pledge above 20% of the promoter's shares. TRIM means sell part, and it fires when a position is above the size its tier allows, or when a weak name still trades well enough to sell at a fair price. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep the shares and sell all of them if a named closing price breaks. HOLD / TRAIL means keep the shares and lift the stop as the price rises. NO TRADE / WATCH means the position is too small to act on. Stops sit at the lower of the recent swing low and a moving average, less one daily range for core holdings and half a range for tactical ones. A challenger replaces a holding only if the holding has triggered a sale, the challenger passed every gate and sits within half a daily range of support, and its reward-to-risk beats the holding's by 1.5 times. Reward-to-risk has to be 3.0 or better for a core holding and 3.5 or better for a tactical one.

A handful of ideas carry the whole report, and each has an everyday comparison. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line drawn through a bumpy road. The Stage system sorts every stock into four seasons: Stage 1 is a base after a fall, Stage 2 is a rising markup, Stage 3 is a tired top or pullback and Stage 4 is a markdown. RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer for price: below 30 the stock has fallen unusually fast and above 70 it has risen unusually fast. ATR (average true range) is a stock's normal daily wobble, which is why a stop placed closer than one ATR is usually knocked out by noise. Volume is how many shares changed hands, which is how many people voted with money. A stop loss is an exit price decided in advance so that fear does not decide it later. Reward-to-risk divides what you can win by what you can lose. Risk-unit sizing sets a position's size from the loss you accept if the stop is hit, so a wide stop earns a smaller position, like buying a smaller car when the road ahead is icy. Effective market exposure says how much of a Nifty move your book really feels once each stock's beta is counted. Hidden overlap asks whether two holdings are secretly one bet because they rise and fall together.

The Let-Winners-Run rule forbids selling a Stage 2 winner mechanically because its RSI passed 70 or its weight passed 15.0%, but only while its fundamentals are intact and its estimates are stable or rising, and only up to a weight of 22.0%. A red line is a binary fault, such as heavy debt, a heavy pledge or profit that never turns into cash, and it overrides the chart. 1.3 Which holdings move in lockstep: hidden overlap

Two holdings are in lockstep when they rise and fall together so reliably that owning both is closer to owning one position twice. The positions file measures this with the correlation of daily returns over one year, where 1.0 means they move identically and 0 means they move independently. The reading is: "Across your 19 equity holdings, no pair has a 1-year daily return correlation of 0.60 or higher, indicating low hidden overlap between positions." No pair reaches the threshold, so the lockstep clusters list is empty. That is a real finding. Your 19 names are in different businesses, from air cargo to cocoa butter, and their day-to-day moves have been largely independent.

The caution is what the measure cannot see. A correlation computed over a calm year understates how shares behave in a fall, when fear makes unrelated stocks drop together. Seven of your holdings are capital goods makers, which together are 30.8% of net worth, and the sibling market report shows only 18.8% of capital goods names above their 50-day average. These holdings are not in lockstep in the daily data, but they share an industry, a customer base of state and corporate capital spending, and a sensitivity to the same orders and interest rates. If that group sold off together in a bad week, the lack of measured overlap would not protect you. Chapter 2 therefore treats the capital goods group as a shared risk when it sets trim sizes, and the money-at-risk table in 2.5 adds up all stops at once, which is the lockstep case in its most severe form.

Chapter 2: Active Portfolio Health and Concentration Risk Audit

Concentration is the quiet way a good portfolio becomes a fragile one. When one share is a fifth of your money, that share decides your year, whatever the other eighteen do. The rules set two ceilings. The standard cap is 15.0% of net worth for any single stock, and a proven Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals may run to 22.0% before it is cut, because selling a leader mechanically on a number is how investors lose the few shares that matter. Your largest holding, RNITAI, is 23.24% of net worth, above both ceilings, and the second, SEDEMAC, is 14.20%. Concentration is not the only fault the audit looks for. A holding that breaks one is routed to a sale even if its chart looks fine, unless a written exception applies. Holdings that pass every red line are then tested against softer floors and against their own stop. 2.2 The trim arithmetic: how the size of each sale is set

A trim has two numbers, how much to sell and where to stop. The size comes from the ceilings above and from risk-unit sizing, which asks how much of net worth you would lose if the stop were hit. For a holding that is too large, the first test is the weight cap. For a holding inside the cap but fragile, the second test is the loss to the stop against the risk budget for its tier: 100, 75, 50 or 35 basis points of net worth for Tiers 1 to 4, which is 1.00%, 0.75%, 0.50% or 0.35%. A wider stop means a larger loss per share, so the same budget allows fewer shares. The tier also caps the weight: 10.0%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2.0%. For a holding the cap works as a trim trigger only when a rule on that holding also fails, such as a red line or a reward-to-risk floor; a share that passes every test and is slightly over its tier ceiling, as SANGHVIMOV is, is held.

Worked example: trimming RNITAI. RNITAI closed at 104.80. You hold 10,000 shares worth ₹1,048,000, which is 23.24% of ₹4,510,119.32. The cap test: 15.0% of net worth is ₹676,518, which buys 6,455 shares at the close, so the minimum sale to reach the cap is 3,545 shares. The plan sells 4,000 and keeps 6,000, worth ₹628,800 or 13.94%, which leaves a one-point buffer so a 7% rise does not put the position back over the cap. The risk test: the stop is 85.20, which is 18.7% under the close, so the 6,000 kept shares would lose ₹117,630, or 2.61% of net worth, if the stop were hit, against ₹196,050 (4.35%) for all 10,000. Liquidity matters too. The ledger shows 16,426 shares traded on Monday, so 4,000 shares is 24% of that day's volume. Work the sale in limit orders between 104.80 and 106.60 across several sessions. The risk-unit size for RNITAI at today's close is 2.67% of net worth, so the 13.94% that remains is about 5.2 times that size. The residual is therefore an exception and not a target. It is kept because a faster sale in a stock this thin would cost an unknown amount in price impact, and it is dated: the case note sets a further cut at the 16 November close unless the half-year cash test is met.

The same logic sizes the other sales. SEDEMAC is inside the 15.0% cap at 14.20%, but its stop of 2,838.73 is 11.4% under the close, so the full position would lose ₹72,714, or 1.61% of net worth, against a tier budget of 0.75%. Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine that all of your money, ₹4,510,119, were in cash this morning, and that you knew nothing about what you paid for anything. What would you buy from the shortlist, and how much of each? That question is the greenfield exercise, and its value lies in what it removes. A real owner is anchored to his cost, to the gain he hates to give back and to the loss he hopes to recover. None of those facts is a reason to own a share tomorrow. The third reading, the greenfield picker, is given only prices, gates, stops and targets, and is asked to choose six to twelve high-conviction ideas at support or to buy nothing.

Two rules shape its answer. A candidate must pass all three gates (the financial gate, the technical gate and the reward-to-risk gate), and it must sit within half an average daily range of support for a full entry. In a Downtrend the regime table also says no new momentum buys. Chapter 1 put the market in a Downtrend, so the bar is high from the start. The shortlist that the picker reads is the vetted list in the shortlist record. It holds 18 names, all of which you happen to own, plus a pipeline of underwritten watchlist names that are not on it. The picker reads both and starts from zero.

3.2 How risk-unit sizing would size a purchase

Risk-unit sizing answers a simple question: how many shares can I buy so that, if the stop is hit, I lose no more than a fixed slice of net worth? Each conviction tier has a risk budget: Tier 1 is 100 basis points (1.00% of net worth), Tier 2 is 75, Tier 3 is 50 and Tier 4 is 35, while Tier 0 gets nothing. The weight is the budget divided by the percentage distance from entry to stop, capped by the tier's own maximum: Tier 1 up to 10%, Tier 2 up to 6.5%, Tier 3 up to 3.5% and Tier 4 up to 2.0%. A wide stop cuts the size automatically. Total heat across all stops is capped at 12.0% of net worth. The sizing applies to new money, and for holdings it serves as a yardstick of how big the position should have been.

Tier and budget come from the shortlist record and the scoring file. Stop distance is the gap from Monday's close to the stop, as a share of the close. The first size is what the formula allows at today's close, and the second is what it allows at the top of the planned entry zone, where the stop is closer. Take WELENT: a stop of 534.39 is 22.7% under the close, so a 50-basis-point budget divided by 22.7% gives 2.20%, and your 2.91% is over it. Chapter 4: Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

The funnel is the audit's quality filter, and it is the same one for a share you own and for a share you might buy. A candidate passes three gates in order. The financial gate asks whether the business is solvent and turns profit into cash, using the four red lines and the floors for returns and debt. The technical gate asks whether the chart is in a Stage 2 markup, or entering one from a base, with a price near support. The asymmetry gate asks whether the target is at least 3.0 times the distance to the stop for a Core Alpha name, or 3.5 times for a Tactical Momentum name. A candidate that passes all three and sits within half an ATR of support may replace a holding that has triggered a sale. Everything else waits, or is rejected.

Two examples show how the gates behave. TARIL fails the first gate and nothing after it matters: a 21.84% promoter pledge against a 20% line stops the funnel regardless of its chart or its strong September quarter. The first example says a good company and a good chart can still be a poor purchase at a given price. The second says a good chart does not outrank a red line. 4.2 The three-condition swap test for every sale

A sale is not a swap. The audit sells weak holdings because a rule fired, and it buys a replacement only if the replacement clears a higher bar. 7.4 How often to rerun the audit

Run the audit again when any of three things happens, whichever comes first. The first is a change of regime label: if the Nifty 50 closes above its 50-day average for three sessions, or under its 52-week low, the posture changes for the whole book and the plan should be rebuilt. The second is a results season: after the half-year statements arrive in the first half of November, the red lines can be tested on fresh cash flow figures, and several names can be reclassified. The third is a trade: if you buy or sell outside the plan, add it to the transaction log, since every number in the next run starts from that log.

Without any trigger, rerun the audit every two weeks while the market is in a Downtrend and monthly once it is in an Uptrend. The shorter interval is not because the rules change; it is because the stops, the stage readings and the heat figure all move with price, and a book that is fully invested in small shares can change character in a fortnight. Each run builds its own ledger and research file, so nothing from this report is reused: the sales and stops here are a starting point and the next audit judges them afresh.

Glossary

Asymmetry: a bet whose possible gain is much larger than its possible loss, measured as reward-to-risk.

ATR: the average daily range of a share over 14 days, used to judge whether a stop is too close.

Beta: a share's historical tendency to move more or less than the index.

Breadth: the share of shares above a moving average; low breadth means few shares support the index.

Cash conversion: three-year cash flow from operations divided by three-year profit; under 0.40 is a red line.

Distribution day: a down day on rising volume, a sign that large holders are selling.

Effective market exposure: the book's value weighted by each holding's beta, showing how much of an index move it feels.

Entry zone: the band of prices where a limit order would buy.

GTT: good-till-triggered order: a standing instruction that sends a limit order when a trigger price is touched.

Hidden overlap: two or more holdings whose one-year daily returns correlate at 0.65 or more, so they act as one bet.

Limit order: an order that fills only at your price or better.

Moving average: the average closing price over a set number of days; the audit uses 20, 50 and 200.

Money at risk (heat): the loss if every stop is hit, as a share of net worth; capped at 12.0%.

Promoter pledge: the part of the founders' shares pledged to lenders as security for loans.

QIP: qualified institutional placement: new shares sold to institutions.

Red line: a binary fault, such as heavy debt, a pledge of 20% or more, cash conversion under 0.40 or a Stage 4 breakdown with no base.

Regime: the market's overall weather: Uptrend (broad), Uptrend (narrow breadth), Correction or Downtrend.

Reward-to-risk: the possible gain to the target divided by the possible loss to the stop.

Risk-unit sizing: sizing a position so that the loss at its stop is a fixed slice of net worth.

RSI: a 0 to 100 speed gauge of recent price moves; below 30 is oversold and above 70 is overbought.

Stage 1 to Stage 4: the four seasons of a share: base, markup, top and markdown.

Stop loss: the exit price chosen before buying.

Swing low: the lowest close of the last 20 sessions, where buyers last stepped in.

Trailing stop: a stop that rises as the price or an average rises and never falls.

VIX: the market's gauge of expected movement over the next 30 days, taken from option prices.
