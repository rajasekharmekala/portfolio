Start here: what this audit is and how to read it

This is an audit of the stocks you own in your Zerodha account. It asks three questions. What is the market doing? Is each holding still earning its place? And if you started today with only cash, what would you buy? It is a research briefing, not an order, and it does not know your tax lots or goals. If you have ten minutes, read the key figures, the lede below and Chapter 6, which lists the orders. If you have an hour, read everything in order. Every chapter ends with a short note on what it means for you and a pointer to the next chapter.

Three independent analysts produce the verdicts. The first judges the market and its sectors. The second is a holdings auditor that tests every position you own against fixed rules and has no loyalty to your past choices. The third is a greenfield picker that is told nothing about what you own, what you paid or what you have gained. It answers one question: if you held 100% cash today, what would you buy from the vetted shortlist? Keeping it blind is deliberate. A buyer who owns a stock tends to defend it. A buyer who owns nothing only buys what passes the tests.

Ten ideas carry the report. A moving average is the average closing price over recent weeks, like a line drawn through a bumpy road to show its direction. The 20-day line covers about a month, the 50-day line about ten weeks and the 200-day line about forty weeks. A stock moves through four stages, like seasons. Stage 2 is the rising phase, where the price sits above rising 50-day and 200-day averages. Stage 4 is the falling phase. Stage 1 is the quiet floor after a fall, and Stage 3 is the tired top before one. RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 means a stock has risen very fast, below 30 means it has fallen very fast. ATR, the average true range, is the size of a normal daily wobble in rupees, and it tells you how close a stop can sit before ordinary noise triggers it.

Volume is the number of shares traded, like a count of votes cast with money. A stop loss is an exit price chosen before you need it, so a wrong idea costs a known amount. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, divides what you could gain by what you could lose: 3.5 means each rupee at risk can earn 3.5. Position size is how much of your money sits in one idea, and it matters more than the idea itself, because a modest position in a bad stock hurts little while a huge position in a good stock can still wreck a year.

The audit uses labels with fixed triggers. EXIT means sell the whole holding because a red line, a stop or a Stage 4 breakdown has fired. TRIM means sell part of it, either because it is too large for its risk budget or because a rule failed but no stop has closed. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep it, add nothing and sell everything on a closing price below a stated level. HOLD / TRAIL means keep it and lift the exit level as the 50-day average rises. NO TRADE / WATCH means the position is too small or too unvetted to act on. The two sizing rules are Risk-Unit Sizing, which sets a position's weight from how far its stop sits below the price, and the Let-Winners-Run rule, which stops you trimming a healthy Stage 2 winner just because it has risen. Core Alpha means slow compounders held for years. Tactical Momentum means stocks held for weeks to months because the chart is working.

1.2 What your portfolio really behaves like: effective market exposure

Your net worth is ₹4,573,985, but a stock portfolio does not move one for one with the index. Each stock has a beta, a number that says how much it tends to move when the index moves 1%. A beta of 1.5 means the stock typically moves 1.5% for each 1% in the Nifty 50, and a beta of 0.5 means half as much. Weighting every holding by its beta gives the portfolio beta, and multiplying it by net worth gives the effective exposure: the size of the Nifty 50 position your book behaves like. 2.2 The trim arithmetic: how the size of each sale is set

A trim is not a guess at the top. It is a size calculation, and the arithmetic is the same each time: compare the weight to the cap that applies and sell down to the cap or to the risk-unit size. Start with RNITAI, the largest case. The 15.0% single-stock cap on a book of ₹4,573,985 allows ₹686,098, which is 6,418 shares at ₹106.90. The holding is 10,000 shares, so the excess over the cap is 3,582 shares. The 22.0% runaway ceiling allows 9,413 shares, an excess of only 587. The plan sells 4,000 shares, leaving 6,000 worth ₹641,400, or 14.02%, just under the 15.0% cap.

2.3 Applying the Let-Winners-Run rule to compounding winners

The rule exists because the worst habit in investing is cutting the winners to feed the losers. A stock that has risen because its profits are rising and its estimates are being raised is usually the reason the portfolio is up at all, and trimming it because the number looks big is a way of selling the best part of the book. The rule therefore asks three questions of any Stage 2 winner: are the fundamentals intact, are earnings estimates stable or rising, and is the weight above the 22.0% ceiling? Only a yes to the first two protects it, and the ceiling is a hard limit on top of that.

2.5 Money at risk if every stop is hit

Heat is a stress measure, not a forecast: stops rarely all trigger at once, and on a gap a stop can fill below its level. It is still the cleanest way to see whether the book is sized for the market it is in.

Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine that tonight your whole account turned into cash, ₹4,573,985 in rupees and nothing else. You do not remember what you paid, which stocks you liked or how much you have gained. You open the vetted shortlist of 16 names and decide what to buy at Friday's prices. That is the greenfield test, and it matters because holding something is a different decision from buying it. An owner carries three biases: the pain of selling at a loss, the pride of a winner and the cost of tax. A buyer carries none of them. If the portfolio you own would not be the portfolio you would choose today, the gap is the price of your past decisions, and the audit's job is to say how large it is.

The greenfield picker follows the policy to the letter and sees no holdings data. It sizes each purchase by Risk-Unit Sizing: the weight is the smaller of the tier's maximum and a risk budget in basis points (hundredths of a percent of net worth) divided by the distance from entry to stop. A TIER_2 name has a 75 basis point budget and a 6.5% ceiling, a TIER_3 name 50 basis points and 3.5%, a TIER_4 name 35 basis points and 2.0%, and a TIER_0 name gets nothing. A name must also pass its asymmetry floor, 3.0 for Core Alpha and 3.5 for Tactical Momentum, sit within 1.2 ATR of support to be sized normally, and clear the hard red lines. The regime adds a layer: in a Downtrend the policy asks for maximum cash and no new momentum longs.

Zero-holding-bias framework: this allocation is produced by the shortlist capital allocator using only the shortlist records and the ledger. It has no information about positions, cost bases or gains. The question it answers is what the best conviction-weighted portfolio of ₹4,573,985 in pure cash would be today.

3.2 How risk-unit sizing would size a purchase

Risk-Unit Sizing starts from how much of your net worth you are willing to lose if the stop is hit, not from how much you like the stock. The result is that stocks with wide stops get small positions and stocks with tight stops get large ones, and the rupee risk stays constant. The formula is weight = the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. It also protects against the common mistake of sizing by conviction alone: a name you love with a 25% stop distance should still be a small position.

Worked example: SEDEMAC at the top of its entry zone

SEDEMAC is TIER_2, with a risk budget of 75 basis points and a ceiling of 6.5%. If you bought at ₹3,200, the top of the entry zone, with the stop at ₹2,834.18, the stop distance is 365.82 / 3,200 = 11.43%, or 1,143 basis points. The weight is 75 / 1,143 = 6.56%, which is above the 6.5% ceiling, so the ceiling decides: 6.5% of ₹4,573,985 is ₹297,309, or 92 shares at ₹3,200. A hit stop would cost 92 × ₹365.82 = ₹33,655, which is 0.74% of net worth, just inside the 0.75% budget. At today's close of ₹3,246.90 the stop distance widens to 12.71%, the formula gives 5.90%, and the position would be ₹269,877, or 83 shares. The wider the stop sits from the price, the smaller the position, which is the reason a chase above the entry zone is penalised.

Chapter 6: 3-Phase Actionable Execution Orders

An order is a plain instruction to a broker with a price, a quantity and a time limit. Most mistakes in execution come from missing one of the three. A sell with no price limit can fill at a bad price on a thin stock. A limit with no expiry can sit open for weeks and fill after the reason for it is gone. A quantity that does not match the plan leaves the book in a half-finished state. This chapter writes every order with all three and gives its trigger, so that the tickets can be placed one by one without further thinking. The orders are exactly the changes in Chapter 5: ten sells, no buys, and exit levels for the shares that stay.

The plan has three phases. Phase 1 frees capital by selling and trimming. Phase 2 is a set of standing re-entry maps with no capital allocated today, because no purchase passes the gates. Phase 3 sets the defensive exit levels on every share you keep. The order of placement follows the logic of the waterfall: protect first, then sell the names with deadlines, then sell the names that are large and thin. If the exchange closes for a holiday during the week, the validity dates shift by a session and the rest of the plan does not change.

6.4 Order sequence, broker steps and gap rules

The sequence matters because each step protects the next one. The first job is to read your broker's profit and loss statement and the exchange holiday list for the week. Glossary

Asymmetry (reward-to-risk)
The gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop. A ratio of 3.5 means each rupee at risk can earn 3.5 rupees.
ATR (average true range)
The size of a stock's normal daily move in rupees, used to place stops outside ordinary noise.
Beta
How much a stock tends to move when the index moves 1%. A beta of 1.5 means a typical 1.5% move.
Cash conversion (CFO/PAT)
Operating cash flow divided by profit after tax. Below 0.40 over three years is a red line; 0.70 is the compounder floor.
Distribution day
A day when an index falls on higher volume than the day before, a sign that large holders are selling.
Effective market exposure
The size of an index position your book behaves like, after weighting each holding by its beta.
GTT
A trigger order that stays open across sessions and sends a limit or market order when the price reaches a set level.
Heat (portfolio heat)
The money at risk if every stop is hit, as a share of net worth. The ceiling is 12.0%.
Let-Winners-Run rule
Never trim a Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals and rising or stable estimates only because RSI is above 70 or weight crossed 15%; allow up to 22.0%.
Lockstep
Two holdings whose daily returns have a one-year correlation of 0.65 or more, so they act as one combined bet.
Red line
A hard test (debt, pledge, cash conversion or Stage 4) whose failure routes a name to avoid or replace.
Risk-Unit Sizing
Weight equals the smaller of the tier ceiling and a risk budget in basis points divided by the stop distance.
RSI (relative strength index)
A 0 to 100 speedometer of recent price change: above 70 is very fast up, below 30 is very fast down.
Stage 1 to 4
The four phases of a stock's life: base, rising, topping and falling. Stage 2 is the only phase for new tactical buying.
Stop loss
An exit price chosen in advance so that a wrong idea costs a known amount.
Swap test
The three conditions that must hold before one stock replaces another: an exit trigger, a challenger that passes every gate near support, and 1.5 times the reward-to-risk.
