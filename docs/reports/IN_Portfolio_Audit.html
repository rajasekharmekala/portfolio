2.2 Concentration and the one exception that applies

The concentration rule caps any single stock at 20% of net worth. The reason is simple arithmetic. If one holding is a fifth of the account, a fall of half in that stock costs a tenth of the whole account, while a holding at 5% costs only one fortieth. The cap limits how much one company's bad news can damage the whole, however good the company looks. RNIT AI is 22.35% of net worth at today's close, so it sits above the cap. Left alone, the rule would require a sale.

The rule has one exception, and the audit tests it rather than assuming it. A winning holding above the cap may be kept, without trimming into strength, when its Stage 2 momentum is intact. The tests are that the price is above a rising 20-day average or 21-day exponential average, that the relative strength over 63 sessions is above 80, and that the RSI is below 82. If all three hold, the rule says do not trim, and instead move the stop up to the rising 21-day exponential average. RNIT AI meets all three tests today. Its close of ₹105.85 is above its 20-day average of ₹103.53, which in turn is higher than the ₹96.72 it stood at ten sessions ago. Its 63-session relative strength is 87.0, and its RSI is 56.2. The exception therefore applies, and the stop becomes the 21-day exponential average, which is ₹104.26.

Worked example: the arithmetic of the cap. Your RNIT AI position is 10,000 shares at ₹105.85, which is ₹10,58,500. Net worth is ₹47,37,004. Divide the first by the second and you get 22.35%. The cap is 20% of net worth, which is ₹9,47,401. The position would have to fall to that value, which means selling 1,050 shares at today's close (10,000 less 8,950) to bring the holding to the cap. If the exception lapses and the stop is hit at the 21-day average of ₹104.26, the same arithmetic gives 913 shares, because the lower price leaves a larger share count at the cap. The exception keeps all 10,000 shares while the three tests hold. The record's own verdict for this name is a trim of 30 to 50 percent, which would be 3,000 to 5,000 shares, releasing ₹3.18 lakh to ₹5.29 lakh at today's close. This audit does not take that trim, because the rule says not to trim a winner that is still in Stage 2, and because the market is in a Downtrend the rule still protects the position only while its trend holds.

Chapter 4: The shortlist funnel and the three-condition test

The shortlist is a funnel. It starts with every company the research process has considered, and each company must pass three layers before it becomes a candidate. The first layer is the financial gate, which asks whether the business earns a good return on the capital it uses, converts its profits into cash, and carries a manageable amount of debt. 5.3 Scenarios: what the book looks like under three stresses

The audit tests the book against three simple stresses, because a plan that works only in calm markets is not a plan. The first stress is that every stop on the remaining positions is hit at its stop price. The second is that the market falls by 10% and every remaining share falls with it. The third is that none of the sales fills, and the book stays as it is today. Each scenario is a simple calculation on the figures in this chapter, not a forecast of what the market will do.

In the first scenario, the remaining positions would all be sold at their stop prices, and the loss would be the money at risk in Chapter 2, which is ₹426,789, or 9.0% of post-order net worth. That is the most the plan can lose if every stop is hit, and it excludes gaps, which can take a price below its stop before a sale is possible. The second scenario applies a 10% fall to the post-order equities of ₹3,366,615, which would remove about ₹336,661 from net worth, or 7.1%. The orders reduce that exposure by ₹1,276,787 compared with the present book, which would have lost ₹464,340 in the same fall. The third scenario is the one that matters most for execution. If none of the sales fills, the book keeps its present risk, and the existing stops, which for RNIT AI sit at the formula level of ₹83.54 until the ratchet is placed, apply. The stop on RNIT AI is therefore the first order to place, because it is the one that changes the most risk.

7.3 The biggest risks and unknowns

A report that lists its rules without listing its risks is only half honest. The first risk is the regime itself. The rules are built to be calm in a Downtrend, but a Downtrend that becomes a crash moves faster than the stops can be placed. The stops in this report are placed at closes, and a gap at the open can take a price well below a stop before a sale is possible. The money-at-risk figure assumes that every stop fills at its level, which is the best case for the plan, not the worst. In a sharp fall the actual loss could be larger than the figure in Chapter 2.

The second risk is concentration in the top names, and it is not fully solved by the rules. RNIT AI is 22.35% of net worth, and the exception keeps it that size while its trend holds. A fall that breaks the trend but does not hit the stop at the same moment could leave the position large for a day or two. The third risk is the Core stops, which are wide. TANFACIND, WELENT, MANORAMA and LAURUSLABS can each lose between 27% and 35% from their closes before their stops are reached. That is a real loss, and the rules accept it in exchange for not being shaken out of companies whose trends are intact. Whether that trade is right for you depends on how much you could tolerate, and that judgment belongs to you rather than to the rules.

The unknowns are the ones the research could not close. The results dates for eighteen of the nineteen holdings were not verified, so an earnings release could move a holding between two sessions without the audit knowing in advance. 7.4 How often to re-run the audit

The audit is a snapshot, and the stops are not. The rules imply a rhythm. The stops should be checked at every session close while the orders are open, which means each day until the sales have filled and the stops are in place. The regime should be checked each week, because it changes slowly and a weekly check catches a change that a daily check would show as noise. The full audit should be re-run whenever one of three things happens: the regime changes, a stop is triggered, or a material unrecorded trade appears in the holdings log. Otherwise the full audit is worth repeating after each sales cycle, so that the next run starts from a holdings log that already reflects the orders.

When you re-run the audit, the first step is the one that most often goes wrong. Check that the holdings log matches the broker, and record every fill, before the ledger is built. The audit trusts the log, so an error in the log becomes an error in every verdict. The second step is to check the research dates, because a results release or a pledge change in the last week may not be in the record. The third is to confirm the ORIANA-SM series once the broker's chart and a second source agree, because that single correction is the largest data judgment in this report.

What would change the rulebook itself

  • If the regime is in Correction or Uptrend for three consecutive weekly checks, the posture table changes and the greenfield picker is asked again, with the zones in Chapter 3 as the first candidates.
  • If the sector feed is repaired and the nine unclassified sectors can be read, the sector tilt rule can be applied, and any holding whose sector is Lagging would be reviewed.
  • If the shortlist records are re-underwritten at current prices, every asymmetry ratio in Chapter 3 and Chapter 4 changes, and the candidates are re-ranked.

What this means for you: the rules are simple and the discipline is in the stops and the checks, not in the forecasts. Glossary, methodology and the list of gate exceptions follow, so that every term, every source and every gap is in one place.

Glossary

Asymmetry. The ratio of the possible gain to the possible loss, measured from the close to the base target and from the close to the stop. The audit asks for at least 3.5 times for tactical ideas and 3.0 times for core ideas. A ratio is only as good as the levels it uses, so the record's levels are treated as stale until they are re-underwritten.

ATR (average true range). The average daily movement of a stock over 14 sessions, counting gaps. It is the normal wobble of the price, and the stops are placed a fraction of it below the averages so that ordinary movement does not trigger a sale.

Bull call spread. An option position that buys a call at a lower strike and sells a call at a higher strike on the same stock and expiry. It limits both the possible gain and the possible loss, and it loses value as expiry approaches if the stock does not rise.

Breadth. The share of stocks trading above their own 50-day averages. A low breadth reading means that a rising index is being carried by a few large companies.

Concentration. The share of net worth in one holding. The audit caps any single stock at 20%, with the exception described in Chapter 2.

Core and Tactical. The two strategy buckets. Core is the longer-term compounding book, which the mandate sets at 30% to 40% of net worth. Tactical is the momentum book, at 50% to 65%. Each holding has a bucket, and the bucket decides which stop formula applies.

Distribution days. Sessions in the last 25 when the index fell on heavier volume than the day before. Many distribution days signal that large investors are selling into the index.

Downtrend. The regime label used when the index is below its averages and breadth is weak. In a Downtrend the posture is maximum cash and no new momentum buying.

Exit, trim and hold. An exit sells the whole position. A trim sells part, here half. A hold keeps the position, with a trail or a hard stop attached.

Good till triggered (GTT). A conditional order that a broker holds and sends when the price reaches the stated trigger. It lets a stop work without you watching the screen. The broker's name for the feature may differ.

Greenfield picker. The third analysis, which asks what to buy with cash alone, ignoring every holding, so that the answer is not shaped by what you already own.

Max pain. The option strike at which the total value of open option contracts would be smallest. Traders often treat it as a reference level near expiry, though it is a description of positioning rather than a forecast.

Money at risk. The sum over holdings of shares multiplied by the distance from the close to the stop. It is a worst case for the plan, not a forecast, and it excludes gaps.

Moving average. The average closing price over a set number of sessions. The 20-day and 50-day averages are short- and medium-term trend lines, and the 200-day average is the long-term one.

Put-call ratio (PCR). Open put contracts divided by open call contracts. A figure above one means more puts than calls are open.

Relative strength (RS). A stock's return over a period compared with the Nifty 50's return over the same period. A positive figure means it has beaten the index. The audit uses 63 sessions for its main test and 28 sessions as a check.

RSI (relative strength index). A momentum reading from zero to one hundred over 14 sessions. The audit uses it only as a check, and the concentration exception uses an upper limit of 82.

Stage. The ledger's classification of a stock's trend into four stages: Stage 1 basing, Stage 2 markup, Stage 3 topping and Stage 4 markdown. Stage 2 is the stage the holding rules want.

Stop. A price at which the position is sold in full without further debate. The audit computes each stop from the stock's own swing low and averages, and it adjusts the stop only by the rules.

Swing low. The lowest low over the last 20 sessions. It anchors the stop formulas, because it marks the most recent level where buyers stepped in.

Swap test. The three-condition test that links a sale to a purchase. A sale is only replaced by a purchase when the holding has triggered an exit, the challenger is at support and passing every gate, and the challenger's asymmetry is at least 1.5 times the holding's.
