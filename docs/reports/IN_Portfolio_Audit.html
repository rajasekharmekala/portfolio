Start here. This report is an audit of the money you hold at Zerodha, written for someone who has never read a stock chart. It does three jobs. First, it checks every share you own against a fixed set of rules and says, for each one, whether to keep it, trim it or sell it. Second, it asks what you would buy if you held only cash today. Third, it turns the difference between those two answers into a list of orders you can place at your broker. It is a decision document, not a forecast. Nothing here predicts where the market goes next; the rules only say what to do when a price does something specific.

Three independent analysts produced the answers. The first judges the market as a whole: is the tide coming in or going out. The second, the holdings auditor, is adversarial: it tries to find a reason to sell each position you own. The third, the greenfield picker, never sees what you own, what you paid or how much you have gained. It answers one question: if you had only cash today, which of the shortlisted companies would you buy, at what price, and how much? Keeping the third analyst blind to your holdings stops a familiar mistake, which is holding a poor stock because you already own it. The chief investment officer then compares the second and third answers and only recommends a swap when three tests, set out in Chapter 4, are all passed.

You will meet a few ideas again and again, so here they are with everyday comparisons. A moving average is the average price over the last N days, like the trend line of a school report card; the 50-day and 200-day averages are the ones we watch. A stage is the season a stock is in: Stage 1 is winter, a flat base after a fall; Stage 2 is spring and summer, a rising trend; Stage 3 is autumn, a top where the rise stalls; Stage 4 is winter again, a falling trend. RSI is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 the price has risen unusually fast, below 30 it has fallen unusually fast. ATR (average true range) is the stock's normal daily wobble in rupees, and we use it to place stops far enough away that ordinary noise does not trigger them. Volume is how many shares changed hands, which tells you how many people voted with money. A stop-loss is an exit price you decide in advance, so that fear does not decide for you. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the gain you could make divided by the loss you would take if the stop is hit; we want at least 2 to 1 for a core holding and 2.5 to 1 for a tactical one. Position size matters because no single stock should be able to hurt you badly: the hard cap is 15% of net worth for one stock, and a winner may run to 22% only if its business numbers stay sound.

The labels mean the following. EXIT or REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER: sell the whole holding, because a hard red line failed or a stop was hit. HOLD / TRAIL: keep it and let the stop rise with the trend. NO TRADE / WATCH: do nothing yet because a level is being watched. 2.3 Money at risk if every stop is hit

A stop-loss only helps if you know in advance what it costs. Money at risk is the number of shares times the distance between today's price and the stop, added over the book and expressed as a share of net worth. The policy ceiling for total portfolio heat is 12.0% of net worth, which is the most you may lose if every stop is hit at its level. Names whose stop is already broken are shown at zero because their loss is no longer a forecast: the price has crossed the line and the loss is the price at which you actually sell. The calculation is for the stops as written, and a gap through a stop on bad news can lose more, a limit the table cannot measure.

Each row lists shares held, the closing price, the stop, the rupee distance per share, and the money lost if the stop is hit, first for today's book and then for the book after the plan. A good row has a small percentage and a visible gap between price and stop; a bad row has a large percentage or a price already through its stop. 2.4 The concentration test, the trim arithmetic and Let-Winners-Run

Two limits cap how much of your money one stock may hold. The hard cap is 15% of net worth. The Let-Winners-Run ceiling is 22%, available only to a Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals and stable or rising estimates, and the stop for such a winner trails the 21-day average or the 50-day average. RNITAI is the only holding above 15% and the only one near 22%, so it is the test case. It is Stage 2 and up 77.7%, which looks like a winner. The rule then asks whether fundamentals are intact. The three-year ratio of operating cash flow to profit is 0.23, under the 0.25 red line, by ₹0.42 crore on ₹4.31 crore of cash against ₹18.91 crore of profit, and about ₹13.2 crore of borrowings sit inside reported operating cash flow. A company that borrows to show cash flow is not a company with intact fundamentals, so the protection does not apply and the 15% cap binds. SEDEMAC at 14.47% is below 15%, so it needs no trim on size alone, and LAURUSLABS is Stage 2 but tiny at 1.46%. The three largest winners therefore give three different answers: RNITAI, a Stage 2 name, is trimmed; SEDEMAC, which is too new to have a stage, is held because the cap is not breached; and LAURUSLABS, the other Stage 2 name, is trailed because nothing is large enough to trim.

Imagine that tonight your broker account held only cash, and the only companies you were allowed to buy were the nineteen on the vetted shortlist. Which would you buy tomorrow, at what price, and how much of each? That thought experiment removes the most common investing error, which is to hold something because you already own it. 3.2 Risk-unit sizing for the conditional order book

If a candidate does reach its entry zone, how much should you buy? The policy answer is risk-unit sizing. Each conviction tier has a risk budget in basis points of net worth (a basis point is one hundredth of one percent): Tier 1 gets 100, Tier 2 gets 75, Tier 3 gets 50 and Tier 4 gets 35. The weight is the budget divided by the distance from entry to stop, so a wide stop earns a smaller position, capped at the tier's maximum weight. The formula is weight = min(tier maximum, risk budget in basis points divided by stop distance in basis points, times 100). Total portfolio heat, the sum of what all open stops would cost, must stay under 12.0% of net worth. Chapter 4: Shortlist Strategy Funnel & Deep Underwriting Pipeline

A funnel is a series of filters, each narrowing what passes to the next. Here there are three, and a company must pass all three before it earns money. The financial gate asks whether the business is sound: no failed red line (a debt, cash-conversion or pledge test, or a Stage 4 breakdown) and an operating score of at least 3.0 out of 5. The technical gate asks whether the chart supports buying now: not Stage 4, within 1.6 ATR of verified support, and passing one of three momentum tests. The asymmetry gate asks whether the reward is large enough: 2.0 to 1 for Core and 2.5 to 1 for Tactical at the actual entry price. The nineteen shortlisted names were underwritten in six batches over the last two sessions; this chapter reads those records against Friday's ledger and explains where each name stands, then applies the three-condition swap test to every holding that Chapter 2 sends out. The result is that the funnel passes no one fully: each name fails at least one gate, and the interesting question is which, because it tells you what to wait for.

4.3 The three-condition swap test, applied to every exit and trim

Selling a holding and buying something else is only justified when the replacement is clearly better. The swap framework sets three conditions that must all be true. Condition 1: the holding has triggered an exit or a trim. Condition 2: the challenger has passed one hundred per cent of the gates and is strictly at support, within half an ATR. Condition 3: the challenger's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's, after tax and friction. The conditions are joined by 'and', so failing any one blocks the swap, and the burden of proof sits on the challenger. The only external challenger on the shortlist is HAL, with 4.23 to 1 at the top of its zone, so each of the eleven holdings leaving the book is tested against it. Capital gains tax and trading costs depend on lot-level buy dates and tax rates that are not in this run's research record, so the friction test is shown as N/A and the arithmetic is done before costs, which flatters the challenger and still does not produce a swap.

6.3 Phase 3: defensive GTT and trailing-stop architecture

A stop-loss only works if it exists before the price moves. Zerodha's GTT (good till triggered) order holds a rule on the exchange's behalf for up to a year: when the last traded price touches the trigger, it sends a limit order at the price you set. For small and mid-sized shares a limit order set exactly at the trigger can miss in a fast fall, so set the limit price 2% below the trigger; a gap through that price leaves the order unfilled, which is the main weakness of GTT stops and the reason the closing-price rule remains the primary check. The levels in the table are closing-price rules from the underwriting records: the rule says sell if the close is below the level, while the GTT fires on a touch during the day. The GTT may therefore sell a little earlier than the rule, and that is accepted, because an early exit is a smaller error than a missed one.

Glossary

20-day, 50-day and 200-day moving average
The average closing price over that many trading days. It smooths daily noise into a trend line; a price above a rising average is a healthy sign.
Stage 1, 2, 3 and 4
The four seasons of a stock: Stage 1 a flat base, Stage 2 a rising trend above rising averages, Stage 3 a top or pullback, Stage 4 a decline below falling averages.
RSI (relative strength index)
A 0 to 100 speedometer of how fast a price has moved. Below 30 means an unusually fast fall; above 70 an unusually fast rise.
ATR (average true range)
The stock's normal daily range in rupees. Stops are placed a multiple of this away so ordinary wobble does not trigger them.
Volume multiple
Today's shares traded divided by the 20-day average. Above 1.5 means unusually heavy trading.
Stop-loss
A price chosen in advance at which you sell, so that fear does not decide for you.
GTT (good till triggered)
A Zerodha order that waits up to a year and sends a limit order when a trigger price trades.
Limit order
An order to buy or sell at a stated price or better; it may not fill.
Reward-to-risk (asymmetry)
The gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop. A ratio of 2.0 means you could win twice what you could lose.
Risk-unit sizing
Sizing a new position by the loss you accept if the stop is hit: weight equals the tier's risk budget divided by the stop distance, capped at the tier maximum.
Basis point (bps)
One hundredth of one percent. 75 bps of net worth is 0.75%.
Portfolio heat
The total loss if every open stop were hit, as a percentage of net worth. The ceiling is 12.0%.
Beta
How much a stock tends to move when the market moves 1%. Beta 1.5 means about 1.5%.
Effective market exposure
Each holding's weight times its beta, added up: how much of the market's move the whole portfolio absorbs.
Correlation
A measure from -1 to 1 of whether two stocks move together. Pairs at 0.60 or higher are treated as one bet.
Breadth
The share of stocks trading above their 50-day (or 200-day) average. Low breadth means the fall is broad.
Distribution day
A session when the index falls on heavier volume than the day before, a sign of large holders selling.
India VIX
The market's expected 30-day volatility from option prices; a fear gauge.
Put-call ratio and max pain
The put-call ratio compares put and call open interest; max pain is the strike at which option writers pay least at expiry.
Promoter pledge
The share of the founders' holding that they have pledged as loan collateral. Above 20% is a red line here.
CFO to PAT
Cash flow from operations divided by profit after tax over three years: whether profit turns into cash. Under 0.25 is a red line.
ROCE and D/E
Return on capital employed is profit earned on the money tied up in the business; debt-to-equity is borrowings divided by shareholders' money.
Red line
A hard disqualifier: a failed solvency, cash or pledge test, or a Stage 4 breakdown. A failed red line routes the name to exit or rejection.
Let-Winners-Run rule
A Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals may grow to 22% of net worth before a trim, while its stop trails the rising 21-day or 50-day average.
Core Alpha and Tactical Momentum
Core Alpha is the slow compounding book held for years; Tactical Momentum is the faster Stage 2 book held for weeks to months.
Price band (circuit limit)
A daily limit set by the exchange beyond which a stock cannot trade. A stock at its lower band can have sellers but no buyers.
ASM (additional surveillance measure)
An exchange measure that tightens margins and trading conditions on stocks with unusual price behaviour; Stage IV is the strictest.
T+1 settlement
Sale proceeds are credited the next trading day.
