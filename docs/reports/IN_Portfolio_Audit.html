This is an audit of the 19 shares you own in your Zerodha account. It asks three questions. What is the market doing? Does each holding still earn its place under written rules? Three independent readings sit behind the numbers, and an editor lays them side by side. The first reads the market and says how much risk the rules allow. The second audits your holdings one by one and owes no loyalty to any of them. The third is the greenfield reading. It is told nothing about what you own, what you paid or what you have earned, and it must say what it would buy with ₹4,380,689.62 in cash. That blindness is deliberate. An owner defends what he already holds, and a blind picker shows what the shortlist is worth on its own merits. When the two disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

Every holding gets one of five labels, and each label has an exact trigger. EXIT means sell every share. It fires when a hard red line is broken and no written exception applies, or when a holding marked HOLD / HARD STOP closes under its named stop price. TRIM means sell part. It fires when a position is larger than its conviction tier allows while at least one rule on that company also fails. The line between the two follows one rule. Red lines 1 and 2 (debt and pledge) are faults in the balance sheet and in governance, and one good quarter cannot cure them, so they lead to an EXIT. Red lines 3 and 4 (cash conversion and a Stage 4 breakdown) can be cured by a filing or by a base, so they lead to a cut down to the tier ceiling with a dated test, and any shares kept are an exception that the test either confirms or removes. A closing price under a named stop is an EXIT whatever the red lines say, because a stop only moves up and is never lowered after the price has fallen. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep the shares and sell all of them if a named closing price breaks. HOLD / TRAIL means keep the shares and lift the stop as the price rises. NO TRADE / WATCH means the position is too small to act on. A closing stop in an underwriting record binds the holding. Where no record exists, stops follow a formula: for core holdings, the lower of the recent swing low and the 50-day or 200-day average, minus one ATR (average true range); for tactical holdings, the lower of the swing low and the 20-day or 50-day average, minus half an ATR.

Size is governed by three rules. No single stock may exceed 15% of net worth. A proven winner may run up to a runaway cap of 22%, and the Let-Winners-Run rule protects it from a mechanical sale only while the chart is in Stage 2, the fundamentals are intact and estimates are stable or rising. Past 22%, or with a red line broken, the rule gives no shelter. Risk-Unit Sizing then asks how much of net worth you would lose if the stop were hit: weight equals the lower of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the distance to the stop. The budgets are 100, 75, 50 and 35 basis points for Tiers 1 to 4, with ceilings of 10%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2.0%. The portfolio's total loss if every stop hit is capped at 12% of net worth. A challenger replaces a holding only if it passes a three-condition swap test: the holding has triggered an exit or trim, the challenger has passed every gate and sits within half an ATR of support, and its reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's. Reward-to-risk must reach 3.0 for a core name and 3.5 for a tactical one.

Four red lines override any chart. Debt to equity at or above 1.20, or interest cover under 2.0. A promoter pledge at or above 20% of the promoter's shares. Three-year operating cash flow under 40% of profit (CFO/PAT under 0.40) without a verified two-quarter turnaround. And a Stage 4 breakdown with no base under it. In a Downtrend, the posture adds maximum cash, cuts to leveraged or Stage 4 names and no new momentum purchases. Tax is shown as N/A throughout, because lot-level data is not in the inputs and a guessed rate would mislead.

A handful of ideas carry the whole report, and each has an everyday comparison. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line drawn through a bumpy road. Stage 1 is a base after a fall, like a landing on a staircase. Stage 2 is the rising markup, a growing season. Stage 3 is a tired top or a pullback, late summer. Stage 4 is a markdown, winter. RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer for price: below 30 means a stock has fallen unusually fast and above 70 means it has risen unusually fast. ATR is a stock's normal daily wobble in rupees, which is why a stop tighter than half an ATR is usually knocked out by noise. Volume is how many shares changed hands, which is how many people voted with money. A stop-loss is an exit price chosen in advance so fear does not choose it later. Reward-to-risk divides what you can win by what you can lose. Position size is the number of shares you hold, chosen so that a stopped-out trade costs a small, fixed slice of your wealth.

Two portfolio-level ideas matter just as much. Beta is how far a share has tended to swing when the Nifty 50 moves 1%, and effective market exposure adds up each holding's weight times its beta to show how much of the index's move your book really feels, like a boat whose pull in the current depends on how much of its hull is below the water. Hidden overlap asks whether two holdings are secretly one bet because they rise and fall together, like two cars bought from the same maker with the same fault. Two quality measures recur in the case notes. ROCE, return on capital employed, is the operating profit earned on the money invested in the business, like the rent a landlord earns on the cost of a building. CFO/PAT divides the cash a business collected from operations by its accounting profit, so a figure near 1.0 means profit turns into cash and a figure near 0.1 means the profit is mostly promises on paper. 1.3 Which holdings move in lockstep: hidden overlap

Two holdings are in lockstep when they rise and fall together so reliably that owning both is closer to owning one position twice. The positions file measures this with the correlation of daily returns over one year, where 1.0 means two shares move identically and 0 means they move independently. Its reading is: "Across your 19 equity holdings, no pair has a 1-year daily return correlation of 0.60 or higher, indicating low hidden overlap between positions." The list of lockstep clusters is empty, and so is the list of pairs at or above 0.65, the audit's line for treating two names as one bet. That is a real finding. Your 19 names span businesses from air cargo to cocoa butter, and their day-to-day moves have been largely independent.

What the measure cannot see matters just as much. A correlation computed over a calm year understates how shares behave in a fall, when fear makes unrelated stocks drop together. It sees only daily price moves, so it cannot see a shared customer, a shared funding source or a shared dependence on government orders. It does not look inside an industry label either. Seven of your holdings are capital goods makers: INTERARCH, ASTRAMICRO, EPACKPEB, TDPOWERSYS, TARIL, APARINDS and BALUFORGE. Their combined value is ₹1,345,252.15, which is 30.71% of net worth (the sector table in 1.4 adds rounded weights and shows 30.72%). The market report shows only 13.0% of the 69 Capital Goods names above their 50-day average, with 21 of them in Stage 4, and a median one-month fall of 9.73%. These seven are not in lockstep in the daily data, but they share an industry, a pool of capital spending and a sensitivity to the same orders and interest rates. If that group sold off together in one bad week, the empty overlap list would not protect you.

2.2 The trim arithmetic: how the size of each sale is set

A trim is a partial sale, and its size is not a mood. Every trim in this plan comes out of a short calculation with three inputs: a cap on the position's share of net worth, a limit on how much money you would lose if the stop were hit, and the real cost of selling in a thin market. The reason to show the working is that a number you cannot reproduce is a number you cannot trust. Two examples follow, one for each reason a trim can exist. RNITAI is a cap trim, forced by the rules. SEDEMAC is a risk-budget trim, and it is labelled discretionary because no red line forces it. After the two examples, a third paragraph explains how the sizes of the smaller trims come from the tier ceilings.

Worked example: RNITAI, a rule-forced cap trim. Step 1, the cap: 15% of your net worth of ₹4,380,689.62 is ₹657,103. At the close of 102.75 that buys 6,395 shares (657,103 divided by 102.75, rounded down). You hold 10,000, so the minimum sale is 3,605 shares. Step 2, the chosen size: the plan sells 4,000 and keeps 6,000. That is 395 shares (about ₹40,600) beyond the minimum, and it leaves 6,000 × 102.75 = ₹616,500, which is 14.07% of net worth. The cushion means RNITAI would need to rise about 6.6% (to about 109.5) before it crossed 15% again. Step 3, the risk test: the stop is 85.14, which is 17.61 below the close, or 17.1%. 2.3 Applying the Let-Winners-Run rule to compounding winners

Selling winners early is one of the most common ways ordinary investors lose money, because a stock that doubles often triples. 2.4 Holdings forensic health matrix

The forensic matrix puts business quality and chart quality side by side, because a stock can fail on either and the cure differs. The business side uses three ratios. ROCE, return on capital employed, is the profit a company earns on all the money put into it, like the yield on a rental flat after counting every rupee spent on it. Think of a shop whose accounts show a profit but whose till stays empty: a ratio under 0.40 is that shop. Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine that tomorrow morning every share in your account has been sold, and the only thing left is ₹4,380,689.62 in cash. You do not remember what you paid for anything. You do not remember which stock made you proud or which one embarrassed you. The question is simple: which businesses would you buy today, at today's prices, under the rules written in this report? That is the greenfield test. The word comes from builders, who call an empty plot a greenfield, because nothing on it has to be knocked down before work begins.

The test matters because owning something bends your judgement. Psychologists call one version of this the sunk-cost trap: you keep a losing stock because selling would make the loss real, and you keep a winning stock because it feels disloyal to sell. Think of packing for a house move. If you must carry every item up three flights of stairs, you suddenly ask which things you would buy again, and the answer is rarely "all of it". The greenfield test makes the same demand of your portfolio. A name that would not be bought on an empty plot has to justify why it deserves your capital while it sits there.

Every candidate must clear three gates, and the gates run in a fixed order. The financial gate asks whether the business is sound: debt, interest cover, promoter pledges and whether profit turns into cash. The technical gate asks whether the share price is in a confirmed uptrend rather than a slide or a base. The asymmetry gate asks whether the possible gain is at least 3.0 times the possible loss for a core name, or 3.5 times for a tactical name. On top of the gates sits the market regime. Today the regime is Downtrend, and the Downtrend rule says: hold the maximum cash, trim the weakest positions, and open no new momentum longs. That rule decides what the arithmetic is allowed to do. This chapter runs the empty-plot test, shows how a purchase would be sized if one were allowed, explains the price at which each rejected name would clear the floor, and then looks at the nearest candidates from outside the book.

3.2 How risk-unit sizing would size a purchase

Risk-unit sizing is the rule that decides how much money a single purchase may take. The idea is that you decide first how much of your net worth you are willing to lose if the stop is hit, and the position size follows from that. The formula is weight = min(tier cap, risk budget in basis points divided by stop distance in percent). A basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so 50 basis points is 0.50% of net worth. The tiers set both numbers: TIER_1 to TIER_4 carry budgets of 100, 75, 50 and 35 basis points and caps of 10.0%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2.0%. A wide stop forces a small position, a tight stop allows a bigger one, and the cap prevents any single name from growing past its tier.

Think of it as a seat-belt budget for a road trip. If you will accept losing half a rupee in every hundred you own, a stock whose stop is 17% below the price can take only about 2.9% of your money, because 2.9% falling 17% costs exactly 0.5%. A stock whose stop is 3% below the price could in theory take 16%, but the 3.5% cap steps in first. Chapter 4: Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

A funnel narrows a wide stream into a narrow one. In this report the stream is every name that might be worth owning, and the funnel has three filters: the financial gate, the technical gate and the asymmetry gate. A name must clear them in that order, and clearing one does not excuse failing the next. The reason for a funnel, rather than a single score, is that each gate guards against a different kind of loss. The financial gate protects you from a business that cannot pay its bills or turn profit into cash. The technical gate protects you from buying into a share price that is falling. The asymmetry gate protects you from paying too much for the upside on offer.

Two examples show how the gates behave. A good quarter does not cure a pledge, which is a loan secured by the promoters' own shares and can force sales if the price falls.

After the funnel comes the swap test, which asks whether selling a holding to buy a challenger improves the portfolio. Then comes the friction audit, which counts the costs of acting: tax, brokerage and price slippage. 4.2 The three-condition swap test for every sale

The swap test is a guard against selling one thing only to buy something equally risky. It has three conditions, and all three must hold before a sale can be turned into a purchase. Condition 1: the holding has triggered an exit or a trim. Condition 2: the challenger has passed 100% of the gates and sits within 0.5 ATR of support. Condition 3: the challenger's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's. If any condition fails, the money goes to cash. The reason for three conditions is that each answers a different worry: whether the sale is justified, whether the replacement is sound, and whether the swap is worth the cost.

Chapter 6: 3-Phase Actionable Execution Orders

A decision on paper earns nothing and protects nothing until it becomes an order in the market. This chapter is the manual for that step, written for someone who has placed a few trades but has never sold fourteen positions in a week. The work comes in three phases. Phase 1 raises cash: fourteen sell orders, five of which close a position and nine of which shrink one. Phase 2 writes down the standing conditions under which any of the sold names could come back, with ₹0 allocated today. Phase 3 protects what you keep, using stop orders that sit in the broker's system and watch the price for you. Three plain terms matter. A limit order says "sell no lower than this price", so you control the price but not whether it fills. A market order says "sell now at whatever the buyers offer", so you control the timing but not the price. A GTT, short for good-till-triggered, is an instruction that waits quietly until a price is touched and then places an order for you. The first session where anything can fill is Thursday 22 October, because NSE and BSE were shut on Tuesday 20 October for Dussehra. Each order in this chapter is a delivery sale, meaning you sell shares that are already in your demat account, and every one is placed fresh each morning if it did not fill.

6.3 Phase 3: defensive GTT and trailing-stop architecture

The third phase protects the shares that remain after the sales. A stop is a price at which you decide the idea is wrong and leave. Without a written stop, a falling price invites a hundred reasons to wait. Zerodha's tool for this is the GTT, which stands for good-till-triggered. You create a single-trigger GTT sell by entering a trigger price and a limit price. When the market touches the trigger, the system places a limit sell at your limit price on your behalf. It is called single-trigger because it has one condition; the two-leg type, which pairs a stop with a profit target, is not used in this plan. Closing-basis and intraday stops are different things, and the difference can cost you money. Most stops in this report are closing stops, which means the rule fires only if the price finishes the day below the level. A wick that dips under the level at 11 AM and recovers by 3:30 PM does not count. A GTT does not work that way. It watches the live price through the session and fires the moment the trigger is touched. So a GTT is a stricter version of the closing rule: it can sell you out on a wick the closing rule would have ignored. There are two sound ways to handle this. Accept the stricter version for the shares where a clean exit matters most, or place the GTT a little lower than the written level. The second suits NETWEB, where a results meeting on Saturday 24 October is a likely gap.

7.4 How often to rerun the audit

An audit is a photograph, and a photograph goes out of date at the speed of the thing it shows. In a Downtrend prices, stops and news change fast, so the rule is weekly. Use a weekly cadence while the regime label stays Downtrend, because weak markets break stops in clusters: in the last week TDPOWERSYS lost 13.6% and APARINDS lost 16.0%, and both broke their stops before any weekly check would have caught them. The second trigger is results. After each company reports, its cash-flow figures, its debtor days and its orders replace the estimates that the red lines rest on, and the rule for that name should be re-read within a day. The third trigger is a closing stop breaking. A closing stop is a price that is acted on only after the day's final price is known, and any close below one turns a hold into a sale, so re-running the list the same evening keeps the order book current.

Between runs, a short routine is enough. Check the closing price of each held share against its stop once per session; once the GTT orders in chapter 6 are placed, the broker does the watching. Look at the Nifty 50 against 22,179.90 and 23,479.08. After 14 November, once the last September-quarter result is out, run the full audit. If the market turns up and the posture changes, the next audit is also the moment to put cash back to work, because a rerun is the only time the swap test and the greenfield gates are checked against new prices. A rerun does not mean you must trade more. One lesson of a falling market is that the most useful output of an audit is often "nothing changed, hold the stops". When the regime improves, the cadence can relax to every two or three weeks. Next comes the shortest version of everything in this chapter.

Glossary

This glossary gives each technical term in the report one plain definition, with an everyday comparison where it helps. Terms are in alphabetical order. If you meet a word in the chapters that you do not recognise, look here first; the definitions are short on purpose, and the chapters give the reasoning and the numbers for your own holdings.

ASM (Additional Surveillance Measure)
A list run by the exchanges for shares whose price moves look unusual. Shares on the list face tighter trading rules, such as a larger deposit for each trade. Stage IV is the strictest level of the long-term version.
ATR (average true range)
The normal daily wobble of a share, in rupees. If the ATR is 50, a 50-rupee move in a day is ordinary, so a stop set 20 rupees below the price would be hit by noise alone.
Beta
How much a share or portfolio tends to move when the Nifty 50 moves 1%. Your book has a beta of 0.89, so a 1% fall in the index is expected to cost about ₹38,898.84.
Block deal and bulk deal
Large trades reported by the exchange. A block deal is a single big trade done in a special window; a bulk deal is any day's trade above 0.5% of a company's shares. They show where large investors are buying or selling.
Call wall and put wall
The option strikes with the most open contracts. A call wall at 23,000 acts like a ceiling that sellers defend, and a put wall at 22,000 acts like a floor.
Capital liberation
The step of selling or trimming positions to free cash, before any purchase. It is the first phase of the order plan.
Cash band
The share of net worth kept in cash, normally 5% to 10%. In a Downtrend the plan holds more, which is why the post-plan cash is 48.58%.
CFO/PAT (cash conversion)
Operating cash flow divided by profit after tax, over three years. A figure of 1.0 means each rupee of reported profit became a rupee of cash. Under 0.40 is a red line, because profit that stays on paper cannot pay debts.
Closing stop
A stop that triggers only when the day's final price is below the level, not when the price touches it during the day. It avoids being shaken out by a brief dip.
Debt-to-equity (D/E)
Borrowings divided by shareholders' money. At 1.20 or more the company owes more than it owns, and the plan treats that as a red line.
Delivery percentage
The share of the day's traded shares that were actually taken home, not bought and sold within the day. A low figure, such as 23.08% for TARIL on 21 October, hints that short-term traders did most of the trading.
Distribution day
A session when the index falls on higher volume than the day before. Eight of them in 25 sessions is a sign of selling by large holders.
Downtrend
The regime label for a market where the index sits below falling 50-day and 200-day averages. It is the ledger's label for 21 October and sets the plan's posture.
Effective market exposure
How much your portfolio behaves like the Nifty 50 once beta is counted. For you it is 88.8% today and 44.9% after the plan.
Entry ladder
The rule that sizes a purchase by how close the price is to support, measured in ATR. Close to support gets a full size; far above it gets no buy.
Face value and stock split
Face value is the nominal value printed on each share. In a split, such as ORIANA-SM's 1:5 sub-division, one share becomes five and the price falls to a fifth, with no change in the company's worth.
FII and DII
Foreign institutional investors and domestic institutional investors, the large funds whose daily net buying or selling the exchanges publish.
GTT (good-till-triggered order)
An order stored at the broker that waits for a price trigger and then places a sell. It is how stops keep working while you are away from the screen.
Hard cap
The 15% ceiling on any one holding's weight. A winner may run to 22% only if it is in Stage 2, with sound cash flow and rising estimates.
Heat (portfolio heat)
The share of net worth you would lose if every stop were hit at once. It is 9.33% today and 5.30% after the plan, against a 12% cap.
India VIX
A fear gauge calculated from option prices. It closed at 15.68, up 37.6% in a month from a low base, which means rising nerves but not panic.
Interest cover
Operating profit divided by interest paid. Under 2.0 means the company has little room to pay interest if profits dip.
Limit order
An order to buy or sell at a set price or better. It prevents a fill at a bad price, but it may not fill at all.
Lockstep (correlation)
Two shares that move together day to day. A correlation of 0.60 or more would mean that owning both is nearer to owning one twice. Your book has no such pair.
Max pain
The index level at which the most option buyers lose money at expiry. For 27 October it is 22,900.
Moving average (50-day, 200-day)
The average closing price over the last 50 or 200 sessions. It smooths noise, like a long-run average speed on a journey. A price under a falling average is in a weak trend.
Nifty 50
The index of the 50 largest companies on the NSE. It is the yardstick for the market in this report.
Pledge (promoter pledge)
Promoters borrowing against their own shares. If the share price falls, lenders may sell the pledged shares, which pushes the price down further. At 20% or more of the promoter holding it is a red line.
Promoter
The founder or controlling owner of a company. Their share of the company and any pledge of it are published every quarter.
Put-call ratio (PCR)
Open put contracts divided by open call contracts. A reading of 0.68 to 0.70 is well under 1, which means more call writers than put writers.
QIP (qualified institutional placement)
A company selling new shares to large institutions. It raises money but dilutes existing owners, who now hold a smaller slice.
Reward-to-risk (asymmetry)
The possible gain to the target divided by the possible loss to the stop. A ratio of 3.5 means three and a half rupees of upside for each rupee at risk.
ROCE (return on capital employed)
Operating profit as a share of the money invested in the business. A higher figure means the company makes more from each rupee of capital.
RSI (relative strength index)
A speedometer from 0 to 100. Under 30 means the price fell unusually fast, over 70 means it rose unusually fast. Neither is a signal alone.
Risk-unit sizing
Setting a position's weight so that hitting the stop costs a fixed slice of net worth, the lower of a tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance.
Runaway winner cap
The 22% weight above which even a protected winner must be trimmed.
Shareholding pattern
The quarterly filing that lists who owns a company: promoters, foreign funds, domestic funds and the public, plus any pledge.
Stage 1 to Stage 4
The four seasons of a share. Stage 1 is a quiet base after a fall, Stage 2 an advance above rising averages, Stage 3 a tired top, and Stage 4 a decline below falling averages.
Stop loss
A price chosen in advance at which you sell, so that a mistake costs a known amount.
Swap test
Three conditions that must all hold before a sale funds a purchase: the holding triggered an exit, the challenger passes every gate and sits near support, and its reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's.
Swing low
The lowest recent price at which buyers stepped in. It is a natural place for a stop.
Tier
A rank from 1 to 4 that sets how much of your money one holding may take, from 10% down to 2.0%, and how many basis points of net worth it may risk.
Time box
A fixed end date for an exception. The 125 INTERARCH shares and the 6,000 RNITAI shares are held only until the 16 November test; whatever fails it is sold, and the last RNITAI shares go by 31 December at the latest.
Trailing stop
A stop that only ever moves up as the price rises, locking in gains while leaving room to run.
Volume multiple
The day's traded volume divided by its 20-day average. RNITAI's 0.19 means it traded at about a fifth of its usual volume.
Weight
A holding's value as a share of your net worth. RNITAI's weight is 23.46%.

Stops and sizes follow a fixed order of precedence. Where an underwriting record gives a closing stop, it binds the holding, and no formula replaces it. ROBU has no record, but the 19 October audit named a closing stop of 979.78 for it, and ROBU closed at 978.85 on 21 October, 0.93 under that level. For greenfield purchases the formula stop is the swing low or the lower average, less one ATR for core names and half an ATR for tactical names. Position sizes use the risk-unit formula; trims use the weight caps and tier ceilings described in chapter 2.

ORIANA-SM has no bar for 21 October, so it is valued at its 19 October close of 259.00 and marked stale. Its ledger fields are distorted by an unadjusted 1:5 sub-division, with face value cut from ₹10 to ₹2 and a record date of 18 September 2026: the Stage 4 label, the RSI of 19.0, and the 50-day and 200-day averages of 722.40 and 1,528.62 are artefacts of the split, come from a split-distorted price series and are not used for the verdict. Finally, the JSON record files are updated only with changes the audit approves. A verdict changes a record only when the plan's rules and the evidence both say so, and the watchlist and shortlist files stay as they were where the audit adds nothing. Nothing in this report is a forecast, an instruction to trade, or tax advice.
