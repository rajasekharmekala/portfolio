The audit is run by three independent analysts and one editor. The first analyst reads the market: is the main index in a rising, falling or sideways phase, and which sectors lead. Keeping the third analyst blind matters because people grow attached to what they own, and a blind analyst has no reason to be kind to a loser. The editor then compares the answers and writes the orders.

Seven ideas carry the report, each with an everyday comparison. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line that smooths out daily wobble; the 50-day average is the last ten weeks of mood. A Stage is one of four seasons in a stock's life: Stage 1 is a flat winter base, Stage 2 is the spring and summer climb, Stage 3 is a topping autumn, Stage 4 is a falling winter. RSI is a speedometer from 0 to 100: below 30 means the stock has fallen unusually fast, above 70 means it has risen unusually fast. ATR (average true range) is the stock's normal daily wobble in rupees, which is why a stop is placed more than one wobble away from the price. A stop-loss is an exit price you decide in advance, before fear arrives. Reward-to-risk compares what you could gain with what you could lose; this system wants at least 2.0 rupees of possible gain per rupee at risk for Core Alpha and 2.5 for Tactical names. Position size is how much of your money sits in one idea, which is the reason no single stock should be able to hurt you badly.

The labels follow fixed rules. EXIT means sell the whole position, and it is triggered by a daily close below the recorded stop, a failed red line such as a promoter pledge of 20% or more, or a Stage 4 chart with no base. TRIM means sell part: it applies when a position is above the 15% single-stock cap, or above the 22% runaway-winner ceiling, or when the underwriting record names a trim rule. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep the shares, add nothing, and sell all of them on a close below a named price. HOLD / TRAIL means keep the shares and move the stop up under the rising 50-day average. The Let-Winners-Run rule says a Stage 2 winner with healthy fundamentals and stable or rising estimates is not sold only because its weight crossed 15% or its RSI passed 70; it may grow to 22% while the stop trails. New positions are sized by risk, not by hope: the weight is the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the distance to the stop. A swap, selling one stock to buy another, needs three yeses: the holding has triggered an exit or trim, the replacement passed every gate and sits within half an ATR of support, and its reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's.

Chapter 1: Macro and Sector Regime Alignment

A market regime is the weather the whole stock market is having, and it matters more than any single company because most stocks move with the weather. In a rising market, average companies drift upward and mistakes are forgiven. In a falling market, good companies fall too, and being right about a business can still lose money for months. The ledger decides the regime with arithmetic rather than opinion: it compares the index with its 50-day and 200-day averages, counts how many stocks in the broad market sit above their own 50-day average, and counts distribution days, which are sessions when the index fell on heavier trading than the day before and which show where large sellers have been active. 2.3 Holdings forensic health matrix

The matrix puts the numbers behind each verdict side by side: what you paid, what it is worth, how good the business looks on the three quality tests, where the chart stands, and the price at which the thesis is wrong. Quality here means return on capital employed (ROCE), which measures profit earned for each rupee of capital the business uses; debt to equity, which measures borrowed money against shareholders' money; and cash conversion, which compares the operating cash a company collects with the profit it reports. A company can show profit and still be short of cash if customers pay late, which is why the cash line is a hard red line. The quality figures come from the underwriting records dated 22 to 24 October, and ROBU has none because its underwriting was never completed.

Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Greenfield means a field with nothing built on it. The thought experiment is simple: suppose you woke up with ₹4,195,770.32 in cash and no holdings. Which shares from the vetted shortlist would you buy today, how many, and at what price? The point of the exercise is to remove a very human bias. When you already own a stock, you remember what you paid, you feel the loss or the gain, and you look for reasons to keep it. The greenfield analyst is given none of that. It sees only the price, the chart, the written underwriting record and the rules, so it has no reason to be kind to a loser or to hold on to a winner out of pride. If the greenfield answer and your actual book differ, the difference is the audit's most useful finding.

Two rules shape the answer. The first is risk-unit sizing from the scoring file: the weight of a new position is the smaller of its tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the distance to the stop. A wider stop therefore gets a smaller position automatically. Tier 2 names get a risk budget of 75 basis points of net worth (0.75%) and a ceiling of 6.5%; Tier 3 names get 50 basis points and 3.5%. The second rule is the regime: in a Downtrend there are no new momentum longs, so Tactical Momentum names get zero, and a Core Alpha name is allowed only by limit order inside a recorded entry zone with at least 2.0 rupees of reward per rupee of risk at the top of that zone. Only 5 of the 19 shortlisted names meet those tests, fewer than the six to twelve the mandate would like, and the picker does not force the number.

3.1 The risk-unit sizing arithmetic

Risk-unit sizing replaces the old habit of choosing a round number such as 5% or 10% for each idea. It starts from how much of your net worth you are willing to lose if the stop is hit, and works backward to the position size. The formula is weight equals the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance, where the stop distance is the gap between the entry price and the stop, as a share of the entry price. The method has one valuable property: when a stock is volatile and its sensible stop is far away, the position shrinks on its own, so that every position risks about the same fraction of your money. Total heat across all open stops is capped at 12.0% of net worth, and the five positions below together use 1.91% of it.

Worked example: sizing a new position in LAURUSLABS. The entry zone is 1,941 to 1,955, so the planning price is the top of the zone, 1,955. The stop is 1,888.35, which is 66.65 below, a distance of 3.41% or 341 basis points. A Tier 3 name risks 50 basis points, so the risk-based weight is 50 divided by 341, which is 14.7% of net worth. The tier ceiling is 3.5%, so the ceiling binds and the weight is 3.5%. That is ₹146,852, or 75 shares at 1,955, costing ₹146,625. If the stop is hit, the loss is 75 x 66.65 = ₹4,999, which is only 0.12% of net worth, well under the 0.50% budget, because the ceiling bound first. The reward at the record's target of 2,101.07 is 146.07 per share, so reward-to-risk is 146.07 divided by 66.65, or 2.19, which clears the Core floor of 2.0.

Chapter 4: Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

The funnel is the sequence of tests every shortlisted name must pass before it can be called a buy. A beginner can think of it as three doors in a row. The first door is financial: is the business solvent, honest and able to turn profit into cash? A name that fails door one is rejected, whatever the chart says; a name that passes door one but fails door two or three is not rejected, it waits, because the technical routing rule separates the quality of a business from the timing of an entry. 4.2 The three-condition swap test for every sale

A swap means selling one stock to buy another, and it is the most dangerous kind of trade because it pays two sets of costs and relies on a forecast about both stocks. 6.4 What to do when an order does not fill or the price gaps

Three things go wrong with orders: they do not fill, they fill at a worse price than planned, and they fill partly. A limit order does not fill when the price never reaches your limit. For a stop-broken name, switch to market after the opening hour, as set out above. For red-line names, leave the limit for the session and re-enter it the next day. A gap is a different problem: the market opens through your level, and the order fills at the first price available, which may be well below the limit you thought you had. Nothing can be done about a gap except to have placed the sale earlier. A partial fill is normal for thin names, and the rule is simple: sell what fills, repeat the order for the balance next session, and stop after three sessions to reassess.

Glossary

  • Moving average: The average closing price over a set number of days; the 50-day average is about ten weeks of prices and the 200-day about ten months.
  • Stage 1 to Stage 4: The four seasons of a stock: base (Stage 1), advance (Stage 2), topping (Stage 3) and decline (Stage 4).
  • RSI: A speedometer from 0 to 100 for how fast a stock has risen or fallen; under 30 is called oversold, over 70 overbought.
  • ATR: The stock's normal daily range in rupees; stops are set at least one ATR away from the price so that ordinary noise does not trigger them.
  • Stop or stop-loss: A price decided in advance at which you sell; here it is a rule evaluated on the daily close and executed at the next open.
  • GTT: Good-till-triggered: a standing order at Zerodha that fires when the price reaches a trigger level and then places a limit order.
  • Limit order: An order that fills only at your price or better; a market order fills at whatever price is available.
  • Reward-to-risk: The possible gain to the target divided by the possible loss to the stop; Core Alpha needs at least 2.0 and Tactical 2.5.
  • Risk-unit sizing: Sizing a position so that hitting the stop costs a fixed share of net worth, capped by a tier ceiling.
  • Beta: How strongly a stock has moved with the index; a beta of 1.5 means a 1% index fall has usually cost the stock 1.5%.
  • Effective market exposure: The share of your net worth that moves with the index after adjusting each holding for its beta.
  • Distribution day: A session when the index falls on heavier trading than the day before, a sign of large sellers.
  • Cash conversion (CFO to profit): Operating cash flow divided by reported profit; under 0.25 over three years is a hard red line.
  • Pledge: Shares promoters have offered as security for loans; 20% or more of their holding is a red line.
  • Let-Winners-Run rule: A Stage 2 winner with healthy fundamentals may grow to 22% of net worth while its stop trails.
  • Lower circuit: The maximum fall an exchange allows in a day for a stock; RNITAI is in a 2% band.
  • Dry powder: Cash held to deploy when conditions improve.
  • Regime: The market's overall trend label: Uptrend, Correction or Downtrend, set from the index, breadth and distribution days.
