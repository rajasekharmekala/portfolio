Ten ideas carry the report. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line drawn through a jittery signal; the 50-day line summarises about ten weeks of mood and the 200-day line about ten months. A Stage is one of four seasons in a share's life: Stage 1 is a flat winter base, Stage 2 is the climb, Stage 3 is a topping autumn and Stage 4 is a falling winter. The RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100: below 30 the price has fallen unusually fast, above 70 it has risen unusually fast. The ATR (average true range) is a share's normal daily wobble in rupees and is used to place stops so ordinary noise does not shake you out. Volume counts how many shares changed hands, which is how many people voted with their money. A stop-loss is an exit price decided in advance, written down before emotion arrives. Reward-to-risk divides what you can win by what you can lose, and the floor here is 2.0 for Core names and 2.5 for Tactical names. Position size is how much of your money one idea may hold, which is why no single share should hurt you badly. The regime is the market's season as a whole: today the ledger names it Downtrend.

Every verdict carries a label and a rule. EXIT sells the whole position and is triggered by a closing price under the written stop, by a failed hard red line (a promoter pledge of 20% or more, three-year cash conversion under 0.25, debt to equity of 1.20 or more, or a Stage 4 chart with no base), or by a failed second test described in Chapter 4. Core means a long-term compounder bucket (30 to 40% of net worth) and Tactical means a shorter-term trend bucket (50 to 65%). 3.1 The Risk-Unit Sizing arithmetic

Risk-Unit Sizing answers a simple question: how much of your net worth are you willing to lose if this one idea is wrong? The scoring file gives each conviction tier a budget in basis points of net worth (a basis point is one hundredth of a percent): Tier 1 is 100, Tier 2 is 75, Tier 3 is 50, Tier 4 is 35. The weight equals the budget divided by the stop distance, expressed as a percentage of the entry price, and is then capped at the tier's maximum notional weight (Tier 3 is capped at 3.5%). A tight stop lets the cap bind, so the position is the maximum for its tier. A wide stop makes the formula bind, so the position shrinks until a stop-out costs exactly the budget. Total heat, the sum of the losses if every stop were hit, is capped at 12.0% of net worth.

Worked example: two stops, two sizes. HAL has an entry zone topping at 4,620.21 and a stop at 4,431.11, a distance of 4.09%, or 409 basis points. A Tier 3 budget of 50 basis points divided by 409 gives 12.2%, but the tier cap is 3.5%, so the weight is 3.5%: ₹150,409 of your ₹4,297,413, which buys 32 shares at the zone top. If the stop is hit, you lose 0.143% of net worth, about 14.3 basis points, well inside the 50-basis-point budget. Now take BETA, a name that is not bought today but shows the formula at work: its entry zone tops at 1,833.00 and its stop sits at 1,548.15, a distance of 15.54%, or 1554 basis points. The budget of 50 divided by 1554 gives 3.22%, which is below the 3.5% cap, so the formula binds and the weight is 3.22%. A stop-out then costs 0.50% of net worth, exactly the budget. The two names show the rule in both directions: a tight stop earns the full tier size, a wide stop earns less.

Chapter 4: Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

A funnel is a sequence of filters that each remove names, and the shortlist is the narrow end of one. Every company in the pipeline passes through three gates before it can receive money. The financial gate asks whether the business is solvent, honest and earning real cash: it checks debt, interest cover, promoter pledges and three-year cash conversion, and a failure of any hard red line routes the name to REJECTED. The technical gate asks whether the chart is in a state that rewards a buyer: a Stage 4 breakdown with no base fails, but a Stage 3 pullback or a Stage 1 base is only a reason to wait. The asymmetry gate asks whether the reward is large enough for the risk: at least 2.0 times for Core names and 2.5 times for Tactical names, measured at the price where an order would fill. A name that passes the financial gate and the asymmetry floor but whose price is not yet at support is labelled PASSED_CONDITIONAL and routed to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT with a written limit order, not rejected.

This chapter does two jobs. It shows all 17 shortlisted challengers through the three gates, with one name that passes and one that fails each gate spelled out. 4.2 The three-condition swap test for every sale

A swap is the only reason to sell a holding and buy something else on the same day. The test has three conditions, and all three must hold. Condition 1: the holding triggered an exit or a trim. Condition 2: the challenger passed every gate and is strictly AT_SUPPORT, meaning the close is no more than 0.5 ATR above the zone top. Condition 3: the challenger's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's, after tax and friction. The rule exists to stop two kinds of mistake: selling a holding only because something shinier appeared, and paying tax and charges to rotate into a worse trade. Each of the twelve sales satisfies Condition 1 by construction, because that is why it is on the sell list. The interesting conditions are the other two.

Glossary

The glossary explains the terms used in this report in the order a beginner is likely to meet them.

  • Moving average (50-day, 200-day). The average closing price over the last 50 or 200 trading days; a trend line that smooths daily noise.
  • Stage 1 to 4. The four seasons of a share: a flat base (1), a rising advance (2), a topping phase (3) and a falling decline (4); Stage 4 with no base is a hard red line.
  • RSI. Relative strength index, a speedometer from 0 to 100; under 30 means the price fell unusually fast, over 70 means it rose unusually fast.
  • ATR. Average true range: the stock's normal daily wobble in rupees, used to place stops and to size a gap.
  • Beta. How strongly a stock has moved with the index over a year; a beta of 1.5 means a 1% index fall has usually cost the stock 1.5%.
  • Effective market exposure. Weight times beta, summed across the book; the share of net worth that behaves like the index.
  • Distribution day. A session when the index fell on heavier trading than the day before, a sign of large sellers.
  • Stop-loss and closing stop. A price decided in advance at which a position is sold; here a close at or under the stop sells at the next open.
  • GTT. Good Till Triggered order: a standing instruction at the broker that places a sale when the price reaches the trigger.
  • Limit order. An order to buy at your price or lower, or sell at your price or higher, never at the market.
  • Reward-to-risk (asymmetry). The distance from the entry to the target divided by the distance from the entry to the stop; the floor is 2.0 for Core and 2.5 for Tactical.
  • Hard red line. A binary disqualifier: promoter pledge of 20% or more, three-year cash conversion under 0.25, debt to equity of 1.20 or more, or Stage 4 with no base.
  • Cash conversion (CFO to profit). Operating cash flow divided by reported profit; a low figure means the profit has not turned into cash.
  • ROCE. Return on capital employed: profit earned for each rupee of capital the business uses.
  • Promoter pledge. Shares that the owners of a company have put up as collateral for loans; a forced sale of pledged shares can drive the price down.
  • Risk-Unit Sizing. Weight equals the risk budget in basis points divided by the stop distance, capped at the tier maximum, so wider stops earn smaller positions.
  • Let-Winners-Run. A Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals and rising estimates is not trimmed only for size or RSI until it reaches 22% of net worth.
  • Heat. The sum of the losses, as a share of net worth, if every stop were hit; the cap is 12.0%.
  • PCR and max pain. The put-call ratio divides outstanding put options by call options; max pain is the strike where option sellers pay least at expiry.
  • Conditional buy. A purchase prepared with a zone, stop and target but switched off until a market-wide test and the name's own conditions are met.
