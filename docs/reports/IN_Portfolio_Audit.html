Start here: what this audit is and how to read it

This is an audit of the shares you own in your Zerodha account. It asks three questions. What is the market doing? Is each holding still earning its place? And if you held only cash today, what would you buy? It is a research briefing and not an order. It does not know your tax lots, your goals or your need for cash. With ten minutes, read the key figures, the lede below and Chapter 6, which lists every order. With an hour, read the chapters in order, because each one leans on the one before.

The audit combines three independent readings and one editor. The first reads the market and says how much risk the written rules allow. The second audits your holdings one by one and has no loyalty to anything you already own. The third is a greenfield reading that is told nothing about your holdings, your cost or your profit, and must say what it would buy with ₹4,506,726 in cash. The editor lays the three side by side. The greenfield reading is blind on purpose: an owner defends what he owns, and a blind picker shows what the shortlist is worth on its own merits.

The labels carry exact triggers. EXIT means sell everything, and it fires when a hard red line is broken and no written exception applies, such as a promoter pledge above 20% of the promoter's shares. TRIM means sell part, and it fires when a position is above the size its conviction tier allows while a rule on that holding also fails. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep the shares and sell all of them if a named closing price breaks. HOLD / TRAIL means keep the shares and lift the stop as the price rises. NO TRADE / WATCH means the position is too small to act on. Stops sit at the lower of the recent swing low and a moving average, less one daily range for core holdings and half a range for tactical ones. The one trim that no rule forces is SEDEMAC, which is a discretionary risk-budget cut that the audit labels as such. A challenger replaces a holding only if the holding has triggered a sale, the challenger passed every gate and sits within half a daily range of support, and its reward-to-risk beats the holding's by 1.5 times. Reward-to-risk has to be 3.0 or better for a core holding and 3.5 or better for a tactical one.

A handful of ideas carry the whole report, and each has an everyday comparison. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line drawn through a bumpy road. The Stage system sorts every stock into four seasons: Stage 1 is a base after a fall, Stage 2 is a rising markup, Stage 3 is a tired top or pullback and Stage 4 is a markdown. RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer for price: below 30 the stock has fallen unusually fast and above 70 it has risen unusually fast. ATR (average true range) is a stock's normal daily wobble, which is why a stop placed closer than half an ATR is usually knocked out by noise. Volume is how many shares changed hands, which is how many people voted with money. A stop loss is an exit price decided in advance so that fear does not decide it later. Reward-to-risk divides what you can win by what you can lose. Risk-unit sizing sets a position's size from the loss you accept if the stop is hit, so a wide stop earns a smaller position, like buying a smaller car when the road ahead is icy. Effective market exposure says how much of a Nifty move your book really feels once each stock's beta is counted. Hidden overlap asks whether two holdings are secretly one bet because they rise and fall together.

Eight more terms recur in the case notes. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the operating profit a business earns on the money invested in it, like the rent a landlord earns on the cost of a building. EPS (earnings per share) is the company's profit divided by its share count. Operating margin is the share of each rupee of sales left after the costs of running the business, and net debt is borrowings minus cash, so a company with more cash than debt has none. Working capital is the cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills, and cash flow from operations is the cash the business actually collected. A base is a flat stretch of trading after a fall, like a landing on a staircase, and a breakout is a move above a price the share could not pass before. An entry zone is the band of prices where you would be willing to buy, and a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better. Asymmetry is reward-to-risk seen as a shape: a bet whose possible gain is much larger than its possible loss.

A red line is a binary fault, such as heavy debt, a heavy pledge or profit that never turns into cash, and it overrides the chart. Chapter 2: Active Portfolio Health and Concentration Risk Audit

Concentration is the quiet way a good portfolio becomes a fragile one. When one share is a fifth of your money, that share decides your year, whatever the other eighteen do. The rules set two ceilings. The standard cap is 15.0% of net worth for any single stock, and a proven Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals may run to 22.0% before it is cut, because selling a leader mechanically on a number is how investors lose the few shares that matter. Your largest holding, RNITAI, is 23.25% of net worth, above both ceilings, and the second, SEDEMAC, is 14.21%. Together they are 37.47% of the portfolio, and the top three holdings, which add INTERARCH at 8.71%, are 46.17%.

Concentration is not the only fault the audit looks for. A holding that breaks one is routed to a sale even if its chart looks fine, unless a written exception applies. Holdings that pass every red line are then tested against softer floors and against their own stop. 2.2 The trim arithmetic: how the size of each sale is set

A trim has two numbers, how much to sell and where to stop. The size comes from the ceilings above and from risk-unit sizing, which asks how much of net worth you would lose if the stop were hit. For a holding that is too large, the first test is the weight cap. For a holding inside the cap but fragile, the second test is the loss to the stop against the risk budget for its tier: 100, 75, 50 or 35 basis points of net worth for Tiers 1 to 4, which is 1.00%, 0.75%, 0.50% or 0.35%. A wider stop means a larger loss per share, so the same budget allows fewer shares. The tier also caps the weight: 10.0%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2.0%. For a holding the cap works as a trim trigger only when a rule on that holding also fails, such as a red line or a reward-to-risk floor; a share that passes every test and is slightly over its tier ceiling, as SANGHVIMOV is, is held.

Worked example. Trimming RNITAI. RNITAI closed at 104.80. You hold 10,000 shares worth ₹1,048,000, which is 23.25% of ₹4,506,725.57. The cap test: 15.0% of net worth is ₹676,009, which buys 6,450 shares at the close, so the minimum sale to reach the cap is 3,550 shares. The plan sells 4,000 and keeps 6,000, worth ₹628,800 or 13.95%, which leaves a one-point buffer so a 7% rise does not put the position back over the cap. The risk test: the stop is 85.20, which is 18.7% under the close, so the 6,000 kept shares would lose ₹117,600, or 2.61% of net worth, if the stop were hit, against ₹196,000 (4.35%) for all 10,000. Liquidity matters too. The ledger shows 16,426 shares traded on Monday, so 4,000 shares is 24% of that day's volume. Work the sale in limit orders between 104.80 and 106.62 across several sessions. The formula size for RNITAI at Monday's price is 2.67% of net worth, so the 13.95% that remains is about 5.2 times that size. The loss budget alone would allow 1,149 shares (2.67% of net worth), so the 6,000 kept are an exception and not a target. They are kept for two reasons: the cap is met and a faster sale in a stock this thin would cost an unknown amount in price impact. The exception has a date. Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine that all of your money, ₹4,506,726, were in cash this morning, and that you knew nothing about what you paid for anything. What would you buy from the shortlist, and how much of each? That question is the greenfield exercise, and its value lies in what it removes. A real owner is anchored to his cost, to the gain he hates to give back and to the loss he hopes to recover. None of those facts is a reason to own a share tomorrow. The third reading, the greenfield picker, is given only prices, gates, stops and targets, and is asked to choose six to twelve high-conviction ideas at support or to buy nothing.

Two rules shape its answer. A candidate must pass all three gates (the financial gate, the technical gate and the reward-to-risk gate), and it must sit within half an average daily range of support for a full entry. In a Downtrend the regime table also says no new momentum buys. Chapter 1 put the market in a Downtrend, so the bar is high from the start. The shortlist that the picker reads is the vetted list in the shortlist record. It holds 18 names, all of which you happen to own, and the picker reads a pipeline of underwritten watchlist names beside it. It starts from zero.

Zero-Holding-Bias Framework: this allocation is built from prices, gates, stops and targets only, with no use of incumbent holdings, cost basis or profit. 3.2 How risk-unit sizing would size a purchase

Risk-unit sizing answers a simple question: how many shares can I buy so that, if the stop is hit, I lose no more than a fixed slice of net worth? Each conviction tier has a risk budget: Tier 1 is 100 basis points (1.00% of net worth), Tier 2 is 75, Tier 3 is 50 and Tier 4 is 35, while Tier 0 gets nothing. The weight is the budget divided by the percentage distance from entry to stop, capped by the tier's own maximum: Tier 1 up to 10%, Tier 2 up to 6.5%, Tier 3 up to 3.5% and Tier 4 up to 2.0%. A wide stop cuts the size automatically. Total heat across all stops is capped at 12.0% of net worth. The sizing applies to new money, and for holdings it serves as a yardstick of how big the position should have been.

Chapter 4: Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

The funnel is the audit's quality filter, and it is the same one for a share you own and for a share you might buy. A candidate passes three gates in order. The financial gate asks whether the business is solvent and turns profit into cash, using the four red lines and the floors for returns and debt. The technical gate asks whether the chart is in a Stage 2 markup, or entering one from a base, with a price near support. The asymmetry gate asks whether the target is at least 3.0 times the distance to the stop for a Core Alpha name, or 3.5 times for a Tactical Momentum name. A candidate that passes all three and sits within half an ATR of support may replace a holding that has triggered a sale. Everything else waits, or is rejected.

Two examples show how the gates behave. LAURUSLABS passes the financial gate with room to spare (cash conversion 2.05, debt to equity 0.45) and passes the technical gate (a Stage 2 chart above its rising averages), but its target of 2,162.40 sits only 93.40 rupees over Monday's close of 2,069.00, so the asymmetry gate gives 0.13 at the formula stop against 3.0 and it fails there. TARIL fails the first gate and nothing after it matters: a 21.84% promoter pledge against a 20% line stops the funnel regardless of its chart or its strong September quarter. The first example says a good company and a good chart can still be a poor purchase at a given price. The second says a good chart does not outrank a red line. 4.3 Friction: tax, brokerage and slippage

Every sale costs something besides the lost holding. Brokerage and exchange charges are small for delivery trades at the sizes here. The depository fee on each delivery sale is a flat amount per share line, which matters for the two dust positions and is the reason they carry no order; its exact amount is not in this run's research record and is therefore left out and not guessed. Slippage matters more: the 4,000-share RNITAI sale is 24% of Monday's volume, so the audit works it in limit orders over several sessions and accepts that some shares will sell lower than the close. Tax is the largest unknown. Gains and losses depend on the buy dates and the holding period of each lot, and the audit's inputs summarise cost per holding and do not carry the lot-level tax calculation. Profit-taking sales (RNITAI, SEDEMAC, EPACKPEB, TARIL) will realise gains and the seven loss-making sales will realise losses that may be set off against them, but the size of either, and the tax rate that applies, is outside this run's evidence.

The effect of that unknown on the advice is limited and directional. The sales that matter most are the red-line cases, TARIL and ORIANA-SM, and the size trims in RNITAI, and in each the rule fires on risk, not on after-tax return. If tax were large enough to change the answer, it would argue for completing the loss-making sales first, because their losses reduce the tax on the gains. Chapter 6 therefore sequences the sales so that red-line exits and loss-making trims come before the profit-taking ones where the calendar allows, and it does so without assuming a tax rate.

Friction cannot change a sale that a red line requires. It can only change how the sale is worked. The last subsection of this chapter tests whether the market report's three setups change the picture.

Chapter 7: Rulebook and Monitoring

A plan decays if nobody watches it. Prices move, filings arrive and the market regime changes, and a decision that was right on Monday's close can be wrong on the following Friday. This chapter collects the rules the audit applied, with today's reading beside each one, so that you can re-run the logic yourself between audits. It then lists the dates and levels that would change the plan, names the biggest risks and unknowns honestly, and says how often to repeat the audit. The aim is that a beginner who has read the earlier chapters can open this one alone, find the rule that matters, and see at a glance whether the portfolio is inside it.

Two habits make the rulebook useful. The first is to check each rule against a number and not against a feeling: the question is never whether a share seems expensive but whether its reward-to-risk is above 3.0 or 3.5. The second is to separate the decision from the order. The audit decides what the book should look like, and Chapter 6 places the orders that move it there. When the two drift apart, because an order did not fill or a price moved, the rules below say which one to follow, and the answer is always the decision.

7.3 The biggest risks and unknowns

Five risks matter most. The first is execution: the RNITAI sale is 24% of Monday's volume, and a thin market can move against a seller faster than the limit band allows. The second is the holdings record itself: the audit assumes the transaction log is accurate and its last entry is dated 10 October 2026, so a trade made since then would not appear here. The third is the evidence that is missing: ROBU has no underwriting record, the BLISSGVS pledge of 95.8% comes from the underwriting record and not from a filing on this run's record, and the June 2026 shareholding file is the latest on the exchange for four holdings. The fourth is the data: eleven sector indices were requested and two returned usable history, so the sector map leans on the market report's industry breadth table. The fifth is the market: a Downtrend can end with a sharp rally that leaves a high-cash book behind, and the cost of being early is real. The plan accepts that cost as cheaper than the alternative.

The unknowns shape the advice in one direction. Where evidence is missing, the audit chose the smaller position and the earlier review, because the downside of being wrong about a hidden problem is larger than the upside of being right about a hidden strength. Tax and lot-level cost are also unknown, and the audit does not guess them. If any of these unknowns resolves, the plan should be re-read: a filing that clears a pledge, a results print that turns cash flow positive, or a rally that lifts breadth above 40% each argues for buying back exposure, not for holding the same cash.

7.4 How often to rerun the audit

Rerun the audit after each major results cluster and whenever the regime label changes. In practice that means once the half-year statements are in, around 16 November, and again if the Nifty 50 closes above 23,514.27 for three sessions or below 22,179.90 at any time. Between audits, check the stops each Friday and the dated triggers in the case notes. A weekly look is enough for a book of this size, and a daily look is a way to act on noise. Each rerun should start from fresh prices and a fresh research file and should not reuse numbers from this report, because every figure here is dated 19 October 2026. The reason to wait for those moments and not to rerun daily is that the inputs the rules depend on, cash flow statements and shareholding filings, change only when companies publish, and the price rules already run through the stops in Chapter 6.

ATR (average true range)
The normal daily price range of a share in rupees. Stops are placed in multiples of it so that ordinary noise does not trigger them.
Beta
How much a share has moved for each 1% move in the Nifty 50 over the last year. A beta of 1.5 means about 1.5% for each 1%.
Cash conversion (CFO to profit)
Operating cash flow over three years divided by reported profit over the same years. Below 0.40 is a red line in this audit.
Distribution day
A down day on rising volume, read as large holders selling. The ledger counts them over the last 25 sessions.
Effective market exposure
The sum of each holding's weight times its beta: how much of a Nifty move the book really feels.
GTT (good till triggered)
A standing order at the broker that sells or buys when the traded price touches a trigger level.
Heat (portfolio heat)
The total loss if every stop were hit, as a share of net worth. The ceiling is 12.0%.
Hidden overlap
Holdings whose daily returns move together so closely (correlation 0.65 or more) that they act as one bet.
Let-Winners-Run rule
A Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals is not trimmed for RSI or weight alone, up to a weight of 22.0%.
Moving average (20-, 50-, 200-day)
The average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 trading days, used as a trend line.
Promoter pledge
Shares that the controlling holders have put up as security for a loan. At 20% or more of their holding, it is a red line.
Red line
A binary fault (debt, pledge, cash conversion or a Stage 4 breakdown) that overrides the chart and routes a holding to a sale.
Reward-to-risk (asymmetry)
The potential gain to the target divided by the potential loss to the stop. The floors are 3.0 for Core Alpha and 3.5 for Tactical Momentum.
Risk-unit sizing
Setting a position's size so that a hit stop costs a fixed slice of net worth, capped by the conviction tier.
RSI (relative strength index)
A speedometer for price from 0 to 100. Below 30 is oversold and above 70 is stretched.
Stage 1 to 4
The four seasons of a share: base, markup, top, markdown.
Stop loss
A price chosen in advance at which you leave a position so that a wrong idea costs a known amount.
Swing low
The lowest price of the last 20 sessions, used as a stop anchor.
