Start here: what this audit is and how to read it

This is an audit of the 19 shares you own in your Zerodha account. It asks three questions. What is the market doing? Does each holding still earn its place under written rules? With an hour, read the chapters in order, because each one leans on the one before.

Three independent readings sit behind the numbers, and an editor lays them side by side. The first reads the market and says how much risk the rules allow. The second audits your holdings one by one and owes no loyalty to any of them. The third is the greenfield reading. It is told nothing about what you own, what you paid or what you have earned, and it must say what it would buy with ₹4,312,005.92 in cash. That blindness is deliberate. An owner defends what he already holds, and a blind picker shows what the shortlist is worth on its own merits. When the two disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

Every holding gets one of five labels, and each label has an exact trigger. EXIT means sell every share. It fires when a hard red line of the incurable kind is broken (heavy debt or a pledged promoter stake), or when a closing price falls under a stop that was written down in advance. TRIM means sell part. It fires when a position is larger than its conviction tier allows while at least one rule on that company also fails, and it is also the label for a red line of the curable kind (weak cash conversion or a Stage 4 chart), which leads to a cut down to the tier ceiling with a dated test. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep the shares and sell all of them if a named closing price breaks. HOLD / TRAIL means keep the shares and lift the stop as the price rises. NO TRADE / WATCH means the position is too small to act on. A closing stop in an underwriting record binds the holding, and a stop only moves up: it is never lowered because the price fell. Where no record exists, stops follow a formula. For core holdings the stop sits at the lower of the recent swing low and the 50-day or 200-day average, minus one ATR (average true range); for tactical holdings it sits at the lower of the swing low and the 20-day or 50-day average, minus half an ATR.

Size is governed by three rules. No single stock may exceed 15% of net worth. A proven winner may run up to a runaway cap of 22%, and the Let-Winners-Run rule protects it from a mechanical sale only while the chart is in Stage 2, the fundamentals are intact and analyst estimates are stable or rising. Past 22%, or with a red line broken, the rule gives no shelter. Risk-Unit Sizing then asks how much of net worth you would lose if the stop were hit: weight equals the lower of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the distance to the stop. The budgets are 100, 75, 50 and 35 basis points for Tiers 1 to 4, with ceilings of 10%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2.0%. The portfolio's total loss if every stop hit is capped at 12% of net worth. A challenger replaces a holding only if it passes a three-condition swap test: the holding has triggered an exit or trim, the challenger has passed every gate and sits within half an ATR of support, and its reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's. Reward-to-risk must reach 3.0 for a core name and 3.5 for a tactical one.

Four red lines override any chart. Debt to equity at or above 1.20, or interest cover under 2.0. A promoter pledge at or above 20% of the promoter's shares. Three-year operating cash flow under 40% of profit (CFO/PAT under 0.40) without a verified two-quarter turnaround. And a Stage 4 breakdown with no base under it, a base being a flat floor that a falling share builds before a possible advance. In a Downtrend the posture is maximum cash, cuts to weak or Stage 4 names and no new momentum purchases. Tax is shown as N/A throughout, because lot-level data is not in the inputs and a guessed rate would mislead.

A handful of ideas carry the whole report, and each has an everyday comparison. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line drawn through a bumpy road. Stage 1 is a base after a fall, like a landing on a staircase. Stage 2 is the rising markup, a growing season. Stage 3 is a tired top or a pullback, late summer. Stage 4 is a markdown, winter. RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer for price: below 30 means a stock has fallen unusually fast and above 70 means it has risen unusually fast. ATR is a stock's normal daily wobble in rupees, which is why a stop tighter than half an ATR is usually knocked out by noise. Volume is how many shares changed hands, which is how many people voted with money. A stop-loss is an exit price chosen in advance so that fear does not choose it later. Reward-to-risk divides what you can win by what you can lose. Position size is the number of shares you hold, chosen so that a stopped-out trade costs a small, fixed slice of your wealth.

Two portfolio-level ideas matter just as much. Beta is how far a share has tended to swing when the Nifty 50 moves 1%, and effective market exposure adds up each holding's weight times its beta to show how much of the index's move your book really feels, like a boat whose pull in the current depends on how much of its hull is below the water. Hidden overlap asks whether two holdings are secretly one bet because they rise and fall together, like two cars bought from the same maker with the same fault. Two quality measures recur in the case notes. ROCE, return on capital employed, is the operating profit earned on the money invested in the business, like the rent a landlord earns on the cost of a building. CFO/PAT divides the cash a business collected from operations by its accounting profit, so a figure near 1.0 means profit turns into cash and a figure near 0.1 means the profit is mostly promises on paper. Every number in this audit comes from the 22 October ledger, the shortlist records or the dated research file, and every verdict names the rule that produced it and the price that would change it.

Ten more terms recur in the notes. A breakout is a move above the highest recent price, usually on heavy volume. An entry zone is the price range in which a buy would be considered. A limit order is an order to buy or sell only at a chosen price or better. Asymmetry is the same idea as reward-to-risk: how much a position can gain relative to how much it can lose. The stop loss is the exit price if the trade is wrong. Operating margin is the share of revenue left after operating costs. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is profit before financing and accounting charges. EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares. Net debt is borrowings minus cash.

1.3 Which holdings move in lockstep: hidden overlap

The positions file tests this by correlating the daily returns of every pair of holdings over the past year. 2.2 The trim arithmetic: how the size of each sale is set

A trim is a partial sale, and its size is not a mood. Every trim in this plan comes out of a short calculation with three inputs: a cap on the position's share of net worth, a limit on how much money you would lose if the stop were hit, and the real cost of selling in a thin market. The reason to show the working is that a number you cannot reproduce is a number you cannot trust. Two examples follow, one for each reason a trim can exist. RNITAI is a cap trim, forced by the rules. SEDEMAC is a risk-budget trim, and it is labelled discretionary because no red line forces it. After the two examples, a third paragraph explains how the sizes of the smaller trims come from the tier ceilings.

Worked example: RNITAI, a rule-forced cap trim

Step 1, the cap: 15% of your net worth of ₹4,312,005.92 is ₹646,801. At the close of 100.70 that buys 6,423 shares (rounded down). You hold 10,000, so the minimum sale is 3,577 shares. Step 2, the chosen size: the plan sells 4,000 and keeps 6,000. That is 423 shares beyond the minimum, and it leaves 6,000 × 100.70 = ₹604,200, which is 14.01% of net worth. RNITAI would need to rise about 7.1% (to about 107.80) before it crossed 15% again.

Step 3, the risk test: the stop is 85.14, which is 15.56 below the close, or 15.5%. On 10,000 shares the loss to the stop would be ₹155,600 (3.61% of net worth). On 6,000 shares it is ₹93,360 (2.17%). The tier-3 budget is 0.50% of net worth, ₹21,560, so even after the trim the loss is about 4.3 times the budget and the 6,000 is an exception, not a clean fit. Step 4, liquidity: only 3,012 shares traded on 22 October, but the 20-day average is about 100,400 a day. Selling 4,000 is about 4% of an average day, so the sale is worked over three sessions, 1,500 then 1,500 then 1,000, with limit sells at 99.50 or better. Step 5, the date: the exception for the 6,000 shares ends at the close of 16 November, two sessions after the half-year results are due by 14 November. Why keep 6,000 shares at all, when the risk-unit size at today's close is 3.24% of net worth, about 1,385 shares? 2.4 Holdings forensic health matrix

The forensic matrix puts business quality and chart quality side by side, because a stock can fail on either and the cure differs. The business side uses three ratios. ROCE is the profit a company earns on all the money put into it, like the yield on a rental flat after counting every rupee spent on it. D/E is debt to equity, the borrowing for each rupee of the owners' own money, and the red line sits at 1.20 because a company that owes more than that is easily hurt by a bad year. CFO to profit compares the cash the business collects from operations with the profit it reports, averaged over three years. Think of a shop whose accounts show a profit but whose till stays empty: a ratio under 0.40 is that shop. Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine that tomorrow morning every share in your account has been sold, and the only thing left is ₹4,312,005.92 in cash. You do not remember what you paid for anything. You do not remember which stock made you proud or which one embarrassed you. The question is simple: which businesses would you buy today, at today's prices, under the rules written in this report? That is the greenfield test. The word comes from builders, who call an empty plot a greenfield, because nothing on it has to be knocked down before work begins.

The test matters because owning something bends your judgement. Psychologists call one version of this the sunk-cost trap: you keep a losing stock because selling would make the loss real, and you keep a winning stock because it feels disloyal to sell. Think of packing for a house move. If you must carry every item up three flights of stairs, you suddenly ask which things you would buy again, and the answer is rarely all of it. The greenfield test makes the same demand of your portfolio. A name that would not be bought on an empty plot has to justify why it deserves your capital while it sits there.

Every candidate must clear three gates, and the gates run in a fixed order. The financial gate asks whether the business is sound: debt, interest cover, promoter pledges and whether profit turns into cash. The technical gate asks whether the share price is in a confirmed uptrend rather than a slide or a base. The asymmetry gate asks whether the possible gain is at least 3.0 times the possible loss for a core name, or 3.5 times for a tactical name. On top of the gates sits the market regime. Today the regime is Downtrend, and the Downtrend rule says: hold the maximum cash, trim the weakest positions, and open no new momentum longs. That rule decides what the arithmetic is allowed to do. This chapter runs the empty-plot test, shows how a purchase would be sized if one were allowed, explains the price at which each rejected name would clear the floor, and then looks at the nearest candidates from outside the book.

3.2 How risk-unit sizing would size a purchase

Risk-unit sizing is the rule that decides how much money a single purchase may take. The idea is that you decide first how much of your net worth you are willing to lose if the stop is hit, and the position size follows from that. The formula is weight = min(tier cap, risk budget in basis points divided by stop distance in percent). A basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so 50 basis points is 0.50% of net worth. The tiers set both numbers: Tiers 1 to 4 carry budgets of 100, 75, 50 and 35 basis points and caps of 10.0%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2.0%. A wide stop forces a small position, a tight stop allows a bigger one, and the cap prevents any single name from growing past its tier.

Think of it as a seat-belt budget for a road trip. If you will accept losing half a rupee in every hundred you own, a stock whose stop is 15% below the price can take only about 3.3% of your money, because 3.3% falling 15% costs exactly 0.5%. A stock whose stop is 3% below the price could in theory take about 17%, but the 3.5% cap steps in first. Chapter 4: Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

A funnel is a sequence of filters, each one narrower than the last. A thousand names enter at the top and a handful come out at the bottom, and the point of the shape is that most ideas should die early and cheaply. The three-layer underwriting used by this fund puts the financial gate first, because a company that fails on debt, pledged shares or unreal profits should not get a chart-based hearing at all. The technical gate comes second, because a sound business in a falling stock is a bet on timing that the rules do not allow. The asymmetry gate comes last, because even a sound business in a rising chart is a poor purchase if the stop is far away and the target is near. Chapter 3 showed what comes out of the funnel when you start from cash. This chapter shows what happens to every name inside it, why the three-condition swap test blocks every replacement, and what acting would cost.

4.2 The three-condition swap test for every sale

A swap replaces one holding with another, and the rules allow it only when three conditions hold together. Condition 1 is that the holding has triggered an exit or a trim. Condition 2 is that the challenger has passed 100% of the gates and sits at support, within half an ATR of its structural low. Condition 3 is that the challenger's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's, so that the move clears the friction of tax and brokerage with room to spare. The three conditions are cumulative. A holding that looks weak is not enough, and a challenger that looks strong is not enough either. Fifteen holdings fire Condition 1 today, which makes this the first test of whether any challenger deserves their cash.

The only outside name with a completed underwriting record is SAGILITY, so it is the challenger in every row. 4.3 Friction: tax, brokerage and slippage

Acting has costs, and a plan that ignores them overstates what it delivers. Three costs matter: tax on realised gains, the broker's charges and slippage, the gap between the price you expect and the price you get. On the average-cost basis in the positions file, the 15 sales realise gains of ₹303,419 on the names sold above cost (RNITAI, SEDEMAC, TARIL and TANFACIND) and losses of ₹154,597 on the rest, for a net ₹148,822. Chapter 6: 3-Phase Actionable Execution Orders

An order is a plan reduced to something you can type into a trading screen: a name, a side, a quantity, a price and a time limit. The orders in this chapter are exactly the deltas in Chapter 5, nothing more. They come in three phases. Phase 1 turns the sales and trims into cash. Phase 2 lists the standing re-entry maps, which allocate no capital today and exist so that a purchase, if the market improves, follows a plan written in advance and not a mood. Phase 3 sets the defensive stops on every share that remains. The first session on which any order can fill is Friday 23 October, because the 22 October session has closed. 6.3 Phase 3: defensive GTT and trailing-stop architecture

7.4 How often to rerun the audit

Rerun the audit at three speeds. The fastest is the daily check of stops and dated events: after each close, compare the closing price of every kept position with its stop and look at the diary. That takes ten minutes and needs no report. The second is the weekly audit while the market is in a Downtrend or any dated test is near: rerun this workflow every week, so that a broken stop, a stage change or a new filing is reflected in the verdicts within days. This audit shows why. Between 21 and 22 October, one stop broke, two stages changed and several prices moved a long way, and each changed a verdict. The third speed is the full rerun after any event that changes a premise: a trade that changes the holdings log, a regime change in the market report, a results date, or a new red line. Slow the rhythm to every two or three weeks only when the regime is Uptrend and no tests are due. What this means for you: the audit is a habit and not a document, and the habit is cheap compared with the loss it avoids.

Glossary

ATR (average true range)
The typical daily price range of a share, in rupees. It sets how far a stop must sit to avoid being hit by ordinary noise.
Base
A flat consolidation floor after a fall, where a share stops dropping and builds a platform before a possible advance.
Beta
How far a share has tended to move when the Nifty 50 moves 1%. A beta of 1.5 means it moved 1.5% on average.
Cash conversion (CFO/PAT)
Operating cash flow divided by profit after tax, usually averaged over three years. Near 1.0 means profit turns into cash; under 0.40 is a red line.
Distribution day
A session on which an index falls on heavier volume than the day before, a sign of institutional selling.
Effective market exposure
The sum of each holding's weight times its beta, showing how much of an index move the whole book feels.
GTT (good till triggered)
A broker order that waits up to a year and fires when the price touches a trigger you set; used here as a stop-loss backstop.
Hidden overlap
Two or more holdings that rise and fall together, so owning both is one larger bet.
Let-Winners-Run rule
A rule that stops mechanical trimming of a Stage 2 winner with clean red lines and rising estimates, up to a 22% cap.
Limit order
An order to buy or sell only at a chosen price or better.
Moving average
The average closing price over the last N days; a trend line through a bumpy price series.
Portfolio heat
The sum of the rupee losses to every stop if all were hit together, as a share of net worth. The cap is 12%.
Red line
A hard disqualifier: debt to equity at or above 1.20, interest cover under 2.0, a promoter pledge at or above 20%, three-year cash conversion under 0.40, or a Stage 4 chart with no base.
Reward-to-risk (asymmetry)
The distance from the price to the target divided by the distance from the price to the stop. The floors are 3.0 for core and 3.5 for tactical names.
Risk-unit sizing
A rule that sets position size from the loss you accept if the stop is hit: weight equals the smaller of the tier cap and the risk budget divided by the stop distance.
ROCE (return on capital employed)
Operating profit divided by the capital put into the business.
RSI (relative strength index)
A 0-100 gauge of how fast a price has moved. Under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought.
Stage 1 to Stage 4
Weinstein's four phases: a base, an advance, a top and a decline.
Stop-loss
A price decided in advance at which you sell to cap a loss. In this report most stops apply to the closing price.
Swap test
A three-condition rule that allows one holding to be replaced by another only if the holding has failed, the challenger has passed every gate at support and its reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times higher.
Tier ceiling
The maximum weight of a position set by its conviction tier: 10%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2.0% for Tiers 1 to 4.
Volume multiple
Today's traded volume divided by its 20-day average. Above 1.5 is read as heavy trading.
