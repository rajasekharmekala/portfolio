Start here: how to read this audit

This is an audit of the Zerodha account: nineteen holdings worth about ₹46.9 lakh. It answers four questions in plain order. What kind of market are we in? What does each holding deserve right now? If you held only cash today, what would you buy? And what exact orders follow? You can read it in ten minutes by taking the key figures, the lede and the first table in Chapter 2. You can read it in an hour by taking everything, including the one-page note on each holding.

Three independent analysts produce the verdicts. The first judges the market and its sectors and knows nothing about what you own. The second judges every holding against fixed rules and knows your cost and profit. The third is the greenfield picker: it is told you have only cash and asks which shortlisted stocks deserve money. The picker is blind to your holdings on purpose, because people defend what they already own. If a stock you hold would not be bought today, that is information, not an insult.

Each label has a trigger. EXIT means sell the whole position, and it is used when a stock is in Stage 4 (a confirmed decline), when its fixed formula stop has been hit, or when a Stage 3 tactical stock also fails its financial gate on the shortlist record (this last route applies to AFCOM only). TRIM means sell part, usually half. It is used when a tactical stock has slipped to Stage 3 (a pullback below its 50 day average), when a core stock below its 50 day average misses a quality hurdle, or when a stock is bigger than the 15% single-name cap even though it is still in Stage 2 (an advance). HOLD / TRAIL means keep it and let a rising average pull the exit level upward. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep it but treat the formula stop as a hard line. NO TRADE / WATCH means the position is too small to matter. The stop formulas use the swing low, which is the lowest price of the last twenty sessions, so a stop sits below the point where buyers recently stepped in. The formulas are: Core Alpha stop = lowest of the recent swing low and the 50 and 200 day averages, minus one ATR (average true range, explained below); Tactical Momentum stop = lowest of the swing low and the 20 and 50 day averages, minus half an ATR.

Key words, each with an everyday comparison. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line drawn through a noisy week of temperatures. Stage 1 to 4 are the four seasons of a stock: basing (winter), markup (spring and summer), topping (autumn) and decline (late autumn into winter). RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100; below 30 means the stock fell fast, above 70 means it rose fast. ATR is the normal daily wobble in rupees, and we place stops in ATR units so a calm stock and a jumpy stock get fair room. Volume is the number of people who voted with money. Put-call ratio and option walls are a fear gauge and the ceilings and floors that option sellers create. A stop-loss is an exit decided in advance, while the market is calm. Risk and reward compares what you can win with what you can lose; this audit asks for 3.5 times for tactical ideas and 3.0 times for core ideas. Position size is how much of your net worth sits in one name, and the 15% cap exists so that no single story can hurt you badly.

Eight more terms appear later. ROCE (return on capital employed) is the operating profit a company earns for every rupee of capital in the business. A base is a sideways stretch of trading after a decline, where a stock rests before its next move. A pivot is the price at the top of that base, and a breakout is a close above it. A retest is a later pullback to the old pivot to see whether it now acts as a floor. The entry zone is the price range in which a buy order is allowed, and a limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better. A stop loss is the planned exit price. Asymmetry means the possible gain is several times the possible loss. Operating margin is the share of sales left as operating profit, and dilution means new shares are issued, so each existing share owns a smaller slice.

Chapter 1: Market and sector regime, and what they mean for this book

A regime is the market's weather. You do not argue with the weather; you decide what to wear. The ledger built for this audit labels the Indian market a Downtrend, which is the same label the market intelligence report for the same session printed. The label comes from three tests that do not depend on opinion: where the benchmark closes against its 50 and 200 day averages, how many stocks are above their own averages, and how often the index has fallen on rising volume. All three point down. The index closed at 22,520.45 after a 1.30% rise on Friday, which looks cheerful until you notice it is 5.1% below its 50 day average of 23,726.47 and 7.2% below its 200 day average of 24,271.24.

Why does this matter for a portfolio of Indian small and mid-caps? Because individual stocks borrow their direction from the market more than their owners like to admit. In a Downtrend, a stock that breaks below its own 50 day average has usually lost the buyers who were holding it up, and a bounce back to that average is more often a place to sell than a place to add. The risk posture that follows from the label is written in the audit standard: in a Downtrend the book carries maximum cash, trims Stage 4 holdings, and makes no new momentum purchases. That is the lens for every verdict in Chapter 2.

Chapter 2: Holdings audit, concentration and money at risk

Concentration is the quiet risk in a portfolio, and yours is concentrated in a way that does not show on a one-line summary. You own nineteen positions, which sounds diversified. The top three are RNIT AI at 23.4%, Sedemac at 13.8% and Interarch at 9.1%, together 46.2% of net worth, and the top eight are about 75%. The remaining eleven positions share the last 25%, and two of them are worth less than ₹1,000 each. In practice, you hold a handful of ideas, and the audit treats them that way.

Concentration matters for a plain reason: losses are not symmetric with gains. A stock that falls 50% needs to rise 100% to recover, and when the stock is a quarter of your wealth, that single fall takes an eighth of your net worth with it. The audit rules therefore set a single-name limit of 20%, with an exception for winners whose momentum is intact, and the scoring file sets a harder cap of 15%. RNIT AI breaches both numbers. The question this chapter answers is what to do about that, and about every other holding, using rules that were written before the prices were seen.

Worked example: how the 15% cap turns into a share count. Net worth is ₹4,689,841, so 15% is ₹703,476. At the close of ₹109.70, that buys 6,413 shares, and you hold 10,000, so the excess is 3,587 shares. 4.2 The three-condition swap test applied to every sale

A sale could be justified in two ways: because the holding is bad, or because a better stock is waiting. The swap rule demands all three of these conditions before a holding is replaced by a specific challenger: the holding must have triggered an exit or trim; the challenger must have passed every gate and sit at support within half an ATR; and the challenger's risk and reward must be at least 1.5 times the holding's, after friction. The test is a safeguard against churn, since each swap costs brokerage, taxes and the slippage between two imperfect prices. In this plan, no sale is justified by a swap; each sale stands on the first condition alone, and the proceeds go to cash.

Glossary

These terms are used throughout the report. Each is defined once here and, where it first matters, also in the text.

  • Cash conversion. Cash from operations divided by profit for the year. A figure below 70% means reported profit is not turning into cash, which fails the core quality test in this audit.
  • Promoter. The founding family or group that controls a listed company. A falling promoter stake means insiders are selling or being diluted, which is a mild caution.
  • Swing low. The lowest price in the last twenty sessions, used as the base of the stop formulas.
  • Tier 0 to Tier 4. Position-size bands in the scoring file: Tier 1 is 7 to 10% of net worth, Tier 2 is 4 to 6.5%, Tier 3 is 2 to 3.5%, Tier 4 is 1 to 2%, and Tier 0 is no position.
  • ATR (average true range). The typical daily trading range of a stock in rupees, averaged over 14 sessions. It is used to place stops so that normal wobble does not trigger them.
  • Asymmetry or risk and reward. The possible gain to the target divided by the possible loss to the stop. This audit asks for at least 3.5 for tactical names and 3.0 for core names.
  • Breadth. The share of stocks trading above a given average, such as their 50-day average. Low breadth means few stocks are participating in a rise.
  • Core Alpha and Tactical Momentum. The two investing buckets. Core is for high-quality companies held for one to three years or more; Tactical is for stocks in an advance (Stage 2) held for weeks or months.
  • Distribution day. A session when the index falls at least 0.2% on higher volume than the day before, a sign of institutions selling.
  • DMA (day moving average). The average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 sessions. Price above a rising average is the basic sign of an uptrend.
  • FII and DII. Foreign and domestic institutional investors. Their daily net buying or selling in the cash market is published provisionally by the exchange.
  • Formula stop. The exit level computed from rules: for core names, the lowest of the swing low and the 50 and 200-day averages less one ATR; for tactical names, the lowest of the swing low and the 20 and 50-day averages less half an ATR.
  • GTT (good till triggered). A Zerodha order that waits for a trigger price for up to a year and then places a limit order.
  • Greenfield picker. The analyst that allocates cash across the shortlist as if you held nothing, in order to remove bias towards what you own.
  • Momentum gate. Two tests in the scoring file. Test A asks whether a stock is in momentum now; Test B asks whether it is entering momentum near a base.
  • Option wall, put-call ratio, max pain. Strikes with heavy call or put open interest that act like a ceiling or a floor; the ratio of put to call open interest; and the strike where option buyers would lose the most at expiry.
  • ROCE. Return on capital employed: operating profit as a percentage of the capital used in the business.
  • RSI (relative strength index). A speedometer from 0 to 100 for how fast a price has moved; below 30 is called oversold, above 70 overbought.
  • Stage 1 to Stage 4. The four seasons of a stock in the Weinstein framework: basing, markup, topping or pullback, and markdown.
  • STCG and LTCG. Short-term and long-term capital gains tax on listed shares: 20% for holdings of twelve months or less, and 12.5% above a ₹1.25 lakh exemption for longer holdings, before cess.
  • Super-performer exception. The audit rule that a winner above the concentration limit is not trimmed while it stays above its rising 20-day average with strong relative strength and RSI below 82.
