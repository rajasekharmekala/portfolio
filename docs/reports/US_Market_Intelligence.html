Start here: how to read this report

This report is a weather forecast for the US stock market, written for someone who has never read one. It answers four questions in order. What kind of market is this? Which parts of it are strong and which are weak? Which individual companies are riding that strength or sliding with the weakness? And what, if anything, should a careful investor do about it? It does not tell you to buy or sell anything on its own. It shows the evidence and the rule behind each conclusion, so that you can judge the conclusion for yourself.

The report covers the session that closed on Friday 9 October 2026. The market was closed on the weekend, so the latest completed session with a verified closing price is Friday. Every price, average and indicator in the tables comes from one data file, the facts ledger, built for that date. The report repeats the ledger's regime label word for word. Reading the summary takes about ten minutes; reading everything takes about an hour.

Nine ideas carry the rest of the report. Each one comes with an everyday comparison.

The moving average is the average closing price over a fixed number of trading days. The 50-day average is the medium-term trend line, like the average water level of a river over the last ten weeks. The 200-day average is the long-term line. A price above both averages, with both pointing up, describes a healthy trend. A price below falling averages describes a stream running downhill.

Stage 1 to Stage 4 is a four-season model for a single stock. Stage 1 is the basing season, when the price moves sideways after a fall. Stage 2 is the markup season, when the price sits above its 50-day and 200-day averages and climbs. Stage 3 is the topping season, when momentum fades and the price wanders near its peak. Stage 4 is the decline, when the price sits below falling averages. The ledger assigns each stock one stage from its own price history, and the report never overrides that assignment.

RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer that runs from zero to one hundred. A reading above 70 means the stock has risen unusually fast and may pause. A reading below 30 means it has fallen unusually fast and may bounce. A fast bounce does not mean the trend has turned, so the report reads RSI as context rather than as a timing signal.

ATR, the average true range, is the normal daily wobble of a stock, measured in dollars. A stock with an ATR of 5 dollars that moves 20 dollars from its average is about four normal days away from home. Stops in this report are placed far enough away that a normal wobble does not trigger them. The stop is the exit decided before you buy, not after the price has fallen.

Volume is how many people voted with their money on a given day. The volume multiple divides today's volume by the 20-day average, so 1.5 means half as much again as usual. A breakout, a move above a previous high or the top of a sideways base, on heavy volume is more believable than one on light volume, which is why the screen asks for a multiple of 1.5 or more before it calls a breakout confirmed.

The put/call ratio divides the number of put options traded, which are bets on a fall or insurance against one, by the number of call options traded, which are bets on a rise. High readings mean fear and have often appeared near market lows. Low readings mean complacency and have often appeared near highs. Option walls are strikes where many option contracts are concentrated. Dealers who sell those options hedge in ways that can slow a rally near a call wall or cushion a fall near a put wall. Walls are context, not guarantees.

Reward-to-risk compares what you could gain with what you could lose. The gain is the distance from the entry price to the 52-week high, used here as the target. The loss is the distance from the entry price to the stop-loss. The mandate's hurdle for a standout setup is 3.5 to 1: the potential gain must be at least three and a half times the potential loss. A setup below that hurdle is watched, not bought.

The report also uses your own portfolio buckets from the mandate. Core Alpha holds long-term compounders. Tactical Momentum holds stocks in a Stage 2 markup. Leveraged Satellite holds leveraged funds and high-beta proxies. Cash is the buffer. Each bucket has a target band as a share of net worth, and section 6 compares your actual mix with those bands.

The lists use qualifiers to say how far each name passed the screen. Confirmed means every test was met: the trend, the momentum band and the volume test. Awaiting volume confirmation means the trend and momentum tests pass but the volume test does not yet. On the bear side, no distribution-volume confirmation means the trend test passes but the heavy-selling volume test does not. The qualifier is the honest label, and the report does not upgrade any name beyond it.

1. The big picture: regime, benchmarks, options and rates

This chapter answers the first question the report asks: what kind of market is this? Think of the regime as the weather over the whole stock market. A sailor who ignores the weather can still steer a boat well, but the wind decides how far and how fast any boat can go. The regime label in the ledger is the market's weather report. It combines three layers of evidence. The first layer is price trend, which asks whether the big indexes sit above rising averages. The second is breadth, which asks how many individual stocks are taking part. The third is positioning, which reads the option market and the bond market for signs of fear, complacency or pressure. Each layer can agree or disagree with the others, and the report says so when they do.

2. Sectors and themes: where the money is moving

Money in the stock market does not move evenly. It moves between sectors, the broad groups of companies that share a business driver, such as health insurers, banks, chipmakers or utilities. A useful comparison is water moving between rooms in a house. The water does not fill every room at once. It flows toward the rooms where the pressure is lower, and then it drains back out when the pressure shifts. A sector rotation map tries to show which rooms are filling and which are emptying. This report uses eleven sectors, the standard US classification, each represented by a fund whose holdings follow it. The ledger gives each sector a Stage from its own price history and a quadrant from its one-month performance against the S&P 500.

The quadrant is the more important of the two labels for rotation. Leading sectors have strong relative performance and a rising trend. Improving sectors have weak relative performance in the past but are starting to recover. Weakening sectors still have decent relative performance but are losing momentum. Lagging sectors have both weak performance and a weak trend. A reader should not treat the quadrant as a buy signal on its own. It describes where the money has recently been flowing, which is useful for deciding where to look first. The Bull List in section 3 and the Bear List in section 4 draw on these groups, and the report names the exceptions when a list departs from the sector story.

4. The Bear List: 30 Stage 4 breakdown watchlist

The Bear List is the mirror image of the Bull List. It names stocks in Stage 4, the decline season, in which a stock's price sits below falling averages. A reader who never sells short should still read this list, because it answers a practical question: which parts of the market are most likely to hurt a portfolio that is long? The list is therefore used to avoid, trim or hedge. It is not, on its own, a signal to short a stock. Shorting carries unlimited theoretical loss and a cost of borrowing, and nothing in this report recommends it.

A stock enters the Bear List only if it passes three tests. The first is the stage test: the ledger must classify the stock as Stage 4. The second is the trend test: the price must sit below the 50-day average, and that average must sit below the 200-day average. The third is the momentum test: the 14-day RSI must sit below 45, which means the stock has been falling with force. A name that also shows heavy selling, with a volume multiple above 1.5 on the down days, is confirmed. A name that passes the trend and momentum tests without that selling evidence carries the qualifier no distribution-volume confirmation. 5. Smart money and the event calendar

Price and averages show what has already happened. Insiders and event dates show what the people closest to a company expect to happen next. Two kinds of evidence matter here. The first is insider trading, which US law requires executives and large shareholders to disclose on a public form, the Form 4, within two business days of a transaction. A single insider buying shares is a weak signal, because executives receive stock as pay and sell it for many reasons. A cluster, meaning two or more insiders buying in the open market over a short window, is a stronger signal, because several people with different information are choosing to spend their own money in the same direction. The second kind of evidence is the calendar of scheduled events, especially earnings reports, which can move a stock sharply in a single session.

The report uses a strict definition of a cluster for this session. It requires at least two insiders, open-market purchases totalling more than $250,000, and trade dates within the 14 days ending on Friday 9 October, which begin on 25 September. It checks only the names that are in the ledger's universe of 1,557 instruments, because the ledger is the source for every price and average in this report. A large cluster in a name outside the universe is noted but not used, since the report cannot tie it to a stage or a trend. The purchase page the report read covers only buying, so this chapter cannot say whether insider selling corroborates the Bear List. That is a gap, and it is disclosed in the methodology.

The earnings calendar is the second kind of evidence. A report date is an event that can break a trend in either direction. Glossary

Breadth. The share of stocks in the screen that sit above a given average. Breadth tells you how many companies are taking part in an index's move, and a narrow reading means the index is carried by a few.

Call wall and put wall. The strikes with the most option contracts concentrated near the price, on the call and put sides. Dealer hedging around those strikes can slow a rally or cushion a fall. They are context, not triggers.

Cluster buy. Two or more insiders buying shares in the open market within a short window, reported on Form 4. The report's strict test also requires more than $250,000 in total and a window of 14 days.

Distribution day. A session in which an index falls on higher volume than the day before. A count of such days over 25 sessions is a measure of selling pressure building beneath the surface.

Gamma flip. The price at which the net hedging of option dealers changes from damping moves to amplifying them. Above it the market tends to be calmer; below it, moves tend to be larger.

Moving average. The average closing price over a set number of trading days. The 20-day, 50-day and 200-day averages are the short, medium and long lines used in this report.

Put/call ratio. Put volume divided by call volume. High readings mean fear and low readings mean complacency, which is why the ratio is read as a contrarian gauge.

Quadrant. The ledger's sector label, Leading, Improving, Weakening or Lagging, based on one-month performance against the benchmark. It describes recent relative strength, not a price trend.

Reward-to-risk. The distance from the entry price to the target divided by the distance from the entry price to the stop. The mandate's hurdle for a setup is 3.5 to 1.

Stage. The ledger's four-season classification of a stock's price structure, from Stage 1 basing through Stage 2 markup and Stage 3 topping to Stage 4 decline. It is assigned from price history alone.

Stop. The exit price decided before a purchase. This report sets it at the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average minus half an ATR.

Volume multiple. Today's trading volume divided by the 20-day average volume. A value of 1.5 means half as much again as usual.

Swing low. The lowest closing price in the most recent 20 sessions. The stop uses it as one of two floors.

Options book. The set of option positions in your account, valued separately from the four buckets.

Top 3. The three setups that clear the 3.5-to-1 reward-to-risk hurdle in section 6.

Relative-strength reading. The ledger's measure of how far a stock has moved compared with the broad market over a set number of days.

Underwriting. The three-layer review that decides whether a watchlist name becomes a shortlist name or goes to the archive.
