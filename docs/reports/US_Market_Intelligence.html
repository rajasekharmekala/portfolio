Start here: what this report is and how to read it

This report describes the US stock market at the close of Friday 23 October 2026 and turns that description into a short list of actions. It is a set of measured facts and if-then rules, not a forecast. Every price, average and ratio comes from a data ledger built for that exact session, and every verdict sits next to the rule and the level that would change it. If you have ten minutes, read the key figures, the lede and chapter 6. If you have an hour, read the chapters in order, because each one hands its conclusion to the next.

Ten ideas carry the rest of the report. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, like the middle line a hiker draws through a bumpy trail: the 50-day line shows the recent trend and the 200-day line shows the long one. A Stage is the season a stock or index is in. Stage 1 is a flat base after a decline (winter ending), Stage 2 is a rising phase above both averages (spring and summer), Stage 3 is a topping or pullback phase (autumn) and Stage 4 is a falling phase below both averages (winter). RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100 for how fast the price has moved: above 70 means unusually fast up, below 30 means unusually fast down. ATR (average true range) is the normal daily wobble of a price in dollars, and it is used to place stops so that ordinary noise does not shake you out.

Volume is the number of shares traded, a count of how many people voted with their money; a move on 1.5 times normal volume has more votes behind it. The put/call ratio compares bets on falling prices (puts) with bets on rising prices (calls) and works as a fear gauge. Option walls are price levels where many option contracts are open, and they act like ceilings and floors because the dealers who sold those options hedge near them. A stop-loss is an exit price decided before you buy, so that a bad idea costs a known amount. Risk and reward compares what you can win with what you can lose: a 3 to 1 ratio means you stand to make three dollars for each dollar you risk. Position size is how much of your money goes into one idea, and it is the reason no single stock can hurt you badly.

The report also uses its own labels. The regime is the market's overall condition, taken verbatim from the ledger. Here it is Correction (below 50-DMA, above 200-DMA): the S&P 500 sits under its 50-day average but over its 200-day average, which means a pullback inside a longer advance, and the rule for that regime is to hold cash at the top of its band and add only names within half an ATR of support. The other regimes are Uptrend (broad), Uptrend (narrow breadth) and Downtrend. A setup is a stock with an entry zone, a stop and a target. Confirmed means a stock passed every hurdle; awaiting volume confirmation means trend and RSI pass but volume does not. Verdict words follow one colour code: keep, accumulate, trim, exit and watch. Nothing here is personal advice; the tables are evidence and the rules are the reasoning.

The Call Wall is the strike with the most open call contracts (a ceiling), the Put Wall is the strike with the most open put contracts (a floor), and the Volatility Trigger is the price where dealer hedging flips from calming to amplifying. Below the trigger, moves tend to feed on themselves. Read SPY: its put wall is 750 and its call wall is 800, with the trigger at 775.2, so a close of 759.97 sits between floor and trigger, in the amplifying zone.

5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

Smart money means two groups: company insiders, who know the business best, and large institutions, who move prices when they trade. Neither is always right, but both leave footprints, and the footprints are public. Insiders must report open-market purchases of their own company's shares to the SEC on a Form 4 within two business days, and a buy is more informative than a sale, because insiders sell for many reasons (taxes, a house, a divorce) and buy for one: they expect the price to rise. A cluster of two or more insiders buying in the same two weeks is the strongest form of the signal. Large institutions disclose their holdings on Form 13F, but only 45 days after each quarter ends, so the third-quarter filings are not due until mid-November and give no read on this week.

The limits matter as much as the signal. A Form 4 tells you what was bought, not why, and the buyer may be early by months. Aggregators such as OpenInsider read the SEC filings for you, and this report relies on one for the cluster list. This chapter asks whether the flow corroborates or contradicts the charts in chapters 3 and 4, and it ends with the second half of its title: the calendar of dated events (the Fed meeting and company results) where a stop can fail because a price gaps overnight.

Glossary

  • Moving average (50-day, 200-day): the average closing price over that many sessions; the 50-day line shows the recent trend and the 200-day line the long one.
  • Stage 1, 2, 3 and 4: the four seasons of a stock: a base, a rising phase, a topping or pullback phase and a falling phase, defined by where the price sits against its averages.
  • Regime: the market's overall condition, named by the ledger; here Correction (below 50-DMA, above 200-DMA).
  • RSI: relative strength index, a 0 to 100 speedometer of recent price speed; above 70 is fast up, below 30 is fast down.
  • ATR: average true range, the normal daily price swing in dollars, used to place stops and to measure distance to support.
  • Distribution day: a day when an index falls on higher volume than the day before, a sign that institutions are selling.
  • Breadth: the share of stocks above their own 50-day average; a healthy advance has more than 60%.
  • Put/call ratio: put option volume divided by call option volume, a fear gauge; 0.7 to 1.0 is neutral.
  • Call wall and put wall: the strikes with the most open call or put contracts, which act like a ceiling and a floor for the underlying fund.
  • Gamma and the volatility trigger: gamma measures how fast dealers must re-hedge; below the trigger their hedging amplifies moves, above it calms them.
  • Reward to risk: the gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop; 3 to 1 means three dollars of reward per dollar risked.
  • Stop: a pre-set exit price below the entry, here the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average minus half an ATR.
  • Entry zone: the price range for a limit order, here from the 20-day average up to a small step above it.
  • Limit order: an order to buy at or below a chosen price, so you never pay more than the zone.
  • Form 4: the SEC filing that insiders submit within two business days of trading their own company's shares.
  • 13F: the quarterly SEC filing of large institutions' holdings, due 45 days after quarter end.
  • Tier 4 sizing: the smallest position tier, 1% to 2% of net worth, with a risk budget of 35 basis points.
  • Heat: the share of net worth that would be lost if every open stop were hit; the cap is 12%.
