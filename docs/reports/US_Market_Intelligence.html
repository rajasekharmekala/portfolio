RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above about 70 means a stock has been driven fast and may be tired, below about 30 means it has fallen fast and may be bruised. A stop-loss is an exit decided in advance, before emotion arrives. Risk and reward is the possible gain divided by the possible loss, so a ratio of 4 to 1 risks one dollar to win four. Position size is the answer to the question "how badly can one stock hurt me if I am wrong".

1. The Big Picture: Macro Regime, US Benchmarks & Derivatives

A market regime is the overall season for stocks, and it matters more than any single stock because most shares rise and fall with the tide. When the tide is rising, ordinary stocks do well; when it is falling, even good companies get pulled down. The ledger classifies the tide with a fixed rule that you can check yourself: if the S&P 500 closes above its 50-day average and above its 200-day average, and at least 55% of stocks are also above their 50-day average, the regime is "Uptrend (broad)". If the index is above the 50-day average but fewer than 55% of stocks are, the regime is "Uptrend (narrow breadth)". If the index falls below the 50-day average but stays above the 200-day, the regime is a "Correction", and below both it is a "Downtrend". 1.2 Options Gamma Wall & Volatility Architecture

Options give two clues that price alone cannot. The first is hedging demand, measured by the put/call ratio: the Cboe total ratio at the 3:15 PM Central reading was 0.88, with 1.02 for index options and 0.73 for equity options. Total readings between about 0.7 and 1.0 are neutral, and an equity ratio under 0.75 leans bullish, so hedging demand is steady and not panicked. The second clue is the option wall. When traders sell many call options at one strike they must hedge by trading the underlying fund, and the same holds for puts, so open interest piles up at round-number strikes that behave like a ceiling (the call wall) and a floor (the put wall) for the next week or two. When the yield on a safe 10-year Treasury note is above 5%, a stock must offer more than that return to be worth the risk, which is hard for companies whose profits are far in the future. High oil lifts costs for airlines, chemical makers and consumers, and raises inflation, which keeps the Federal Reserve from cutting rates. 5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

Insiders are the officers and directors of a company, and when one of them buys shares with personal money on the open market, the purchase must be reported to the SEC on a form called Form 4 within two business days. The reasoning for watching them is simple: executives know more about their own business than anyone outside it, and they have many reasons to sell (taxes, a house, diversification) but only one reason to buy. A cluster, meaning several insiders buying in the same few weeks, is the strongest version of the signal. The limits matter just as much. Insider buying is a weak timing tool, it says nothing about the next week, and it is skewed toward small companies and funds, where executives often buy to show confidence in a low stock price.

The second half of the discussion is the calendar. A company's quarterly results are the single most common reason for a chart to gap, which means open at a new price with no trades in between, so a stop-loss placed below the chart cannot protect against it. Glossary

  • Moving average (DMA). The average closing price over the last 50 or 200 trading days. It smooths daily noise so that you can see the direction of the trend.
  • Stage 1, 2, 3 and 4. Four phases of a stock's life: a flat base, a rising trend above both averages, a topping phase, and a falling trend below both averages.
  • RSI (relative strength index). A 0-to-100 speedometer of recent price momentum. Above about 70 is stretched; below about 30 is washed out.
  • ATR (average true range). The normal daily price movement of a stock in dollars over 14 days. It sets how far a stop must sit from the price to avoid ordinary noise.
  • Volume multiple. Today's trading volume divided by the 20-day average. A multiple of 1.5 means a day half as busy again as the recent norm.
  • Distribution day. A session in which an index falls on heavier volume than the day before, a sign that large holders are selling. Five or more in 25 sessions is a warning.
  • Breadth. The share of stocks above their 50-day average. It tells you whether a rising index is lifted by many stocks or by a few.
  • Regime. The report's label for the market's season, set by a fixed rule from the index, its averages and breadth.
  • Put/call ratio. Put option volume divided by call option volume. A high reading means more hedging or bearish bets; a low reading means more bullish bets.
  • Call wall and put wall. The strikes with the largest call or put open interest. They behave like a ceiling and a floor for a week or two.
  • Open interest. The number of option contracts that exist and have not been closed. It is counted once a day, so it describes the previous night.
  • Stop-loss. A price decided in advance at which a position is sold, so that a loss stays bounded.
  • Limit order. An instruction to buy only at a stated price or lower, or to sell at a stated price or higher.
  • Reward-to-risk. The distance from the entry to the target divided by the distance from the entry to the stop. A ratio of 4 means the possible gain is four times the possible loss.
  • Basis point. One hundredth of one percent. Twelve and a half basis points is 0.125%.
  • Form 4 and cluster buy. A Form 4 is the filing in which an insider reports a trade to the SEC. A cluster buy is several insiders buying within a few weeks.
  • Quadrant (Leading, Improving, Weakening, Lagging). A sector's position relative to the S&P 500 over one month and one week. It is a relative score, so it must be read with the stage.
  • Gap. An opening price far from the prior close with no trades in between, which a stop-loss cannot protect against.
  • Leveraged fund. A fund that aims for a multiple (such as two times) of the daily move of an index or stock. Daily resetting makes it lose ground in choppy markets.
