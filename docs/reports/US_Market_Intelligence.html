1. The Big Picture: Macro Regime, US Benchmarks & Derivatives

A market regime is the weather the whole market is in, and it matters more than any single stock because most stocks move with the weather. The ledger classifies it with one plain rule. 1.2 Options Gamma Wall & Volatility Architecture

Options are contracts that bet on where an index will be on a given date, and the people who sell them (dealers) must trade the underlying shares to stay neutral. Where many contracts cluster at one strike, those hedging trades create a ceiling or a floor. A call wall is the strike with the most open call contracts; sellers of those calls tend to sell the fund as it approaches, so the strike acts as a ceiling. A put wall is the strike with the most open put contracts; sellers of those puts tend to buy as the price falls toward it, so it acts as a floor. The "gamma flip" is the price at which the dealers' combined hedging changes sign. Above it, dealers buy dips and sell rallies, which calms the market. Below it, they sell into falls and buy into rises, which amplifies moves.

5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

Insiders are the executives and directors who know their company best. When several of them buy shares with their own money on the open market, it is a stronger signal than a single buy, because they pay tax on the gains and cannot easily hide a mistake. The signal has limits. Insiders often buy early, a stock can keep falling for months after a cluster of purchases, and a purchase by a large holder can be a deal tactic and not a view on the business. Form 4 is the filing in which an insider reports each trade to the Securities and Exchange Commission within two business days, and OpenInsider collects the filings into a table.

The calendar half of the chapter is a defence tool. A company's results can move its stock 10% or more in one session, far past any stop, because the price gaps from the prior close to the next open. The pre-earnings rule is simple: no new order that could fill on the last session before results, and a held position either has a smaller size or a stop you accept can be jumped. 7.1 What the Three-Layer Underwriting Tests Next

Underwriting has three layers. Layer 1 tests financial strength: whether the company turns profit into cash (operating cash flow near or above net income), whether its return on invested capital clears 12%, and whether its debt is below three times earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. Layer 2 tests five business indicators for the company's type and needs at least three to pass. Layer 3 tests the chart and the arithmetic, including the 3.5 to 1 reward to risk. A name must pass all three before it can reach the shortlist. For these three names the sharpest tests are different. For Intercontinental Exchange it is the MarketAxess deal and whether the cash conversion that earlier records showed (152% of net income over three years, from the watchlist record's early read of SEC annual filings) still holds. For Molina Healthcare it is Layer 1: GAAP net income of $38 million on $11,082 million of revenue and a pre-tax margin of 0.5% leave little cushion, and the return-on-capital floor may fail. For Keurig Dr Pepper it is Layer 3, because the reward to risk clears the hurdle by only 0.2.

What would get each name rejected is stated in advance so the test cannot be bent afterward. Intercontinental Exchange is rejected if its results on 29 October break the Stage 2 chart or if the MarketAxess deal changes its leverage above the three-times limit. Glossary

These are the terms the report relies on, in plain English, so the tables and verdicts can be read without a dictionary beside you.

Moving average (50-day, 200-day)
The average closing price over the last 50 or 200 sessions. It smooths noise into a trend line; roughly ten weeks and ten months.
Stage 1 to 4
The four seasons of a stock: a flat base (1), a rising markup (2), a topping phase (3) and a falling markdown (4), judged from the price against its two averages.
RSI
Relative strength index, a 0 to 100 speedometer. Above about 70 means the price has risen fast; below about 30 means it has fallen fast.
ATR
Average true range: the normal daily movement of a stock in dollars. Stops are placed at distances measured in ATR so that ordinary noise does not trigger them.
Distribution day
A session in which an index falls on heavier volume than the day before, a sign that large holders are selling.
Put/call ratio
Put contracts divided by call contracts traded. Above 1 means more downside protection than upside bets; it is a fear gauge.
Call wall, put wall, gamma flip
The strikes with the most open call and put contracts, and the price at which dealers' hedging changes from calming to amplifying moves.
Limit order
An order to buy or sell only at a chosen price or better; it may not fill.
Stop
A price decided in advance at which a losing trade is closed. A gap past the stop can make the real loss larger.
Reward to risk
The gain at the target divided by the loss at the stop; a ratio of 3.5 means winning $3.50 for each $1.00 risked.
Form 4
The filing in which a company insider reports each purchase or sale of shares to the SEC within two business days.
Breadth
The share of stocks trading above a given average; it shows whether a rise or fall is broad or concentrated.
Regime
The market's current weather, set by whether the S&P 500 is above or below its 50-day and 200-day averages.
Pinned stock
A stock subject to a cash takeover offer, whose price stays near the offer and does not reflect ordinary demand.
