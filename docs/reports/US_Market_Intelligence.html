Start here: what this report is and how to read it

This report describes the American stock market at the close of Tuesday 27 October 2026 and turns that description into a short list of decisions. It is written for a reader who has never opened a market report. If you have ten minutes, read the lede that follows this guide, the paragraph headed "What this means for you" at the end of each chapter, and the five-point summary in chapter 7. If you have an hour, read everything, because the reasoning behind each verdict lives in the prose and the tables are reference material. The report does not predict prices and it is not tax advice. It says what the price history shows, which rule produced each verdict and which level would change it.

Ten ideas carry the rest of the report, and each has an everyday comparison. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, a trend line drawn by arithmetic; the 50-day average is the last ten weeks of buyers' behaviour in one number. The four Stages are the seasons of a stock: Stage 1 is a flat winter base, Stage 2 is the rising spring and summer, Stage 3 is a topping autumn and Stage 4 is a falling winter. RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above about 70 a stock has risen unusually fast and below 30 it has fallen unusually fast. ATR (average true range) is the stock's normal daily wobble in dollars, which is why stops sit a fraction of an ATR beyond a support level instead of on it. Volume is how many people voted with their money, and a move on 1.5 times normal volume is a louder vote.

The put/call ratio is a fear gauge. Puts are bets on a fall, calls are bets on a rise, and a ratio above 1.0 means more bets on falls. Option walls are strikes where so many contracts are open that they act as ceilings and floors, because the dealers who sold those options hedge by buying or selling the underlying fund. A stop-loss is an exit price chosen before you buy, so that a mistake has a known cost. Risk and reward are compared as a ratio: what you can win divided by what you can lose, and a setup below 2.5 dollars of reward for each dollar of risk does not qualify here. Position size is the reason no single stock can hurt you badly, because you decide the loss you can afford first and let it set the number of shares.

The report also has its own labels. The regime is one phrase for the market's condition, taken from the S&P 500's position against its 50-day and 200-day averages and from breadth, the share of stocks above their own 50-day average. This session it is Uptrend (narrow breadth): the index is above both averages, but fewer than 55% of stocks are, so the rise rests on a minority. The Bull List holds Stage 2 stocks and the Bear List holds Stage 4 stocks; the qualifier on each row says whether the volume vote confirmed the move. Leading, Improving, Weakening and Lagging describe whether a sector is strong or weak against the market and whether it is gaining or losing ground this week. Verdicts such as ACCUMULATE, WATCH and EXIT always travel with the rule that produced them.

A handful of working terms complete the vocabulary. A breakout is a move above a level where the price stalled before, and a retest is the pullback that checks whether that level now holds as support. A limit order is an instruction to buy at a chosen price or lower, so it never chases the market. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the distance from entry to target divided by the distance from entry to stop. Net debt is total borrowing minus cash, and EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, the profit figure that debt is measured against. Free cash flow is the cash a company has left after paying for its operations and its equipment. EPS stands for earnings per share, the profit attributed to each share. ARR is annual recurring revenue, the yearly value of subscriptions that renew, and guidance is a company's own published forecast for the coming period. 1.2 Options Gamma Wall & Volatility Architecture

Options are a second market that sits on top of the stock market and sometimes pushes it. Traders buy and sell contracts that give the right to buy (calls) or sell (puts) a fund at a fixed strike price. The firms that sell those contracts, called dealers, hedge by trading the underlying fund, and the size of their hedging depends on how many contracts are open at each strike. The strike with the most open calls acts like a ceiling and the strike with the most open puts acts like a floor, which is why traders call them walls. A third level, the volatility trigger or gamma flip, divides two behaviours: above it, dealer hedging calms the market, and below it, dealer hedging adds to each move.

The flip point comes from a Black-Scholes model fed with each contract's implied volatility, so treat it as a close estimate, not an exchange statistic.

1.3 US Treasury Yield Curve & Macro Transmission

Interest rates are the gravity of the stock market. A share is worth the profits it will earn in the future, converted into today's dollars, and the conversion uses an interest rate: the higher the rate, the less those distant profits are worth today. That is why stocks that earn their money far in the future, such as software and homebuilders, move most when yields move. The Federal Reserve sets the shortest rates. Its target range is 3.75% to 4.00% after a quarter-point increase on 16 September, the first since July 2023, and its minutes of 7 October said most participants saw another increase as likely appropriate by year end. The market expects the Federal Reserve to hold tomorrow, with the next increase more likely on 9 December, based on pricing quoted on 9 October.

5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

"Smart money" is a loose phrase for the buyers who know the most: the executives and directors of a company, and the large funds that research it full time. The best public evidence of what insiders think is the Form 4, a filing in which an officer, director or owner of more than 10% of a company's shares reports a trade to the Securities and Exchange Commission within two business days. Sales are a weak signal because insiders sell for tax bills, houses and diversification. Open-market purchases with their own money are a stronger one, because there is only one reason to buy, and a cluster of several insiders buying within a few weeks is the strongest form. Even so, an insider purchase is a clue about value, not a timing signal, and the filing often arrives weeks after the trade.

Institutions leave a slower trail. What the market does reveal in real time is the event calendar, because a company's report date is the single most likely moment for its price to jump or fall. 5.2 Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

An earnings report is a scheduled shock. A company with a stock price of $200 and a normal daily wobble of $6 can open $20 higher or lower the morning after a report, and a stop-loss order cannot help, because a stop becomes a market order once the price touches it and fills at whatever price is available. 6.3 Sizing Arithmetic and Book-Wide Triggers

Sizing is the step that turns an idea into a number of shares, and it is the step beginners skip. The method used here starts with the loss you will accept if the stop is hit and works backward to the position size. The sizing table gives each tier a risk budget measured in basis points of net worth (a basis point is one hundredth of one percent): Tier 4 allows 35, and caps the position at 2.0%. The tighter the stop, the larger the position the formula would allow, and the cap then binds.

Worked example: sizing Veracyte. The band midpoint is 45.90 and the stop is 42.64, so the distance to the stop is 3.26 per share, which is 7.10% of the entry price or 710 basis points. The risk budget of 35 basis points divided by 710 gives a weight of 4.93% of net worth, but the Tier 4 cap is 2.0%, so the cap binds: 2.0% of $85,454.62 is $1,709.09. That buys 37 shares at 45.90 for $1,698.30. If the stop is hit, the loss is 37 x 3.26 = $120.62, which is 0.141% of net worth, about 14 of the 35 basis points allowed. The reward if the target of 60.91 is reached is 37 x 15.01 = $555.37, which is 4.6 times the risk.

Glossary

  • Moving average (20-, 50-, 200-day): the average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 trading days, a trend line drawn by arithmetic. A close above it means recent buyers are ahead.
  • Stage 1 to Stage 4: four phases of a stock's cycle. Stage 1 is a flat base, Stage 2 a rising markup, Stage 3 a topping phase and Stage 4 a falling markdown.
  • RSI and ATR: the relative strength index is a 0 to 100 speedometer of recent price speed; the average true range is the stock's normal daily wobble in dollars, used to place stops.
  • Breadth: the share of stocks above their own 50-day average. Above 55% with the index above its averages is a broad uptrend; below 55% is narrow.
  • Distribution day: a session in which an index falls on heavier volume than the day before, a sign that large holders are selling.
  • Put/call ratio: puts traded divided by calls traded. Above 1.0 means more bets on a fall than on a rise.
  • Call wall, put wall, volatility trigger: the strike with the most open calls, the strike with the most open puts, and the price above which dealer hedging calms the market and below which it amplifies moves.
  • Limit order: an order to buy at a chosen price or lower. It never chases the market, and it may never fill.
  • Stop-loss: a pre-chosen exit price. A stop becomes a market order when touched, so a gap past it fills at the gap price.
  • Reward-to-risk: the distance from entry to target divided by the distance from entry to stop. This report requires 2.5 to 1 or better.
  • Basis point: one hundredth of one percent; 35 basis points is 0.35%.
  • Regime: one phrase for the market's condition, taken from the S&P 500's position against its 50-day and 200-day averages and from breadth.
