Start here: what this report is and how to read it

This is the daily market report for the United States. It does three jobs. It tells you what kind of market the session produced, which parts of it are leading and which are lagging, and what a careful investor should do about that. It is not a list of stocks to buy, and it does not replace your own judgment or your broker's warnings. Read the first screen, then the lede, then whichever chapter answers the question you came with. The whole report takes about an hour. The first two chapters take about fifteen minutes and give you the picture. The case notes are the slow part, and they are where the rules are applied to named companies.

The report uses a small vocabulary, and every term is explained where it first matters. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, which works like a trend line drawn through the recent past. The 50-day average is the medium trend and the 200-day average is the long one. A stock or index that closes above both, with both sloping up, is in a healthy uptrend. A stock that closes below both, with both sloping down, is in a downtrend. Between the two, the market is in transition, and the report says so.

Analysts borrow the four seasons to describe a stock's life. Stage 1 is a base, which means the quiet period after a fall when sellers have run out. Stage 2 is the markup, the season of rising prices and rising averages. Stage 3 is the top, where the trend stalls and prices move sideways or wobble. Stage 4 is the markdown, the season of falling prices and falling averages. The ledger assigns one stage to every name, index and sector ETF, and the report uses that stage verbatim.

The RSI, or relative strength index, is a speedometer for momentum. It runs from zero to one hundred, and a reading above 70 means the price has risen unusually fast, while a reading below 30 means it has fallen unusually fast. Neither reading is a signal on its own. A stock can be overbought and keep rising, which is why this report pairs RSI with the stage. The ATR, or average true range, is the typical daily wobble of a price. If a stock normally moves two dollars a day, a stop placed one dollar below the price will be hit by ordinary noise, so the report measures stops in ATRs, not in arbitrary amounts.

The volume multiple compares today's trading with the 20-day average. A multiple of 1.5 means one and a half times the usual volume at that price level, and a multiple of 2.0 means twice the usual volume. The report treats volume as the number of people voting with their money. A breakout on heavy volume is an argument that many buyers agree. A breakout on light volume may be a few traders pushing a thin market, and it is easier to reverse. That is why the bull list needs a volume multiple of at least 1.5 before it counts as a clean breakout.

Two measures of the whole market matter as much as any single stock. Breadth is the share of stocks trading above their own 50-day average. When an index rises while breadth falls, the rise rests on fewer companies, which is the situation the regime label calls narrow breadth. A distribution day is a session in which the index falls by a meaningful amount on volume higher than the day before. Several of them in a few weeks suggest that large holders are selling into strength. The report counts them over the last 25 sessions.

The put/call ratio divides the number of put options traded, which pay off when prices fall, by the number of call options, which pay off when prices rise. A high ratio means many investors are buying protection, which can be read as fear, and fear that is already priced in can leave less selling to come. A low ratio means few investors are buying protection, which can be read as complacency. Market makers who sell options hedge their positions by trading the underlying index. Where many options are concentrated at one strike, that hedging can slow price moves near the strike, creating what the report calls a call wall (a ceiling) or a put wall (a floor). The gamma flip is the level where that hedging changes character. Above it, hedging tends to calm prices. Below it, hedging can amplify them.

Trade levels follow a fixed rule so that no number is invented on the day. The stop is the price at which you exit a position to limit a loss. The report sets it at the higher of the lowest price of the past 20 sessions and the 50-day average minus half an ATR. The target is the price at which you take a gain, and for these setups the report uses the 52-week high, a level the market has already proven it can reach. The reward-to-risk ratio is the distance to the target divided by the distance to the stop. The mandate for a standout setup is at least 3.5 to 1. A ratio below that is a watch, not a setup, however attractive the company looks.

Each label carries a posture, which is a rule for how much to invest and where. The posture is explained in chapter 6, and the rungs of the ladder are set out there so you can see what would move the market to a different label. Finally, the qualifier that appears in the bull and bear tables says how strongly each name passed its tests. A name marked confirmed passed every hurdle. A name marked awaiting volume confirmation passed the trend and momentum tests but not the volume test. The qualifier is copied from the ledger and is never changed by the writer.

1.2 Option walls, gamma and the put/call reading

Option markets produce their own structure. When many investors buy the same strike, the market makers who sold those options hedge by buying or selling the index as its price moves. Near a heavily traded strike, that hedging can slow price moves and create a level the index tends to pin near. The report calls the strike with the largest call open interest a call wall, and the strike with the largest put open interest a put wall. The gamma flip is the price at which the aggregate hedging changes sign. This is the closest thing options markets offer to an official map of support and resistance, and it is only an estimate built from published open interest. It is context, not a forecast.

On the close of 12 October 2026 the S&P 500 sat above its gamma flip, which places the market in a positive-gamma regime. In that regime the modelled hedging tends to dampen moves, so ranges are expected to be narrow unless a shock arrives. 2 Sector rotation and the themes behind it

Sector rotation describes the way money moves between groups of companies. An investor who expects higher oil prices may move from software into energy, and a manager who expects lower rates may move from banks into homebuilders. The movement is never total, and it is rarely announced. What the investor can see is the result: some sectors start to outperform the market, and others start to lag it. The ledger measures that result with a four-part map. Each sector ETF is placed on two axes, its strength against the market over the past months and its direction of change. A sector in the Leading quadrant is outperforming and still strengthening. An Improving sector is still weaker than the market over the longer window but has started to gain. A Weakening sector is still stronger than the market but is losing ground. A Lagging sector is both weaker and getting weaker.

Two features of the map must be read carefully, and the report explains them rather than leaving the reader to guess. The first is that a sector can carry a poor absolute trend and still be classed as Improving. The consumer discretionary and utilities sectors are both in Stage 4 on the ledger, meaning their own prices are falling against their averages, yet both are in the Improving quadrant. That combination says these sectors are falling less than the market has been falling, so their relative strength is improving from a low base. It does not say they are rising in the ordinary sense. The second feature is that the map is relative, so a sector can be Lagging on a day when the whole market is weak. The consequence is that the map should be read as a ranking of who is winning, and the stage column as a reading of who is actually rising.

3 The bull list: thirty Stage 2 momentum leaders

The bull list is the screen's answer to a single question: which companies are in a confirmed uptrend that is strong enough to be worth watching for a breakout? A Stage 2 company is one whose price is above a rising 50-day average, which is above a rising 200-day average. That arrangement says buyers have been in control for months, not days. The screen then applies four further hurdles. The price must be above the 50-day average, and the 50-day above the 200-day, so the trend is layered. The volume on the most recent breakout must be at least 1.5 times the 20-day average, because a breakout without that support is easier to reverse. The fourteen-day RSI must sit between 55 and 72, which keeps out names that have stalled and names that are already overheated. The reward-to-risk ratio, measured against the 52-week high, must reach 3.5 to 1, so that the potential gain is at least three and a half times the potential loss.

4 The bear list: thirty Stage 4 breakdown names

The bear list is the mirror of the bull list, and it answers the opposite question: which companies have fallen into a confirmed downtrend, with prices below falling averages and momentum weak? A Stage 4 company is one whose price is below its 50-day average, which is below its 200-day average, and both averages are falling or flat. The screen then asks two more things. The down days should carry heavy selling, which the ledger calls distribution volume, and the fourteen-day RSI should sit below 45, which means momentum is weak rather than merely soft. The list is not a list of short sales. A reader who has never sold short should read it as a list of companies to avoid buying, to trim if they are held, or to protect with a stop.

An ordinary investor needs this list because a falling trend is usually the last thing a buyer notices. It is also the first thing a holder should worry about. Stocks that have fallen a long way often look cheap, and the temptation is to buy them on the basis of their former prices. The Stage 4 classification is a discipline against that temptation. It says the market has repriced the company, and that the burden of proof is on the buyer to show why the repricing is wrong. The report does not say a bear-list name cannot recover. It says the recovery has not started on the ledger's evidence, and it names the level at which the evidence would change.

Of the thirty names, three are marked confirmed, meaning the distribution-volume test is met. Four are marked Stage 4 but RSI 45 or higher, which means the trend is down but momentum has already turned up enough to fail the momentum test. The remaining twenty-three are marked no distribution-volume confirmation. The distribution test is strict, and the usual result in a narrow market is that falling prices are not accompanied by the heavy volume that would confirm a real exodus. That does not make the trend less real. It means the selling is quieter, which can last longer.

The bear list is drawn from a wide range of sectors, and that is an important point to read correctly. 5 Smart money and the event calendar

The phrase smart money describes the buying and selling of people who know a company better than the public does: its directors and executives, and the large institutions that research companies for a living. Their trades are reported to regulators, and two sources matter here. Corporate insiders must file a Form 4 within two business days of an open-market purchase or sale, and institutions that manage large portfolios file a quarterly list of their holdings, which is known as the 13F. Clusters matter more than single trades. When two or more insiders of the same company buy within a short window, the pattern is harder to explain as personal finance and easier to read as a shared view that the price is too low. The same logic applies to selling clusters, which carry less weight because executives sell for many reasons, including taxes and diversification.

Those signals have limits, and the report states them before it uses them. Insider purchases are a stronger signal than sales, because a sale can be pre-planned or required by an option expiry. A single purchase by a director with a long history of buying can mean little. Institutional filings are published with a delay of up to 45 days, so a 13F shows what a fund held some weeks ago and not what it holds today. Neither source tells you why a person or fund traded, and neither can be used as a reason to buy by itself. The useful reading is whether the flow confirms or contradicts the trend picture in chapters 3 and 4.

5.2 The pre-event rule, with arithmetic

The pre-event rule exists because an earnings release can move a stock by more than its stop allows for. A stop is designed to fire on the ordinary daily noise of a price, measured by the average true range. A results day can produce a move several times that size, and a stop set for ordinary noise can be jumped entirely by a gap at the open. The rule therefore asks two things. First, the position should not be sized as though the stop protects it on the results day. Second, a new entry before the results should be sized so that a gap to the stop would still be an acceptable loss.

Glossary

The terms below are used throughout the report. Each is defined in plain words, and the first chapter where it matters is given in brackets so that a reader can return to the explanation in context.

Regime label [chapter 1]
The single phrase the ledger assigns to the market's posture, here Uptrend (narrow breadth). Every chapter uses the same words.
Moving average [primer]
The average closing price over a set number of sessions, such as 50 or 200, which smooths out daily noise and shows the trend.
Stage 1 to Stage 4 [primer]
The four phases of a stock's trend: base, markup, top and markdown. Stage 2 is the markup, the phase in which the trend is rising.
RSI [primer]
The relative strength index, a fourteen-day momentum reading from zero to one hundred. Readings above 70 show unusually fast rises and readings below 30 show unusually fast falls.
ATR [primer]
The average true range, the typical daily move of a price. It is used to set stops at a distance that ordinary noise will not trigger.
Volume multiple [primer]
Today's trading volume divided by the 20-day average. A value of 1.5 or more is the report's hurdle for a confirmed breakout.
Breadth [primer]
The share of stocks trading above their own 50-day average. Narrow breadth means a rising index is carried by few names.
Distribution day [primer]
A session in which an index falls meaningfully on higher volume than the day before. Several in a short period suggest large holders are selling.
Distribution-volume confirmation [chapter 4]
The ledger's test that a bear-list name has shown heavy selling on down days. A name without it is in a falling trend whose selling has been orderly.
Put/call ratio [primer]
The number of put options traded divided by the number of call options. High readings show protection being bought, and low readings show few hedges.
Call wall, put wall and gamma flip [chapter 1]
Strike levels where option open interest and dealer hedging are concentrated. The call wall is a possible ceiling, the put wall a possible floor, and the gamma flip the level at which hedging changes character.
Stop [primer]
The price at which a position is closed to limit a loss. The report sets it at the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average less half an ATR.
Target [primer]
The price at which a gain is taken. The report uses the 52-week high, a level the stock has already reached.
Reward-to-risk ratio [primer]
The distance from the close to the target divided by the distance from the close to the stop. The mandate for a standout setup is 3.5 to 1 or more.
Qualifier [primer]
The ledger's label for how a name passed or failed each test: confirmed, awaiting volume confirmation, or the bear-list equivalents.
Quadrant [chapter 2]
The sector map position of a sector ETF: Leading, Improving, Weakening or Lagging, which compares relative strength against the market and its direction of change.
Pullback entry [chapter 6]
A purchase planned at a named average below the close rather than at the close. Its ratio is partly fixed by the stop formula, which the report explains.
Form 4 and 13F [chapter 5]
The filings in which corporate insiders report their trades within two business days, and in which institutions report their quarterly holdings with a delay of up to 45 days.
Basis point [chapter 1]
One hundredth of a percentage point. A spread of 47 basis points is 0.47 of a percentage point.
