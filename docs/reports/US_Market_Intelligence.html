This report is a map of the US stock market for one trading day, Tuesday 13 October 2026. It tells you what kind of market we are in, which groups of stocks are leading and which are lagging, which individual names pass a strict set of rules for strength or weakness, and what the rules say you should do about them. It is not a list of recommendations to buy or sell any particular stock. It is also not a forecast. It describes what the prices did and what the rules say follows from them. You can read it in about ten minutes by reading the lede and the playbook, or in about an hour from front to back, which is the way it is meant to be read because each section explains the next.

The report leans on about ten ideas. The first is the moving average, which is the average closing price over a fixed number of trading days. Think of it as a trend line drawn through the recent past. The 50-day average shows the medium-term direction and the 200-day average shows the long-term direction. A price above both, with both rising, is the healthy shape. A price below both is the weak shape.

The second is the Stage system, which borrows the four seasons of a year. Stage 1 is a base, the quiet period when a stock stops falling and trades sideways. Stage 2 is the markup, the season of rising prices. Stage 3 is the top, where the rise stalls and the stock trades around its averages with no clear direction. Stage 4 is the markdown, the season of decline. This report looks for Stage 2 names to lead the bull list and Stage 4 names to lead the bear list.

The third is RSI, the relative strength index, which is a speedometer for momentum on a scale from 0 to 100. A reading above 70 means the stock has risen fast and may be overheated. A reading below 30 means it has fallen fast and may be oversold. The report uses 55 to 72 as the healthy band for a bull candidate and below 45 as the weak band for a bear candidate. A reading outside a band does not make a verdict wrong, but it changes how much you should trust the trend.

The fourth is ATR, the average true range, which measures a normal day's wobble in dollars. If a stock usually moves about $1 a day, a $0.20 move is noise and a $5 move is news. The report uses ATR to place stop-loss levels so that a normal wobble does not knock you out of a position by accident.

The fifth is volume, which is how many shares traded. Volume is the count of people who voted with their money. A breakout on heavy volume means many buyers agreed, while a breakout on thin volume is easier to reverse. The report calls a day's volume a volume multiple when it compares that day with the average of the prior 20 days. A multiple of 1.5 means half again the usual activity.

The sixth is the put/call ratio, which compares the number of put options (bets that prices will fall, or insurance against a fall) with call options (bets that prices will rise). A high ratio means more fear and more hedging. The published CBOE ratio is the market-wide version. The report also computes a related number from option open interest, which counts contracts that are still open, and it explains that number separately so it is not confused with the CBOE figure.

The seventh is the option wall, the strike price where the most option contracts are open. Option sellers who hedge their positions can make a price tend to stop near a large call wall above it and to find support near a large put wall below it. A wall is a tendency, not a guarantee. It matters most close to the expiry date, when the hedging is most concentrated.

The eighth is the stop-loss, a price you decide in advance at which you will exit a position so that a mistake stays small. A stop is only useful if it is set by a rule, not by hope. In this report a stop is the higher of the last 20-day swing low and the 50-day average minus half an ATR. That rule places the exit beneath the level where the trend would be broken.

The ninth is risk and reward, which compares what you can win with what you can lose. If a stock is $100 and your stop is $95, you risk $5 per share. If the target is $120, you can win $20, so the reward-to-risk ratio is 4 to 1. The report requires at least 3.5 to 1 before it calls a name a setup. A lower ratio is not a reason to hate a stock. It is a reason to watch it rather than to buy it.

The tenth is position size, which is the share of your money you put in one name. The sizing rule is the reason no single stock should be able to hurt you badly. If a stop is 7 percent below your entry and you commit 10 percent of a portfolio, a stop-out costs about 0.7 percent of the portfolio. That is survivable. The same stop on a 50 percent position costs 3.5 percent in one day.

1.3 Treasury yields and what they do to stocks

Interest rates are the price of money, and they set the discount rate that investors use to value future profits. When the US 10-year Treasury yield rises, a dollar of earnings expected far in the future is worth less today, which tends to hurt companies whose value depends on distant growth. When yields fall, the same arithmetic helps them. The official Treasury curve gives the yield on each maturity every business day, and the spread between the 2-year and the 10-year, often written 2s10s, is the usual measure of whether the curve is steep, flat or inverted. A steep curve usually means the market expects growth or inflation to pick up, while an inverted curve has often come before slowdowns.

On 13 October the 10-year closed at 5.21 percent and the 2-year at 4.79 percent. The spread is therefore 42 basis points, which is a positive, moderately steep curve. The ledger carries a 5.22 percent 10-year reading from a different data feed, which differs from the official figure by one basis point, and this report uses the Treasury figure because it is the primary source. The 9 October reading in the Treasury table was 5.24 percent on the 10-year and 4.80 percent on the 2-year, a spread of 44 basis points. The curve has therefore flattened by 2 basis points since then, because the 10-year fell by slightly more than the 2-year did, and it is not yet a sign of a changing trend.

Worked example: what a 10-basis-point move does to a growth company

Suppose a software company is expected to pay out $1 a share next year, and for that payout to grow at 3 percent a year after that. A simplified present value at a 5.21 percent discount rate and a 3 percent long-run growth assumption gives a value of roughly $1 divided by 0.0221, or about $45 a share. Raise the discount rate by 10 basis points to 5.31 percent and the denominator becomes 0.0231, so the value falls to about $43. That is a loss of roughly 4 percent from one tenth of a percentage point of yield. This is why a single day of rate movement can change how the market prices growth, even when nothing about the company has changed.

2 Sector rotation: where money is moving

Rotation is the movement of investment money from one group of stocks to another, the way water settles into the lowest rooms of a house as the level rises. Sectors are the rooms. Over a few weeks, some sectors attract buyers, their prices climb above their averages, and their member stocks start to appear on the bull list. Other sectors lose buyers, their prices fall below their averages, and their members appear on the bear list. The rotation matters because a stock's sector explains a large share of its move. A good company in a lagging sector can still fall, and a weak company in a leading sector can still rise for a while. For that reason this report draws the bull list mainly from leading sectors and the bear list mainly from lagging ones, and it names every exception.

4 The bear list: Stage 4 breakdown watchlist

Stage 4 is the markdown from the primer: the season in which a stock has broken its trend, with its price below its averages and the averages themselves pointing down. The bear list is the screen's record of the stocks that show that shape. The report publishes the list for two reasons, and neither is a recommendation to short. The first is protection. If you hold a name on this list, the rules say the trend has broken, and the list tells you which holdings need an exit or a hedge decision. The second is context. A bear list that is full of lagging-sector names confirms the rotation described in section 2. A bear list that contains names from leading sectors is a warning that the rally is not as broad as the index suggests.

The screen's bear hurdles mirror the bull hurdles. A name is in Stage 4, its price is below its 50-day average, the 50-day average is below the 200-day average, the latest session was a down day on volume of at least 1.5 times the 20-day average, and the 14-day RSI sits below 45. The first four tests describe the trend and the last describes the momentum. The fifth, the heavy down-day volume, is the one that separates a steady decline from a rout by sellers. Eight names pass every bear hurdle. Four Large-cap names are in Stage 4 with their RSI at or above 45, and so they fail the momentum test and carry the qualifier "Stage 4 but RSI 45 or higher". The other names fail the distribution-volume test. In most of those cases the stock is in Stage 4 but it either rose on the session or fell on lighter volume than the rule requires. That distinction is important, because a decline on light volume is often a slow leak rather than a flood, and the report treats it differently.

The bear list is more mixed than the bull list, and that is the most important thing to understand about it. The sector map says the lagging sectors are consumer discretionary and utilities. Those sectors supply only a few of the thirty names. The bear list also contains seven Information Technology names, which is a leading sector, along with three real-estate names, five industrial names and two communication-services names, which are in Stage 1 bases rather than in decline. Each of those exceptions is named in the spotlight and tables below, and each is a warning that the sector map alone does not explain every falling stock. The screen works name by name, and a stock can be in Stage 4 while its sector is in Stage 2. That is why the report applies the rules to each name and does not simply copy the sector verdict.

5 Smart money and the event calendar

Smart money is a shorthand for the investors who have the most information or the most resources, such as company insiders, large funds and professional traders. Their actions are worth watching because they put their own cash behind a view. Insiders are the people who run a company or sit on its board, and US law requires them to report their share purchases and sales on a Form 4 within two business days. A purchase made in the open market, with the insider's own money, is the signal most often studied, because the insider has the most detailed knowledge of the business. A group of two or more insiders buying in a short window is called a cluster, and clusters are read as a stronger signal than a single purchase.

Smart money has real limits, and this part takes them seriously. Insiders buy for many reasons, including a desire to show confidence after a fall, a scheduled compensation plan, or a belief that the stock is cheap relative to its long-term value. Insiders sell for reasons that have nothing to do with the stock's prospects, such as taxes, diversification or a home purchase. Institutional funds file quarterly reports on their holdings, which arrive weeks after the trades, so they describe the past rather than the present. The report therefore asks a narrow question: does the flow agree with the price trend, or does it disagree? Agreement strengthens a verdict and disagreement is a reason to look harder before acting.

The second part of the section looks at the event calendar. An earnings report is the most predictable source of a large, sudden move. Prices can gap several percent overnight on results or on the guidance that comes with them, and a stop placed under the normal daily wobble is not protection against a gap. The calendar below lists the next company report for every name in the consolidated holdings, shortlist and watch list that the ledger tracks. It tells you which positions have a known event inside the next few weeks, so that you can decide in advance whether to hold through it.

6 Tactical playbook and capital allocation

The playbook is where the evidence becomes an instruction. Every earlier section described the market; this one says what the rules ask you to do with it. The first step is the regime posture, which the report takes from its standard table. For the label Uptrend (narrow breadth), the posture is normal deployment into leaders only, with no chasing of names that have run far above their averages, and with cash held at the policy target. The report does not know your cash policy target, because it is a personal setting that is not in the ledger, so the posture is expressed as a rule on what to buy rather than as a percentage of your money. The posture is deliberately cautious. Narrow breadth means that a small group of names is carrying the index, and a rule that asks for normal deployment rather than an aggressive one reflects that.

The second step is the setup test. A setup is a bull-list name that passes every hurdle and offers a reward-to-risk ratio of at least 3.5 to 1, with its target anchored to a technical level such as the 52-week high, a measured move from the base, or a sourced analyst figure. The reward is the distance from the close to the target, and the risk is the distance from the close to the stop. The stop is the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average less half an ATR. On 13 October four names pass the hurdles, but none of them reaches 3.5 to 1 against a 52-week high, which is the nearest technical anchor the ledger holds. The three names with the highest reward-to-risk ratio among those four are the three watch-only names. Glossary

The terms below are used in the report with the meanings given here. They are arranged alphabetically so that you can look up a word you meet later. Where a term has a number attached, the number is the threshold the report uses.

ATR (average true range)
The average size of a stock's daily move over 14 sessions, in dollars. It is the measure of a normal wobble that stop levels are built on.
Basis point
One hundredth of a percentage point. A move in the 10-year yield from 5.24 to 5.21 percent is a change of 3 basis points.
Breadth
The share of stocks participating in a move. Here it is the percentage of the 1,497-stock pool above its 50-day average, which is 32.0 percent on this session.
Cluster buy
Open-market purchases of a company's stock by two or more insiders in a short window, usually 14 days. It is treated as a stronger signal than a single purchase.
Distribution day
A session on which an index falls on higher volume than the day before. Six of them in 25 sessions is the count the regime uses.
Form 4
The filing that company insiders must make with the SEC within two business days of a trade in their company's stock.
Moving average
The average closing price over a fixed number of sessions. The report uses the 20-, 50- and 200-day averages.
Open interest
The number of option contracts that are still open and have not been closed or exercised. It is the basis for the option walls in section 1.
Option wall
The strike price with the largest open interest. A call wall is the strike with the most open calls and a put wall is the one with the most open puts. Walls are tendencies that can slow or pin a move.
Put/call ratio
The number of put options divided by the number of call options. The CBOE publishes the market-wide version, which this run could not verify, and the report's own open-interest ratio is a separate proxy.
Quadrant
A sector's place on the four-part rotation map: leading (Stage 2), improving (Stage 1), weakening (Stage 3) or lagging (Stage 4).
Regime
The label the ledger gives the market's condition from index trend and breadth. This session's regime is Uptrend (narrow breadth).
Reward-to-risk ratio
The distance from the close to the target divided by the distance from the close to the stop. The setup mandate requires at least 3.5 to 1.
RSI (relative strength index)
A momentum gauge from 0 to 100 over 14 sessions. The bull band is 55 to 72, the bear band is below 45, and readings above 70 or below 30 are called overheated or oversold.
Setup
A bull-list name that passes every hurdle and offers a reward-to-risk ratio of at least 3.5 to 1 against a sourced technical target. No setup exists on this session.
Stage (1 to 4)
The four-season model of a stock or sector: Stage 1 base, Stage 2 markup, Stage 3 top, Stage 4 markdown.
Stop-loss
A price fixed in advance at which a position is closed to limit a loss. Here it is the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average less half an ATR.
Volume multiple
The session's trading volume divided by the average over the prior 20 sessions. The breakout and breakdown tests require 1.5 or more.
Watch-only
A name that passes the trend tests but not the full mandate. It is kept in the report for later review and is not acted on.

The report was built from a single facts ledger dated to the 13 October 2026 session, and every price, average, RSI, ATR, volume multiple, stage and screen result comes from that ledger. The ledger holds 1,557 instruments with no build failures and no stale bars. Every such figure lists its source address and the date it describes, and the research record also lists the queries tried and the sources checked for each item.

The index and sector moves are the ledger's own calculations, and the report does not recompute them by hand. The option walls are computed in code from the live option chains of SPY, QQQ and IWM for the 13 October and 16 October expiries, using the Yahoo Finance option data provided by the yfinance library. The put/call open-interest ratio is computed from the same chains and is a proxy. It is not the CBOE ratio. The CBOE ratio for 13 October could not be read from the source, because the CBOE market-statistics page loads its figures through script. Third-party sites published three different values for the day (0.56, 0.91 and 1.26), and the report does not use any of them. The 10-year and 2-year Treasury yields come from the official daily par yield curve table published by the US Treasury, cross-checked against a second financial site for the 10-year.

The earnings dates are read from each company's Yahoo Finance calendar page as fetched for this run, and a date is cited only where the page shows it. Twelve operating companies in the screen's calendar list show a readable date, the Elevance and IFF dates are read from their calendar pages as catalysts, BE's page shows no date that matches the calculated one and is not cited, and the fund and leveraged products have no earnings date to report. The filing-gate figures are computed from three years of annual statements. Cash-flow-to-net-income is the sum of operating cash flow over the sum of net income for the three years. Net debt to EBITDA, where EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, is total debt less cash, divided by that EBITDA. Return on invested capital is approximated as operating income after tax over debt plus equity less cash. Some of these measures are not available for some names, and the record marks each missing value rather than estimating it. The Form 4 cluster list comes from OpenInsider's latest cluster-buy page, and the filter applied is described in section 5.

The consolidated holdings record is used as the authoritative record of positions, as the run protocol requires, and the report does not independently reconcile it against the brokerage statements.
