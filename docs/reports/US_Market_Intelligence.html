This report describes the American stock market at the close of Friday 23 October 2026 and turns that description into a short list of decisions. It is not advice tailored to your tax position, and it is not a prediction. It states what the price history shows, which rule each verdict follows, and which level would change it.

Ten ideas carry the rest of the report. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line drawn by arithmetic; the 50-day average describes the last ten weeks of buyers' behaviour. The four Stages are the seasons of a stock: Stage 1 is a flat winter base, Stage 2 is a rising spring and summer, Stage 3 is a topping autumn, and Stage 4 is a falling winter. RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100; above about 70 a stock has risen unusually fast, and below 30 it has fallen unusually fast. ATR (average true range) is the stock's normal daily wobble in dollars, which is why stops sit a fraction of an ATR beyond a support level rather than on it. Volume is how many people voted with their money; a move on 1.5 times normal volume is a louder vote.

The put/call ratio is a fear gauge: puts are bets on a fall, calls are bets on a rise, and a ratio above 1.0 means more bets on falls. Option walls are strikes where so many option contracts are open that they act as ceilings and floors, because the dealers who sold those options hedge by buying or selling the stock. A stop-loss is an exit price chosen before you buy, so that a mistake has a known cost. Risk/reward divides what you can win by what you can lose; a setup that offers less than 2.5 dollars of upside for each dollar of risk does not qualify here. Position size is the reason no single stock can hurt you badly: you decide the loss you can afford first and let it set the number of shares.

The report also uses its own labels. The regime is one phrase for the market's condition, taken from the S&P 500's position against its 50-day and 200-day averages; this session it is Correction (below 50-DMA, above 200-DMA), which means the long-term trend is intact but the short-term trend has turned down. A Bull List row is a Stage 2 stock; a Bear List row is a Stage 4 stock. The qualifier on each row says whether the volume vote confirmed the move. Leading, Improving, Weakening and Lagging describe whether a sector is strong or weak and gaining or losing against the market. Verdict labels such as ACCUMULATE, WATCH and EXIT always come with the rule that produced them.

Six working terms complete the vocabulary. A breakout is a move of the price above a level where it had stalled before. A retest is a pullback that returns to that level to check whether it now holds as support. Reward-to-risk is the distance from entry to target divided by the distance from entry to stop. Net debt is total borrowing minus cash. Free cash flow is the cash a company has left after paying for its operations and its equipment. Finally, EPS stands for earnings per share, the profit attributed to each share, and it is the number companies report each quarter.

1.2 Options Gamma Wall & Volatility Architecture

Option contracts do not only reflect opinion; they push prices around. When traders hold a large number of options at one strike price, the dealers on the other side hedge by buying or selling the underlying fund as it moves, and that hedging creates ceilings and floors. A strike with many open calls tends to act as a ceiling (the call wall), a strike with many open puts as a floor (the put wall), and a level called the volatility trigger decides whether hedging calms moves or amplifies them. 2. US 11-Group Sector & Thematic Rotation Matrix

Money in the stock market behaves like water moving between rooms of a house: it rarely leaves the building, it moves from the room that is draining to the room that is filling. A rotation map shows which rooms those are. The US market is divided into eleven groups, called sectors, and each is tracked here through a fund that holds the sector's large companies. For every sector the ledger records two things: whether its price trend is strong or weak, and whether it is gaining or losing ground against the S&P 500. Combining the two gives four quadrants: Leading, Improving, Weakening and Lagging.

The reason to start with sectors is that stocks seldom win against their own group for long. A Stage 2 breakout inside a Lagging sector has to fight the tide, while the same breakout inside a Leading sector gets help from it. Chapter 3 therefore draws its Bull List mostly from the leading groups and chapter 4 draws its Bear List mostly from the lagging ones, and any exception is named and explained. The quadrant matters most for one reason: the market fell this fortnight, and in a falling market even weak sectors can lead if they fall less than the index. 5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

People who run a company know more about it than any analyst, which is why investors watch what executives and directors do with their own money. An insider who buys shares on the open market, paying full price with after-tax cash, is making a statement about confidence. A cluster, meaning two or more insiders buying within days of each other, is more meaningful than a single trade.

5.2 Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

An earnings report is a scheduled gap. A company can announce results after the market closes, and the stock opens the next morning at a price that skips every level in between, so a stop-loss order set at a sensible level may not protect you. 6.3 Sizing Arithmetic and Book-Wide Triggers

Sizing is where a good idea becomes either a manageable trade or an accident. The method goes in a fixed order: decide the dollar loss you can accept, measure the distance from entry to stop, and let those two numbers set the share count. A cap on position cost then limits the result, because a very tight stop can otherwise imply a position too large to hold comfortably.

Worked example: sizing Noble. Net worth is $82,368.77. A 0.5% risk budget is $411.84, and the 10% initial cost cap is $8,236.88. Noble's entry band top is 45.28 and the stop is 43.52, so the risk per share is 1.76. The risk budget alone allows 411.84 / 1.76 = 234 shares, costing 234 x 45.28 = $10,595.52, which breaks the cap. The cap allows 8,236.88 / 45.28 = 181 shares. You take the smaller number: 181 shares, costing $8,195.68. If the stop hits, the loss is 181 x 1.76 = $318.56, which is 0.39% of net worth. If the target hits, the gain is 181 x 9.70 = $1,755.70, which is 5.51 times the risk. The cap, not the stop, set the size, so you accept a smaller possible loss than your budget.

7.1 What the Three-Layer Underwriting Will Test Next

Underwriting is the process of asking, in a fixed order, whether a company can be owned with confidence. Layer 1 is a set of pass-or-fail gates on the financials: return on invested capital of at least 12%, free cash flow margin of at least 12% (not applicable to banks and insurers), net debt below 3.0 times earnings before interest, tax and depreciation, and stock-based compensation below 8% of revenue, with exceptions the mandate spells out. Layer 2 is a scorecard of three to five measures specific to the company's industry. Layer 3 is the entry posture, which asks whether the price sits close enough to support that the reward-to-risk ratio still works.

Glossary

These plain-English definitions cover every term the report uses. Each one is followed by the place where it matters most.

ATR (average true range)
The stock's normal daily price swing in dollars over 14 days. Stops sit a fraction of an ATR beyond support so that ordinary noise does not trigger them.
Breadth
The share of stocks above a given moving average. At 49.6% above the 50-day average, about half the market is in a short-term uptrend.
Call wall and put wall
The strikes with the most open call or put contracts. Dealer hedging around them tends to make them act as a ceiling and a floor.
Correction
This report's regime when the S&P 500 is below its 50-day average and above its 200-day average.
Distribution day
A session when an index falls on higher volume than the day before, a sign that large holders are selling.
Gamma and volatility trigger
Gamma measures how fast option hedging changes with price. Below the trigger level, dealers' hedging adds to moves; above it, hedging calms them.
Limit order
An order to buy at a chosen price or lower, so that you never pay more than planned.
Moving average (50-day, 200-day)
The average closing price over the last 50 or 200 sessions; a trend line made of arithmetic.
Put/call ratio
Put volume divided by call volume. Above 1.0 means more bets on a fall than on a rise.
Qualifier
The label on each list row saying whether volume confirmed the move (confirmed, awaiting volume confirmation, or no distribution-volume confirmation).
Reward-to-risk (R:R)
The distance from entry to target divided by the distance from entry to stop. The mandate wants at least 2.5 to 1.
RSI (relative strength index)
A 0 to 100 speedometer. Above about 70 is stretched; below about 30 is oversold.
Stage 1, 2, 3, 4
The four phases of a stock: base, markup, topping and decline.
Stop-loss
A price chosen before you buy at which you sell to limit the loss.
Swing low
The lowest price in the last 20 sessions, used as a support level when setting stops.
Two-ATR gap
This report's measure of an ordinary bad earnings reaction: a move of twice the stock's normal daily swing, applied to its weight in the portfolio.
