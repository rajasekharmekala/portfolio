Here are the ten ideas the rest of the report leans on. A moving average is the average closing price over the last N days, like a trend line that smooths out daily noise; the 50-day average is roughly ten weeks of trading and the 200-day roughly a year. A Stage is the season a stock is in, using the framework of the analyst Stan Weinstein: Stage 1 is a flat base (winter, waiting), Stage 2 is a sustained rise (spring and summer, the only stage this report buys), Stage 3 is a top (autumn, growth slowing) and Stage 4 is a decline (winter again, the stage to avoid). RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 the stock has run unusually fast, below 30 it has fallen unusually fast. ATR, the average true range, is the normal daily wobble in dollars, which is why stops are placed a fraction of an ATR beyond a support level rather than right on it. Volume counts how many shares changed hands; it is the number of people voting with their money, so a breakout on two times normal volume means more than the same price move on thin trading.

The put/call ratio divides the number of put options (bets on a fall) by call options (bets on a rise); it works as a fear gauge. Option walls are strikes where option sellers hold a lot of open contracts; they act like a ceiling above the price and a floor below it, because the people on the other side hedge by buying or selling the stock. A stop-loss is a pre-decided exit price, written down before you buy so that fear does not make the decision later. Risk/reward (written R:R) is what you can win divided by what you can lose; a 2.5 to 1 ratio means the possible gain is two and a half times the possible loss. Position size is how much of your portfolio goes into one idea, chosen so that even a failed trade costs you only a small, planned slice.

2. US 11-Group Sector & Thematic Rotation Matrix

Money in the stock market moves between sectors the way water moves between rooms of a house: it drains from some and pools in others, and the pattern changes through the business cycle. Watching that movement tells you where the wind is at your back. A sector fund is a single ticker that holds a whole sector, so it is a convenient thermometer. The ledger follows the eleven Select Sector funds and sorts them into four quadrants. The sort uses two measures against the S&P 500: the one-month return (is the sector ahead of the market over the month?) and the one-week return (is it gaining or losing ground this week?). Ahead and gaining is Leading; ahead but losing ground is Weakening; behind but gaining is Improving; behind and losing ground is Lagging.

The quadrant is a relative measure, and that creates a trap. A sector can be Leading while the fund itself is falling, because the market is falling faster. 5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

Insiders (officers, directors and owners of more than 10 percent of a company) must report every open-market trade to the SEC on Form 4 within two business days. That makes their purchases public, and a purchase with their own cash is a vote on the future of the business that an officer cannot easily fake: nobody buys shares in the open market to make the company look healthy. Sales carry less information, because insiders sell for many reasons, including taxes and diversification. Large investors file less often (quarterly, with a lag), so their holdings are old news by the time you read them. So the freshest insider purchases and the earnings calendar are the two inputs examined here.

The limits matter as much as the signal. An insider purchase is a stronger signal when several insiders buy within days of each other (a cluster), when the amount is large relative to what they already own, and when the stock is in an uptrend. A purchase in a Stage 4 stock is a bet against the trend and has a poor record of timing, because insiders are often early. The report treats insider buying as a tie-breaker between two stocks that already pass the technical tests, never as a reason to buy a stock that fails them. Glossary

Each term that follows is defined the way this report uses it, so a number in the sections above can be read without outside help.

  • Moving average (20-, 50-, 200-day): the average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 trading days. It smooths daily noise into a trend line. The 50-day line is about ten weeks, the 200-day line about a year.
  • Stage 1 to Stage 4: the four seasons of a stock in Stan Weinstein's framework: Stage 1 is a flat base, Stage 2 a sustained rise, Stage 3 a top or pause, Stage 4 a decline.
  • Regime: the overall market weather. This report's label is set by the S&P 500's position against its 50- and 200-day averages and by the share of stocks above their own 50-day averages.
  • Breadth: the share of stocks taking part in a move. If an index rises but under half of stocks are above their 50-day averages, breadth is narrow.
  • Distribution day: a session in which an index falls on higher volume than the day before, a sign that large holders are selling.
  • RSI (relative strength index): a 0 to 100 speed gauge over 14 days. Above 70 is hot, below 30 is oversold, and neither level tells you the direction of the next move by itself.
  • ATR (average true range): the typical daily price range over 14 days, in dollars. Stops are placed in fractions of an ATR beyond support so that normal wobble does not trigger them.
  • Volume multiple: today's share volume divided by the 20-day average. Above 1.5 means unusual activity.
  • Confirmed: a Bull List label meaning Stage 2, RSI between 55 and 72, volume at least 1.5 times normal and a higher close on the day. On the Bear List it means Stage 4, RSI under 45 and heavy volume on a lower close.
  • Awaiting volume confirmation: a Bull List label for a Stage 2 stock whose volume was lighter than 1.5 times normal or whose close was lower on the day.
  • Watch only: a row whose reward-to-risk ratio at today's close is under 2.5 to 1, so the report places no order.
  • Support: a price level where buyers have stepped in before, such as a moving average or a recent low.
  • Stop-loss: a pre-decided exit price, entered as an order so that a loss is limited before you can talk yourself out of it.
  • Reward-to-risk (R:R): the dollars you can gain to the target divided by the dollars you lose if the stop is hit. 2.5 to 1 means the gain is two and a half times the loss.
  • Limit order: an order to buy at your price or lower, or sell at your price or higher. It may not fill.
  • Position size: the share of the portfolio in one idea. The report caps these three tactical orders at 2 percent of net worth each.
  • Put and call options: a call is the right to buy a stock at a set price, a put the right to sell. Calls lean bullish and puts bearish or protective.
  • Put/call ratio: put volume divided by call volume. Below 0.7 is optimistic, 0.7 to 1.0 neutral, above 1.0 cautious. Index options run higher because they are used for hedging.
  • Open interest and option walls: open interest is the count of option contracts not yet closed. The strike with the most call open interest above the price is the call wall; the strike with the most put open interest below it is the put wall.
  • Gamma and volatility trigger: gamma measures how fast option dealers must buy or sell stock as the price moves. Below the trigger price dealers' hedging tends to amplify moves; above it, to damp them. The trigger here is a model estimate.
  • VIX: the Cboe Volatility Index, the market's expected swing in the S&P 500 over the next 30 days. The portfolio's cash ladder reads it.
  • Yield curve and spread: the line of Treasury yields from short to long maturities. The spread here is the 10-year yield minus the 2-year yield, in basis points (hundredths of a percentage point).
  • Form 4: the SEC filing in which company insiders report their trades within two business days.
  • 10 percent owner: a holder of more than 10 percent of a company's shares, who must also file Form 4.
  • Cluster buy: purchases by two or more insiders of the same company within a short window; usually a stronger signal than one buyer.
  • Combined ratio: an insurer's claims and expenses divided by premiums earned. Below 100 percent means an underwriting profit; a higher figure means a smaller one.
  • Remaining performance obligations (RPO): revenue already contracted but not yet recognised, a measure of the order backlog for a software company.
  • Free cash flow (FCF): cash from operations minus spending on equipment and buildings. It is what is left for owners after the business is kept running.
  • Three-layer underwriting: the workflow's test of a company: Layer 1 hard financial gates, Layer 2 sector measures scored pass, watch or fail, and Layer 3 entry timing and reward-to-risk.
  • Watchlist seed: a company entered into the pending queue of the research pipeline; it is an instruction to test, not a recommendation to buy.
