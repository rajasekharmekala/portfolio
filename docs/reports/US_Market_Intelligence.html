Start here: what this report is and how to read it

This report describes the US stock market at the close of Friday 23 October 2026 and turns that description into a short list of actions. It is a set of measured facts and if-then rules, not a forecast. Every price, average and ratio comes from a data ledger built for that exact session, and every rule is stated next to the number that triggered it. You can read the key figures and the lede in about ten minutes. Reading each chapter in order takes about an hour and teaches the method, so that by the end you could explain in your own words what is happening, why each name is on a list and what would change the call.

The report leans on a handful of ideas. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, like a trend line drawn through a bumpy road. The 50-day average shows the last ten weeks of mood and the 200-day average shows the last year. Stages are the four seasons of a stock: Stage 1 is a flat base after a decline, Stage 2 is the markup season when the price climbs above rising averages, Stage 3 is the topping or pullback season and Stage 4 is the decline below falling averages. RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100: below 30 means a stock has fallen unusually fast, above 70 means it has risen unusually fast. ATR, the average true range, is the normal daily wobble of a price in dollars, and it is used to place stops outside the everyday noise. Volume is the count of shares that changed hands, a tally of votes cast with money; a move on heavy volume carries more weight than one on thin trading.

Two measures describe the crowd. The put/call ratio divides the number of put options (bets on falling prices and insurance) by the number of call options (bets on rising prices); it works as a fear gauge, and a reading near 0.9 is ordinary. Option walls are strike prices where a lot of options are outstanding. Sellers of those options hedge by trading the underlying fund, so the biggest call strike above the price tends to act like a ceiling and the biggest put strike below it like a floor. A stop-loss is an exit price chosen before you buy, so that a wrong idea costs a known amount. Risk and reward compare the distance to the target with the distance to the stop; this report asks for at least 2.5 to 1 before it calls anything a setup. Position size is how much of your money sits in one name, and it is kept small enough that one failure cannot hurt the whole portfolio.

The report also has its own labels. The regime is one of four words for the market as a whole (Uptrend broad, Uptrend narrow, Correction, Downtrend) and it sets how much cash to hold: today the label is Correction (below 50-DMA, above 200-DMA). On the Bull List, confirmed means a stock passed every test, awaiting volume confirmation means trend and RSI pass but volume is below the bar or the heavy volume came on a down day, and a Stage 2 name with an RSI outside 55 to 72 is flagged as such. On the Bear List, confirmed means falling averages plus heavy selling volume, and no distribution-volume confirmation means the trend is down but the selling was not heavy or the price did not fall on the day. A setup is a name with an entry zone, a stop and a target whose reward is at least 2.5 times the risk; anything weaker is watch only. 1.2 Options Gamma Wall & Volatility Architecture

Options create invisible furniture in the market. When many traders hold calls at one strike, the firms that sold those calls hedge by trading the underlying fund, and that hedging tends to cap rallies near the strike. Heavy put strikes do the reverse and act as a floor. A third level, the gamma flip, divides two regimes: above it, hedging calms prices because dealers sell rallies and buy dips; below it, hedging amplifies moves because dealers must sell into weakness. These levels are estimates modelled from open interest on contracts expiring within 35 days, so they describe pressure points and not guarantees. The Cboe put/call ratio gives the crowd's mood: at the Friday close the total ratio was 0.93 (about 5.88 million puts against 6.31 million calls), the equity-only ratio was 0.80 and the index-only ratio was 1.10, a neutral reading that shows hedging but no panic.

How to read it. Call Wall is the strike above the price with the most open call contracts, the likely ceiling. Put Wall is the strike below the price with the most open put contracts, the likely floor. Volatility Trigger is the modelled gamma flip. Take SPY: it closed at 759.97, between a put wall at 750 and a call wall at 800, and well under the flip at 775.6, so the model puts it in negative gamma. Good news is a spot price above the trigger; bad news is a spot price sitting just above its put wall with nothing underneath.

5. Smart Money Radar & Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

Company insiders (officers, directors and owners of more than 10% of the shares) know their business better than any outsider, and they must report their trades to the Securities and Exchange Commission on Form 4 within two business days. The old rule of thumb from Peter Lynch is that insiders might sell for any number of reasons, but they buy for only one: they think the price will rise. That makes purchases more informative than sales, and cluster purchases (several insiders buying in the same window) the strongest version. The evidence has limits. A filing is a snapshot of one decision, 10% owners are often institutions rather than managers, and an insider can be early: buying at the wrong time is common in a correction.

Chapter 5 therefore uses insider data for one purpose only: to ask whether the people closest to a business agree or disagree with the chapter 3 and 4 lists and the chapter 2 themes. 5.2 Pre-Earnings Defense Calendar

A stop-loss protects you only if the price trades through your level; an earnings report can open the stock far below it, and the order then fills at the opening price instead of the stop price. That is gap risk, and it is why professionals reduce or hedge positions before results rather than hoping the stop works. The rule used in this report has three parts. First, no new entry within three sessions before a company's report. Second, for an existing position, compare the stop distance with two ATRs (two typical daily ranges): if the stop is closer than that, an average surprise can gap through it, so decide before the report whether to trim, hold or exit. Third, never move a stop down to avoid a trigger.

How to read it. Security is the company or event. Event says what happens. Date is when. Your Exposure shows the weight in your portfolio and where the price closed. Pre-event Rule says what to do. A good row is one outside the three-session window, where you can act freely. A bad row is a large position with a close stop one or two days before results. Glossary

These are the terms the report uses, in plain words, so that a reader can look up a word without leaving the page.

  • ATR (average true range): The typical daily price range of a stock in dollars; stops are placed outside it so ordinary noise does not trigger them.
  • Breadth: The share of stocks that close above a given average. Here, 49.6% of 1,497 stocks close above their 50-day average.
  • Call wall and put wall: The strikes above and below the price with the most open call and put contracts; likely ceiling and floor.
  • Correction: The ledger regime for an index below its 50-day average but above its 200-day average.
  • Distribution day: A session in which the index falls on heavier volume; five in 25 sessions is a warning.
  • Gamma flip: The modelled price at which dealer option hedging switches from calming to amplifying moves.
  • Limit order: An order to buy at or below a stated price, or to sell at or above one.
  • Moving average (50-day, 200-day): The average closing price over 50 or 200 sessions; a trend line.
  • Pivot: The price a stock must clear to confirm a breakout; here the 52-week high.
  • Put/call ratio: Put contracts divided by call contracts; near 0.9 is ordinary for the total ratio.
  • Qualifier: The tier shown on each list row: confirmed, awaiting volume confirmation or no distribution-volume confirmation.
  • Reward to risk: Distance from entry to target divided by distance from entry to stop; at least 2.5 to 1 here.
  • RSI (relative strength index): A 0 to 100 speedometer of recent price speed; under 30 is oversold and over 70 is overbought.
  • Stage 1 to 4: The four seasons of a stock: base, markup, topping and decline.
  • Stop-loss: An exit price chosen before buying; it caps the loss if the idea is wrong.
  • VIX: The Cboe index of expected 30-day volatility in the S&P 500; a fear gauge.
  • Volume multiple: Today's volume divided by the 20-day average volume.
