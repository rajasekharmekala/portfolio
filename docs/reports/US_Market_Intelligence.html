Start here: how to read this report

This report is a daily weather forecast for the US stock market, written for someone who has never read one. It answers four questions in order. What kind of market is this? Which parts of it are strong or weak? Which individual stocks are riding that strength or sliding with the weakness? And what, if anything, should a careful investor do on Monday morning? You can read it in ten minutes by taking the key figures, the section openings, and the three setups in section 6. You can read it in an hour by working through every table guide and every case note. Nothing here is a promise. Each verdict names the rule that produced it and the price that would change it.

Ten ideas carry the whole report. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, like a trend line drawn through the noise; the 50-day (50-DMA) shows the last ten weeks and the 200-day (200-DMA) shows the last year. A stage is one of four seasons a stock passes through: Stage 1 is a quiet base, Stage 2 is the uptrend, Stage 3 is the top where progress stalls, and Stage 4 is the decline. RSI is a speedometer from 0 to 100; above 70 means a stock moved up unusually fast, and below 30 means it fell unusually fast. ATR is the normal daily wobble in dollars, and we use it to place stops so that ordinary noise does not knock you out of a good position.

Volume counts how many shares changed hands; a rise on heavy volume is like a vote with real money behind it, while a rise on thin volume is a vote taken in an empty room. The put/call ratio compares bets on falling prices (puts) with bets on rising prices (calls); it is a fear gauge. An option wall is a price where many option contracts are open, and it can act like a ceiling or a floor because the dealers who sold those contracts hedge around them. A stop-loss is an exit price decided in advance, so a bad day does not force a decision made in panic. Risk and reward compares how much you can make with how much you can lose; we want at least $3.50 of possible gain for each $1 of planned loss. Position size is how many shares you buy, chosen so that being wrong costs a small, known amount.

The report also uses its own labels. The regime is the one-line description of the market: today it is Uptrend (narrow breadth), which means the S&P 500 sits above its 50-day average but fewer than 55 percent of stocks do. The posture that goes with it is normal deployment, leaders only, no chasing extended names, and cash at the policy level. ACCUMULATE means the chart qualifies for conditional research and a pullback entry; it is not a buy order. WATCH means a level is being monitored and nothing is owed yet. 1. The big picture: regime, benchmarks, options and rates

A market regime is the answer to one question: is the tide coming in, going out, or stuck? Every other decision in this report, from which stocks to study to how much cash to hold, depends on that answer, because a good stock in a falling market usually falls too. Think of the regime as the weather before a hike. You can pick the best trail, but if a storm is forming you pack differently. Our regime label is computed by a fixed rule: if the S&P 500 closes above its 50-day average and above the 200-day average, and at least 55 percent of stocks are also above their 50-day averages, the label is Uptrend (broad). If the index is above its 50-day average but fewer than 55 percent of stocks are, the label is Uptrend (narrow breadth). Below the 50-day but above the 200-day is Correction, and below both is Downtrend.

On 9 October 2026 the S&P 500 closed at 7,811.54, above its 50-day average of 7,696.92 and its 200-day average of 7,250.63, and the breadth reading was 28.9 percent. It says the index trend is intact, and it says the foundation under that trend is thin: seven out of every ten stocks in our 1,497-stock universe sit below their 50-day averages even though the index itself is within 0.42 percent of its 52-week high. When a few giants lift the average while most members slip, the index can still rise for a while, but it becomes more sensitive to bad news about those few giants. The distribution-day count of 7 supports that caution: a distribution day is a session when the index fell on higher volume than the day before, a sign that large holders were selling into strength, and seven in 25 sessions is a noticeable amount.

1.2 Options positioning: put/call ratios and the walls near the index

The put/call ratio is the simplest read of sentiment: it divides the number of put contracts traded by the number of call contracts traded. 2. Sectors and themes: where the money is moving

Sector rotation is money moving from one corner of the market to another, like water moving between rooms in a house that has a fixed amount of water. The market does not rise or fall as one block. In any week some of the 11 standard groups, such as Health Care, Energy or Utilities, attract buyers while others lose them, and the pattern tells you what investors expect from growth, interest rates and inflation. We measure each group with its sector fund, the Select Sector SPDR that tracks it, and we place each group in one of four quadrants by comparing two things. The first is the group's one-month return against the S&P 500's, which shows whether it is beating or lagging the market. The second is its one-week return against the S&P 500's, which shows whether that is speeding up or slowing down.

The four quadrants follow from those two tests. Leading means ahead of the market over a month and still accelerating this week. Weakening means ahead over a month but slower this week. Improving means behind over a month but picking up this week. Lagging means behind on both. For 9 October the S&P 500 index gained 2.90 percent in a month and 1.15 percent in a week, so a group needs more than those figures to be called ahead. The result is one Leading group (Health Care), one Weakening group (Information Technology), seven Improving groups and two Lagging groups (Communication Services and Industrials), which is a spread with few clear leaders and many groups bouncing from weak bases.

5. Smart money and the earnings calendar

Smart money is a loose name for the people who know a company or a market best, or who move the most capital. Two groups are worth reading. Company insiders, meaning executives and directors, must report every open-market purchase or sale of their own company's stock to the Securities and Exchange Commission on a document called Form 4, within two business days. When several insiders buy with their own money in the same few weeks, it is called a cluster buy. The signal is imperfect: insiders sell for many private reasons, but they buy for one, which is that they expect the stock to rise. Large institutions are the second group, and their quarterly holdings reports (called 13F filings) arrive with a delay, so they show where money went months ago, not where it is going.

A cluster of buys by two or more insiders, above $250,000 in total, filed in about the two weeks before this report (we use 25 September onward), is our flow screen. The screen is a corroboration test. If insiders are buying the stocks on the Bull List, the technical picture gains support, and if they are buying names on the Bear List the picture gets more ambiguous. The two parts that follow apply that test and then turn to the calendar, because the earnings date of any stock you might buy is a risk that no moving average can show.

5.2 The earnings calendar and the pre-event rule

An earnings report is the single most common cause of a price gap, a jump from one price to another with no trading in between. A stop-loss order is a plan to sell at a set price, but if the stock opens far below that price the order fills at the open, not at the stop, so the loss can be much larger than planned. That is why we keep a pre-event rule: a new position is not opened in the five trading sessions before a company reports. If you want exposure anyway, buy half the planned size, or wait until the report is out and the gap has settled. Existing positions are different, because selling before every report would mean never holding anything; for those the rule is to size so that a gap of twice the planned loss is survivable.

Glossary

Plain-English meanings for the terms this report uses. 50-day moving average (50-DMA): the average closing price over 50 sessions, a trend line for the last ten weeks. 200-day moving average (200-DMA): the same over 200 sessions, a trend line for about a year. ATR (average true range): the normal daily price swing in dollars, used to place stops. Breadth: the share of stocks above a moving average. Distribution day: an index decline on higher volume than the day before. Stage 1 to 4: basing, rising, topping and declining phases of a stock. RSI: a 0 to 100 speed gauge; under 30 is oversold, over 70 is overbought. Put/call ratio: put volume divided by call volume. Open interest: contracts still outstanding. Call and put wall: the strike with the most open call or put contracts. Form 4: the SEC filing for insider transactions. Reward-to-risk: possible gain divided by planned loss. Gap: a jump in price between sessions. Regime: the one-line description of the market's trend and participation.

Methodology and data gaps

Session and run. The report covers the Friday 9 October 2026 session, the latest completed US session with a verified final benchmark bar; the run was on Saturday 10 October 2026 Pacific time. Prices and indicators. Every close, moving average, RSI, ATR, volume multiple, stage, breadth figure and sector quadrant comes from a ledger built from Yahoo Finance daily history for the S&P 500, S&P 400 and S&P 600 universe (1,503 stocks), with 50- and 200-day averages computed by arithmetic and RSI and ATR by the Wilder 14-day method. Stops are the higher of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average minus half an ATR, and targets are 52-week highs.

Research sources. Treasury yields come from FRED and are dated 8 October because FRED publishes with a one-session lag. The Cboe put/call figures are from Cboe's Options Exchange daily statistics page for 9 October; a second source (thetrading.tools) covers all exchanges through 8 October and differs slightly (0.89 total on 8 October against Cboe's 0.82 on 9 October), so the two are not the same series and the comparison is directional. The option walls are computed from open interest in the Yahoo Finance option chain for the Monday 12 October expiry. Insider data come from OpenInsider and are a convenience copy of SEC Form 4 filings. Earnings dates come from the Yahoo Finance calendar and are estimates until a company confirms them. News facts for Humana, T-Mobile, Alignment Healthcare, MarineMax, Coca-Cola Consolidated, Perrigo and Azenta come from the pages cited in the references.

What could not be verified. No independent options-dealer gamma-exposure estimate (call wall, put wall and zero-gamma flip) was available, so only the open-interest walls are reported; the zero-gamma flip level is not. Participant-level institutional flows (13F) were not researched because the filings lag by a quarter. Company fundamentals were not verified for the Bull List names beyond what the pipeline has already done; the three setups are technical candidates only. The Yahoo calendar returned no earnings date for exchange-traded funds and fund wrappers. Index-level data for the day are as recorded in the ledger and may differ slightly from other vendors, and the Cboe figures can be revised. The holdings come from the transaction logs, which are assumed accurate, and the Schwab and Robinhood data were last updated on 1 October and 30 September 2026 respectively, so recent trades may not appear.
