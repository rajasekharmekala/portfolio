Start here: how to read this audit

This audit is a decision map, not a prediction. Imagine three independent analysts standing outside the portfolio. The market analyst asks whether the weather is favorable; the holdings analyst asks whether each boat is seaworthy; and the greenfield picker asks what boat would be chosen if the harbor were empty. Keeping those jobs separate matters because ownership can make a familiar name feel safer than its current evidence. The market analyst therefore starts with the exact regime label, Uptrend (narrow breadth), rather than with a favorite stock. “Uptrend” means the broad index can still rise, while “narrow breadth” means only a minority of the 1,497-stock pool is above its 50-day average. The practical analogy is a parade whose lead vehicles are moving while much of the crowd is standing still: follow leaders, but do not assume the whole street is healthy.

The action labels are mechanical. HOLD / TRAIL means Stage 2 momentum is intact and the position stays only while price respects a trailing stop. HOLD / HARD STOP means the business may be worth monitoring, but the chart is not earning fresh risk. TRIM means reduce exposure when Stage 3, concentration, or leverage makes the payoff less favorable. EXIT means remove a broken Stage 4 or structurally unsuitable position rather than waiting for a story to rescue it. NO TRADE / WATCH is a greenfield decision: the name may be interesting, but it is not at support. “Stage 2” is a hill-climbing phase above key moving averages; “Stage 4” is the downhill phase. A moving average is like a household’s recent spending trend: price above a rising 50-day line shows current demand is stronger than the recent norm.

Stops translate volatility into a known loss. For Core Alpha the rule is the lower of the recent swing low and relevant moving average, minus 1.0 ATR; for Tactical Momentum it is the lower of swing low and 20/50-day average, minus 0.5 ATR. ATR is the stock’s typical daily travel, like leaving extra space when parking a larger vehicle. Targets must be tied to forward earnings and a three-year median multiple or a measured move; the asymmetry hurdle is at least 3.0:1 for Core and 3.5:1 for Tactical. A single stock normally has a 20.0% ceiling. The exception protects a winning Stage 2 name only when its 20-day average and 21-day exponential moving average (21-EMA) are rising, relative strength (RS) is above 80, and RSI is below 82; this ledger does not provide every RS input.

Daily-reset leveraged ETFs target multiple times each day’s move, not multiple times the long-run return; alternating days can erode value even if the index finishes flat. LEAPS are long-dated options: they provide exposure with a clock attached, so time decay, implied volatility, and strike price matter. Read each table as a chain: close tells where the asset is, stage tells its season, RSI describes speed, ATR sizes typical noise, and the 50-day line gives a trend reference. First, the regime is Uptrend (narrow breadth), so leaders are allowed but chasing is not. The index can rise while many individual charts weaken; breadth is the guardrail against treating an index headline as permission to buy everything. Second, Stage 4 holdings and daily-reset leverage deserve defense before new ideas. A rebound story does not erase a broken trend, and path dependence can make leveraged recovery mathematically difficult. Third, the 20% concentration rule is a ceiling, not a reason to ignore correlation. Several smaller positions can still be one crowded factor. Fourth, a greenfield challenger must be at support, have a clean stop, and clear the 1.5× asymmetry swap test after friction. A watchlist name is not an order. Fifth, every future number must come from a fresh facts ledger or dated research source. Rebuild the bridge after fills, recheck option expiry, and preserve the evidence date. The useful habit is simple: write the rule before the emotion arrives, then let the next close decide whether the rule was met. The report is a map for that habit, not a substitute for live prices or tax advice. Keep a small decision journal with the date, the evidence used, the intended action, and the invalidation level. When the next ledger disagrees, change the action rather than changing the old fact. Separate the question “is this a good company?” from “is this a good position today?” and separate both from “is this the best use of the next dollar?” Those three questions prevent a familiar name from receiving an automatic exemption. They also make the review teachable: another reader can follow the denominator, inspect the source, reproduce the stop, and understand why cash was or was not deployed. The portfolio does not need constant activity; it needs a short list of rules that survive a volatile close, an unfilled order, a missing tax lot, and an attractive headline. That is the purpose of the five reminders: preserve optionality, protect evidence, and make the next decision easier to audit.

  • If a material earnings or option-expiry event changes a top-five risk, pause deployment until the case note is refreshed.
  • If a cited source cannot be reproduced, downgrade the claim to a data gap rather than carrying it forward.
