3. Contracted LNG operations and competition

Cheniere buys pipeline gas, moves it to Sabine Pass in Louisiana or Corpus Christi in Texas, removes impurities, liquefies it, stores the product and loads it onto ships. Its pipelines, trains, tanks, berths and commercial desk work as one system. Buyers contract for volumes under sale and purchase agreements; they may take cargoes at the terminal or arrange delivery. Cheniere also markets third-party LNG and can optimize destinations. This scale and operating record create a real advantage, but outages, shipping constraints and gas spreads still affect returns. [2][6][7][8][9]

Contracts support utilization and planning, not a fully fixed margin. The CFO said a $1 change in market margins would affect annual EBITDA by about $50 million. Q3 revenue was $6.17 billion and adjusted EBITDA was $1.90 billion, but nine-month net income was $736 million versus $3.03 billion a year earlier. Derivative marks can precede cargo settlement, so GAAP earnings, operating cash, DCF and adjusted EBITDA should be read together. [1][2][3]

Expansion is the key execution test. Corpus Christi Stage 3 Train 7 reached commercial operations, while Trains 8 and 9 remained under construction at the Q3 cutoff. New capacity adds value only when it earns more than its construction, financing, maintenance and dilution costs. Venture Global announced commercial operations at Plaquemines Phase 1 on October 31, with Shell lifting its first cargo; NextDecade’s Rio Grande project remains in development and construction. New supply may widen buyer choice and pressure future contract terms. [2][5][27][28][29]
