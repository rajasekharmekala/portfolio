Read each row from left to right. The Layer 1 result tells you whether the candidate is investable under the constitution. Technical posture tells you whether a passing business is buyable today. Glossary

ROIC is return on invested capital, a measure of profit earned on the capital the business uses. Free cash flow is cash left after operating needs and investment. Stock-based compensation is pay delivered in shares rather than cash, which can dilute owners. Net debt to EBITDA compares debt after cash with operating earnings. Asymmetry compares potential reward with the stop-defined risk. Stage 2 means a sustained markup above rising moving averages; Stage 3 means a topping or pullback phase. A support ladder maps the distance from a reference average in ATR, or average true range, units.

1. What the company does

Unum Group sells income protection to employees through their employers. If a worker cannot work because of illness or injury, a Unum policy pays part of their salary. The company earns money in two ways: it collects premiums (about $10.8 billion in 2025) and it invests the premiums while it waits to pay claims (about $2.0 billion of net investment income in 2025).[1]

The four segments

Unum US is the largest, covering group disability, group life and supplemental products. Colonial Life sells voluntary benefits at the workplace. Unum International is mostly the United Kingdom group income protection business. The Closed Block holds older individual disability and long-term care policies that are no longer sold.[1] In 2025 adjusted operating income by segment was Unum US $1,271.9 million, Colonial Life $463.6 million, International $152.3 million and the Closed Block $63.5 million.[1]

Why long-term care matters to the whole company

Long-term care pays for nursing homes and home care years after a policy was sold. Prices were set decades ago on assumptions that proved too optimistic. In the third quarter of 2025 Unum cut its reserve assumptions, a pretax charge of $478.5 million, which dragged 2025 net income down to $738.5 million from $1,779.1 million a year earlier.[1] In July 2026 the company agreed to pass the rest of its individual long-term care block (about $3.8 billion of statutory reserves, about 50,000 policies) to Fortitude Re, with closing expected in 2026 pending regulatory approval.[6][7]

Glossary

Adjusted operating income is profit before investment gains and one-off items, and it is the number management guides to. A benefit ratio is claims paid as a share of premiums, so a higher ratio means less profit. RBC ratio (risk-based capital) compares an insurer's capital to the minimum regulators require, and Unum reported about 480% at mid-2026.[4] Reinsurance is when one insurer pays another to take over a block of risk. A Weinstein stage is a four-step reading of the price trend, where Stage 2 is an advance and Stage 3 is a stall near the highs.

What this means for an owner: you own a slow-growing group benefits business whose profit is predictable, plus a long-term care block whose cost can jump. The next chapter asks whether the good business outweighs the bad one.

1. What the company does

Intuitive Surgical builds and sells soft-tissue surgical robots. A surgeon sits at a console a few feet from the patient and moves hand controls; the robot translates those movements into tiny, steady motions of instruments inserted through small cuts. The aim is less pain, shorter hospital stays and more precise surgery than open operations allow. The company's own filing describes the da Vinci system as its flagship, with the Ion system used for lung biopsies as a second platform.[1]

How it earns money

There are three revenue streams, and the mix matters more than the total. In 2025, instruments and accessories brought in $6,018.9 million, systems (the robots themselves) $2,473.7 million, and service contracts $1,572.1 million.[1] Instruments are single-use or limited-use parts that wear out with each operation, so they behave like razor blades. Together with service, they made up 75.4% of 2025 revenue, which we call the recurring share.[1] A hospital that buys or leases a robot keeps paying every time it operates.

The company is shifting how it sells robots. In the second quarter of 2026 it placed 468 da Vinci systems, of which 254 were under operating leases and 131 of those were usage-based, meaning the hospital pays per procedure instead of an upfront price.[4] That lowers the barrier to entry for hospitals, and it pushes more of the revenue into the recurring bucket over time. It also makes system revenue less lumpy, but it defers cash.

Where it sells

About 68% of 2025 revenue came from the United States and 32% from the rest of the world.[1] In March 2026 the company bought three distributors (ab medica, Abex and Excelencia Robótica) for about $533.1 million to take direct control of sales in several markets.[3] The company had roughly 17,021 employees at the end of 2025.[1]

The size of the installed base

At the end of the second quarter of 2026 there were 11,710 da Vinci systems in hospitals (up 12% in a year) and 1,096 Ion systems (up 21%).[4] Worldwide procedures grew 16% in the quarter, with da Vinci procedures up 15% and Ion procedures up 36%.[4] Each installed robot is a small toll booth, and the count of robots times the use per robot is the engine of the whole company.

Glossary

These terms recur in the rest of the book.

  • ATR: average true range, the typical daily price swing; we use it as a ruler for distances on the chart.
  • Free cash flow: cash from operations minus spending on equipment and buildings.
  • Asymmetry: expected gain to the target divided by the loss to the stop. Higher is better.
  • 200-day average: the average closing price of the last 200 sessions, a common line between long-term strength and weakness.
  • Stage 1: a stock that has stopped falling and is moving sideways, not yet rising.
  • SBC (stock-based compensation): share-based compensation, the cost of paying staff in stock.

Why these estimates might be wrong

Where the rejection could be too harsh

The two failing gates use GAAP figures, which count acquisition amortization of $892.7M TTM and share-based pay. Where the bullish story could be too generous

Sales depend on a distributor with 44% of revenue and a direct customer with 16%.[4] The Google warrant ties up to 58.97 million shares to as much as 240 tranches of $500 million of revenue, which Reuters reads as up to $120 billion of revenue.[15] A deal like that can also signal that Marvell is paying in equity to win business. The $8.52 billion of foundry commitments and the 36% China revenue share add supply and policy risk.[4][2]

Filing lists were checked on SEC EDGAR for new insider or ownership filings, and none was analysed in detail.[17][19] We did not verify customer names behind the distributor, insider selling, a three-year median P/E, fourth-quarter guidance or the date of the next earnings call.
