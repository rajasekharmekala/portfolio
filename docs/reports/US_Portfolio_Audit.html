Start here: what this audit is and how to read it

This report checks every position you own against a fixed set of rules, using prices from the close of Friday 23 October 2026. It then says what to sell, what to keep and why. It is not a forecast. It is a set of if-then rules applied to numbers that were measured, so you can follow each decision back to the figure that caused it. You can read the key figures and the lede in ten minutes. Reading each chapter in order takes about an hour and teaches the method.

Three independent analysts produced it. The first reads the market: is the broad market rising, falling or stuck, and which sectors are leading. The second audits what you hold, one position at a time, and ignores how long you have owned it or what you paid. The third, the greenfield picker, asks a thought experiment: if you had only cash today, what would you buy? It is not allowed to know your holdings, so it cannot make excuses for them. The audit then compares the two answers.

Several ideas carry the whole report. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, like a trend line drawn through a bumpy road. The 50-day and 200-day averages are the ones that matter here. Stages are the four seasons of a stock: Stage 1 is a flat base after a decline, Stage 2 is the markup season when the price climbs above rising averages, Stage 3 is the topping or pullback season, and Stage 4 is the decline below falling averages. RSI is a speedometer from 0 to 100: below 30 means the stock fell unusually fast, above 70 means it rose unusually fast. ATR (average true range) is the normal daily wobble of a stock in dollars, and we use it to put stops outside the normal noise. Volume is how many shares changed hands, a count of votes cast with money.

A stop-loss is an exit price chosen before you buy. In this report a stop on shares or a fund is an intraday stop-market order that fires as soon as the price trades at the level, so a note that says a close below the stop means the same level; LEAPS lines and fractional remainders use a price alert and an exit at the next open. For Core Alpha holdings the stop is the lower of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day average, minus one ATR, or the 63-day base low minus one ATR when the stock is basing. For Tactical Momentum and leveraged holdings it is the lower of the 20-day swing low and the 20-day average, minus half an ATR. Risk and reward compare what you can win with what you can lose: a new position needs at least 2.0 to 1 for Core and 2.5 to 1 for Tactical. Position size is how much of your money sits in one name. A single stock may not exceed 15% of net worth. A Stage 2 winner with intact earnings may run up to 22% before it is trimmed, because the rule says never to cut a compounding winner only because it went up. New positions are sized by risk: the weight is the risk budget in basis points divided by the distance to the stop, so a wide stop earns a smaller position.

Two product types need extra care. A daily-reset leveraged ETF such as TQQQ or KORU multiplies each day's move by two or three and resets every night, so over a choppy month it can lose money even when the index ends flat. A LEAPS option is a call option with more than a year of life when bought; it moves with the stock but can lose its whole value. Beta measures how hard a holding moves when the market moves: beta 2 means a 1% market drop usually costs 2%.

The labels used in the tables each have a trigger. EXIT means sell the whole position: a daily-reset leveraged product in Stage 4, a LEAPS line on a Stage 4 stock or with 120 days or less to expiry, the smallest LEAPS line that cures a breach of the options bucket's 10% hard maximum, or any holding that closes below its stop. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep it, with a stop order that sells automatically. TRIM means sell part, and applies when a position passes the 15% cap without Stage 2 protection. NO TRADE / WATCH means no action until a named level is reached. Core Alpha is the long-term compounding book, Tactical Momentum is the shorter-term Stage 2 book, and the Leveraged Satellite and LEAPS buckets are small, high-risk slices with hard maximums.

2.1 The concentration arithmetic, worked through

Before any ladder, it helps to see how the caps work in dollars. A cap is a ratio, so it can be tested by asking what share price would put a holding at the cap. The answer tells you how far a winner has to run before the rule asks anything of you, and it shows why the rule leaves compounding winners alone. The example uses your largest holding.

Risk-unit sizing answers how big a first position should be, and it starts from the stop, not from conviction. Chapter 4: US Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

The funnel is the path a stock must travel before it earns money: a financial gate, a technical gate and an asymmetry gate. The financial gate asks whether the business is solvent and earns real cash: for a US compounder that means return on invested capital of at least 12%, free cash flow margin of at least 12%, net debt under 3 times operating profit and stock-based pay under 8% of revenue, with softer tests for turnarounds. The asymmetry gate asks whether the possible gain is at least 2.0 times the possible loss for Core and 2.5 times for Tactical or turnaround names. A stock that fails a hard red line is rejected; one that merely arrives at the wrong price waits.

5.3 Scenarios: what the plan does if things go wrong

A plan is only as good as its answer to "what if". Four cases matter, and they are static sensitivities, not forecasts. In the first, every kept stop executes at its own level. Worked example: how a gap changes the stop arithmetic

GOOG reports on Wednesday 28 October after the close. Your stop is 308.57, and the position is 24.8076 shares at 317.28. If the stock simply drifts to the stop, the loss is 24.8076 x (317.28 - 308.57) = $216.07, 0.26% of net worth. If the report sends GOOG to 295 at the next open, the stop-market order fills near 295, and the loss is 24.8076 x (317.28 - 295.00) = $552.71, 0.67% of net worth. A stop caps the loss only if the price trades through the stop level; it does not cap a gap. A stop-limit order at the same level would not fill at all if the price opened below the limit, which is why Chapter 6 uses stop-market orders for the hard stops.

The market-fall case matters most for judging the plan. Dates marked as estimates come from calendars that project last year's schedule, so confirm them against the company's own announcement before relying on them. 7.3 The biggest risks and unknowns

Five risks could make this plan wrong, and each has a limit on what I know. The first is the market itself. The Correction label rests on a single level, the 200-day average at 7,291.31, which is 4.4% under the close, and a gap through it would trigger more selling. The second is the option marks: the options file is stale in places (it records AMZN at 215.40 against a ledger close of 248.00), so book values may not match what the market will pay. The third is the effective-exposure estimate, which counts options at 2.5 times value as a stand-in and may understate their true sensitivity; on recorded deltas, the LEAPS book alone is equivalent to about 126% of net worth in stock. The fourth is the greenfield gap: with no underwritten names the plan has no way to redeploy cash, and it could be early to be out of the market if a rally starts. The fifth is information about the businesses: no financial figures were refreshed for held names in this run, so a thesis could have changed without the audit knowing. For each, the response is a level or a date, not a guess.

What would change the plan as a whole

  • The S&P 500 closes above 7,699.64 for three sessions: the regime improves, and cash can fall toward 7.5% as names clear underwriting.
  • The S&P 500 closes below 7,291.31: raise cash toward 20%, sell the four Stage 3 leveraged funds and rerun the audit within two sessions.
  • The VIX closes above 22: tighten every ratchetable stop to the latest swing low and expect gaps.
  • Any held company announces a guidance cut, accounting issue or going-concern warning: treat it as an immediate exit regardless of price.

7.4 How often to rerun the audit

The audit should run again after any of four things: a session in which the S&P 500 closes through the 50-day or 200-day average, a stop that fires, the first name that clears underwriting, or four weeks without any of these. Between runs, the working rules are simple: check the closes against the stop table each Friday, move stops up and never down, and keep the cash overshoot until a vetted name needs it. The fourth trigger covers a quiet market: four weeks is the longest gap, because earnings reports change the financial gates and the stage of every holding changes slowly. The audit is a measurement, and measurements go stale.

What this means for you. You now have the rules, the dates and the levels in one place. Most weeks, the correct action is nothing: the stops are placed and the cash is waiting. The cost of not following the rules is the one you saw in Chapter 1: a book that behaves like $220,000 of stock exposure on an $82,000 net worth. The next audit should be the first with names to buy.

Glossary

Effective market exposure. The dollar size of the market bet your holdings add up to once beta, leverage and options are counted.

Beta. How many percent a holding moves, on average, for a 1% move in the S&P 500 over the past year.

Hidden overlap. Holdings that move together (one-year return correlation of 0.65 or more) and so act as one bet.

Risk-unit sizing. Setting a position size so a stop-out costs a fixed share of net worth, wider stops getting smaller positions.

Let-Winners-Run rule. A Stage 2 winner with intact earnings is not trimmed for RSI or weight alone, and may run up to 22% of net worth.

Stage 1 to 4. The four seasons of a stock: base, markup, topping or pullback, and decline.

Moving average. The average closing price over a set number of days, used as a trend line.

ATR. Average true range: the normal daily wobble of a stock in dollars.

RSI. A 0 to 100 speedometer of recent price moves; below 30 is oversold, above 70 overbought.

Stop-loss. An exit price set in advance; the order sells automatically when the price reaches it.

Daily-reset leveraged ETF. A fund that multiplies each day's index move by a fixed factor and resets every night, so multi-day results drift from the factor times the index.

LEAPS. Long-dated call options, with more than a year to expiry when bought.

Delta. The share of the stock's move an option captures; 0.40 means about 40 cents per dollar.

Wash sale. A loss disallowed for tax when a substantially identical position is bought within 30 days of the sale.

Put/call ratio. Put contracts traded divided by call contracts traded: a sentiment gauge.
