Three independent analysts produce the verdicts. The first judges the market and its sectors. The second audits your holdings and has no loyalty to your past choices. Keeping it blind is deliberate. An owner defends what he holds, while a buyer with only cash buys what passes the tests. Where the owner and the buyer disagree, the gap is the cost of past decisions, and Chapter 4 prices it.

Ten ideas carry the report. A moving average is the average closing price over recent weeks, like a line drawn through a bumpy road to show its direction; the 20-day line covers about a month, the 50-day line about ten weeks and the 200-day line about forty weeks. A stock moves through four stages, like seasons: Stage 2 is the rising phase, Stage 4 the falling phase, Stage 1 the quiet floor after a fall and Stage 3 the tired top before one. RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 means a stock has risen very fast and below 30 means it has fallen very fast. ATR, the average true range, is the size of a normal daily wobble in dollars, and it tells you how close a stop can sit before ordinary noise triggers it. Volume is the number of shares traded, a count of votes cast with money. A stop-loss is an exit price chosen before you need it, so a wrong idea costs a known amount. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, divides what you could gain by what you could lose: 3.5 means each dollar at risk could earn 3.5. Beta says how much a stock tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%. Position size is how much of your wealth sits in one idea, and it is the reason no single mistake should hurt you badly.

Two product types need an extra word. A leveraged fund promises two or three times one day’s move of an index or a stock and resets every day, so over weeks it can lose money even when what it follows ends flat; the SEC staff has warned that such funds may differ significantly from the index over periods longer than a day. A LEAPS is a call option with more than nine months to run, a ticket that pays only if the stock climbs above its strike price before the ticket expires. Free cash flow is the cash left after paying for the equipment a business needs, return on invested capital is profit divided by the money put into the business, and net debt is borrowings minus cash, measured against EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), a rough measure of operating profit.

Chapter 2: Active US Portfolio Health and Concentration Risk Audit

Concentration is the habit of letting one idea carry too much of your wealth. It matters because losses are lopsided: a position that falls 50% needs a 100% gain to recover, so the damage from one oversized mistake outlasts the pleasure from several small wins. The audit tests concentration three ways. The first is size: a new position is capped at 10.0% of net worth at cost, and any single stock at 15.0% after it has grown. The second is heat, the total you would lose if every stop were hit at once, which the rules cap at 12.0% of net worth. The third is overlap, covered in Chapter 1. On size, the book is fine: the largest position is TCEHY at 7.21% of net worth, and no holding is near 10.0%. On heat it fails today, at 15.07%.

Let-Winners-Run check. The rule that protects a compounding Stage 2 winner from mechanical trimming, up to 22.0% of net worth (the runaway-winner cap), has almost nothing to protect in this book. A winner qualifies only with a Stage 2 chart, intact fundamentals and stable or rising estimates. The ledger shows 2 holdings in Stage 2 today (Qualcomm and Strategy, and Strategy scores one of five on its underwriting checks), 8 in Stage 1, 9 in Stage 3, 11 in Stage 4 and 1 with no stage because its history is too short. 3.2 Risk-Unit Sizing: how a new position would be sized

Risk-Unit Sizing sets a position’s weight from the risk you are willing to take and not from how much you like the story. The formula is weight = the smaller of the tier’s maximum and the risk budget divided by the stop distance, where the budget is measured in basis points of net worth (1 basis point is 0.01%). A Tier 2 name has a budget of 75 basis points and a cap of 6.5%; a Tier 3 name has 50 basis points and a cap of 3.5%. A wide stop shrinks the position automatically, because the same dollar budget has to cover a larger loss per share. Portfolio heat, the total at risk across all stops, may not exceed 12.0%, and a new position may not exceed 10.0% of net worth at cost or 15.0% after it grows.

Worked example: sizing a hypothetical Bloom Energy purchase. Bloom’s close is $222.65 and its record stop is $205.21, a distance of 7.83%, or 783 basis points. The most that stop would cost is 13.15 x $17.44 = $229.33, or 0.27% of net worth. This is an illustration: Bloom is TIER_0 today, so the actual size is zero.

4.2 The three-condition swap test for every sale

A swap is a sale paired with a purchase, and the audit allows one only if three conditions are all true. Condition 1: the holding has triggered an exit or trim. Condition 2: a challenger has passed 100% of the gates and sits at support, no more than half an ATR above it. Condition 3: the challenger’s asymmetry is at least 1.5 times the holding’s, enough to pay for the capital gains friction. Every sale in the plan meets Condition 1 by construction. None meets Condition 2, because no shortlist name passes every gate, so no swap is made and the proceeds stay in cash. The nearest challenger by payoff among names not being sold is TCEHY at 11.66 to 1; TCEHY fails on the chart and the business checks, so Conditions 2 and 3 are false even though the ratio alone would clear the 1.5 times hurdle against most of the sold names.

6.4 Broker steps, gaps and the checklist

Schwab and Robinhood both accept resting orders, but neither offers a native trigger on the closing price. The practical way to apply a closing stop is to set a price alert at the stop level in the app, and to place the sale order at the next open if the alert fires and the day closes below it. If you prefer a resting order, place a stop or stop-limit sell with a duration of good till cancelled: it will fire on an intraday touch, which is slightly stricter than the audit’s rule and costs the occasional whipsaw. The menu names differ by app version, so look for order type, then stop or stop limit, then duration. For a limit sell, choose limit, enter the price in the table, and choose day or good till cancelled as the trigger column says. For the options, choose sell to close, limit, and the price shown, and read the quote before you confirm, because the spread is the cost.

Gaps are the main way a plan fails in practice. If a stock opens below a limit sell, the order does not fill and the position is now worth less than the limit; the rule is to convert it to a market order at the next open unless the position has fallen more than 20% below the limit, in which case stop and re-read the record. If a stock gaps through a stop, the sale fills at the open, not at the stop, and the loss is the gap. If a fund such as KORU or NBIL shows no bid at the open, wait ten minutes and use the limit at the bid rather than a market order. And if a catalyst date moves, the stop does not move with it. 7.3 Risks, unknowns and how often to re-run the audit

The plan can be wrong in several ways, and it is better to name them. The first is the rally risk. The regime label is an Uptrend, the S&P 500 is within 2% of its high, and the plan holds more than half of net worth in cash; if the index runs, the plan will look timid, and the scenario in Chapter 5 puts the cost of a 5% rally at about $6,285.66 of forgone gain. The second is the options mark: the book is valued at stale marks, live quotes imply a different total, and the five closures may realise less or more than the table says. The third is the tax unknown. Lot-level cost, holding periods and your bracket are not in the record, so the tax effect of the sales is N/A, and the wash-sale rule could bite if you buy back the same security within 30 days of a loss sale. The fourth is the holdings record itself: the transaction logs end on 1 October and 30 September, and any trade since is missing. The fifth is the research: earnings dates come from aggregators and the filing figures from published dossiers.

The audit should be re-run on a schedule and on triggers. On a schedule, run it weekly while the plan is being executed and monthly after that. On triggers, run it when the S&P 500 closes below its 50-day average for two sessions, when the VIX closes above 22, when two kept stops hit in a week, when a shortlist name passes its gates on a refreshed record, or when you trade. Before each run, update the transaction logs, because the audit replays them and a stale log is the single largest source of error. If you only remember five things from this report, remember these.

  2. If every stop and every option failed, you would lose 15.07% of net worth today and 6.76% after the plan; the ceiling is 12.0%.
  3. 17 equity positions and 5 option lines are sold because their own rules fired, not because the market looks bad.
  4. Nothing on the shortlist passes all three gates, so the plan buys nothing and holds the cash.
  5. Every kept position has a stop; the first dates to watch are 22 October (Bloom), 23 October (American Express) and 28 October (the Fed and Alphabet).

Glossary

The terms below are used throughout the report. Each is also explained where it first appears, so this list is a place to look something up when you are partway through a chapter.

Asymmetry (reward-to-risk)
The distance from the price to the target divided by the distance from the price to the stop. A figure of 3.0 means a possible gain three times the possible loss.
ATR (average true range)
The size of a normal daily price move in dollars, averaged over 14 days. Stops are placed in multiples of it so that ordinary noise does not trigger them.
Beta
How many percent a stock tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%, measured over one year.
Breadth
The share of stocks above a moving average, used to tell a broad rise from a narrow one.
Closing stop
A stop applied to the closing price: the position is sold at the next session only if the day ends below the level.
Correlation
A number from 0 to 1 that says how often two holdings move in the same direction. Above 0.65 the audit treats two holdings as one bet.
Daily-reset leveraged fund
A fund that targets a multiple of one day’s move and rebuilds its exposure every day, so over weeks its result can differ sharply from the multiple of the period’s move.
Delta (options)
How many dollars an option gains when the stock gains one dollar. Here it is estimated from implied volatility, not taken from a broker.
Effective market exposure
The size of the market position your holdings behave like once beta, leverage and options are counted, as a share of net worth.
Free cash flow margin
Cash left after equipment spending, as a share of sales.
Heat (portfolio heat)
The total dollars you would lose if every stop were hit at once, as a share of net worth.
LEAPS
A call option with more than nine months to run.
Lockstep
Holdings whose one-year returns correlate at 0.65 or higher, so that they rise and fall together.
Moving average
The average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 sessions, which shows the direction of a trend.
Regime
The audit’s label for the market’s trend, set from the S&P 500 against its 50-day and 200-day averages and from breadth.
Risk-Unit Sizing
A way to size a new position from how far its stop sits below the price, so that every position risks a similar share of net worth.
ROIC (return on invested capital)
Operating profit after tax divided by the money invested in the business.
Stage 1 to 4
The four phases of a stock: Stage 1 base, Stage 2 advance, Stage 3 top, Stage 4 decline.
Stock pay
Shares given to employees as pay, measured as a share of revenue; a high figure dilutes owners.
VIX
An index of the 30-day price movement that option traders expect for the S&P 500; the market’s fear gauge.

Equities are valued at the ledger close. The shortlist records carry their own prices from the 16 to 18 October underwriting, so their entry zones, stops and targets are used as written and the reward-to-risk ratios are recomputed at Tuesday’s close as (target minus close) divided by (close minus stop). Stops come from the underwriting record where one exists; otherwise the audit’s formula applies (Tactical and Leveraged: the lower of the 20-day swing low and the 20-day or 50-day average, less 0.5 ATR), and the funds the plan keeps without a record use the 200-day average. Money at risk is shares times the distance from the close to the stop, plus the full option mark. Effective market exposure is the position file’s figure; after the plan it is the kept equities’ exposure plus the kept options at the same multiple the file implies (about 2.5). The hidden-overlap clusters are the file’s own, built from one-year daily return correlations of 0.65 or higher.

Research came from the duck-duck-go search and fetch tools and from direct fetches, each recorded with its source address, as-of date and retrieval time. Participant-level flow data and a gamma-flip level were not obtained.
