Start here: what this audit is, and how to read it

This report is a check-up of your whole US portfolio on one date: the close of Friday 9 October 2026. It is not a live trading feed, and it does not tell you what the market will do next week. It asks three questions. Are the positions you own still behaving the way the rules say a healthy position should behave? Which of them should be sold, trimmed, or simply protected with a stop? And if you had only cash today, what would the rules buy? The report is built from three independent analysts who never see each other's conclusions. The first is the market analyst, who reads the broad index, the sector map and the number of stocks above their averages. The second is the holdings auditor, who checks each position against its own trend, momentum and stop. The third is the greenfield picker, who sees only the list of candidates and the cash you would have, and knows nothing about what you already hold. Its job is to remove the bias of owning something already.

Every verdict is a label, and every label has a rule behind it. HOLD / TRAIL means the position stays, and a trailing stop moves up as the price rises, so a fall below that stop closes the position. HOLD / HARD STOP means the position stays, but the stop is fixed and does not move up. TRIM means you sell part of a position. EXIT means you sell all of it. NO TRADE / WATCH means the name is interesting but does not yet meet the entry rule. KEEP_COMPOUNDING, ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP, TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING and REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER are the longer-term holding labels; IMMEDIATE_BUY, ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP, WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT and REJECTED are the labels for a candidate before it is bought.

A few terms do most of the work. A moving average is the average closing price over a fixed number of days, like a trend line drawn through the recent past. The 50-day average is a medium trend and the 200-day average is a long trend. A stock above a rising 50-day average is in an uptrend; a stock below a falling 200-day average is in a downtrend. Stage 1 to Stage 4 is a seasons model: Stage 1 is a base after a decline, Stage 2 is the markup season, Stage 3 is the top where growth stalls, and Stage 4 is the decline. RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer from 0 to 100; a reading below 30 means a stock has fallen unusually fast and above 70 means it has risen unusually fast. ATR, the average true range, measures a normal daily wobble in dollars. The stop-loss is a price you decide in advance at which you sell without debate; the audit sets it at the lower of a recent swing low or a key average, minus a fraction of the ATR. The point of the stop is to keep one bad position from hurting you badly, the same reason we size each position so that a single loss is survivable.

Risk and reward is the ratio of what you can win to what you can lose. The audit requires at least 3.5 to 1 for tactical names and at least 3.0 to 1 for core names before a buy is allowed. Position size limits the damage from any one idea: no single company may exceed 20 percent of net worth, and the report checks that before it recommends anything. The leveraged satellite is a bucket for leveraged exchange-traded funds, which move a multiple of the daily return of an index or stock. Those funds reset every day, so over weeks their returns can differ sharply from the multiple of the underlying move. A 3x fund can lose far more than three times the underlying's decline in a choppy market, so the audit caps this bucket at 15 percent of net worth. A LEAPS is a long-dated option; the options book is valued separately and is discussed in Chapter 2.

The report uses your transaction logs as the authoritative holdings record. The data run used the ledger close for every price, which is 9 October 2026; where the consolidated snapshot shows a different price, the ledger close is used and the difference is noted once in the methodology section. Read the report in two passes. On the first pass, read the lede and the three chapter essays, which take about fifteen minutes and give you the decision. On the second, read the case note for each holding, which takes roughly an hour and explains every verdict with its numbers and the level that would change it. Each table has a reading guide before it and a takeaway after it. A glossary and a methodology note close the report.

4 Funnel: how each candidate and holding was tested

The funnel is the set of tests a name passes or fails on the way to an order. There are three layers. The financial gate asks whether the business is sound, using the shortlist record's return on invested capital, free cash flow and leverage figures. The technical gate asks whether the price is in a trend the rules allow, using the ledger stage, the averages and the RSI. The asymmetry gate asks whether the reward is large enough compared with the risk, using the ratio that the audit computes from the ledger stop and the base target. A name must pass all three. The audit writes the test for every displacement and every non-displacement, because a reader should be able to see why one name replaced another, or why none did.

Consider one passing case and one failing case. Amazon passes the financial gate in the shortlist record, is in Stage 2 in the ledger, and has a reward-to-risk ratio of 3.10 at its limit, so it passes all three. D.R. Horton, a Stage 4 name with a shortlist record that reads ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP, fails the technical gate outright: a Stage 4 name is in decline, and the rule does not allow a buy in that state whatever the financial figures show. The gap between the two is the reason the funnel uses the technical layer as a hard filter rather than a weighting.

What would change this view:

  • If a challenger passes all three layers and its close is within half an ATR of support, record it as a displacement candidate, then test condition one against the holding it would replace.
  • If no holding meets condition one, do not displace anything, even if a challenger looks attractive; the cash stays cash until a trigger fires.

The displacement test asks whether a candidate should replace an existing holding. The rule requires three conditions. Condition one: the holding triggered an exit or a trim. Condition two: the challenger passed all gates and is at support within half an ATR. Condition three: the challenger's asymmetry exceeds the holding's by at least 1.5 times, after capital-gains friction. For this run, condition one is met for the four EXIT names and the NFXL trim. Condition two is not met by any shortlist name, because none is within half an ATR of support while also passing all gates; Amazon is closest, at 0.56 ATR, which is just outside the half-ATR line. So no displacement is recommended. The four exits release cash for the policy band rather than being swapped into new names. That is the honest answer: the rules did not find a clean replacement, so the cash stays cash.

Friction matters for every sale. Commissions are negligible at most US brokers, but the bid-ask spread on a leveraged fund can be a meaningful share of a small position, and the audit's exit sizes are modest enough that the spread is a few dollars per trade. Capital-gains tax is the larger unknown. The report has no lot-level data, so it cannot say whether the sale of a position with a large gain would fall into a short-term or long-term tax bracket, and it shows the amount N/A rather than an estimate. Holding periods are not recorded in the ledger, so the audit cannot check whether any sale would cross the one-year threshold. The consequence is that the phase-one sales should be checked against your own tax records before they are placed, which is listed in the orders in Chapter 6.

What would change this view:

  • If a Stage 4 core name is a displacement candidate only after its close reclaims its 50-day average, the displacement test is re-run with the new close.
  • If the challenger's asymmetry exceeds the holding's by 1.5 times or more and the challenger is within half an ATR of its support, a displacement is allowed once the tax check is complete.

6 Orders: the plan in the order to place it

This chapter turns the verdicts into orders. The orders are the deltas from Chapter 5 and nothing else: each sell, each buy, and each stop is a line in the bridge. They are written as plain instructions, with a trigger, an expiry, and what to do if the order does not fill. The sequence matters. Place the sales first, so the proceeds exist when the buy is placed. Place the stops after the trades fill, so each stop is set against the real cost basis. Keep the buy as a limit order rather than a market order, so you never pay more than the limit.

A limit order is an instruction to buy or sell only at a stated price or better; a market order fills at whatever the market gives. A stop order becomes a market sell when the price trades through the stop level. A good-till-cancelled (GTC) order stays open until it fills or you cancel it; a day order expires at the close. Schwab and Robinhood both support limit and stop orders, and both allow a GTC expiry on equities and most ETFs. Targets must be anchored to a measured move or to forward earnings times a three-year median multiple; the base targets in this report are the shortlist's targets, and they are flagged as not refreshed in this run. The asymmetry hurdle is 3.5 to 1 for tactical names and 3.0 to 1 for core names. Glossary

Each term used in the report carries the meaning given here. The 50-day average is the mean closing price of the last fifty sessions, a medium trend line. The 200-day average is the same over two hundred sessions, a long trend line. Stage 1 is a base after a decline, Stage 2 is an uptrend markup, Stage 3 is a top where growth stalls, and Stage 4 is a decline. RSI is the relative strength index, a 0 to 100 gauge of how far and how fast a price has moved, where below 30 reads as oversold and above 70 as overbought. ATR is the average true range, a measure of the typical daily move in dollars. A stop is a pre-set price that closes a position. A trailing stop moves up with the price; a hard stop stays put. Reward to risk, or asymmetry, is the ratio of the distance to the base target to the distance to the stop. A limit order fills only at the stated price or better. A good-till-cancelled order stays open until it fills or is cancelled. A leveraged fund holds derivatives to deliver a multiple of the daily return of an index or stock, and it resets each day. LEAPS are long-dated options, usually more than a year to expiry. Money at risk is the amount that would be lost if every stop were hit at its level. Net worth is the consolidated equity and cash from the broker snapshot plus the options book, valued at the ledger close.
