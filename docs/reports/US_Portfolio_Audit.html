Each chapter ends with a short note on what it means for you and a pointer to the next one.

Three independent analysts produce the verdicts. The first judges the market and its sectors. The second audits your holdings and has no loyalty to your past choices. The third is a greenfield picker that is told nothing about what you own, what you paid or what you have gained, and answers one question: if you held 100% cash, what would you buy from the vetted shortlist? Keeping it blind is deliberate. An owner defends his stocks, while a buyer with only cash buys what passes the tests. When the owner and the buyer disagree, the gap between them is the cost of your past decisions, and Chapter 4 prices it.

Ten ideas carry the report. A moving average is the average closing price over recent weeks, like a line drawn through a bumpy road to show its direction. The 20-day line covers about a month, the 50-day line about ten weeks and the 200-day line about forty weeks. A stock moves through four stages, like seasons. Stage 2 is the rising phase, with the price above rising 50-day and 200-day averages. Stage 4 is the falling phase. Stage 1 is the quiet floor after a fall and Stage 3 is the tired top before one. RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 means a stock has risen very fast and below 30 means it has fallen very fast. ATR, the average true range, is the size of a normal daily wobble in dollars, and it tells you how close a stop can sit before ordinary noise triggers it.

Volume is the number of shares traded, a count of votes cast with money. A stop-loss is an exit price chosen before you need it, so a wrong idea costs a known amount. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, divides what you could gain by what you could lose: a ratio of 3.5 means each dollar at risk could earn 3.5. Beta says how much a stock tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%. A leveraged fund promises two or three times one day’s move of an index or a stock and resets every day, so over weeks it can lose money even when the thing it follows ends flat, a drag the report calls decay. A LEAPS is a call option with more than nine months to run, a ticket that pays only if the stock climbs above its strike price before the ticket expires.

Fourteen working terms appear in the case notes. The momentum gate is a three-part chart test: it asks whether a stock is already trending up, is turning up from a flat stretch of trading, or is bouncing from a floor after a fall. A reading of no momentum means it passes none of the three, and entering momentum means it passes the turn-up or the floor-bounce test, which the report calls a turnaround setup. A swing low is the lowest price of the last 20 sessions, the floor the chart has just defended. A base is a flat stretch of trading after a fall, where a stock rests before its next move, and a breakout is a close above the top of that base. The entry zone is the price band in which a purchase is allowed. A limit order is an order that fills only at your price or better, and a stop loss is an order that sells when the price falls to a level you chose in advance. The operating margin is the share of sales left after the costs of running the business. FCF, short for free cash flow, is the cash left after paying for the equipment and buildings the business needs. Net debt is borrowings minus cash, and EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit. ROIC, return on invested capital, is profit divided by the money put into the business, and SBC is stock-based compensation, pay handed out in shares. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share.

The audit uses labels with fixed triggers. EXIT means sell the whole holding because a trend-failure rule, a stop or an underwriting verdict has fired. TRIM means sell part of it. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep it, add nothing and sell everything if it closes below a stated price. HOLD / TRAIL means keep it and lift the exit level as the 50-day average rises. NO TRADE / WATCH means the position is too small or too unvetted to act on. Two sizing rules matter. Risk-Unit Sizing sets a new position’s weight from how far its stop sits below the price, so wide stops get small positions. The Let-Winners-Run rule stops you from trimming a healthy Stage 2 winner just because it has risen, and lets it grow to 22.0% of net worth. 2.2 The trim arithmetic: how the size of the one trim is set

Most sales in this plan are all-or-nothing, because a failed trend has no partial answer. TCEHY is the only position that gets a trim, and its size comes from arithmetic that you can check. The Core thesis checkpoint says that a Core name in Stage 4 whose analyst estimates are falling has failed two of the three tests that justify holding it for years, so the position is cut by about half. Half of 115 shares is 57.5, which is not a tradeable number, and it would split the position unevenly between your two accounts. The plan sells 55 shares instead, the 45 shares in Robinhood plus 10 in Schwab. That empties the smaller lot and leaves 60 shares in Schwab, where the position is easier to manage under a single stop.

Worked example: the TCEHY trim and what it does to the Core bucket

TCEHY closed at $52.75. Selling 55 shares frees 55 × $52.75 = $2,901.25. The 60 shares that remain are worth $3,165.00, or 3.83% of net worth, down from 7.34%. The Core bucket falls from 44.17% to 40.66%, which is 0.66% points above the 40% ceiling of its mandate. The plan does not sell more to close that gap, because no further Core name trips a rule. At the average cost of $60.17 a share, the 55 shares carry a loss of about -$408.36; the log has no lot dates, so the tax effect is N/A. The stop on the remaining 60 shares is $51.27, which risks 60 × ($52.75 - $51.27) = $88.80, or 0.11% of net worth.

The consequence is that TCEHY’s risk shrinks in two ways at once. Fewer shares are exposed, and the shares that remain sit only 2.8% above a hard stop, so even a disorderly week costs little. If TCEHY then recovers, you still own 60 shares and lose only the gain on the half you sold. That asymmetry, a small cost if the call is wrong and a large saving if it is right, is the reason to trim rather than exit. 2.3 Applying the Let-Winners-Run rule to compounding winners

The Let-Winners-Run rule exists to stop a mechanical habit: selling a stock because it has gone up. It protects a healthy Stage 2 winner whose analysts still raise their forecasts, and it lets that stock grow to 22.0% of net worth while the stop trails a rising average. A sale in this plan may look like a violation if it removes a position that is showing a large gain, so the test is run on every holding with a gain of 20% or more on cost. Seven qualify: TQQQ, SPXL, FAS, UDOW, ASX, MSTR and TARK. The rule has three conditions: the ledger must show Stage 2, the 50-day average must be rising, and estimates must be stable or rising. A fund has no estimates, so a daily-reset fund can never pass the third test; that is deliberate, because decay erodes a leveraged fund even when it is winning.

Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine that tonight your whole account turned into cash, $82,699.61 and nothing else. You do not remember what you paid, which stocks you liked or how much you have gained. You open the vetted shortlist and decide what to buy at Friday’s prices. That is the greenfield test, and it matters because holding something is a different decision from buying it. An owner carries three biases: the pain of selling at a loss, the pride in a winner and the cost of tax. A buyer carries none of them. If the portfolio you own is not the portfolio you would choose today, the gap is the price of your past decisions, and the audit’s job is to say how large it is.

The picker follows the policy to the letter and sees no holdings data. It sizes each purchase by Risk-Unit Sizing: the weight is the smaller of the tier’s maximum and a risk budget in basis points (hundredths of a percent of net worth) divided by the distance from entry to stop. A TIER_1 name has a 100 basis point budget and a 10% ceiling, TIER_2 has 75 and 6.5%, TIER_3 has 50 and 3.5%, TIER_4 has 35 and 2%, and TIER_0 gets nothing. A name must also clear its asymmetry floor (3.0 for Core, 3.5 for Tactical), sit within 1.2 ATR of support to be sized normally, and pass the hard red lines. The regime adds a layer: in a Correction, new money goes only to Stage 2 names within half an ATR of support.

Zero-holding-bias framework: this allocation is produced by the shortlist capital allocator using only the shortlist records and the ledger. It has no information about positions, cost bases or gains. The question it answers is what the best conviction-weighted portfolio of $82,699.61 in pure cash would be today.

3.2 How risk-unit sizing would size a purchase

Risk-Unit Sizing starts from how much of your net worth you are willing to lose if the stop is hit, not from how much you like the stock. Stocks with wide stops get small positions and stocks with tight stops get large ones, so the dollar risk stays steady. The formula is: weight equals the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. It protects against the common mistake of sizing by conviction alone: a name you love with a stop 15% below the price should still be a small position. Because no name qualifies today, the worked example that follows is a hypothetical that shows how the rule would work if ANET cleared its gates as a TIER_2 name.

Chapter 4: US Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

The funnel is the process that turns a name on a list into a purchase, and it has three gates in a fixed order. The financial gate asks whether the business is sound: return on invested capital of at least 12%, free cash flow margin of at least 12%, net debt under 3.0 times EBITDA and stock-based compensation under 5% of sales, with exceptions the rules define. 4.3 Friction: tax, trading costs and what the log does not tell us

The transaction log records quantities and costs but not purchase dates by lot, so the holding period and the tax rate are N/A. Chapter 6: 3-Phase Actionable Execution Orders

An audit is useful only if it ends in orders you can place. This chapter turns the money bridge into three phases. Phase 1 sells and trims, the only phase with money in it today. Phase 2 would buy, but no name passes, so it is a map of the conditions under which a buy would be allowed. Phase 3 puts a defensive stop under every share you keep. The orders are exactly the deltas of Chapter 5, nothing more and nothing less. They are instructions for Monday 19 October 2026, the next session, and every limit price is Friday’s close, so an order fills if the market opens at or above it.

Glossary

  • ATR (average true range): The size of a normal daily price swing in dollars; stops are placed in multiples of it.
  • Asymmetry / reward-to-risk: What you could gain divided by what you could lose, from the target and the stop.
  • Beta: How much a stock tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%.
  • Correction (regime label): The S&P 500 is below its 50-day average and above its 200-day average.
  • Daily-reset (leveraged) fund: A fund that targets two or three times one day’s move and resets daily, so it can lose money over weeks even if the underlying ends flat; the loss is called decay.
  • Delta: How much an option’s price moves for a $1 move in the stock.
  • Effective market exposure: The size of the S&P 500 position your portfolio behaves like, after beta, leverage and options.
  • Gap: A price that opens far from the previous close, so a stop can fill below its trigger.
  • Heat: Total money at risk if every stop is hit, as a share of net worth; the ceiling is 12.0%.
  • KILL line: The options rule that closes a contract once it has lost half of its cost or its underlying breaks support.
  • LEAPS: A call option with more than nine months to expiry when bought.
  • Let-Winners-Run rule: A Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals is not trimmed for being up; it may grow to 22.0% of net worth.
  • Lockstep cluster: Holdings whose one-year daily returns correlate at 0.65 or more and so act as one bet.
  • Moving average: The average closing price over 20, 50 or 200 days; a line that shows the trend.
  • RSI: A 0-100 speedometer of recent price change; under 30 is oversold, over 70 overbought.
  • Risk-Unit Sizing: Weight equals the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance.
  • Stage 1-4: Weinstein stages: base, uptrend, top, downtrend.
  • Stop-loss: A price chosen in advance at which you sell because the idea is wrong.
  • Wash-sale rule: A loss is disallowed if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale.
