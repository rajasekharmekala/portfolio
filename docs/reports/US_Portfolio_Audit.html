Start here: what this audit is and how to read it

Before the first table, a handful of company terms are worth fixing in mind. Free cash flow is the cash left after a business pays for its operations and its equipment, and FCF (free cash flow) margin is that cash divided by revenue. ROIC (return on invested capital) is how much profit a company earns on the money invested in it. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a rough measure of operating profit, and net debt is borrowings minus cash, so net debt divided by EBITDA says how many years of operating profit it would take to repay what the company owes. EPS (earnings per share) is profit divided by the number of shares. SBC (stock-based compensation) is pay in shares, which dilutes the owners. Gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of the product, and operating margin is the share left after all running costs. ARR (annual recurring revenue) is the yearly value of subscriptions, GMV (gross merchandise value) is the total value of goods sold through a marketplace, book-to-bill is orders received divided by sales shipped, and guidance is the forecast a company gives for its next quarter or year. A base is a flat stretch of trading after a decline where a stock builds strength, Stage 2 is the rising trend that follows it and Stage 4 is the decline. A limit order is an order to buy or sell at a stated price or better, and asymmetry is the lopsidedness of a bet, measured as reward-to-risk: what you can win divided by what you can lose.

This report checks every position you own against a fixed set of rules, using prices from the close of Friday 23 October 2026, and then says what to sell, what to keep and why. It is a set of if-then rules applied to numbers that were measured, not a forecast, so every decision can be traced back to the figure that caused it. You can read the key figures and the lede in ten minutes. Reading the chapters in order takes about an hour and teaches the method, so that by the end you could explain each verdict in your own words.

Three independent analysts produced it. The first reads the market: is it rising, falling or stuck, and which sectors lead. The second audits what you hold, one position at a time, and ignores how long you have owned it or what you paid. The third, the greenfield picker, answers a thought experiment: if you held only cash today, what would you buy? It is not allowed to know your holdings, so it cannot make excuses for them. The audit then compares the three answers and writes the orders that close the gap.

A few ideas carry the whole report. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, like a trend line drawn through a bumpy road; the 50-day and 200-day lines matter most. Stages are the four seasons of a stock: Stage 1 is a flat base after a decline, Stage 2 is the markup season when the price climbs above rising averages, Stage 3 is the topping or pullback season and Stage 4 is the decline below falling averages. RSI is a speedometer from 0 to 100: below 30 means the stock fell unusually fast and above 70 means it rose unusually fast. ATR (average true range) is a stock's normal daily wobble in dollars, which is why stops are placed more than one wobble below support. Volume is the count of shares that changed hands, a tally of votes cast with money.

A stop-loss is an exit price chosen before you buy, so a wrong idea costs a known amount. A stop in this report is judged on the daily close: if the close is below the level, you sell at the next open. Risk and reward compare what you can win with what you can lose; a new Core position needs at least 2.0 to 1 and a Tactical one 2.5 to 1. Position size is how much of your money sits in one name. New positions use Risk-Unit Sizing: the weight is the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget in basis points (hundredths of a percent of net worth) divided by the distance to the stop, so a wide stop earns a smaller position. A single stock may not exceed 15% of net worth, and the Let-Winners-Run rule lets a Stage 2 winner with intact earnings grow to 22% before anyone trims it, because cutting a compounding winner only for rising is a mistake.

Two product types need extra care. A daily-reset leveraged ETF such as TQQQ or KORU multiplies each day's move by two or three and resets every night, so over a choppy month it can lose money while the index ends flat. A LEAPS option is a call with more than a year of life when bought; it moves with the stock but can lose its whole value. Two readings come from the portfolio file. Effective market exposure is how large a market bet your holdings behave like once beta (how hard a stock moves when the market moves), leverage and options are counted. Hidden overlap flags holdings whose daily returns moved together over a year, so they act as one bet.

Every table row carries an action label with a trigger. EXIT sells the whole position: a failed rule that cannot be repaired by waiting, such as a Stage 4 breakdown. TRIM sells part of it, down to a size ceiling or ahead of a dated event. HOLD / HARD STOP keeps the shares with a written exit price and no new money. NO TRADE / WATCH means do nothing yet, with the level that would change that. The verdict words come from the underwriting records: KEEP_COMPOUNDING, ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP, TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING and REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER for holdings, and IMMEDIATE_BUY, ACCUMULATE_ON_DIP, WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT and REJECTED for candidates. Concentration is how much of your money rides on one idea, and it is the quickest way a sound portfolio becomes a fragile one. 2.5 Case notes: one for every holding, in ladder order

A case note is the short story behind each verdict. It says what the company or fund does, why it is on this page, what the numbers show and what they mean, the rule that produced the verdict and the price that would change it. The notes follow ladder order, so the exits come first. Read them with the ladder open: the table gives the numbers in a grid and the notes give the reasoning. Business descriptions come from the shortlist record or the sponsor's own page, never from memory, and a few dated events carry a link to the page that supports them.

Three things recur and are worth knowing in advance. The stop levels are judged on the daily close, and gap risk is real: a report can open a stock well under its stop, and the sale then fills at the opening price. The leveraged funds are scored by rules that do not apply to ordinary shares, because they reset every night and can lose value in a sideways market. And six names carry a note that they have no current underwriting record, which means the verdict is the audit's own and a full pipeline run would be a better basis before any add.

2.6 Money at risk if every stop is hit

A stop protects a single position, but the number that protects the portfolio is the sum of all of them. The scoring file sets a ceiling on that sum, called portfolio heat, of 12.0% of net worth. It is a budget for the distance between each close and its stop, so it measures the loss if every stop filled exactly at its level; a close-basis exit sells at the next open, and a gap can make the fill worse, so a gap-adjusted figure is shown beside it. The calculation multiplies the shares in each position by the distance from the close to its stop. Options have no clean stop, so the audit counts the whole premium as at risk, which is the maximum loss on a long call. Positions with no stop level in the underwriting record use the stop formula from the scoring file, and the table says which.

2.7 The LEAPS options book

A LEAPS option is a long-dated call that gives you the right, not the duty, to buy 100 shares at a fixed strike price before a fixed date. It moves with the stock but with more leverage, and it can fall to zero. Your ten lines are 16.00% of net worth against a 10% hard maximum for the options bucket, which makes the bucket the single largest breach in the book. The marks are the figures in your options file; no option chain was priced in this audit, so live deltas and implied volatility are not known and sale values are estimates. Three lines (DOCN, CRWV and ANET) carry a mark exactly equal to cost, which suggests the mark was never updated, and CRWV in particular has fallen sharply since.

Two tests are used. The options skill sells a LEAPS line (its KILL rule) when the option has lost 50% or more with a broken trend, when the underlying closes under its 200-day average, or when delta falls under 0.25 with fewer than 120 days left. The second is the expiry-matched test from the WMT underwriting: take the shortlist's bear, base and bull prices for the underlying, subtract the strike, and see whether the call is worth more than its current mark at expiry in enough of the scenarios. A call with 84 days left on a stock 29% under its strike fails almost any version of that test. A call with 237 days left on a stock 21% under its strike passes in the bull case only.

3.1 Risk-Unit Sizing: how big a first position should be

Risk-Unit Sizing answers how big an initial position should be, and it starts from the stop and not from conviction. The scoring file gives the rule: weight equals the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget in basis points divided by the stop distance in basis points, times 100. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent of net worth. Tier 1 carries a budget of 100 basis points and a ceiling of 10%, Tier 2 carries 75 and 6.5%, Tier 3 carries 50 and 3.5%, and Tier 4 carries 35 and 2.0%. The idea is that every new position should cost about the same if its stop is hit, so a wide stop earns a small position and a tight stop earns a larger one, up to the cap. The record's own risk-unit weight is kept where it is lower than the formula, because the underwriting run may have trimmed it for a reason such as customer concentration or Taiwan production risk.

The entry price used is the top of the limit zone, which is the worst price at which the order can fill, and the shares are rounded down to whole shares. Whole-share rounding applies to seven of the eight picks; MELI is the one fractional starter, because its 1.75% record cap buys 0.83 of a $1,728.10 share, which both brokers accept. A portfolio heat ceiling of 12.0% sits above all of it: the total money lost if every stop fires may not exceed that. The eight picks together risk $1,380.79 (1.68% of net worth) if every stop is hit.

Worked example: sizing COF

Capital One's limit zone runs from $192.30 to $198.40 with a stop at $184.73 and a base target of $238.00. The worst fill is $198.40, so the stop distance is $13.67, which is 6.89% of the price, or 689 basis points. A Tier 3 budget of 50 basis points divided by 689 gives 7.26%, but the tier ceiling is 3.5%, so the weight is 3.50%, or $2,882.91. At $198.40 that buys 14 whole shares for $2,777.60. If the stop is hit, the loss is 14 x $13.67 = $191.38, which is 23.2 basis points of net worth. If the target is reached, the gain is 14 x $39.60 = $554.40, a reward-to-risk of 2.90 to 1, which clears the 2.0 to 1 Core floor. TSM shows the other side: its stop at $359.92 is 1221 basis points from $410.00, so the formula allows 3.50%, and the record's lower figure of 2.5% rules, giving 5 shares for $2,050.00.

The consequence is that the wide stop is the price of owning a volatile name through ordinary swings, and the formula passes that cost to the position size automatically. With that in hand, the target table shows what the picker would own.

Chapter 4: US Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

The funnel is the path a stock must travel before it earns your money. It has three gates, and the order matters. The financial gate asks whether the business is solvent and earns real cash: for a US compounder that means return on invested capital of at least 12%, free-cash-flow margin of at least 12%, net debt under 3 times operating profit and stock-based pay under 8% of revenue, with softer tests for turnarounds, banks and funds. The technical gate asks whether the price is in a structure that rewards buying: only a Stage 4 breakdown with no base is an outright rejection, while a pullback or an extended price is routed to a limit order in a support zone. The asymmetry gate asks whether the possible gain is at least 2.0 times the possible loss for Core and 2.5 times for Tactical or turnaround names, measured at the price where you would actually buy.

A stock that fails a hard red line is rejected; one that merely arrives at the wrong price waits. That distinction is why 13 of the shortlist's candidates carry conditional passes: they clear the business tests and wait for a price. Layer 2 sits beside the gates and scores three to five company-specific measures (for example Azure growth for Microsoft or net charge-offs for Capital One) as pass, watch or fail, and a name qualifies when the weighted score is at least 3.0 of 5.0 with no more than one fail. This chapter shows how the 13 candidates fared, then tests each sale in Chapter 2 against the swap rule, which is the only moment the audit replaces one holding with another.

The funnel is built on the shortlist record file, whose rows were written by the underwriting pipeline on 23 October. The ledger supplies the technical numbers (the momentum gate, support distance and stage), and the research record supplies dates and catalysts. Where the record and the ledger differ on a price, the ledger's close is used and the record's reward-to-risk is recomputed from its own stop and target. Both agree on every close, so the recomputed ratios match the records.

4.2 The three-condition swap test applied to every sale

A swap replaces a holding with a challenger, and it needs all three conditions. First, the holding has triggered an exit or a trim. Second, the challenger has passed all the gates and is strictly at support, within 0.5 ATR. Third, the challenger's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's, after friction. A sale without a swap is not a failure of the test: it means the cash waits. The audit sells 15 positions (five exits and ten trims) and nine option lines plus two AMZN contracts, and it swaps none, for one repeated reason: no challenger meets condition 2 today.

The closest challenger is COF. It passes the gates, its momentum gate reads ENTERING_MOMENTUM, and its reward-to-risk is 2.21 to 1 at the close. Chapter 5: Master Capital Reallocation and Money Bridge (Holding vs Target)

A money bridge shows where every dollar was, where it goes and why the totals still add up. It exists because a plan that sells in one place and buys in another can hide a leak: a rounding error, a forgotten position or a purchase funded twice. The bridge in this chapter has three parts. The cash waterfall starts from the $2,780.20 you hold today and adds each sale in the order the dates fall, ending at the cash balance after every order. The holding-to-target table sets each position's current value beside its target value and gives the difference. The scenarios ask what the plan does if things go wrong.

Three conventions keep the arithmetic honest. All current values use the ledger close of 23 October, and the sales are valued at that close even though most limits sit a little above it, so the true proceeds are likely to be slightly higher. Option lines are valued at the mark in your options file, which three lines carry at cost and one (SOFI) is priced by its underwriting at a lower figure. And nothing in the table is invented: the sums of Chapter 2's sales, Chapter 3's staged orders and Chapter 6's order list must match to the cent, and the totals row says so. Brokerage commissions are not in the log and are taken as zero; exchange fees on a sale are a few cents and are not modelled.

Chapter 6: 3-Phase Actionable Execution Orders

Orders are the plan in a form a broker can execute. Every order in this chapter is the difference between a row in Chapter 5's current column and its target column, nothing more. Each has a trigger (the date or price at which to place it), a size, a limit and an expiry, and each says what to do if it does not fill. The phases follow the order of urgency. Phase 1 sells and trims, starting with the positions whose reports arrive first. Phase 2 stages the limit buys, none of which is live on Monday. Two kinds of order do most of the work. Glossary

Effective market exposure. The dollar size of the market bet your holdings behave like once each holding's beta, its leverage and the options book are counted. The portfolio file multiplies weight by the larger of the one-year beta and the stated leverage, and counts options at 2.5 times their value.

Hidden overlap or lockstep cluster. A group of holdings whose daily returns have moved together (correlation of 0.65 or more over a year), so they act as one combined bet.

Beta. How hard a holding moves when the market moves: beta 2 means a 1% market fall usually costs 2%.

Correlation. A number from -1 to +1 showing how closely two holdings' daily returns move together; +1 means in step.

Risk-Unit Sizing. The rule for sizing a new position: weight is the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget in basis points divided by the stop distance in basis points, times 100.

Basis point. One hundredth of a percent; 50 basis points of net worth on $82,368.77 is $411.84.

Portfolio heat. The total money lost if every stop is hit, as a share of net worth; the ceiling is 12.0%.

Tier ceiling. The largest share of net worth a name may hold, set by the underwriting record: Tier 1 up to 10%, Tier 2 6.5%, Tier 3 3.5%, Tier 4 2.0%, Tier 0 none.

Let-Winners-Run rule. A Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals and stable or rising estimates is not trimmed only for RSI above 70 or a weight above 15%; it may grow to 22% while its stop trails the 21-day average or 50-day average.

Stage 1 to 4. The four seasons of a stock: Stage 1 a base, Stage 2 a rising trend, Stage 3 a top or pullback, Stage 4 a decline.

RSI. The relative strength index, a 0 to 100 speedometer of how fast a price has moved; under 30 is oversold, over 70 is overbought.

ATR. Average true range, a stock's normal daily move in dollars, used to place stops outside ordinary noise.

Moving average. The average closing price over a set number of days, drawn as a trend line.

Daily-reset leveraged ETF. A fund that targets a multiple (two or three times) of an index's or stock's return for one day and resets nightly, so it can lose money over longer periods even if the index is flat.

LEAPS. Long-dated call options with more than a year of life at purchase; they move with the stock but can lose all their value.

KILL test (options). The options skill's rule to sell a LEAPS line when it has lost 50% or more with a broken trend, when the underlying closes under its 200-day average, or when delta is under 0.25 with fewer than 120 days left.

Expiry-matched test. Comparing the bear, base and bull prices for a stock with an option's strike at expiry to see whether the option pays in enough of the scenarios.

Limit order. An order to buy or sell at a stated price or better; it protects the price but not the fill.

Good-till-cancelled (GTC). An order that stays open until it fills or you cancel it.

Gap risk. The chance that a price opens past a stop or limit after a report or overnight news, so the fill is worse than the stop.

Wash-sale rule. A US tax rule that can disallow a loss if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale.

ROIC, FCF margin, SBC. Return on invested capital, free-cash-flow margin (free cash flow divided by revenue) and stock-based compensation as a share of revenue.

Verdict words and action labels. In the underwriting records REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER means selling the whole holding (EXIT here) or, for DOCN, only its excess over the ceiling, TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING means a trim to a ceiling (TRIM here, not a profit harvest), KEEP_COMPOUNDING and WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT on a holding mean hold with a stop (HOLD / HARD STOP), although WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT can accompany a trim to the ceiling, as for SPXL, and in the order tables a sale is called Sell and a stop-based exit a close-basis exit.

Reward-to-risk. The distance from the entry price to the target divided by the distance from the entry price to the stop.

Prices, averages, RSI, ATR, stages, the regime label, breadth, sector quadrants and the Treasury curve come from a facts ledger built for this run for the session of 23 October 2026. The run happened on Saturday 24 October, so the Friday close is the latest completed session, and the S&P 500's final bar for that date was verified. Prices are daily bars from Yahoo Finance through the yfinance library; moving averages are simple, and RSI and ATR use Wilder's 14-day method. Treasury yields come from the Federal Reserve Economic Data service and are dated 22 October, one session behind, so they are labelled as prior-session. The universe is the S&P 1500 (1,497 names with enough history for the breadth count). The ledger's S&P 500 close of 7,623.68 matches the Yahoo Finance page cited in the references.

Quantities and cost come from replaying the Schwab and Robinhood transaction logs, which are treated as the authoritative record and assumed accurate; both accounts show their last transaction on 2026-10-23, and no freshness warning arose. The portfolio file is the record of truth for the net worth of $82,368.77, which is the snapshot total of $69,192.30 plus the options book of $13,176.47. The options marks are the figures in your options file; no option chain was priced, so live deltas and implied volatility are unknown. Business tests (ROIC, free-cash-flow margin, debt, stock-based pay, Layer 2 scores, reward-to-risk and stops) come from the shortlist and watchlist records written on 23 October, and the published dossiers carry the source documents. Dates for reports come from the Yahoo Finance calendar, company pages and sponsor pages, with the research record holding each URL and as-of date. No record file was changed by this run.

Data gaps limit the conclusions in six ways. Six holdings (SKHY, GGLL, KORU, FAS, ETHE and NKE) have no current underwriting record, so their business tests are not run and their stops come from the scoring-file formulas. The portfolio metadata names several products inconsistently with their sponsors: it lists GGLL as a 1.5X fund (the Direxion page says 2X), KORU and NBIL with a sponsor name the sponsors do not use, and SKHY as Shin-Etsu Handotai / SK Hynix; the audit follows the sponsor pages and flags the metadata for correction. The effective-exposure arithmetic uses one-year betas and a 2.5 times proxy for options. Lot-level holding periods, tax rates and commissions are not in the log, so tax and friction are stated as not known. Reuters and the Associated Press blocked automated access, so the market narrative rests on the Yahoo Finance page, the Cboe statistics page and the same-session market report. The Cboe intraday table and two other vendors disagree on the total put/call ratio (0.86 against 0.93); all readings sit in the neutral range, and the exchange figure is used.

The reward-to-risk ratios in Chapters 3 and 4 are recomputed from each record's own stop and target and match the records. The bucket bands follow the scoring file; the skill text states different bands for the Tactical and Leveraged buckets, and the scoring file is the single source of truth. Sale proceeds are valued at the 23 October close, and most limits sit above it. The correlation clusters after the plan are recomputed from the fifteen strongest pairs in the portfolio file, so weaker links could join clusters the file leaves apart.
