Three independent analysts wrote the audit. The first reads the market and sets the posture. The second, the holdings auditor, tries to find a reason to sell every position you own. The vocabulary is short. A moving average is the average closing price over a number of days, like a trend line drawn through a noisy road; the 50-day line is the medium trend and the 200-day line the long one. A Stage is one of four seasons of a stock: Stage 1 is a quiet base, Stage 2 an uptrend, Stage 3 a top and Stage 4 a decline. The RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100; under 30 means the stock has fallen unusually fast and over 70 unusually fast upward. The ATR (average true range) is the normal daily wobble in dollars, and stops are placed a fraction of it below a support level so ordinary noise does not trigger them. Volume counts how many people voted with their money. The put/call ratio is a fear gauge: puts are bets on a fall, calls are bets on a rise. A stop-loss is an exit price decided in advance. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, is the gain you can reasonably expect divided by the loss you accept; the floor here is 2.0 to 1 for Core Alpha and 2.5 to 1 for Tactical Momentum. Position size is how much of your money sits in one idea, and the point of the whole audit is that no single idea should hurt you badly.

Three terms are specific to this book. A daily-reset leveraged fund (TQQQ, SPXL, KORU and others) promises two or three times one day's move of its index or stock, then resets the next morning, so over weeks its return can differ a lot from the multiple you expect, much as a car that doubles its speed each time you press the pedal also doubles how fast it can leave the road. A LEAPS option is a long-dated call option, a rented right to buy a stock at a fixed price, which loses value every day through time decay and can fall to zero. Effective market exposure is how many dollars of market movement each dollar of your portfolio behaves like once volatility, leverage and options are counted.

Each label in the audit has a trigger. Chapter 2: Active US Portfolio Health & Concentration Risk Audit

A holdings audit asks two plain questions about every position you own. Is it the right size, and is it still behaving like something you would buy today if you were starting from cash? Size matters because a good company in a bad-sized position can still hurt you, while a poor company in a tiny position barely matters. Behaviour matters because a position is a bet on a future, and the chart, the company numbers and the calendar of coming events are the evidence that the bet is alive. Concentration is the word for too much of your money riding on one outcome. The largest position is GOOG at 8.19% of net worth, and with its leveraged twin GGLL it is 10.24%. The three largest positions together are 21.34%.

The audit sizes positions with Risk-Unit Sizing. Every position gets a risk budget in basis points of net worth (one basis point is 0.01%): 100 for TIER_1, 75 for TIER_2, 50 for TIER_3 and 35 for TIER_4, and zero for TIER_0. The weight allowed is the smaller of the tier's maximum (10%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2%) and the budget divided by the distance to the stop. A stock whose stop sits far below its price therefore gets a smaller position, because a fall to the stop would cost more. Total money at risk across all stops is capped at 12.0% of net worth, and no single stock may exceed the 15.0% hard cap. Where a position is above its ceiling the audit trims it to that ceiling, rounded to whole shares, unless the 28 October underwriting record asks for a deeper cut. In that case the audit takes the stricter of the two, as long as the record's reason still holds at today's close. 2.3 The Let-Winners-Run test on your Stage 2 winners

Selling a stock only because it has gone up is how investors cut their best ideas short. The Let-Winners-Run rule exists to prevent that. It says a Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals and stable or rising earnings estimates is not trimmed solely because its RSI is above 70 or its weight crossed 15%. Its weight may grow to 22% of net worth while its stop trails the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average. The rule has three tests: the chart must be in Stage 2, the business must pass its quality gates, and estimates must be steady or rising. The audit applies the test to every Stage 2 holding, and today there are two: QCOM and ASX. No holding is near the 15% hard cap, and the largest, GOOG at 8.19%, is leaving for a different reason.

ASX is the compounding winner the rule was written for, and it earns a conditional pass. It is up 46.1%, it closed at $44.55 above a rising 50-day average of $41.41 and a 200-day average of $33.23, and Test A passes. Its estimates are moving the right way on two pieces of evidence. The ledger's consensus snapshot labels them RISING, with the current-year earnings estimate up 7.37% in 30 days and 13.84% in 90 days, and this morning brought a third-quarter beat, an ATM gross profit margin of 30.6% that the company says has surpassed its old structural ceiling, and a fourth-quarter outlook of 6% to 8% sequential revenue growth (MarketBeat call transcript, 29 October). What the rule cannot yet see is the fundamentals test: vendor ROIC is 10.88%, under the 12% floor, and the record shows free cash flow margin of -5.3% as capital spending runs ahead of operating cash. That is why the pass is a bounded exception and not a full one: the extra 31 shares above the Risk-Unit ceiling add about $259 (0.30% of net worth) of risk to the formula stop, the record's operating cash flow covers only 0.82 times capital spending, and the company's margin news is a statement on a call and not yet a filing. The audit keeps all 82 shares for five sessions, sets the working stop at the 50-day average of $41.41, and reruns the underwriting by Wednesday 4 November. Without the exemption, the Risk-Unit ceiling would have sold 31 shares today.

QCOM passes the chart test and fails the other two. It closed at $185.41, above a rising 50-day ($177.75) and 200-day ($169.52) average, and Tests A and C pass. But the record's gate fails (the base target of $184.00 is under the price), only 2 of its 5 KPIs pass, handset revenue fell 19.6%, and the ledger's consensus snapshot labels its estimates FALLING (current-year -2.99% and next-year -7.31% over 90 days), so the third test fails too. Each name is handled on the rule's own terms. Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine that you woke up with $85,557.38 in cash and no memory of what you owned. Which stocks would you buy today, how many shares of each, and at what price? That is the thought experiment behind the greenfield allocation. It removes bias on purpose. A holding you bought at $332.71 feels different from one you bought at $190, even though the market does not care what you paid, and a position you have owned for a year feels safer than one you have never heard of. Chapter 4: US Shortlist Strategy Funnel & Deep Underwriting Pipeline

A funnel is a sequence of filters, each one stricter than the last, so that only the ideas that survive every test reach your account. 4.1 How each gate works, with one name that passes and one that fails

The financial gate tests solvency and quality. Under the scoring rules a business is "ownable" when it clears the hard red lines (net debt above four times EBITDA, which is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, with thin interest cover; a Stage 4 breakdown with no base; or fraud) and qualifies under one of three archetypes: a compounder with return on invested capital (ROIC) of at least 12% and free-cash-flow margin of at least 12%, a turnaround with improving trends and a cheap price, or a tactical breakout with accelerating growth. AAPL is a clean example of a pass. Its record shows ROIC of 101.57% and a free-cash-flow margin of 29.28%, the vendor page prints the same ROIC and a margin of 29.28%, and net debt to EBITDA is negative because the company holds net cash. DOCN fails. Its ROIC is 7.6% in the record and 7.89% on the vendor page, under the 12% floor, its free-cash-flow margin is 1.59% on the vendor page, and the vendor's debt to EBITDA is 4.33 times. Where two sources print different figures, the audit uses the underwriting record and notes the vendor figure beside it.

The technical gate tests the chart with three momentum tests. Test A asks whether the stock is already in a Stage 2 uptrend that is not stretched (the close above rising 50-day and 200-day averages, relative strength ahead of the S&P 500 over 63 days, and within 1.6 ATR of support). Test B asks whether it is breaking out of a quiet base. Test C asks whether a beaten-down stock has found a floor (a higher low, a turn in the short-term trend, and RSI at or above 32). A Stage 4 stock that passes none is a red line. GOOG is the example that fails today: its close of $282.32 is under a falling 50-day average of $336.93 and a 200-day average of $338.99, and Tests A, B and C all fail. VRT's chart was Stage 4 last week; with the close above its 50-day average it is now scored Stage 1, and Test C passes because it has formed a higher low.

The asymmetry gate tests the price. Reward-to-risk is (target minus entry) divided by (entry minus stop), where the entry is the close for a buy at the market and the zone top for a limit order. The floor is 2.0 to 1 for Core Alpha and 2.5 to 1 for Tactical Momentum. QCOM is the example that fails: its record target of $184.00 sits barely above the $185.41 close, so the ratio at the zone top of $177.73 is only 0.41. AMD is the example that passes: a base target of $610.00 against a zone top of $509.30 and a stop of $489.00 gives 4.96 to 1, but the close of $566.42 is 2.20 ATR above the zone, so the order waits.

4.3 The 3-condition swap test, pair by pair

The audit swaps one holding for another only when three conditions hold at once. Condition 1: the holding has triggered an exit or trim. Condition 2: the challenger has passed 100% of its gates and is strictly at support, which means no more than 0.5 ATR above its zone. Condition 3: the challenger's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's, which makes the swap worth its friction. The reason for three conditions is that a swap is two decisions in one: selling something and buying something else. Selling can be right while buying is premature, and the rule stops a good sale from being turned into a hasty purchase.

Here is the test run for GOOG and PLMR, the closest Core pair. Condition 1 is met: GOOG is an exit because its written stop broke. Condition 2 is not met: PLMR closed at $137.17, which is 1.94 ATR above its zone top of $129.00, and the rule requires 0.5 ATR or less. Condition 3 would also fail: PLMR's 2.45 at the zone top against GOOG's 2.26 at its close (using the record target of $315.00 and the audit's formula stop of $267.83) is a ratio of 1.1, under the 1.5 needed. Because condition 2 fails, there is no swap. GOOG is sold on its own merits, the $7,003.68 goes to cash, and PLMR waits as a limit order at $129.00 or lower with a stop of $118.78. 4.4 Friction: what the sales cost and what they may save

Every sale has costs that a spreadsheet leaves out: taxes on gains, the gap between the price you see and the price you get (slippage), and fees. Selling a loser can also save tax, because a realized loss offsets gains elsewhere. The audit's estimate is based on average cost per position, since the transaction logs hold the purchase lots but this audit did not match them to the sale lots, and the logs carry no fee lines. On that basis the planned share and fund sales realize a net result of -$4,581.56: gains of $889.57 (QCOM $323.64, MSTX $279.54, MSTR $170.88, UDOW $83.12, ORCL $32.38) against losses of $5,471.13 (led by TCEHY $1,320.75, GOOG $1,250.03, GGLL $745.07, KORU $702.13, BE $425.49, NBIL $378.45). The eight LEAPS closes realize about $5,789.08 of loss at the record marks, which are stale.

Three honest limits apply. The long-term or short-term character of each gain is not computed, so the tax rate on the $889.57 of gains is unknown and is not estimated here. Selling a position at a loss and buying the same security back within 30 days would disallow the loss for tax (the wash-sale rule). The plan buys no name that it sells except VRT, where a limit buy in the $181 to $188 zone after a sale at $245 could fall inside 30 days, and GOOG and GGLL are both exits, so a later re-entry into either inside 30 days should wait. And slippage on Friday's open is the main cost for the names with news: MSTR released results after Thursday's close, and Alphabet's shares are indicated near the $280 level after hours, so the proceeds figures are close-based estimates. Because the net result of the sales is a loss, not a gain, friction does not argue against any sale, and it argues mildly for them.

7.1 The decision rules, restated with today's numbers

The rulebook comes from the scoring file and the skill. It has twelve rules that decide almost everything in this report. Some are about a single stock (the stop, the Stage 4 red line, the Risk-Unit ceiling), some about the whole portfolio (heat, the leveraged and LEAPS bands, cash) and some about changing one holding for another (the swap rule). Each rule has a threshold that a number can cross, and the table shows the threshold beside today's reading and the reading after the plan. Where a number is close to a threshold, the rule is about to bind, and where it is far, the rule is not the reason for any order today.

7.4 How often to rerun the audit, and five things to remember

Rerun the audit after every session in which one of three things happens: a holding closes under its written stop, a company in the book reports, or the S&P 500 closes under its 50-day average. In a quiet week a rerun every Friday is enough. A full rerun costs little compared with a single missed stop, and the numbers in this report are valid for the 29 October close only; by the following Friday the prices, the stops and the zones will have moved.

If you remember five things from this report, remember these. First, the book behaves like a bet of 274% on the market, and the plan cuts that to about 124%. Second, Alphabet closed under its written stop, so it is an exit, and the exit is the rule rather than a prediction. Third, nothing is bought at market: every purchase is a limit order that fills only if the price comes to you. What this means for you. The rulebook is short enough to keep beside your broker screen. If a position breaks a stop, sell it. If it is over its ceiling, trim it. If something new looks attractive, wait for its zone and place a limit. If you are unsure, do the exit orders first and the rest after.

Glossary

Asymmetry (reward-to-risk). The gain you can reasonably expect divided by the loss you accept if the stop is hit. A ratio of 2.0 means you stand to make twice what you risk. The floors are 2.0 for Core Alpha and 2.5 for Tactical Momentum.

ATR (average true range). The typical size of a stock's daily swing in dollars over 14 days, like the normal wobble of a boat in harbour. Stops are placed a fraction of an ATR under support so ordinary noise does not trigger them.

Beta. How hard a stock has swung against the S&P 500. A beta of 2 means a 1% index move has typically meant a 2% move in the stock.

Daily-reset leveraged fund. A fund that aims for two or three times one day's move of an index or stock and resets each morning, so over longer periods its result can differ a lot from the multiple promised.

Distribution day. A session on which the index falls on heavier volume, a sign that large holders are selling. Five in 25 sessions is a caution.

Effective market exposure. How many dollars of market movement each dollar of your portfolio behaves like once beta, leverage and options are counted.

Gap. A price that opens far from the previous close, usually after news, so that no order between the two prices can fill.

Heat. The total loss if every stop were hit, shown in dollars and as a share of net worth.

LEAPS. Long-dated call options, a rented right to buy a stock at a fixed price until a fixed date.

Let-Winners-Run rule. A Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals and stable or rising estimates is not trimmed only because it has risen; it may reach 22% of net worth while the stop trails.

Limit order. An order to buy at your price or lower, or to sell at your price or higher; it fills only if the market reaches the price.

Moving average. The average closing price over a number of days; the 50-day and 200-day lines are the medium and long trends.

RSI (relative strength index). A 0 to 100 gauge of recent buying strength; under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought.

Risk-Unit Sizing. Position weight equals the smaller of the tier maximum and the risk budget in basis points divided by the stop distance in basis points.

Stage 1 to Stage 4. The four seasons of a stock: base, uptrend, top and decline.

Stop. A price decided in advance at which you accept that the idea was wrong. In this audit it is judged on the close.

Test A, B and C. A: already in a Stage 2 uptrend that is not stretched. B: breaking out of a base. C: a beaten-down stock that has found a floor.

Wash sale. A tax rule that disallows a loss if you buy the same security within 30 days before or after selling it at a loss.
