It is a research briefing and not an order, and it does not know your tax lots, your other accounts or your goals. If you have ten minutes, read the key figures, the lede below and Chapter 6, which lists the orders. If you have an hour, read everything in order. Each chapter ends with a short note on what it means for you and a pointer to the next one.

Three independent analysts produce the verdicts. The first judges the market and its sectors. The second audits your holdings and has no loyalty to your past choices. The third is a greenfield picker that is told nothing about what you own, what you paid or what you have gained, and answers one question: if you held 100% cash, what would you buy from the vetted shortlist? Keeping it blind is deliberate. An owner defends his stocks, while a buyer with only cash buys what passes the tests. When the owner and the buyer disagree, the gap between them is the cost of your past decisions, and Chapter 4 prices it.

Ten ideas carry the report. A moving average is the average closing price over recent weeks, like a line drawn through a bumpy road to show its direction. The 20-day line covers about a month, the 50-day line about ten weeks and the 200-day line about forty weeks. A stock moves through four stages, like seasons. Stage 2 is the rising phase, with the price above rising 50-day and 200-day averages. Stage 4 is the falling phase. Stage 1 is the quiet floor after a fall and Stage 3 is the tired top before one. RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 means a stock has risen very fast and below 30 means it has fallen very fast. ATR, the average true range, is the size of a normal daily wobble in dollars, and it tells you how close a stop can sit before ordinary noise triggers it.

Volume is the number of shares traded, a count of votes cast with money. A stop-loss is an exit price chosen before you need it, so a wrong idea costs a known amount. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, divides what you could gain by what you could lose: a ratio of 3.5 means each dollar at risk could earn 3.5. Beta says how much a stock tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%. A leveraged fund promises two or three times one day’s move of an index or a stock and resets every day, so over weeks it can lose money even when the thing it follows ends flat, a drag the report calls decay (the SEC has warned for years that such funds suit short holding periods). A LEAPS is a call option with more than nine months to run, a ticket that pays only if the stock climbs above its strike price before the ticket expires.

Some working terms appear in the case notes. The momentum gate is a three-part chart test: it asks whether a stock is already trending up, is turning up from a flat stretch of trading, or is bouncing from a floor after a fall. A reading of no momentum means it passes none of the three. A swing low is the lowest price of the last 20 sessions, the floor the chart has just defended. A base is a flat stretch of trading after a fall, where a stock rests before its next move. The entry zone is the price band in which a purchase is allowed, and a limit order fills only at your price or better. Free cash flow, or FCF, is the cash left after paying for the equipment the business needs. ROIC, return on invested capital, is profit divided by the money put into the business. SBC is stock-based compensation, pay handed out in shares, which dilutes owners. Net debt is borrowings minus cash, and EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a rough measure of operating profit.

The audit uses labels with fixed triggers. EXIT means sell the whole holding because a trend-failure rule, a stop or an underwriting verdict has fired. TRIM means sell part of it. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep it, add nothing and sell everything if it closes below a stated price. HOLD / TRAIL means keep it and lift the exit level as the 50-day average rises. NO TRADE / WATCH means the position is too small or too unvetted to act on. Two sizing rules matter. Risk-Unit Sizing sets a new position’s weight from how far its stop sits below the price, so wide stops get small positions. The Let-Winners-Run rule stops you from trimming a healthy Stage 2 winner just because it has risen, and lets it grow to 22.0% of net worth. Core Alpha means slow compounders held for years, Tactical Momentum means stocks held for weeks to months because the chart is working, and Leveraged Satellite means the daily-reset funds.

Chapter 2: Active US Portfolio Health and Concentration Risk Audit

Concentration is the habit of letting one idea carry too much of your wealth. It matters because losses are lopsided: a position that falls 50% needs a 100% gain to recover, so the damage from one oversized mistake outlasts the pleasure from several small wins. The audit tests concentration three ways. The first is size: a new position is capped at 10.0% of net worth at cost, and any single stock at 15.0% after it has grown. The second is heat, the total you would lose if every stop were hit at once, which the rules cap at 12.0% of net worth. The third is overlap, covered in Chapter 1. 2.6 Money at risk if every stop is hit

Money at risk answers a blunt question: if the market went against every one of your positions at once and every stop were hit, how much would you lose? 3.2 Risk-Unit Sizing: how a new position would be sized

When a name does pass, the size of the purchase is set by risk and not by enthusiasm. The rule is Risk-Unit Sizing: weight = the smaller of the tier cap and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. The risk budget is the share of net worth you accept losing if the stop is hit: 100 basis points (1.00%) for a Tier 1 name, 75 for Tier 2, 50 for Tier 3 and 35 for Tier 4. The stop distance is how far below the entry price the stop sits, as a percentage. A wide stop therefore shrinks the position automatically, and a tight stop allows a larger one up to the tier cap. Total heat across all open stops is capped at 12.0% of net worth, and the initial position may not exceed 10.0% of net worth at cost.

Worked example: sizing a hypothetical Tier 2 purchase of MercadoLibre

MercadoLibre does not pass today and is being sold. For arithmetic only, suppose it cleared every gate as a Tier 2 name (risk budget 75 basis points, cap 6.5%). The top of the record’s conditional zone is $1,779.00 and the stop is $1,611.63, so the stop distance is (1,779.00 - 1,611.63) / 1,779.00 = 9.41%.

The risk-budget weight is 75 / 941 x 100 = 7.97% of net worth, which is above the 6.5% Tier 2 cap, so the cap binds and the weight is 6.5%, or $5,375.47. That is 3 shares at the top of the zone. If the stop were hit the loss would be $502.11, or 0.61% of net worth, under the 0.75% budget. A name with a stop 15% below would be sized by the budget instead: 75 / 1500 x 100 = 5.0%.

The example shows why the sizing rule rarely produces the maximum. Most stops on this shortlist are 2% to 10% below the entry, which lets the tier cap bind, but any name whose stop sits more than 11.5% below a Tier 2 entry would be sized down by the budget. An order is a plain instruction that a broker can carry out without asking you anything. Each order below names the security, the number of shares or contracts, the price rule, how long the order lives and what to do if it does not fill. Three kinds of order appear. A limit order fills only at your price or better, which protects the price but risks no fill. A market order fills at once at whatever price is available, which guarantees the exit and gives up the price. A stop order sits dormant until the price touches a level, then becomes a market order. Closing-price stops, which this plan uses, are different again: they are rules that you check after the close and act on at the next open, because an intraday stop can be triggered by a brief spike that reverses by the close.

Glossary

  • Asymmetry (reward-to-risk): The potential gain to the target divided by the potential loss to the stop. 3.5 means $3.50 of upside for each $1.00 at risk.
  • ATR (average true range): The size of a stock’s normal daily range in dollars over 14 sessions, used to place stops outside ordinary noise.
  • Beta: How many percent a holding tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%, measured over one year.
  • Breadth: The share of stocks above a moving average; 34.3% above the 50-day line means the average stock is weaker than the index.
  • Closing-price stop: A rule, not an order: if the close is at or below the level, sell at the next open.
  • Correction (regime): The ledger label when the S&P 500 is below its 50-day average and above its 200-day average.
  • Daily-reset fund: A fund that targets a multiple of one day’s move and resets each day, so over weeks it can fall even when the underlying is flat.
  • Effective market exposure: Net worth weighted by each holding’s beta, which shows how hard the portfolio moves when the market moves.
  • Entry zone: The price band in which a purchase is allowed once every gate passes.
  • FCF (free cash flow) margin: Operating cash flow less capital spending, divided by sales.
  • KILL (options): The option matrix action: sell a contract that has lost 40% to 50% of its premium or whose underlying has broken its trend.
  • Layer 2: The scorecard of five operating measures that a candidate must pass four of, after the three gates.
  • LEAPS: A call option with more than nine months to expiry.
  • Limit order: An order that fills only at your price or better.
  • Lockstep cluster: A group of holdings whose one-year daily returns correlate at 0.65 or more and that behave like one bet.
  • Momentum gate: A three-part chart test: in momentum now, entering momentum from a base, or turning up from a floor.
  • Portfolio heat: The loss if every stop is hit at once, capped at 12.0% of net worth.
  • Risk-Unit Sizing: Setting a new position’s weight from a risk budget divided by the stop distance, capped by tier.
  • ROIC: Return on invested capital: operating profit after tax divided by the capital invested in the business.
  • Stage 1 to Stage 4: The four seasons of a chart: basing, rising, topping and falling.
  • Swing low: The lowest price of the last 20 sessions.
  • Three-condition swap test: A sale becomes a swap only if the exit fired, the challenger passes every gate and sits within 0.5 ATR of support, and its asymmetry is 1.5 times the holding’s.
