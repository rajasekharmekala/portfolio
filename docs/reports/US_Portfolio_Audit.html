Three independent analysts produce the verdicts. The first judges the market and its sectors. The second audits your holdings and has no loyalty to your past choices. Keeping it blind is deliberate. An owner defends what he holds, while a buyer with only cash buys what passes the tests. Where the owner and the buyer disagree, the gap is the cost of past decisions, and Chapter 4 prices it.

Ten ideas carry the report. A moving average is the average closing price over recent weeks, like a line drawn through a bumpy road to show its direction; the 20-day line covers about a month, the 50-day line about ten weeks and the 200-day line about forty weeks. A stock moves through four stages, like seasons: Stage 2 is the rising phase, Stage 4 the falling phase, Stage 1 the quiet floor after a fall, often called a base because the price rests there before it moves, and Stage 3 the tired top before one. RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 means a stock has risen very fast and below 30 means it has fallen very fast. ATR, the average true range, is the size of a normal daily wobble in dollars, and it tells you how close a stop can sit before ordinary noise triggers it. Volume is the number of shares traded, a count of votes cast with money. A stop loss is an exit price chosen before you need it, so a wrong idea costs a known amount. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, divides what you could gain by what you could lose: 3.5 means each dollar at risk could earn 3.5. Beta says how much a stock tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%. Position size is how much of your wealth sits in one idea, and it is the reason no single mistake should hurt you badly. A limit order is an order to buy or sell only at a price you set or better, and an entry zone is the price range in which a purchase is allowed. A company’s guidance is its own forecast of sales or profit, and dilution means that new shares shrink each existing share’s slice of the company.

Two product types need an extra word. A leveraged fund promises two or three times one day’s move of an index or a stock and resets every day, so over weeks it can lose money even when what it follows ends flat; the SEC staff has warned that such funds may differ significantly from the index over periods longer than a day. A LEAPS is a call option with more than nine months to run, a ticket that pays only if the stock climbs above its strike price before the ticket expires. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after paying for the equipment a business needs, return on invested capital (ROIC) is profit divided by the money put into the business, and net debt is borrowings minus cash, measured against EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), a rough measure of operating profit.

The audit uses labels with fixed triggers. Concentration is the habit of letting one idea carry too much of your wealth. It matters because losses are lopsided: a position that falls 50% needs a 100% gain to recover, so the damage from one oversized mistake outlasts the pleasure from several small wins. The audit tests concentration three ways. The first is size: a new position is capped at 10.0% of net worth at cost, and any single stock at 15.0% after it has grown. 3.2 Risk-Unit Sizing: how a new position would be sized

Risk-Unit Sizing answers the question “how much should I buy?” by starting from how much you are willing to lose, not from how much you would like to own. Each conviction tier has a risk budget in basis points of net worth (one basis point is one hundredth of a percent): Tier 1 is 100, Tier 2 is 75, Tier 3 is 50 and Tier 4 is 35. The weight of a new position is the smaller of two numbers: the tier’s maximum weight (Tier 1 10.0%, Tier 2 6.5%, Tier 3 3.5%, Tier 4 2.0%) and the risk budget divided by the stop distance. A wide stop gives a small position automatically, and a tight stop gives a larger one up to the tier’s ceiling. The initial cost of any single stock is capped at 10.0% of net worth, the standard single-stock cap is 15.0%, and total heat across all open stops is capped at 12.0%. After the plan, heat is 6.70%, which leaves 5.30% ($4,286.58) of room for new positions.

Worked example: sizing a Core position from its stop. Suppose Qualcomm had cleared every gate as a Tier 2 Core position, entering at the top of its record zone, $172.42, with the record’s stop at $161.60. The stop distance is ($172.42 - $161.60) / $172.42 = 6.28%, or 628 basis points. The risk budget of 75 basis points divided by 628 gives a raw weight of 11.95%, which is larger than the Tier 2 maximum of 6.5%, so the maximum binds and the position is 6.5% of $80,849.81, or $5,255.24. That buys 30.48 shares, and if the stop hits the loss is $329.79, which is 41 basis points of net worth, under the 75-point budget. Now widen the stop: a $100 stock with a stop at $80 has a 20% stop distance, or 2,000 basis points, and a Tier 1 budget of 100 divided by 2,000 gives 5.0%, half the Tier 1 maximum, so the position is $4,042.49 and the loss at the stop is exactly 100 basis points ($808.50). Chapter 4: US Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

A funnel is a series of filters, each one narrower than the last. Every shortlisted name passes through the same three: the financial gate (does the business earn its keep and keep its debt in check?), the technical gate (is the chart in an uptrend, or at least basing?) and the asymmetry gate (is the possible gain at least three or three and a half times the possible loss?). A name that clears all three is a candidate, and a candidate that also sits within 1.2 ATR of support can be bought. The point of walking every name through the same gates is fairness. A holding does not get a lower bar because you own it, and a challenger does not get a higher one because you do not. The result for this week is that no name clears all three on today’s evidence, and the sections below show where each one fell and what the swap test makes of each sale.

Glossary

The terms below are used throughout the report. Each is also explained where it first appears, so this list is a place to look something up when you are partway through a chapter.

Asymmetry (reward-to-risk)
The distance from the price to the target divided by the distance from the price to the stop. A figure of 3.0 means a possible gain three times the possible loss.
ATR (average true range)
The size of a normal daily price move in dollars, averaged over 14 days. Stops are placed in multiples of it so that ordinary noise does not trigger them.
Beta
How many percent a stock tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%, measured over one year.
Breadth
The share of stocks above a moving average, used to tell a broad rise from a narrow one.
Closing stop
A stop applied to the closing price: the position is sold at the next session if it closes under the level, not the moment a price touches it.
Correction
The regime label used when the S&P 500 closes under its 50-day average but above its 200-day average.
Delta
How many dollars an option gains when the stock rises one dollar. A delta of 0.30 means 30 cents.
Distribution day
A session in which a major index falls on higher volume than the day before, a sign of institutions selling.
Effective market exposure
Net worth multiplied by the beta and leverage of each holding, which shows how much market risk the book carries compared with its dollar size.
Heat
The total dollar loss if every stop were hit at once, shown as a share of net worth. The rules cap it at 12.0%.
Hidden overlap
Holdings whose daily returns move together at a correlation of 0.65 or higher, which act as one combined bet.
Leveraged fund
A fund that tries to deliver two or three times the daily move of an index or a stock, resetting every day, so it can lose money over weeks even when the underlying is flat.
LEAPS
A long-dated option, here a call with more than nine months to run when bought.
Let-Winners-Run rule
The rule that stops a healthy Stage 2 winner from being trimmed only because it has risen, up to a weight of 22.0% of net worth.
Moving average
The average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 sessions, used as a trend line.
Put/call ratio
Put-option volume divided by call-option volume: a high number means more hedging, and a very high one can mark a washout.
Risk-Unit Sizing
Sizing a new position from the loss you accept if its stop hits: the weight is the smaller of the tier maximum and the risk budget divided by the stop distance.
RSI
The relative strength index, a 0 to 100 speed gauge; above 70 is fast-rising, below 30 is fast-falling.
Stage 1 to Stage 4
The four phases of a stock’s life: basing, uptrend, topping and downtrend.
VIX
The market’s fear gauge, an index of expected S&P 500 movement over the next 30 days.
