Three independent analysts produced the work. The market analyst sets the regime, the label for the market's current weather. The holdings auditor judges each position you own against fixed rules. The greenfield picker ignores what you own on purpose and asks what you would buy with only cash, so that old purchases, tax habits and attachment cannot bend its list. Where the auditor and the picker agree, confidence rises; where they differ, the report says why.

Each holding receives one action label. EXIT means sell all of it. It applies when a stock is in a confirmed Stage 4, meaning its price sits under a falling 50-day average that is itself under a falling 200-day average, and it also fails Test C, the check for a recovery above a 63-day floor. For daily-reset leveraged funds the audit uses the ledger's own Stage 4 label without waiting for the 200-day average to turn down, because the fund's decay punishes waiting. TRIM means sell part: it applies when a position is larger than its risk-unit ceiling or when the company's numbers fail a quality floor. A reward-to-risk ratio that is under its floor does not cause a trim; it only blocks new buying, which is why several holdings are kept with the instruction to add nothing. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep the shares, add nothing, and sell everything if the stop price is hit. HOLD / TRAIL means the stop rises with the trend; no holding earns that label today.

Ten ideas carry the rest of the book. A moving average is the average closing price over a number of days, like a trend line drawn through a bumpy trail; the 50-day line shows the recent trend and the 200-day line the long one. A Stage is a season: Stage 1 is a flat base after a decline, Stage 2 a rising phase, Stage 3 a topping or pullback phase, Stage 4 a falling phase. RSI is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 is unusually fast up, below 30 unusually fast down. ATR (average true range) is the normal daily wobble in dollars, and stops are placed in ATR units so that ordinary noise does not trigger them. Volume counts how many people voted with their money. A stop loss is an exit price decided before you buy, risk/reward is what you can win divided by what you can lose, and position size is the reason no single stock can hurt you badly.

Four more terms matter here. Effective market exposure measures how hard your portfolio moves when the market moves, after adjusting for each holding's beta (its usual swing compared with the market) and for leverage. Hidden overlap means holdings that move almost together, so that eight tickers may be one bet. A daily-reset leveraged fund promises two or three times the day's move of an index or stock and resets each day, which means that in a choppy market it can lose money even when the index ends flat. A LEAPS option is a long-dated call option: the right, not the duty, to buy 100 shares at a fixed price before a fixed date. It can lose all of its value, and it loses a little every day through time decay.

Three sizing rules appear throughout. Risk-Unit Sizing sets a new position's weight by how far away its stop is, so that wide stops get smaller positions: weight equals the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget in basis points divided by the stop distance. The Let-Winners-Run rule says a rising Stage 2 winner with sound fundamentals is not trimmed merely because its RSI is high or its weight passed 15%; it may grow to the 22% runaway cap while its stop climbs to the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average. The 3-condition swap test says a holding is replaced only if it triggered an exit or trim, the replacement sits within 0.5 ATR of support, and the replacement's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's. Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine that you woke up with $85,454.62 in cash and no memory of what you owned. Which stocks would you buy today, how many shares of each, and at what price? A holding you bought at $332.71 feels different from one you bought at $190, even though the market does not care what you paid; a position you have owned for a year feels safer than one you have never heard of. 7.4 How often to rerun the audit, and five things to remember

Rerun the audit after any of three things: a regime change in the ledger label, a stop or a zone being hit, or two weeks without a rerun. A full audit is expensive and most days nothing changes, so the daily job is smaller: check the stops, the calendar and the cash level in the table above. When you rerun, discard everything and rebuild from the ledger; prices and verdicts from this page are history after a week. Five things matter most if you remember nothing else. One, the plan sells what the written rules say to sell and does not sell because a business is bad. Two, every position kept has a stop price, and every stop must be live by Wednesday's close. Three, buys happen only at the written zones, never at today's prices. Four, the LEAPS book may not exceed 10% of net worth, and today it is cut to 7.98%. Glossary

A glossary is a short dictionary for the words the rest of the book leans on, so that you can look a term up without hunting back through the chapters. Each entry gives the plain meaning first and then the way this audit uses it, with the number from this report where one helps.

ATR (average true range): the typical daily price swing of a stock over 14 sessions, in dollars. A stop placed half an ATR or one ATR under support survives ordinary noise but not a real break.

Base: a flat resting area on a chart, after a decline and before a new rise. Stage 1 is a base.

Beta: how much a stock usually moves when the S&P 500 moves 1%. A beta of 4.39, as BE has, means the stock tends to move 4.39% for each 1% in the index, in either direction.

Call option and LEAPS: a call is the right to buy 100 shares at a fixed strike price before a fixed date. A LEAPS is a call that lasts a year or more. It cannot lose more than its price but can lose all of it, and it loses value every day through time decay.

Delta: how many dollars an option moves for a one-dollar move in the stock. A delta under 0.25 with fewer than 120 days left is called the far-out-of-the-money trap in this audit.

Distribution day: a day when the index falls on higher volume than the day before, which suggests large investors are selling. Five in 25 sessions is a caution.

Effective market exposure: the beta-weighted size of the portfolio expressed as a percent of net worth. At 269.9% a 1% index move changes net worth by 2.70%.

Entry zone: the price band where you would accept buying, anchored to support.

Free-cash-flow margin: the share of sales left as cash after operating and capital spending. A floor of 12% is used for quality tests.

Gap: a price that opens far from the previous close, so that a stop order fills at the open price and not at the stop.

Hard stop and trailing stop: a hard stop is a fixed exit price; a trailing stop moves with the trend, for example with the rising 50-day average.

Heat: the total money at risk across all open stops, as a percent of net worth; the cap is 12%.

Hidden overlap (lockstep): two or more holdings whose daily returns have a one-year correlation of 0.65 or more, so that they act as one bet.

Leveraged daily-reset fund: a fund that seeks two or three times the daily return of an index or stock and resets each day. In choppy markets it decays even if the index ends flat.

Limit order: an order to buy at or below, or sell at or above, a chosen price. It may not fill.

Momentum gate (Tests A, B, C): three tests on the price snapshot: A, in a Stage 2 uptrend now; B, entering a trend from a flat base; C, a recovery above a 63-day floor.

Put/call ratio: put-option volume divided by call-option volume. Below 0.7 is bullish, 0.7 to 1.0 is neutral and above 1.0 is bearish.

Regime: the market's current weather as a label, here Uptrend (narrow breadth).

Return on invested capital (ROIC): profit earned per dollar of capital invested in the business. A floor of 12% is used for quality tests.

Reward-to-risk (asymmetry): the possible gain divided by the possible loss: (target minus entry) divided by (entry minus stop).

Risk-Unit Sizing: the rule that sets a position's weight as the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget (in basis points) divided by the stop distance.

RSI (relative strength index): a speedometer from 0 to 100. Above 70 is unusually fast up, below 30 unusually fast down.

Stage 1 to 4: four seasons of a stock: 1 base, 2 rising, 3 topping or pullback, 4 falling.

Support: a price where buyers have stepped in before, such as a swing low or a moving average.

Tier: a conviction class (TIER_1 to TIER_4) with a weight range and a risk budget; TIER_0 means zero weight.

VIX: the market's expected volatility over the next month, drawn from index option prices. Below 15 is calm and above 22 is stressed.

Recomputation. Position weights are market value divided by net worth. Risk-unit weights are the smaller of the tier ceiling and the tier budget in basis points divided by the stop distance, with entry at the close for holdings and at the zone top for buys. Stops use the formulas in the scoring file. Money at risk is shares times the gap between close and stop for shares and funds, and the full record mark for options. Effective exposure is the sum of weight times beta, with options at an assumed beta of 2.5, exactly as the positions file computes it. The liberated-capital figures assume each sale fills at the 27 October close, and the buy costs assume each fill at the top of its zone, so actual figures will differ by the slippage of each fill.
