Three independent analysts produce the verdicts. The first judges the market and its sectors. The second audits your holdings and has no loyalty to your past choices. Keeping it blind is deliberate. An owner defends his stocks, while a buyer with only cash buys what passes the tests. Where the owner and the buyer disagree, the gap is the cost of past decisions, and Chapter 4 prices it.

Ten ideas carry the report. A moving average is the average closing price over recent weeks, like a line drawn through a bumpy road to show its direction; the 20-day line covers about a month, the 50-day line about ten weeks and the 200-day line about forty weeks. A stock moves through four stages, like seasons: Stage 2 is the rising phase, Stage 4 the falling phase, Stage 1 the quiet floor after a fall and Stage 3 the tired top before one. RSI, the relative strength index, is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 means a stock has risen very fast and below 30 means it has fallen very fast. ATR, the average true range, is the size of a normal daily wobble in dollars, and it tells you how close a stop can sit before ordinary noise triggers it. Volume is the number of shares traded, a count of votes cast with money. A stop-loss is an exit price chosen before you need it, so a wrong idea costs a known amount. Reward-to-risk, also called asymmetry, divides what you could gain by what you could lose: 3.5 means each dollar at risk could earn 3.5. Beta says how much a stock tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%.

Two product types need an extra word. A leveraged fund promises two or three times one day’s move of an index or a stock and resets every day, so over weeks it can lose money even when what it follows ends flat; the SEC staff has warned that such funds may differ significantly from the index over periods longer than a day (SEC statement). A LEAPS is a call option with more than nine months to run, a ticket that pays only if the stock climbs above its strike price before the ticket expires. Free cash flow is the cash left after paying for the equipment a business needs, return on invested capital is profit divided by the money put into the business, and net debt is borrowings minus cash measured against EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), a rough measure of operating profit.

The audit uses labels with fixed triggers. EXIT means sell the whole holding because a trend-failure rule, a stop or an underwriting verdict has fired. Risk-Unit Sizing sets a new position’s weight from how far its stop sits below the price, so wide stops get small positions. The Let-Winners-Run rule stops you from trimming a healthy Stage 2 winner just because it has risen, and lets it grow to 22.0% of net worth. Chapter 2: Active US Portfolio Health and Concentration Risk Audit

Concentration is the habit of letting one idea carry too much of your wealth. It matters because losses are lopsided: a position that falls 50% needs a 100% gain to recover, so the damage from one oversized mistake outlasts the pleasure from several small wins. The audit tests concentration three ways. The first is size: a new position is capped at 10.0% of net worth at cost, and any single stock at 15.0% after it has grown. The second is heat, the total you would lose if every stop were hit at once, which the rules cap at 12.0% of net worth. The third is overlap, covered in Chapter 1. 3.2 Risk-Unit Sizing: how a new position would be sized

Risk-Unit Sizing sets a position’s weight from the risk you are willing to take and not from how much you like the story. The formula is weight = the smaller of the tier’s maximum and the risk budget divided by the stop distance, where the budget is measured in basis points of net worth (1 basis point is 0.01%). A Tier 2 name has a budget of 75 basis points and a cap of 6.5%; a Tier 3 name has 50 basis points and a cap of 3.5%. A wide stop shrinks the position automatically, because the same dollar budget has to cover a larger loss per share. Portfolio heat, the total at risk across all stops, may not exceed 12.0%, and a new position may not exceed 10.0% of net worth at cost or 15.0% after it grows.

Worked example: sizing a hypothetical Bloom Energy purchase. Bloom’s close is $226.22 and its record stop is $205.21, a distance of 9.29%, or 929 basis points. If it were Tier 3 with a budget of 50 basis points, the formula gives 50 / 929 = 5.38% of net worth, which is above the 3.5% cap, so the cap binds: 3.5% of $83,359.98 is $2,917.60, or 12.9 shares. The most that stop would cost is $270.97, or 0.33% of net worth. This is an illustration: Bloom is TIER_0 today, so the actual size is zero.

Chapter 4: US Shortlist Strategy Funnel and Deep Underwriting Pipeline

The funnel is the order in which an idea is tested. A name enters at the top as a shortlist candidate and passes through three gates, and each gate removes names that fail it. Glossary

The terms below are used throughout the report. Each is defined the first time it appears, and the list repeats them for reference so a reader can look one up without searching.

ATR (average true range)
The size of a normal daily price swing in dollars, used to place stops far enough away that routine noise does not trigger them.
Asymmetry (reward-to-risk)
The distance from the price to the target divided by the distance from the price to the stop. A ratio of 3.0 means a dollar at risk could earn three.
Basis point
One hundredth of one percent. A budget of 75 basis points is 0.75% of net worth.
Beta
How many percent a holding tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%.
Breadth
The share of stocks above a moving average, a measure of whether an index move is broad or carried by a few names.
Call option
A contract that gains value when a stock rises above its strike price before the contract expires.
Closing-price trigger
A stop that fires on the day’s closing price and not on an intraday dip.
Correction (regime)
The ledger label for an index that closes below its 50-day average and above its 200-day average.
Delta
How much an option’s price moves for a one-dollar move in the stock.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit.
Effective market exposure
How strongly the whole portfolio moves with the S&P 500 after adjusting for beta, leverage and options.
Free cash flow
The cash left after paying for the equipment and buildings a business needs.
Hidden overlap
Holdings that move together at a correlation of 0.65 or higher and so act as one bet.
LEAPS
Options with more than nine months to run.
Leveraged fund
A fund that targets a multiple of one day’s move of an index or stock and resets daily, which causes decay over longer periods.
Lockstep
A correlation of 0.65 or higher between two holdings over one year.
Momentum gate
A three-part chart test: already trending up, entering a trend from a flat base, or recovering from a floor.
Moving average
The average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 sessions, a trend line.
Portfolio heat
The total you would lose if every stop were hit at once, as a share of net worth.
Risk-Unit Sizing
A sizing rule that sets a position’s weight from its stop distance and a risk budget in basis points.
ROIC
Return on invested capital: profit divided by the money put into the business.
RSI
Relative strength index, a 0 to 100 speed reading of recent price moves; below 30 is oversold and above 70 is overbought.
Stage (1 to 4)
The Weinstein stage of a chart: 1 basing, 2 rising, 3 topping, 4 declining.
Stop-loss
A price chosen in advance at which you sell to cap a loss.
Swap test
Three conditions that must all hold before one holding is replaced by another.
