Start here: what this audit is and how to read it

Fourteen terms recur throughout. Stage 2 is a rising trend, Stage 4 is a fall, and a base is the sideways pause in which a stock builds before its next move. A stop loss is a price at which you sell, and a limit order buys or sells only at a stated price or better. The entry zone is the price band where the rule allows a buy. Asymmetry is another name for reward-to-risk, the upside to the target divided by the downside to the stop. RSI means relative strength index, a momentum gauge. ATR means average true range, the typical daily move of a stock. Debt-to-equity compares a company's borrowing with its own capital, and free cash flow is the cash a business generates after the spending it needs to keep running. ROIC means return on invested capital, the profit a business earns on the money put into it, and EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit.

This report is a check-up on your whole US book: the holdings in your two brokerage accounts, the options you have open, the cash, and the list of stocks your research says are worth owning. It answers three questions. What does the market look like today? Which of the things you own still fit the rules you set for them? And if you started again with nothing but cash, what would those rules buy? Because the answer to the last question is independent of what you own, the audit treats three analysts separately and then reconciles them in one plan. You can read the lede and the orders in about ten minutes. Reading every chapter takes about an hour, and the case notes are the place to slow down if you own a particular name.

Three analysts do the work. The first is the market analyst, who reads the broad index averages, the sectors, the breadth of the rally and the volatility gauge, and then names a regime. The second is the holdings analyst, who looks at each position against the rules below. The third is the greenfield picker, who sees only the research list and the cash, and who is told nothing about what you own. The picker exists so that the list of buys is not biased toward the names already in the book. The Chapter 3 target is its answer. Chapter 6 is what the book actually does, which is the picker's answer filtered through your current holdings, your concentration limits and the regime.

The vocabulary is short but it matters. A moving average is the average closing price over a fixed number of sessions. It works like a trend line that moves with the price. The 50-day and 200-day averages are the two that matter most here; the 20-day is used for short-term stops. A stock is in Stage 2 when it is trending up above rising averages, Stage 1 when it is still building a base after a fall, Stage 3 when the uptrend has stopped and the price is topping out, and Stage 4 when it has broken down and is falling. Think of the four stages as the four seasons of a stock. RSI is a speedometer on the price: a reading above 70 means the stock has run hot and below 30 means it has been sold hard. ATR, the average true range, measures a stock's normal daily wobble. A good stop sits far enough below the price that a normal wobble does not trigger it.

A stop is a price you decide in advance at which you sell, so that the decision is made when you are calm rather than when you are frightened. This audit sets two formulas. For Core Alpha holdings, the stop is the lower of the recent 20-day swing low and the 50-day or 200-day average, minus one ATR. For Tactical Momentum and leveraged holdings, it is the lower of the swing low and the 20-day or 50-day average, minus half an ATR. The tighter formula for the short-term names reflects that they must be sold sooner when they fail. Reward-to-risk is what you can win divided by what you can lose. The skill requires at least 3.0 to 1 for Core names and 3.5 to 1 for Tactical names before a buy is allowed. Position size is why no single name should hurt you badly: this audit caps any single stock at 20% of net worth and checks the money at risk against every stop.

The labels are the working vocabulary of the report. HOLD / TRAIL means keep the position and raise the stop as the price rises. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep it, with a fixed stop that is not moved, because the trend is weaker or the record is stale. TRIM means sell part of the position, usually half, because a rule says the position is too large or its trend has weakened. EXIT means sell it all, because a stage breakdown or a leveraged position below its long-term average has failed its rule. ADD means buy more on a pullback to a named level. WATCH means no trade yet, with a level that would change the answer. Each verdict in this report names its rule and the price that would change it. If a verdict is not followed by a rule, you should treat it as an error to report.

Two kinds of leveraged product appear. A leveraged ETF promises a multiple of the daily move of its index, for example 2X or 3X, but only for one day. Over several days the multiple compounds on the path the price takes, so a 3X fund can lose far more than three times a three-day fall in a choppy market, and it can also fail to gain three times a rally. That is why the leveraged bucket has a band and a short holding horizon. A LEAPS option is a long-dated option; your options book in this run has no LEAPS line identified in the record. The options are valued at the current value in the record, with no strike or expiry given, and they are not traded in this run.

Figures in this report come from one facts ledger built for the session date. Where a number is from the research pass, its source and date appear beside it. Where a number is not available, the report says N/A and gives the reason. Where the shortlist file records a price that differs from the ledger close, the ledger wins, and the difference is noted once. The holdings are taken from your transaction log, which this audit treats as the accurate record, as the workflow instructs.

Breadth is the share of stocks that are above their own 50-day average. A rising index with high breadth means the rally is shared. The ledger's regime table turns the label into a posture. 1.3 What the regime and sectors mean for your portfolio

The regime and the sector map are not verdicts on individual stocks. They set the posture within which the stock verdicts are made. Under Uptrend (narrow breadth), the posture is normal deployment with a narrow list of buys, and that means the book should keep its leaders, cut the names that have broken down, and not add risk to lagging sectors. The posture also means that the cash position should sit at the policy target. Your cash is at 5.2 percent today, which is at the bottom of the 5 to 10 percent band. The audit's plan raises cash to 19.0 percent, because the sells free more money than the gates allow the book to redeploy. That is an exception to the cash target, and Chapter 5 explains it with numbers.

The leveraged satellite is the biggest place where the regime and the book disagree. Leveraged funds amplify the moves of their indexes, so they behave like a larger bet on the same trend. A book with 26.5 percent in leveraged funds is taking a larger bet on the trend than its mandate allows, and in a narrow market that bet is concentrated in the same few leaders. If the leaders stop leading, the leveraged funds fall faster than the index, and they do so when the market's breadth is already weak. That is why the leveraged band is the first correction, and why the sells in Chapter 6 start with the funds that have already broken down.

The Core Alpha sleeve is in a better position. Its 42.7 percent weight sits inside the 35 to 45 percent band, and its largest holdings, Alphabet, Vertiv, Qualcomm and Tencent, are either in Stage 2 or are long-term compounders that the rules hold with stops rather than exits. The Tactical sleeve is the weakest part of the mandate: at 17.8 percent it is far below its 40 to 55 percent target. The audit does not fill that gap by buying names that fail the gates, because the narrow regime is the reason those names fail. The gap is therefore a real finding about the market, not a clerical error in the book, and Chapter 3 sets out what would have to change for it to close.

2.2 Forensic health matrix: the trend, the cost and the stop

The matrix goes one step behind the ladder. It asks, for each position, where the price sits against its averages, what you paid on average, and what the stop would be. A position that sits above rising averages with a stop well below the price has room to run. A position that sits below falling averages has no room, and its stop is the only protection it has. The matrix is the place to check whether a verdict is consistent with the trend and whether the stop is a reasonable distance from the price.

The average cost column is your cost basis per share, which is the amount you paid divided by the shares you hold, and it is set against today's close. Where the close is below your average cost, the position is at a loss, and the stop tells you how much further it can fall before the rule takes you out. A stop more than 20 percent below the close is a very wide stop, and it means the position's risk is large relative to its stop. The matrix flags that in the case notes. Where the shortlist record shows a different price or a stale date, the ledger close is the one used, and the difference is noted once in the methodology section.

Each row is one position. The Security and Bucket columns identify it. The Average cost and close column sets your average price per share against today's close, so you can see at a glance whether the position is at a profit or a loss. The Trend column says whether the close is above or below the 50-day and 200-day averages, and whether the 50-day is rising or falling. The RSI and stage column is the speedometer and the season. The Stop column is the formula's level, and the Verdict column is the action. 4 Funnel: how each candidate and holding was tested

A funnel is a sequence of tests, and a name that fails an early test does not reach the later ones. This chapter narrates the three gates the audit applies to every shortlist name, shows one name that passes and one that fails at each gate, and then sets out the full funnel for all 37 names in one table. It finishes with the displacement test, which decides whether a new buy should replace a holding, and a note on the costs that the rules do not yet measure.

The financial gate asks whether the business is good enough to own for years. For a Core name the shortlist record must show a return on invested capital above 16 percent, a strong conversion of profit into free cash, a clean balance sheet and a passing health check. The record for Adobe shows a return on capital of 24.8 percent and a free cash flow margin of 40.8 percent, which passes; a third-party page reports a return on capital of 57.2 percent on its own definition, and the gate uses the record. The record for MELI shows a gate status of FAIL, which fails at once, whatever the price. The gate is read from the record, so its age matters. A record dated 2025 cannot pass or fail a gate for a stock that is trading in October 2026, and such names are marked stale rather than judged.

The technical gate asks whether the price is in an uptrend the rules recognise. A name passes when it is in Stage 2 and its 63-day relative strength is positive, meaning it has beaten the market over three months. Bloom Energy passes: it is in Stage 2 with a relative-strength reading of plus 4.3 percent. Astera Labs fails, although its record shows a pass and its stage is 2. Its 63-day return is 13.9 percent below the market, which is a warning that its stage label is ahead of its performance. The technical gate therefore catches the names that have stopped leading even while their trend label still reads Stage 2. A name in Stage 1 is allowed only for a Core name at support, and Adobe is the one that uses that allowance.

The asymmetry gate asks whether the reward is large enough to be worth the risk. The reward is the distance from the close to the record's base target. The risk is the distance from the close to the bucket's stop. The ratio must reach 3.0 for Core names and 3.5 for Tactical names. Adobe passes at 6.4 to 1 at the close. Taiwan Semiconductor fails at 2.78 to 1, because its target of $620 is far above the price while the Core stop sits only $63 below it. The gate is strict on purpose: a ratio below the floor means that a modest mistake in the stop costs more than the target can repay.

The funnel also tests entry. A name is bought only when its close is within half an ATR of its entry zone, which the rule calls at support. The shortlist's entry zones were set on earlier prices, so the entry test uses the current ledger close against them. A name more than half an ATR above its zone is not at support and waits, and a name more than half an ATR below its zone has already fallen through it and is re-tested on the next run. Six names with fresh records pass the financial and technical gates but fail the entry test (MSFT, ASX, FNGU, TQQQ, SPXL and AMD), which is why the funnel shows them as excluded despite strong trends.

4.2 Displacement: the three-condition test for each swap

A displacement is a swap: a holding is sold so that a challenger can be bought with the proceeds. The audit allows a swap only when three conditions all hold. Condition 1 is that the holding has triggered an exit or a trim under its rule. Condition 2 is that the challenger has passed every gate and sits strictly at support. Condition 3 is that the challenger's reward-to-risk exceeds the holding's by at least 1.5 times, which must also clear the tax and friction cost of selling the holding. A swap that fails any one condition is not made, and the cash is deployed through the normal buy path instead.

Take Uber as the worked example. Condition 1 holds: the shares are in Stage 4 in the Tactical sleeve and the rule exits them. Condition 2 holds for Adobe, which is the challenger the audit would otherwise consider. Condition 3 does not hold. Uber's reward-to-risk on its target of $100 and its Tactical stop of $66.33 is about 5.5 to 1 at the close. Adobe's is 6.4 to 1, which is 1.16 times Uber's and below the 1.5 times hurdle. The swap fails, and the Uber sale proceeds to cash, with Adobe bought from cash as a Core addition. The distinction matters: Uber is sold because its rule says so, and Adobe is bought because it passes, and neither decision depends on the other. The same test applied to ON gives the same result, because ON's reward-to-risk is not higher than the challenger's by 1.5 times.

Worked example. You sell 20 shares of Uber at $71.50, which realises $1,430.00, and buy 8 shares of Adobe at $235, which costs $1,880. Adobe's ratio on the limit is 7.6 to 1. Uber's ratio at the close is about 5.5 to 1. The condition is 1.5 times Uber's ratio, which is 8.25 to 1. Adobe's 7.6 falls short, so the swap is not made as a swap. The $1,430 of Uber proceeds goes to cash, which is the same amount the cash waterfall in Chapter 5 shows.

The Chapter 5 cash waterfall uses that outcome. A swap would have netted the cash to zero on the Uber line, while the audit as written leaves the Uber proceeds in cash until the Adobe and Bloom Energy buys are filled. The distinction has no effect on net worth at closing prices, but it governs the size of the Core and Tactical positions that the book finishes with.

4.3 Tax, friction and what the rules do not yet measure

The rules measure price, trend and reward against risk, but they do not measure the costs of trading. Those costs are real. Selling a position that has gained creates a capital gain that is taxed, and selling one that has lost creates a loss that can offset other gains. Each sale also carries a commission or spread cost, and a leveraged fund's spread can be wider than a stock's. None of those figures are in the record, and the lot-level cost basis that would give the exact tax is not available for every position. The report therefore shows them as N/A with the reason, and it does not estimate tax or fees. The pre-tax gain and loss figures are kept.

The effect is largest on the sells that realise gains. The MSTX trim realises an estimated gain of about $596 on the half sold, the FAS trim about $172, and the UDOW trim about $76. Those are paper gains measured against the cost on the consolidated record, and the tax treatment depends on how long each lot was held, which the record does not show. The sells that realise losses, led by NFXL at about $988 and ORCX at about $835, reduce the tax on those gains if they are in the same tax year. The audit cannot say whether the net is positive or negative without the lot records, so it says so.

The rules also do not measure liquidity in the leveraged funds and the OTC lines. SKHY trades on OTCQX and KORU and NBIL are small funds, and a sale of their full size may move the price. The audit's order instructions use limit orders with a floor below the close, and they note where the size is large enough to need care. The costs are a reason to prefer orders placed in the first hour after the open only where the spread is narrow, and a reason to check the sell price on the broker's screen before accepting a fill.

The funnel has said which names can be bought and what the costs look like. The next chapter adds the money: every sale and buy, in order, to show that the cash and the sleeves reconcile.

5 Master capital reallocation: the money bridge

The money bridge is where the report proves its own arithmetic. It starts from the cash you hold today, adds what the sells release, subtracts what the buys cost, and arrives at the cash you would hold after the plan. It then sets each position's value before and after the plan, and it checks that the totals agree with the holdings chapter and with the picker's chapter. Every dollar either moves or stays. If the bridge does not reconcile, the plan is wrong, and the report would say so rather than round the difference away.

Two conventions govern the figures. Sales and buys are valued at the ledger close for the position totals, so the holdings reflect today's market value. The cash column uses the price you would actually pay or receive, which for the two buys is the limit price, so the cash movement and the position value differ by the small amount that the limit saves or costs. That difference is $27.93 for the two buys, and it appears on the net-worth line rather than being hidden in a position. Fees and taxes are not deducted, for the reasons given in Chapter 4.

5.3 Scenarios: what could happen next

A plan is only as good as its response to the outcomes it did not expect. Three scenarios test the plan against the outcomes the reader is most likely to face. Each uses the ledger's values and simple, stated assumptions. None is a forecast of the market.

In the first scenario, every stop on the retained positions is hit on the same day. The loss is the money at risk on the post-plan book, which is $7,038.29, or 7.9 percent of net worth. That is a severe outcome, because it assumes the price falls to every stop at once and does not gap through them. It is a different measure from the market-fall scenario below, which counts a market move rather than stops. It is useful as a ceiling on what the stops protect against. Net worth would then sit near $82,592, with the cash unchanged.

In the second scenario, the market falls 10 percent over a short period. The Core and Tactical holdings are assumed to fall in line with the index, which means a loss of about $5,507 on the $55,072 they represent after the plan. The leveraged funds are assumed to move by their stated multiple of that fall. Their combined post-plan value of $10,542.25 carries a leveraged exposure of about $25,078, so the loss is about $2,508. The total is about $8,015, or 8.9 percent of net worth. This is roughly the same size as the stop scenario, and it shows that the leveraged band is what makes the book sensitive to a fall. Had the leveraged sleeve stayed at its pre-plan size, the same fall would have cost about $5,650 on the leveraged slice alone, more than twice the $2,508 now expected.

In the third scenario, none of the buys fill. The cash stays at $21,560.34, or 24.1 percent of net worth, and the book is more defensive than planned. The Core sleeve remains at 42.7 percent and the Tactical sleeve at 13.7 percent. The leveraged sleeve is unchanged from the plan at 11.8 percent. The result is a book that is protected but under-invested, and the report regards that as an acceptable outcome, since the buys were small and were limited to names that the rules pass.

A bear case from the red-team review tests the two new buys with a five percent gap through each stop. Both fill at their limits and gap, which would cost about $598 on the $4,500 of new money, a loss of 13.3 percent on that money. Without the gap, a stop-out costs about 8.7 percent. The gap is an assumption, not a forecast, and it is the reason the buys are held to the smallest size the gates allow.

What would change this view:

  • If the market falls 10 percent, the expected loss is about $8,000 on the post-plan book, and the Chapter 2 stops govern each position.
  • If the buys do not fill within ten sessions, the cash reserve stays above the ceiling and the buys are re-evaluated against the gates on the next run.
  • If a stop gaps through its level at the open, the sale is made at the open and the loss is taken against the plan.

6 Orders: the plan in the order to place it

This chapter turns the plan into instructions you can carry out at the broker. The orders are in the order they should be placed, and each has a trigger, an expiry and a note on what to do if it does not fill. The sells come first, because they reduce risk and they release the cash. The buys follow, and they are small and limited to names that pass the gates. The stops come last, because a stop can only be set once the position it protects exists. Every order in this chapter is the Chapter 5 delta: nothing is sold or bought that the bridge does not show.

The reference prices are the ledger closes for 12 October 2026. The orders are limit orders with a floor just below the close, so that a sale fills at or near today's price and does not sell into a sudden fall at any price. The limit is a guard, not a prediction. If the market opens below the limit, the audit's rule for an exit is that the sale proceeds at the open, because a stop or an exit rule is not overridden by a limit. The buys work the other way: if the stock opens above the limit, the buy is cancelled and not chased.

7 Rulebook and monitoring

The plan rests on a small set of rules, and the reader should be able to restate them. This chapter sets the rules out with the current numbers, lists the levels and dates that would change the plan, names the largest risks, and says how often the audit should be run. The rules do not change between runs. The numbers in them do, because they come from the ledger on each run.

The bucket rules are as follows. A Core Alpha name must have a return on invested capital above 16 percent, strong free-cash conversion and a passing record gate, and its reward-to-risk must reach 3.0. Its stop is the lower of the 20-day swing low and the 50-day or 200-day average, minus one ATR. A Tactical Momentum name must be in Stage 2 with positive relative strength, its reward-to-risk must reach 3.5, and its stop is the lower of the swing low and the 20-day or 50-day average, minus half an ATR. A leveraged fund is exited when it is in Stage 1 or Stage 4, below its 200-day average, and down more than 15 percent in a month. It is trimmed by half when it sits in Stage 3 below its 50-day average. A Stage 4 Tactical holding is exited. A Core holding in Stage 4 is held with its hard stop. The 20 percent concentration limit applies to any single stock, and no name is above it today.

A leveraged holding above its band is trimmed to bring the sleeve within 15 percent. A Core holding in Stage 3 is reviewed for a trim, and a close below its 50-day average is the trigger for that review. The bands are the policy. Core Alpha should hold 35 to 45 percent of net worth, at 44.8 percent after the plan. Tactical Momentum should hold 40 to 55 percent, at 16.7 percent after the plan. The leveraged satellite should hold 5 to 15 percent, at 11.8 percent after the plan. Cash should hold 5 to 10 percent, and it holds 19.0 percent after the plan, which is an exception that Chapter 3 explains. The regime sets the posture, and today's posture is normal deployment restricted to leaders, with no chasing.

7.3 How often to run the audit

The audit should be re-run after each set of orders fills, and in any case after the 28 October results, because they land on three of the retained holdings and on the regime's narrowest names. A full run, with the research pass and the reviewer gate, takes the same process each time. A lighter check of the stops and bands can be made weekly. A stop that triggers is not waited on: it is executed, and the audit is re-run on the next business day to record the outcome. Each run should also check the research record for the dates it left as N/A, because a fresh date can change a gate: an earnings date that falls inside a position's holding horizon is a reason to tighten its stop before the date, not after it.

The rules are the same whatever the market does. The numbers change with each run, and the next run will say whether the plan above held.

Glossary

A short list of the terms used in this report, in plain words. Each one appears in the primer as well. A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of sessions, and it works like a trend line. A Stage is one of four trend classes: 1 is basing after a fall, 2 is rising, 3 is topping, and 4 is falling. RSI is a momentum gauge between 0 and 100, where readings above 70 mean overheated and below 30 mean sold hard. ATR, the average true range, is the typical daily price move used to size stops. A stop is the price at which the rule says to sell. Reward-to-risk is the distance to the target divided by the distance to the stop. Breadth is the share of stocks above their own 50-day average. A distribution day is a session where the index falls on heavier volume. A limit order buys or sells only at a stated price or better. A good-till-cancelled order stays open until it fills or you cancel it. A leveraged fund multiplies the day's move of an index, resets each day, and can diverge from the multiple over longer periods. A LEAPS option is a long-dated option. Concentration is the share of net worth in one name, and the limit here is 20 percent. Money at risk is the loss if every stop is hit at once.
