Three independent analysts produced the work. The market analyst sets the regime, the label for the market's current mood. The holdings auditor judges every position you own against the rules and assigns a label. The greenfield picker ignores what you own and asks what a cash-only investor would buy today. It ignores your holdings on purpose, so that old purchases, tax habits and attachment cannot bend its list. Where the auditor and the picker agree, confidence rises; where they differ, the report says why.

Each holding receives one label. EXIT means sell everything: it applies when a stock is in Stage 4 (price under a falling 50-day average that is itself under a falling 200-day average) and fails Test C, the check for a recovery above a 63-day floor, or when its record sets size at zero (Tier 0). TRIM means sell part: it applies when a weight sits above its ceiling, when a look-through pushes one company past the 15% single-stock cap, or when total money at risk breaches the 12% heat cap. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep the shares, add nothing, and sell all of them if the stop price is hit. STAGED BUY is a pre-set limit order for a greenfield pick, armed only under stated conditions. SELL, for an options line, applies under the kill rules: down 50% with a broken trend, the stock closing under its 200-day average, or delta under 0.25 with fewer than 120 days left.

A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, like a thermometer reading smoothed over a week; the 50-day and 200-day are the ones used here. Stage 1 to 4 are four seasons: basing in winter, markup in spring, topping in summer, markdown in autumn. RSI is a speedometer from 0 to 100 for how fast price moved recently; above 70 is hot, below 30 is stretched downward. ATR (average true range) is a stock's normal daily wobble, the average distance it travels in a day, which is why stops sit a few ATRs from support instead of a few cents. Volume is how many shares traded, and it shows how many people stand behind a move.

A stop-loss is an exit price decided in advance, while you are calm. For Core Alpha names it sits below the lower of the 20-day swing low or the 50-day average, minus 1.0 ATR; for Tactical Momentum names it sits below the lower of the 20-day swing low or the 20-day or 50-day average, minus 0.5 ATR. Reward-to-risk compares the gain to the target with the loss to the stop, and the floors are 2.0 to 1 for Core and 2.5 to 1 for Tactical and Turnaround. Position size is capped by tier ceilings: Tier 1 10%, Tier 2 6.5%, Tier 3 3.5%, Tier 4 2.0%, Tier 0 zero, with no single stock above 15%. Risk-unit sizing makes every new position cost about the same if its stop is hit, so a wider stop earns a smaller position. Portfolio heat is the money lost if every stop is hit at once, capped at 12% of net worth.

Effective market exposure is what your portfolio really behaves like once beta, leverage and options are counted. Lockstep holdings are names whose daily returns correlate at 0.65 or higher, so they act as one bet. A daily-reset leveraged fund re-sets its multiple every day, which means choppy sideways markets wear it down even if the index ends flat. LEAPS are long-dated call options. The Let-Winners-Run rule stops a Stage 2 winner with sound fundamentals from being trimmed just because it ran, up to a 22% cap. The three-condition swap test allows replacing a holding only if it triggered an exit or trim, the challenger passed every gate within 0.5 ATR of support, and its reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's. 2.2 The Let-Winners-Run screen

Selling winners too early is the other mistake a size rule can cause, so the audit runs a screen before it trims anything that is in profit. 2.6 Money at risk if every stop is hit

A stop is a price you decide on in advance, at which you sell because the idea behind the purchase has failed. Money at risk is the dollar cost of being wrong on every holding at the same moment: for each position it is shares multiplied by the gap between today's price and the stop. Think of it as the total of all your deductibles. If the market has a bad day and every stop fires, this is roughly the cheque you write. Professionals call the percentage of net worth that this represents "portfolio heat", and this portfolio's rulebook caps it at 12.0%. On your net worth of $82,368.77 that cap is $9,884.25 (12.0% x $82,368.77).

The stops come from two places. Where the underwriting records written on 23 Oct 2026 give a stop, the audit uses it. For SKHY, FAS, ETHE and NKE, which have no record stop, the audit applies the scoring file's formula. A tactical holding takes the lower of its 20-day swing low and its 20-day average minus 0.5 ATR (ATR is the normal daily wobble of the price, so the stop sits outside ordinary noise). A core holding takes the lower of its swing low and its 50-day average minus 1.0 ATR. GGLL and KORU use their 20-day swing low, which is why GGLL's stop of $85.26 sits only 0.05% under its $85.30 close.

Options are treated differently, because a LEAPS call (a long-dated call option, explained in the next part) has no stop that reliably protects you. The price can gap, and the whole premium can vanish. So every dollar of an option's book value counts as money at risk. That single rule is why the options book, at $13,176.47, outweighs all your stock and fund stops combined.

2.7 The LEAPS options book

A LEAPS call (a long-dated call option) is a contract that gives you the right, but not the duty, to buy 100 shares of a company at a fixed price, called the strike, on or before a fixed date. You pay a premium up front, a bit like a deposit on a house you may or may not buy. If the share price ends well above the strike you profit with far more leverage than owning the shares, but if it ends under the strike the contract expires worthless and the whole premium is gone. That is why options can fall to zero while the company itself is perfectly healthy.

Your options book has ten lines across nine companies, worth $13,176.47 at book value, or 16.00% of net worth. The rulebook's hard maximum for this bucket is 10%, so the book is 6.00 points over the limit, and it also carries about 40.0 points of the 267.6% effective market exposure described earlier. The mandate band is 5% to 10%, and after the plan the bucket is $1,614.19, or 1.96%. That is below the band, and the plan accepts it because bands are limits and targets, not quotas, and a Correction is not the moment to refill them.

Each line faces two tests. The kill rules say to sell when the option has lost 50% or more of its cost while the trend is broken, when the underlying closes under its 200-day average, or when delta (how much the option moves for each $1 move in the share) is under 0.25 with fewer than 120 days left. The second test sits in each underwriting record and is called expiry-matched: take the record's bear, base and bull prices for the share, subtract the strike, and compare what the option would then be worth with today's mark. If the weighted payoff does not beat the mark, the line fails. All ten lines fail one or both tests, and the plan keeps one AMZN contract only while a stated condition holds.

Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine you woke up this morning with $82,368.77 in cash, the exact size of your portfolio today, and owned nothing at all. No Alphabet bought at a higher price, no leveraged funds you are fond of, no options that went wrong. What would a disciplined picker buy, and at what price? That question is the thought experiment behind this chapter. It matters because every holding you own comes with a story attached: what you paid, how much you are down, how long you have waited. A stock that has fallen 20% since you bought it feels like it must come back. The market does not know what you paid, and a loss you have not realised is not a reason to hold. Asking what you would buy with a clean slate separates the evidence about the company from your feelings about the position.

The picker follows a fixed selection rule. It starts from names in the Core Alpha bucket, which is the bucket for high-quality companies you intend to own for years. Qualifying names are ranked by reward-to-risk (how many dollars you could gain at the base target for each dollar you could lose at the stop), measured at the top of the entry zone, or at the close if the price already sits inside the zone. The Core bucket holds at most 10 names, so the picker stops at ten. Every pick is a limit order, a standing instruction to buy only at or below a stated price at a support level, so the picker never chases a stock at the market price.

3.1 Risk-Unit Sizing: how big a first position should be

Risk-unit sizing means you decide first how much money you are willing to lose if the stop is hit, and let that loss budget set the size of the position. It is like deciding how much water a boat can safely take on before you decide how many passengers to carry. The formula is: weight = the smaller of (the tier ceiling) and (the risk budget in basis points divided by the stop distance in basis points, times 100). A basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so 50 basis points of your $82,368.77 is $411.84. The budgets are 100 basis points for Tier 1, 75 for Tier 2, 50 for Tier 3 and 35 for Tier 4. The ceilings are 10%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2.0%, and a Tier 0 record means zero size. A wider stop means a smaller first position, because a stock that can fall further before the plan is wrong must be held in smaller size. After the formula, the picker buys whole shares only, rounded down, priced at the top of the entry zone (the worst fill you could get). If a record sets its own lower cap, for example because a report is days away, the lower cap wins.

Worked example: COF (Capital One). The entry zone is $192.30 to $198.40, the stop is $184.73 and the base target is $238.00. The worst fill is the top of the zone, $198.40. Stop distance is $198.40 minus $184.73 = $13.67, which is 6.89% of the price, or 689 basis points. Tier 3 budget 50 / 689 = 7.26%, but the Tier 3 ceiling is 3.5%, so 3.50% applies: 3.5% of $82,368.77 = $2,882.91. At $198.40 a share, that buys 14 whole shares (14.53 rounded down). 14 x $198.40 = $2,777.60, which is 3.37% of net worth. If the stop is hit you lose 14 x $13.67 = $191.38, or 23.2 basis points of net worth, well inside the 50-basis-point budget. If the target is reached you gain 14 x ($238.00 minus $198.40 = $39.60) = $554.40. Reward-to-risk is $554.40 / $191.38 = 2.90 to 1, above the Core floor of 2.0 to 1.

Second example: a wide stop, TSM (Taiwan Semiconductor). The zone top is $410.00 and the stop is $359.92, a distance of $50.08, which is 12.21% or 1,221 basis points. The formula gives 50 / 1,221 = 4.10%, which is above the 3.5% ceiling, so the ceiling would bind at 3.50% ($2,882.91). The TSM record goes further and caps the first purchase at 2.5% until the Q4 report: 2.5% of net worth is $2,059.22. A sixth share would take the cost to $2,460.00 (2.99%), over the cap, so the picker buys 5 whole shares for 5 x $410.00 = $2,050.00 (2.49%). Loss at the stop is 5 x $50.08 = $250.40, or 30.4 basis points. For a Tier 3 name the formula itself starts to shrink the position only when the stop sits further than 14.29% from the entry (50 / 3.5), so even NVDA with a 12.48% stop still reaches its ceiling.

Heat is the third number the picker watches. It is the total dollars you would lose if every stop on the list were hit on the same day, shown as a share of net worth, and the policy caps it at 12.0%. Adding the ten loss-at-stop figures gives $1,772.88, or 2.15% of net worth, far under the cap. Chapter 4: US Shortlist Strategy Funnel & Deep Underwriting Pipeline

A funnel is a sorting tool: many names go in at the wide top, and only a few come out of the narrow bottom. The funnel in this chapter does the sorting for the greenfield exercise, the thought experiment in which your portfolio is all cash and a disciplined picker may buy only what the written rules allow. It matters to a beginner because it shows the difference between a stock that is bad and a stock that is merely expensive today. The first kind is thrown out. The second kind is given a price at which it would be welcome and then left alone until the market offers that price.

Each candidate passes through three gates in a fixed order. Layer 1 is the financial and technical gate. It checks the business against hard red lines (profitability floors, debt limits, share-based pay) and checks the chart against the four seasons of a stock, called Stages 1 to 4. A name that breaks a red line is rejected. Layer 2 asks company-specific questions, called KPIs (key performance indicators), that suit the kind of business it is: for a bank it is credit losses and capital, for a software firm it is recurring revenue. Each KPI is scored PASS 1.0, WATCH 0.5 or FAIL 0.0, and a name qualifies with 3.0 of 5.0 and at most one FAIL. Layer 3 turns the survivor into an order: a limit price near support, a stop, and a position size from risk-unit sizing, which means the size is set by how much money you lose if the stop is hit rather than by how much you like the name.

One routing rule keeps the funnel honest about timing. Only a Stage 4 chart (price under a falling 50-day average that sits under a falling 200-day average) combined with a failed Test C is a technical disqualifier. A Stage 3 pullback, a Stage 1 base or a Stage 2 name that has run too far above support is not rejected; it is routed to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, meaning it waits for a better price. Today, with the market in Correction (below 50-DMA, above 200-DMA), ten names came out of the bottom as picks, nine of them as staged buys (GOOG is already held), each an armed limit order that fills only at its zone price. Twenty-five Core and Tactical names were cut and watched, along with one leveraged duplicate. Nothing here is a buy at the current price: it is a list of where the picker would be willing to act.

The momentum gate is the check that sits between the chart and the order. Chapter 5: Master Capital Reallocation & Money Bridge (Holding vs Target)

A plan that says "sell this, trim that, buy later" is only useful if you can see where the money goes. The money bridge is that view. Every dollar that leaves a position can be followed to where it lands, and the book has to balance at both ends: what you own before the plan, and what you own after it. If a dollar disappears between the two, something is wrong with the plan or with the arithmetic. Think of it like a household budget after a house sale: the price you receive has to show up in the bank, in the new home, or in the repair fund, and the totals must agree.

Three conventions apply to every figure in this chapter, and you should keep them in mind when you read the tables. First, all values are taken at the 23 Oct close, the latest finished trading session. Second, sales are valued at that close even though the real fills will differ by the bid-ask spread (the small gap between what buyers offer and sellers ask) and by the limit prices on the orders, which are mostly set above the close. Third, the options are valued at the book values in the options file, and those values are stale for several lines because live prices were not available when the file was written. The plan books them at those numbers and says so. The staged buys, nine limit orders that wait for better conditions, are armed but are not counted in the after-plan cash.

Chapter 6: 3-Phase Actionable Execution Orders

Everything in the earlier chapters was analysis: what you own, how it behaves, which rule applied and what the target looks like. Orders are that same plan written in a form a broker can carry out. Every order in this chapter is the gap between one row of Chapter 5's current column and its target column. If your Chapter 5 row says 24.8076 shares of GOOG now and 16.8076 after, the order is "sell 8 shares", and the price and timing rules attached to it say how and when. Nothing here adds a new opinion; it only turns the opinions you already have into keystrokes.

The orders come in three phases. Phase 1 liberates capital: it sells and trims the positions that failed a rule or sit above a ceiling, and it frees $43,595.82 in total. Phase 2 stages limit buys for nine high-conviction names, placed only at support and only after an arming condition is met. Phase 3 is the defensive layer: every position you keep gets a stop level, so a loss has a size you chose in advance instead of one the market chooses for you. Phase 1 runs first because Phase 2 spends its proceeds, and Phase 3 stands behind both.

The order in which you place things matters as much as the orders themselves. Several holdings report earnings or hold events inside the next three sessions: ORCL has its Investor Day on 28 Oct, GOOG reports after the close on 28 Oct, VRT and SKHY report on 28 Oct, and ASX and MSTR report on 29 Oct. An earnings report can move a stock several percent overnight, past any price you set, so these names go first, while you can still choose your price. Names with deadlines in November, such as DOCN, BE, QCOM and TCEHY, can wait for their own windows.

A few plain-English definitions will make every row easier to read. A limit order says "sell no lower than this price" (or "buy no higher than this price"); it protects the price but may not fill at all. A day order lives until that day's close and then disappears. A good-till-cancelled order, written GTC, stays alive across days until it fills or you cancel it, so you must remember it exists. A stop order is a standing instruction to sell once the price falls to a level you set. A close-based stop is different: you wait for the day's closing price, and if it ended under the level you sell at the next open; it ignores brief dips during the day.

If a limit order does not fill, do not wait and hope. Most records say what to do next: replace it with a limit at the current bid, which is the price a buyer is showing right now and the quickest honest way to sell, and then let the market sweep take whatever is left at the end of the window. The GOOG record, for example, steps its limit down each day and ends with a 3:30 pm market sweep on 28 Oct. The ORCL record re-enters the unsold quantity at $124.00 on 27 Oct and sells what is left at the open on 28 Oct, before the Investor Day. A sweep is not a failure; it is the plan doing what it was written to do.

If a stock gaps past your limit, meaning it opens far below the price you wanted, the answer depends on the record. For names with a hard stop, the rule is to sell at the open and accept the price, because the stop level is the line where the original reason for holding failed. Never chase upward either: if a stock jumps above your limit you simply leave the order resting. Selling a bit lower than hoped costs a small, known amount, while holding a broken position through an event can cost far more.

Placing the orders is a matter of using the order ticket, the screen where you type the ticker, quantity, order type and time in force. Time in force is how long the order lives: day or good-till-cancelled. At Robinhood, which holds KORU, NBIL, MSTX and NKE plus the rest of TCEHY, QCOM, DOCN, BE, ANET and ETHE, the steps are the same: choose sell, change the order type from market to limit, enter the price and quantity, choose the time in force, and review. Fractional quantities need extra care. DOCN, BE, KORU and a few other positions hold fractions of a share, and brokers often restrict fractional sales to market orders. Before you place a limit on a fractional quantity, check whether the broker accepts a limit order for it. If it does not, sell the whole-share part with a limit and the fraction at the close, or use a market order for the lot in a liquid hour. The dollar difference is small, and your records say what proportion belongs to each account, so the total sold still matches the table. 6.3 Phase 3: defensive stop architecture

A stop is the price at which you agree your reason for holding no longer works. Phase 3 sets one for every position you keep, and the table includes the nine staged buys, whose stops take effect only from the fill. There are two ways to enforce a stop. A resting stop-market order sits at the broker and turns into a market sale when the price touches your level; it needs no attention but can trigger on a brief intraday dip, and in a fast market it can fill well below the level. A close-based stop is a rule you apply yourself: you look at the closing price, and only if it ends under the level do you sell at the next open. Every stop in this plan is close-based.

Glossary

Investing has its own vocabulary, and most of it describes simple ideas dressed in unfamiliar clothes. This glossary gives each term used in the report one or two plain sentences, with an everyday comparison where one helps. Terms are listed alphabetically, except that related words sit under one entry when they only make sense together, such as the stop-loss and the close-based stop. Where a term has a number attached in this audit, such as a ceiling or a cap, the entry names the rule so you can match it to the chapter that applies it. If a word in a case note is unfamiliar, this is the first place to look before you act on a verdict that uses it.

ATR (average true range)
The size of a stock's normal daily wobble, measured as its typical high-to-low move over recent sessions. It works like the usual swing of a pendulum: stops placed closer than one swing get knocked out by ordinary noise.
Asymmetry (reward-to-risk)
How much you stand to gain compared with how much you stand to lose if the stop is hit, written as "2.90 to 1". The floors here are 2.0 to 1 for Core names and 2.5 to 1 for Tactical and Turnaround names.
Basis point
One hundredth of one percent. When the rules set a risk budget of 100 basis points, they mean 1.00% of net worth.
Beta
How strongly a holding moves when the S&P 500 moves. A beta of 1.5 means a 1% market fall has typically meant a 1.5% fall in that holding, like a passenger who feels every bump of the bus a bit more at the back.
Breadth
The share of stocks taking part in a market move. Here it is the percentage of S&P 1500 stocks above their 50-day average (49.6% on 23 October); thin breadth means a few big names are hiding weakness underneath.
Bucket (Core Alpha, Tactical Momentum, Leveraged Satellite, LEAPS)
A labelled share of the portfolio with its own mandate. Core Alpha is the 35-40% slow compounding base, Tactical Momentum is 50-65% for trend trades, Leveraged Satellite is 20-25% (hard maximum 30%) for geared funds, and LEAPS is 5-10% (hard maximum 10%) for long-dated options.
Call option
A contract that gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy a stock at a fixed price before a fixed date. It is like paying a deposit to hold a house at an agreed price; if the house falls in value you lose the deposit, not more. LEAPS are call options with a long life, often a year or more.
Close-based stop (and stop-loss)
A stop-loss is a preset price at which you sell to cap a loss. A close-based stop triggers only if the day ends below the level, which stops a brief midday dip from forcing a sale.
Correction (and regime)
A regime is the market's current mood as defined by the S&P 500's position against its averages. The label for this session is Correction (below 50-DMA, above 200-DMA), a pullback inside a longer uptrend, and the rules respond by lifting cash and buying only Stage 2 names at support.
Cost basis
What you paid for a holding, including the price of each lot. It is the starting line for measuring profit or loss and, later, for working out tax.
Daily-reset leveraged fund
A fund that aims for two or three times the market's daily move and re-sets that target every day. Because it re-sets, it loses ground in choppy, sideways markets, like a treadmill that speeds up and slows down under your feet; GGLL, KORU, SPXL, TQQQ, UDOW, FAS and MSTX are of this kind.
Delta
How many dollars an option gains when the stock gains one dollar. A delta of 0.50 means the option moves about half as much as the shares. A delta under 0.25 with fewer than 120 days left triggers a kill rule.
Distribution day
A day when the market falls on higher volume than the day before, a sign that large investors are selling. Five of them in 25 sessions, as on 23 October, count as a warning.
Effective market exposure
How much of the market's movement your portfolio really carries, after adjusting every holding for its beta, any leverage and the options. At 267.6% your book moves like 2.68 times the market; after the plan it is 119.0%.
Free-cash-flow margin
The share of a company's sales left as real cash after running costs and investment. A higher margin means the business can pay for its own growth, much as a shop that keeps more of each sale can restock without borrowing.
Gamma wall (call wall, put wall, gamma flip)
Price levels in an index where option dealers' hedging tends to slow or speed price moves. The put wall (7,625 for the S&P 500) is a floor-like level, the call wall a ceiling-like one, and the gamma flip is the line below which hedging adds to swings instead of damping them.
Limit order (good-till-cancelled)
An order that buys or sells only at your chosen price or better. A good-till-cancelled order stays live until it fills or you cancel it, so you can name your price once and walk away.
Heat
The total money you would lose if every stop-loss in the portfolio were hit, shown as a percentage of net worth. The cap is 12.0%; today it is 23.22% and the plan brings it to 5.91%.
Kill rule
A preset condition that forces you to close an option line, such as a loss of 50% or more with a broken trend, an underlying close under its 200-day average, or delta under 0.25 with fewer than 120 days left. It exists so that hope never makes the decision.
Let-Winners-Run rule
A protection against trimming a strong Stage 2 winner just because it looks overbought or has grown large. If its fundamentals are intact it may run to 22% of net worth while the stop trails upward.
Lockstep
Holdings whose one-year daily returns have a correlation of 0.65 or higher, so they tend to rise and fall together. Holding eight lockstep names is closer to holding one big bet than eight small ones.
Look-through
Counting what a fund really owns, not just the fund's name. GGLL is a 2X fund on Alphabet, so Alphabet's true weight is its direct 9.56% plus twice GGLL's 2.77%, or 15.10%, above the 15% hard cap.
Momentum gate (Tests A, B, C)
Three pass-or-fail checks on a stock's trend and momentum that it must clear before you buy or keep it. A name in Stage 4 that also fails Test C is a hard exit.
Moving average
The average closing price over a set number of days, such as 21, 50 or 200. It smooths the daily noise into a line, like judging a season's weather by the monthly mean instead of one afternoon.
Net worth
Everything this audit counts as yours: shares and funds, the options book at its recorded value, and cash. It is $82,368.77, and every percentage in the report is measured against it.
Put/call ratio
The number of put options (bets on falls) traded for each call option (bets on rises). The Cboe total reading of 0.86 sits inside the neutral band of 0.7 to 1.0.
Risk-unit sizing
A way to size a position by how far away its stop is. Weight equals the smaller of the tier ceiling and the risk budget divided by the stop distance, so a wider stop earns a smaller first position.
ROIC (return on invested capital)
How many cents of profit a company earns on each dollar put into the business. Higher is better, in the way a farm that yields more per acre is the better farm.
RSI (relative strength index)
A speedometer from 0 to 100 for how fast a price has risen or fallen lately. Readings above 70 suggest a stretched run, below 30 a stretched fall, and neither alone is a reason to sell under the Let-Winners-Run rule.
Stage 1 to 4 (and base)
The four seasons of a stock: Stage 1 is the sideways base after a decline, Stage 2 the rising phase, Stage 3 the topping, and Stage 4 the downtrend under a falling 200-day average. A base is a quiet, flat stretch where a stock builds the footing for its next move.
Support
A price area where buyers have stepped in before and might again, like the floor under a bouncing ball. The audit measures how many ATRs a price sits above it: within 0.6 ATR is full size, up to 1.2 standard, up to 1.6 starter only.
Swap test
The three checks that must all pass before you sell a holding to buy a challenger: the holding triggered an exit or trim, the challenger passed every gate within 0.5 ATR of support, and its reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's.
Tier ceiling
The largest share of net worth a name may hold, set by its quality tier: Tier 1 10%, Tier 2 6.5%, Tier 3 3.5%, Tier 4 2.0%, Tier 0 0%. Names without an underwriting record get a default ceiling of 2.0%.
VIX
The market's fear gauge, an estimate of how much the S&P 500 may swing over the coming month. At 19.45 it sits between 15 and 22, so the cash ladder asks for 7.5%.
Wash sale
A tax rule that disallows a loss for tax purposes if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale. Check it before you sell at a loss and plan to rebuy.
WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, KEEP_COMPOUNDING, TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING, REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER
The verdict words. WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT means do not buy yet; wait for price to fall into the entry zone. KEEP_COMPOUNDING means hold because the thesis is intact. TRIM_PROFIT_BOOKING means sell part to bring size back to its ceiling. REPLACE_UNDERPERFORMER means sell and swap into a better challenger that passes the swap test.

How effective exposure is measured

Effective market exposure takes each holding's beta from one year of daily returns against the S&P 500 and multiplies it by the holding's weight and its leverage factor. Options are counted with a delta proxy of 2.5, a simple stand-in for how strongly the contracts respond to the shares behind them. The lockstep threshold is a one-year daily return correlation of 0.65 or higher. These are measurements of the past year, so a quiet year can understate how a book behaves in a fast fall; the plan's figure of 119.0% should be read as an estimate, not a promise. Treat the 2.68 times and 1.19 times as the direction and rough size of the change: the plan cuts your market sensitivity by more than half, and the exact number will drift as prices and betas move.
