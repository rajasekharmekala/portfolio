Three independent analysts wrote the audit. The first reads the market and sets the posture. The second, the holdings auditor, tries to find a reason to sell every position you own. The vocabulary is short. A moving average is the average closing price over a number of days, like a trend line drawn through a noisy road; the 50-day line is the medium trend and the 200-day line the long one. A Stage is one of four seasons of a stock: Stage 1 is a quiet base, Stage 2 an uptrend, Stage 3 a top and Stage 4 a decline. The RSI (relative strength index) is a speedometer from 0 to 100; under 30 means the stock has fallen unusually fast and over 70 unusually fast upward. The ATR (average true range) is the normal daily wobble in dollars, and stops are placed a fraction of it below a support level so ordinary noise does not trigger them. Volume counts how many people voted with their money. The put/call ratio is a fear gauge: puts are bets on a fall, calls are bets on a rise. A stop-loss is an exit price decided in advance. Reward-to-risk (also called asymmetry) is the gain you can reasonably expect divided by the loss you accept; the floor here is 2.0 to 1 for Core Alpha and 2.5 to 1 for Tactical Momentum. Position size is how much of your money sits in one idea, and the point of the whole audit is that no single idea should hurt you badly.

Three terms are specific to this book. A daily-reset leveraged fund (TQQQ, SPXL, KORU and others) promises two or three times one day's move of its index or stock, then resets the next morning, so over weeks its return can differ a lot from the multiple you expect, much as a car that doubles its speed each time you press the pedal also doubles how fast it can leave the road. A LEAPS option is a long-dated call option, a rented right to buy a stock at a fixed price, which loses value every day through time decay and can fall to zero. Effective market exposure is how many dollars of market movement each dollar of your portfolio behaves like once volatility, leverage and options are counted.

Each label in the audit has a trigger. Size matters because a good company in a bad-sized position can still hurt you, while a poor company in a tiny position barely matters. Behaviour matters because a position is a bet on a future, and the chart, the company numbers and the calendar of coming events are the evidence that the bet is alive. Concentration is the word for too much of your money riding on one outcome. The largest position is GOOG at 9.05% of net worth, and with its leveraged twin GGLL it is 11.72%. The three largest positions together are 21.95%.

The audit sizes positions with Risk-Unit Sizing. Every position gets a risk budget in basis points of net worth (one basis point is 0.01%): 100 for TIER_1, 75 for TIER_2, 50 for TIER_3 and 35 for TIER_4, and zero for TIER_0. The weight allowed is the smaller of the tier's maximum (10%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2%) and the budget divided by the distance to the stop. A stock whose stop sits far below its price therefore gets a smaller position, because a fall to the stop would cost more. Total money at risk across all stops is capped at 12.0% of net worth, and no single stock may exceed the 15.0% hard cap. Where the audit trims a position it does so to this ceiling, rounded to whole shares, and where a position is below its ceiling it keeps it. Rounding leaves four small residual overages that Chapter 6 lists.

Here is the trim arithmetic for VRT, a position that shows how the rule reacts to a price jump. Vertiv closed at $242.94 after a 16.5% results jump, so your 23 shares are worth $5,587.62, which is 6.33% of $88,229.73. Its record tier is TIER_2, with a 75 basis point budget. The structural stop is the 63-day low of $187.24 minus one ATR of $13.45, which is $173.79, or 28.46% under the close, 2,846 basis points. Risk-Unit Sizing says weight = the smaller of 6.5% and 75 / 2,846 = 2.64%, so the ceiling of 2.64% binds. At the 28 October close that is 2.63% of net worth, $2,325, or 9.57 shares. You hold 23, so the surplus is 13.43 shares and the plan sells 13, which frees $3,158.22. After the sale you hold 10 shares (2.75%), and the money at risk to the $173.79 stop falls from $1,590.45 (1.80% of net worth) to $691.50 (0.78%). The jump made the stock more valuable and the stop farther away at the same moment, which is why the rule asks for a smaller position.

2.3 The Let-Winners-Run test on your Stage 2 winners

Selling a stock only because it has gone up is how investors cut their best ideas short. The Let-Winners-Run rule exists to prevent that. It says a Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals and stable or rising earnings estimates is not trimmed solely because its RSI is above 70 or its weight crossed 15%. Its weight may grow to 22% of net worth while its stop trails the rising 21-day average or the 50-day average. The rule has three tests: the chart must be in Stage 2, the business must pass its quality gates, and estimates must be steady or rising. The audit applies the test to every Stage 2 holding, and it has three: QCOM, ASX and MSTR.

Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine that you woke up with $88,229.73 in cash and no memory of what you owned. Which stocks would you buy today, how many shares of each, and at what price? A holding you bought at $332.71 feels different from one you bought at $190, even though the market does not care what you paid, and a position you have owned for a year feels safer than one you have never heard of. A funnel is a sequence of filters, each one stricter than the last, so that only the ideas that survive every test reach your account. A name that fails a gate is not necessarily a bad company. It may be a good company at the wrong price, or a fair company on a healthy chart that costs too much for what it can earn. Under the scoring rules a business is "ownable" when it clears hard red lines (net debt above four times EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) with thin interest cover, a Stage 4 breakdown with no base, or fraud) and qualifies under one of three archetypes: a compounder with return on invested capital (ROIC) of at least 12% and free-cash-flow margin of at least 12%, a turnaround with improving trends and a cheap price, or a tactical breakout with accelerating growth. Among the passes, EOG is a clean example. Its record shows ROIC of 22.4% and a free-cash-flow margin of 24.5%, the recorded vendor figures are 19.75% (stockanalysis.com ratios page, stamped 4 August 2026) and 24.75% (trailing twelve months to 30 June 2026), and net debt to EBITDA is 0.23. Both ROIC readings clear 12%, the balance sheet is nearly debt free and the gate passes. DOCN fails. Its ROIC is 7.6% in the record and 7.89% on the vendor page, under the 12% floor, its free-cash-flow margin is 1.59% on the vendor page, and the vendor's net debt to EBITDA is 3.92. Where two sources print different ROIC figures, the audit uses the underwriting record, which follows the scoring rule's adjustments, and notes the vendor figure beside it. For every shortlist name the gate outcome is the same on either figure.

The technical gate tests the chart with three momentum tests. Test A asks whether the stock is already in a Stage 2 uptrend that is not stretched (the close above rising 50-day and 200-day averages, relative strength ahead of the S&P 500 over 63 days, and within 1.6 ATR of support). Test B asks whether it is breaking out of a quiet base. Test C asks whether a beaten-down stock has found a floor (a higher low, a turn in the short-term trend, and RSI at or above 32). A Stage 4 stock that passes none is a red line. GOOG is the example that fails today: its close of $321.89 is under a falling 50-day average of $338.12, which is under a falling 200-day average of $339.26, and Tests A, B and C all fail. VRT is Stage 4 too, but Test C passes, because it has formed a higher low, so the red line is not met.

The asymmetry gate tests the price. Reward-to-risk is (target minus entry) divided by (entry minus stop), where the entry is the close for a buy at the market and the zone top for a limit order. The floor is 2.0 to 1 for Core Alpha and 2.5 to 1 for Tactical Momentum. QCOM is the example that fails. Its record target of $170.76 sits under the $182.97 close, so the ratio at the zone top of $172.91 is -0.20: you would be paying more than the stock is worth on the underwriting. AMD is the example that passes: a base target of $610.00 against a zone top of $509.30 and a stop of $489.00 gives 4.96 to 1, but the close of $578.44 is 2.60 ATR above the zone, so the order waits.

4.3 The 3-condition swap test, pair by pair

The audit swaps one holding for another only when three conditions hold at once. Condition 1: the holding has triggered an exit or trim. Condition 2: the challenger has passed 100% of its gates and is strictly at support, which means no more than 0.5 ATR above its zone. Condition 3: the challenger's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's, which makes the swap worth its friction. The reason for three conditions is that a swap is two decisions in one: selling something and buying something else. Selling can be right while buying is premature, and the rule stops a good sale from being turned into a hasty purchase.

Here is the test run for GOOG and AXP, the closest pair. Condition 1 is met: GOOG is an exit because it is Stage 4 with Test C failing. Condition 2 is not met: AXP closed at $317.94, which is 1.24 ATR above its zone top of $310.12, and the rule requires 0.5 ATR or less. Condition 3 would also fail: AXP's 2.37 at the zone top against GOOG's 2.71 at its close (using the record target of $366 and the $305.59 stop) is a ratio of 0.9, well under 1.5. Because condition 2 fails, there is no swap. GOOG is sold on its own merits, the $7,985.32 goes to cash, and AXP waits as a limit order at $310.12 or lower with a stop of $290.77. If AXP fills later, that is a new purchase at the right price, not a swap.

4.4 Friction: what the sales cost and what they may save

Every sale has costs that a spreadsheet leaves out: taxes on gains, the gap between the price you see and the price you get (slippage), and fees. Selling a loser can also save tax, because a realized loss offsets gains elsewhere. The audit's estimate is based on average cost per position, since the transaction logs hold the purchase lots but this audit did not match them to the sale lots, and the logs carry no fee lines. On that basis the planned equity sales realize a net loss of about $1,724.45: gains of $1,576.43 (ORCL $230.96, ASX $563.85 on 42 shares, MSTX $341.14, QCOM $268.81 and MSTR $171.67) against losses of $3,300.88 (led by TCEHY $1,270.15, KORU $516.08, BE $377.30, NBIL $296.45, GOOG $268.39 and VRT $266.19). The eight LEAPS closes realize about $5,789.08 of loss at the record marks, which are stale.

Three honest limits apply. The long-term or short-term character of each gain is not computed, so the tax rate on the $1,576.43 of gains is unknown and is written as not computable here. Selling a position at a loss and buying the same security back within 30 days would disallow the loss for tax (the wash-sale rule); the plan buys no name that it sells, but GOOG and GGLL are both exits, and a later re-entry into either inside 30 days should wait. And slippage on the open is the main cost for GOOG: the after-hours move of more than 9% means the sale at the open will fill far from the $321.89 close, so the $7,985.32 proceeds figure is a close-based estimate. Because the net tax result is a loss and not a gain, friction does not argue against any sale, and it argues mildly for them.

Glossary

Asymmetry (reward-to-risk). The gain you can reasonably expect divided by the loss you accept if the stop is hit. A ratio of 2.0 means you stand to make twice what you risk. The floors are 2.0 for Core Alpha and 2.5 for Tactical Momentum.

ATR (average true range). The typical size of a stock's daily swing in dollars over 14 days, like the normal wobble of a boat in harbour. Stops are placed a fraction of an ATR under support so ordinary noise does not trigger them.

Beta. How much a stock tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1%. A beta of 2 means about 2% in the same direction.

Core Alpha. The bucket for long-term compounders with strong returns on capital and cash flow, intended to be held for years.

Daily-reset leveraged fund. A fund that aims to deliver a multiple (2x or 3x) of one day's return of an index or stock, then resets. Over longer periods the result can differ greatly from the multiple, and choppy markets erode it.

Delta. The share of a stock's price move that an option captures. A delta of 0.40 means the option gains about 40 cents for each $1 the stock rises.

Distribution day. A session when the index falls on higher volume than the day before, a sign of institutions selling. The ledger counts them over 25 sessions.

Effective market exposure. The dollars of market movement each dollar of your portfolio behaves like, after beta, leverage and options are counted. 274.8% means you behave like 2.75 times your net worth in the S&P 500.

Entry zone. The price band at which the audit would accept buying, taken from support levels in the underwriting record.

Gamma flip and option walls. Strike prices where option sellers' hedging changes. A call wall tends to cap a rally and a put wall tends to cushion a fall, but the levels are model estimates and vendors differ.

Gap. An opening price far from the previous close, for example after overnight news.

Hard stop. A written price at which the whole position is sold if the session closes below it.

Heat (portfolio heat). The total money lost if every stop is hit, as a share of net worth. The ceiling is 12.0%.

LEAPS. Long-dated call options, usually more than nine months from expiry. They decay with time and can end at zero.

Let-Winners-Run rule. A Stage 2 winner with intact fundamentals and stable or rising estimates is not trimmed solely because its price rose or its weight crossed 15%; it may grow to 22% of net worth while its stop trails upward.

Limit order. An order to buy at or below, or sell at or above, a chosen price. It fills only if the market reaches that price.

Lockstep cluster. A group of holdings whose daily returns have a one-year correlation of 0.65 or more, so they act as one bet.

Moving average. The average closing price over a number of days. The 50-day line shows the medium trend and the 200-day line the long trend.

Put/call ratio. Put volume divided by call volume, a fear gauge. A total ratio near 0.93 is neutral to cautious.

Risk-Unit Sizing. Position weight equals the smaller of the tier maximum and the risk budget in basis points divided by the stop distance in basis points, so wider stops mean smaller positions.

ROIC (return on invested capital). Profit earned for each dollar invested in the business. The compounder floor is 12%.

RSI (relative strength index). A 0 to 100 speedometer of recent price momentum. Under 30 is oversold and over 70 is overbought.

Stage 1 to 4. The four seasons of a stock: 1 a quiet base, 2 an uptrend, 3 a topping or pullback phase, 4 a decline. Stage 4 means the close is under a falling 50-day average that is under a falling 200-day average.

Tactical Momentum. The bucket for stocks in or entering an uptrend, held for weeks to months.

Test A, B and C. The audit's momentum tests. A asks whether a stock is already in a Stage 2 uptrend. B asks whether it is breaking out of a base. C asks whether a beaten-down stock has formed a floor and begun to turn.

Tier (TIER_1 to TIER_4, TIER_0). The conviction ranking that sets the risk budget (100, 75, 50, 35 and 0 basis points) and the maximum weight (10%, 6.5%, 3.5%, 2% and 0%).

Undercut-and-reclaim. A close under a stop that is followed by a close back above it on a positive day; the earlier breach is forgiven and the stop is reset below the shakeout low.

Wash-sale rule. A tax rule that disallows a loss if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale.
