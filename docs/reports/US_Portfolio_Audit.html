Words used throughout are collected here. ATR (average true range) is the normal daily price swing in dollars. A stop loss is an exit price decided before you buy, and a limit order is an order to buy or sell only at a chosen price or better. An entry zone is the price band where you would accept buying. Stage 2 is the rising phase of a stock's cycle and Stage 4 is the falling phase, and a base is a flat resting area on the chart. Asymmetry, also called reward-to-risk, is the possible gain divided by the possible loss. Gross margin is the share of sales left after direct costs, and operating margin is the share left after running costs. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a rough measure of operating cash profit, and net debt is borrowings minus cash. ROIC (return on invested capital) measures the profit earned on money put into the business. FCF stands for free cash flow, the operating cash left after capital spending. SBC (stock-based compensation) is pay in shares, which dilutes owners. Guidance is management's own forecast, and book-to-bill is new orders divided by sales billed.

Each holding receives one label. EXIT means sell all of it. It applies when a stock is in Stage 4 (its price sits under a falling 50-day average that is itself under a falling 200-day average) and also fails Test C, the check for a recovery above a 63-day floor, or when the record sizes it at zero. TRIM means sell part: it applies when the reward-to-risk ratio at the current price falls under the floor, when fundamentals fail, or when a size cap is breached. HOLD / HARD STOP means keep the shares, add nothing, and sell everything if the stop price is hit; HOLD / TRAIL means the stop rises with the trend. STAGED BUY is a limit order waiting at support.

Ten ideas carry the rest of the book. A moving average is the average closing price over a number of days, like a trend line drawn through a bumpy trail; the 50-day line shows the recent trend and the 200-day line the long one. A Stage is a season: Stage 1 a flat base after a decline, Stage 2 a rising phase, Stage 3 a topping or pullback phase, Stage 4 a falling phase. RSI is a speedometer from 0 to 100: above 70 is unusually fast up, below 30 unusually fast down. ATR (average true range) is the normal daily wobble in dollars, and stops are placed in ATR units so that ordinary noise does not trigger them. Volume counts how many people voted with their money. A stop-loss is an exit price decided before you buy. Risk-reward divides what you can win by what you can lose; a ratio of 2.5 to 1 means $2.50 of possible gain for each $1.00 at risk. Position size is how much of your net worth one idea may take, so that no single mistake can hurt you badly.

Four more terms matter here. Effective market exposure measures how hard your portfolio moves when the market moves, after adjusting for each holding's beta (its usual swing compared with the market) and for leverage. Hidden overlap means holdings that move almost together, so that eight tickers may be one bet. A daily-reset leveraged fund promises two or three times the day's move of an index or stock and re-sets each day, which means that in a choppy market it can lose money even when the index ends flat. A LEAPS option is a long-dated call option: the right, not the duty, to buy 100 shares at a fixed price before a fixed date. It can lose all of its value and it loses a little every day through time decay.

Three sizing rules appear throughout. Risk-Unit Sizing sets a new position's weight by how far away its stop is, so that wide stops get smaller positions. The Let-Winners-Run rule says a rising Stage 2 winner with sound fundamentals is not trimmed merely because its RSI is high or its weight passed 15%; it may grow to 22% while its stop climbs. The 3-condition swap test says a holding is replaced only if it triggered an exit or trim, the replacement sits within 0.5 ATR of support, and the replacement's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's. 2.4 Money at risk if every stop is hit

A stop is a price you set in advance: if the close falls under it, you sell the whole position. "Risk to stop" is what that sale would cost you, measured as shares times the gap between today's price and the stop. It is the honest answer to "how bad could this get if I am wrong?", because it is a dollar figure, not a feeling. Think of the deductible on an insurance policy: it is the amount you plan to pay if the stop fills exactly where you set it. It is a planning loss, not a ceiling, because an overnight gap, slippage on the fill or a repriced option can make the real loss larger. Add up the risk to stop across every position and you get the portfolio's heat, the loss you would take if every stop were hit together. The scoring rules cap that heat at 12% of net worth. Today the portfolio is far over the cap, and the plan brings it back under it.

2.5 The Let-Winners-Run check

Every rule that tells you to sell has a mirror image that tells you to wait, and a good process needs both. The Let-Winners-Run rule is the waiting rule. It says that a Stage 2 winner (a stock in its advancing phase, trading above a rising 50-day average) whose business is still sound and whose profit estimates are steady or rising must not be trimmed merely because its RSI is above 70 or its weight has passed the 15% single-stock cap. Weight may run all the way to the 22% runaway cap, and in the meantime the protective stop is moved up behind the price, trailing the rising 21-day EMA (an average that gives recent days more say) or the 50-day average. Think of a gardener who pulls weeds but does not cut down the healthiest plant because it grew tall. The rule exists because cutting winners early is the most common beginner error: the few big winners in a portfolio pay for many small losers, and selling one after a 20% gain because it feels risky removes exactly the part of the portfolio that is working. Before this audit sells anything for being too big or too hot, it checks that the rule does not forbid the sale.

2.6 The LEAPS options book

A LEAPS is a long-dated call option. A call gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy 100 shares of a stock at a fixed price (the strike) any time before a fixed date (the expiry); "long-dated" means more than a year when the contract is issued. You pay a premium up front, a bit like a deposit on a house, and you lose it if the shares never rise above the strike. That makes a LEAPS a magnified bet: a small premium controls 100 shares per contract, and the value can fall to zero. It also loses value every day through time decay, which traders call theta, the slow melting of the part of the price that pays for time. Delta is the rough share of a one-dollar move in the stock that the option captures: a delta of 0.44 means the option moves about 44 cents for a dollar move in the shares. Because of this fragility the scoring rules cap the whole LEAPS bucket at 10% of net worth, with 5 to 10% as the target band. Chapter 3: Greenfield Unconstrained Shortlist Capital Allocation (Zero-Bias Stock Picker)

Imagine you woke up this morning with no portfolio at all: just $82,368.77 in cash, the same figure as your net worth today. That figure is the $66,412.10 in shares and funds, plus $13,176.47 for the options book at the record marks, plus $2,780.20 in cash. One caution on the options part: the marks come from the options state file, and the underlying prices written in that file are stale against the ledger, so treat the exact net worth as good to the nearest few hundred dollars rather than the nearest cent. The question of this chapter is simple. If that cash were all you held, and nothing else in the world existed except the vetted shortlist, what would you buy? We call this the greenfield test, borrowing the builder's term for a plot of empty land where nothing has to be demolished first. It matters because owners are not neutral. You may feel attached to a stock you bought on a good day, you may hate to sell something that is down because the loss feels permanent, and you may hold a stock for years simply because selling would create a tax bill. Those habits have nothing to do with where the stock goes next. By pretending you own nothing, the test removes attachment, tax habit and cost-basis bias in one move, and it gives Chapter 5 a clean benchmark: the portfolio you would build today, against the one you actually have. The rules for this imaginary picker are strict. It may choose only from the vetted shortlist of 35 names. Each name must pass the financial gates (return on invested capital, free-cash-flow margin, debt and stock-pay tests), show a clean chart (not a Stage 4 decline), and offer an asymmetry floor: a reward at least 2.0 times the risk for Core names and 2.5 times for Tactical names. And it may buy only at support, using a limit order, which is an instruction to buy at a stated price or lower and never above it. Prices here are the 23 October closes. Monday's trading has already happened, so every limit order must be re-checked against the live quote before you place it.

Zero-Holding-Bias framework: the picker ignores what you own, what you paid and what selling would cost in tax, and asks only whether a name is worth buying today at a price that gives at least 2.0 to 1 (Core) or 2.5 to 1 (Tactical) reward against the distance to its stop. Each position is sized by Risk-Unit Sizing, so a wide stop means a smaller position, and the market regime sets how much cash stays on the side. A stock you already hold gets no credit for being held and no penalty for being at a loss.

3.2 Risk-Unit Sizing: how the weights were set

Risk-Unit Sizing answers one question: how much of your net worth may you put into this name so that hitting the stop costs only a fixed, small slice of it? Think of a seatbelt rule for each position: the further away the stop, the smaller the seat you are allowed to take. In words, the weight is the smaller of two numbers: the tier maximum, and the risk budget (in basis points, where 100 basis points is 1%) divided by the stop distance (also in basis points) times 100. The formula is weight = min(tier max, risk budget bps / stop distance bps x 100). The budgets are 100 for Tier 1, 75 for Tier 2, 50 for Tier 3 and 35 for Tier 4, and the tier maxima are 10%, 6.5%, 3.5% and 2%. In the greenfield table the tier maximum is the binding limit in all ten rows, because every stop sits less than 8% below the entry zone. That is a useful finding: the stops are tight enough that the stop distance never has to shrink a position here, so the weights are set by tier.

Chapter 4: US Shortlist Strategy Funnel & Deep Underwriting Pipeline

Nobody can study thirty-five companies in full depth every week, so the audit runs every name through a funnel: a fixed series of gates, each one asking a single question, in a fixed order. A funnel works like airport security. Each checkpoint looks for one kind of problem, and a traveller who clears the last one is allowed to board. Here the traveller is a stock, and boarding means money from you. The first gate is financial. It applies Layer 1, the test of a company's quality and solvency: does it earn more than its cost of capital (return on invested capital, ROIC, with a 12% floor), does it turn sales into real cash (free cash flow margin, also a 12% floor), is its debt manageable, and does it pay staff in shares at a sensible rate. Layer 1 also holds the hard red lines, which are fatal on their own. The second gate is technical, which asks where the chart stands. Stage 4 (the markdown phase, with the price under a falling 50-day average that is itself under a falling 200-day average) is the only technical disqualifier. Every other chart result does not say no; it says wait, and routes the name to WAIT_FOR_SUPPORT, which means a limit order placed at a support level instead of a purchase at the market price. The third gate is asymmetry: the ratio of what you could gain to what you could lose, measured from the price you would actually pay. The floor is 2.0 for Core names and 2.5 for Tactical names, so you risk one dollar only to chase at least two (or two and a half).

Alongside the three gates runs Layer 2, the operational scorecard. It picks three to five measures that fit that one company (cloud revenue growth for a software firm, charge-offs for a card lender), scores each as pass, watch or fail, and qualifies the company at a weighted score of 3.0 out of 5.0 with at most one FAIL. The table counts only the measures that pass outright, so a name can show 2/5 and still qualify on the weighted score; TCEHY scored 3.5 of 5.0 on 2 of 5 outright passes. The reason a good company can still fail the funnel today is that the gates judge the business and the price separately. VRT earns a 22.9% ROIC and a 25.5% free cash flow margin, which clears Layer 1, yet it stands at 1.37 against a 2.0 floor on the asymmetry gate. The business is fine. At the price you would pay today, the reward does not repay the risk. That is a message about timing and cost, so the verdict is a smaller position or a lower limit order, not a judgement that the company is bad. Prices here are the closing prices of 23 October; Monday 26 October has already traded, so each limit order must be re-checked against the live quote before you place it.

4.2 The 3-condition swap test, one holding at a time

Selling a holding and buying a replacement are two separate decisions, and the swap test governs only the second. It exists to stop churning: moving money from one idea to another costs spread, taxes and attention, and a replacement that is only slightly better is not worth that cost. The test has three conditions and all three must hold. Condition 1: the holding has triggered an exit or a trim. Condition 2: the named replacement (the challenger) has passed every gate and sits within 0.5 ATR of its support, so you are buying near the price where the stop is close and the risk is small. Condition 3: the challenger's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's, so the new idea must be clearly better, not merely different. If any condition fails, the proceeds stay in cash. Cash is a position too: with the market in the regime called Correction (below 50-DMA, above 200-DMA), the policy is to hold cash at the top of its band and add only names within 0.5 ATR of support.

4.3 Costs, taxes and what could not be measured

Friction is everything that stands between a decision on paper and the dollars that arrive in your account. It matters here because the plan sells nine positions in full, trims four, and exits most of the options book. Commissions on shares are $0 at both Schwab and Robinhood. Sells do carry small regulatory fees, and this audit does not model them, so the sale values shown are market values at the close. Selling LEAPS (long-dated options) carries a more real cost: the bid-ask spread, the gap between the price a buyer will pay and the price a seller asks. The options book is recorded at $13,176.47, but those are marks, not fills, and the options state file's underlying prices are stale against the ledger. A sale will normally fill nearer the bid, so use limit orders between the bid and the ask and expect a figure below the mark. The $11,562.28 of options the plan sells ($13,176.47 minus the $1,614.19 kept in one AMZN call) is therefore an upper estimate.

Taxes are the larger unknown. Gains on shares held for a year or less are taxed as short-term gains at ordinary rates, and gains on shares held longer are taxed at lower long-term rates, but that depends on the date each lot was bought, and the transaction log does not carry a date for every lot. So the tax effect of the plan is not computed. What is known is the direction of each exit, from cost and market value in the ledger. Six exits realise losses against cost: TCEHY -$1,237.95, DOCN -$814.03, ORCL -$412.34, GGLL -$214.58, KORU -$1,083.09 and NBIL -$460.95, which sum to -$4,222.94. Three realise gains: MSTX +$811.07, SPXL +$258.54 and UDOW +$161.75, which sum to +$1,231.36. The net across the nine exits is -$4,222.94 + $1,231.36 = -$2,991.58, a net realised loss. The trims of QCOM, VRT, BE and ASX are not included, since a partial sale depends on which lot is sold.

Worked example, DOCN: you hold 54.558738 shares at a cost of $5,899.99 and a market value of $5,085.96, so the exit realises $5,085.96 - $5,899.99 = -$814.03. That figure is before any wash-sale adjustment, which the next paragraph explains.

The wash-sale rule is the main caution. If you sell a security at a loss and buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss is disallowed for now. The window runs in both directions and covers all your accounts. The plan buys different names, so it does not trigger the rule on its own. GOOG is kept, and GGLL is a separate fund, so the GGLL loss sale is not a repurchase, but you should not add to GGLL for 30 days after selling it. The same applies to DOCN, KORU, NBIL, TCEHY and ORCL. The ledger shows $2,427.79 already realised on DOCN at Schwab, and with no lot dates for every purchase, this audit cannot test for buys in the 30 days before each loss sale. This is not tax advice; confirm the lot dates and the tax treatment with a tax professional before you place the orders.

What would change this view

  • If lot dates show a purchase of any loss-sale name within 30 days before its sale, the loss for that lot would be disallowed and the net figure of -$2,991.58 would shrink; check the lot dates before the first sale is placed.
  • Do not buy back GGLL, KORU, NBIL, DOCN, TCEHY or ORCL until 30 days have passed after each sale; the earliest repurchase date is 30 days after that name's sale date.

5.3 Scenarios: if every stop hits, if the market falls 10%, if nothing fills

A scenario is a what-if drill, the portfolio equivalent of a fire drill: you do not expect the building to burn, but you want to know the exits work. Three drills matter here. First, every stop is hit, which gives an indicative figure for the damage your own rules would allow. Second, the S&P 500 falls 10%, which tells you how exposed you are to the market as a whole. Third, no buy fills, which tells you what the book looks like if the market never gives you your entry prices. Each is shown for three portfolios: today, after the plan with every buy filled, and after the plan with no buy filled.

Beta-weighted exposure is how much market you effectively own, counting each holding by how strongly it tends to move against the S&P 500 (its beta), and counting leveraged funds and options as more than their dollar value. It is a first-order estimate: it assumes betas stay where they are, and it ignores that betas tend to rise in sell-offs, so a real 10% fall could hurt more than the table says. The "every stop is hit" figure is called heat. It is a planning number built at record option marks and exact stop fills, so read it as indicative, never as a ceiling on loss.

Chapter 6: 3-Phase Actionable Execution Orders

An execution plan is the list of exactly what to type into the order ticket, in what order, and on which day. It matters because a good decision can still be spoiled by a bad sequence. Think of leaving a house in a storm: you close the windows first, then fetch what you need from the garden, and only then lock the door behind you. This chapter has the same three steps. Phase 1 sells and trims first, because those orders raise cash and cut risk before the things you cannot control: the Federal Reserve meeting on 27 and 28 October, and the 28 October results of Alphabet (GOOG), Vertiv (VRT) and SK hynix (SKHY). Cash raised early is cash that cannot fall in price while you wait. Phase 2 places staged limit buys, which are standing offers to buy at a support price you chose in advance, so that new money only goes in on a dip to a level where the stop is close. Phase 3 places the stops, the safety nets that sell a position if it falls to the level where the case for owning it has failed. The order matters because each phase pays for or protects the next: sales fund the buys, and the stops cap the loss on whatever you keep or buy.

One date caution comes first. This audit is dated to the closing prices of Friday 23 October. Monday 26 October has already traded, but no verified final stock bars were available for the audit, so every price in the tables is a 23 October close. Your first order window is therefore the open of Tuesday 27 October, and every limit must be compared with the live quote before you place it. If a stock has gapped past a limit, that is, opened beyond it so the order could never fill at your price, do not chase it. The rule (the stage of the stock, the stop, and the asymmetry of reward to risk) governs the decision, not the old price. A limit buy at a support level exists to buy cheaply, so if the cheap price is gone, the trade is gone too, and you wait for the next audit.

What to do when a price gaps past your limit

A gap happens when a stock opens at a very different price from where it closed, usually after news or a big market move. Monday 26 October has already traded, so some prices in this book may already have moved away from the 23 October closes. The audit cannot see that, so you must. Before any order, look at the live quote and compare it with the limit in the table. For a sell, a gap down past your limit means a limit order would sit unfilled while the price keeps falling. For the exits and for VRT, switch to a market order, because the reason to sell (a broken rule) has not changed. For a buy limit, a gap up means the stock has moved away from support, so the order will not fill, and you leave it in place until its cancel date without raising it. A gap down through the entry zone is different: the stock may be breaking support, which means the stop logic has failed before you own it, so cancel and wait for the next audit. In both cases the rule governs the decision, which is stage, stop and asymmetry, not the old price, and not the wish to have the trade.

Glossary

A glossary is a short dictionary for the words the rest of the book leans on, so that you can look a term up without hunting back through the chapters. Each entry gives the plain meaning first and then the way this audit uses it.

ATR (average true range): the typical daily price swing of a stock over 14 sessions, in dollars. A stop placed half an ATR or one ATR under support survives ordinary noise but not a real break.

Beta: how much a stock usually moves when the S&P 500 moves 1%. A beta of 4.41, as BE has, means the stock tends to move 4.41% for each 1% in the index, in either direction.

Call option and LEAPS: a call is the right to buy 100 shares at a fixed strike price before a fixed date. A LEAPS is a call that lasts a year or more. It can lose all of its value, and it loses some every day through time decay.

Daily-reset leveraged fund: a fund that aims for two or three times the one-day move of an index or stock and re-sets each evening. Because it re-sets, the result over several days differs from two or three times the multi-day move, and choppy markets erode it.

Distribution day: a session when an index falls on heavier volume than the day before, a sign that large holders are selling. The S&P 500 logged five in the last 25 sessions.

Effective market exposure: the sum of each position's weight multiplied by its beta (with options at an assumed beta of 2.5). It says how much of a market move your portfolio feels: 267.6% today.

Entry zone and limit order: an entry zone is the price band where support sits and where you are willing to buy. A limit order is an instruction to buy at or below a chosen price, never above it.

Hard red line: a condition in scoring.json that disqualifies a stock outright, such as a Stage 4 breakdown with no base, or net debt of at least 4.0 times EBITDA with interest cover under 2.0.

Let-Winners-Run rule: a Stage 2 winner with sound fundamentals is not trimmed solely for a high RSI or a weight above 15%; it may grow to 22% while its stop trails the rising 21-day EMA or 50-day average.

Lockstep or hidden overlap: holdings whose one-year daily returns have a correlation of at least 0.65, so that together they act as one bet. Eight of your holdings form one such cluster.

Moving average (20, 50, 200-day): the average closing price over that many sessions. Price above a rising average marks buyers in control; price under it marks sellers.

Portfolio heat: the total dollars you would lose if every stop were hit, shown as a percentage of net worth. The cap in scoring.json is 12%.

Reward-to-risk (asymmetry): the gap from entry to target divided by the gap from entry to stop. The floors are 2.0 for Core names and 2.5 for Tactical names.

Risk-Unit Sizing: the formula weight = min(tier maximum, risk budget in basis points divided by stop distance in basis points, times 100). A wider stop gets a smaller position.

RSI (relative strength index): a 0 to 100 speedometer of recent price speed. Under 30 is called oversold and over 70 overbought.

Stage 1 to Stage 4: Stage 1 is a flat base, Stage 2 a rising phase above both averages, Stage 3 a topping or pullback phase, Stage 4 a falling phase under a falling 50-day average that is under a falling 200-day average.

Stop-loss: an exit price decided before you buy. A stop-market order sells at the next available price once the stop is touched; a stop-limit order sells only at or above a second price and may not fill in a gap.

Swap test (3-condition): a holding is replaced only if it triggered an exit or trim, the challenger sits within 0.5 ATR of support and has passed every gate, and the challenger's reward-to-risk is at least 1.5 times the holding's.

Test C: the turnaround check in the momentum gate: a floor that holds above the 63-day low, a short-term turn, nearness to support and a stabilising RSI of at least 32.

Wash sale: a US tax rule that disallows a loss if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the loss sale. This audit does not give tax advice.
